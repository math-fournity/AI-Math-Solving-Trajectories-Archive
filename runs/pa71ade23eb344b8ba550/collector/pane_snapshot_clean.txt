Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether $\int_{a_n}^{a_n+\delta b_n
} o\!\left(\frac{t-a_n}{b_n^2}\right)dt \to 0$ as $n\to\infty$.

**Answer: Yes, the integral tends to 0.**

**Proof.**

Let $r_n(t)$ denote the integrand, satisfying $r_n(t) = o\!\left(\frac{t-a_n}{b_
n^2}\right)$ as $n \to \infty$, uniformly for $t$ in the integration interval. A
ssume $\delta > 0$ (the case $\delta < 0$ is analogous; $\delta = 0$ is trivial)
.

**Step 1: Substitution.** Set $t = a_n + s\,b_n$, so $dt = b_n\,ds$ and $s$ rang
es over $[0, \delta]$. The integral becomes:
$$I_n = \int_{a_n}^{a_n+\delta b_n} r_n(t)\,dt = \int_0^{\delta} r_n(a_n + s\,b_
n)\cdot b_n\,ds.$$

**Step 2: Apply the little-o bound.** By the uniform little-o condition, for eve
ry $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N$ such that for all $n \ge N$ and all $t \in
 (a_n,\, a_n + \delta b_n]$:
$$|r_n(t)| \le \varepsilon \cdot \frac{t - a_n}{b_n^2}.$$

Substituting $t = a_n + s\,b_n$ (so $t - a_n = s\,b_n$):
$$|r_n(a_n + s\,b_n)| \le \varepsilon \cdot \frac{s\,b_n}{b_n^2} = \frac{\vareps
ilon\, s}{b_n}.$$

Multiplying by $b_n$ (from $dt = b_n\,ds$):
$$|r_n(a_n + s\,b_n)\cdot b_n| \le \varepsilon\, s.$$

**Step 3: Bound the integral.** For $n \ge N$:
$$|I_n| \le \int_0^{\delta} |r_n(a_n + s\,b_n)|\cdot b_n\,ds \le \int_0^{\delta}
 \varepsilon\, s\,ds = \frac{\varepsilon\,\delta^2}{2}.$$

Since $\varepsilon > 0$ is arbitrary, $I_n \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$.

**Step 4: Case $\delta < 0$.** Here $s$ ranges over $[\delta, 0]$, so $|s| = -s$
 and the same bound gives $|I_n| \le \int_0^{|\delta|} \varepsilon\, u\,du = \fr
ac{\varepsilon\,\delta^2}{2} \to 0$.

**Key observation.** The $b_n^2$ in the denominator (rather than $b_n$) is essen
tial. After the substitution, the factor $b_n$ from $dt = b_n\,ds$ is exactly ca
ncelled by the extra $1/b_n$ coming from $\frac{t-a_n}{b_n^2} = \frac{s}{b_n}$,
leaving the bound $|r_n \cdot b_n| \le \varepsilon\, s$—independent of $n$. This
 makes the integral vanish. Had the denominator been $b_n$ instead, the bound wo
uld be $\varepsilon\, s\, b_n$, and the integral would grow like $b_n \to \infty
$.
