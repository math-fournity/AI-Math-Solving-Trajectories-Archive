## Solution

**Yes, the converse of Lewy's theorem is true.** It is an immediate consequence
of the inverse function theorem, and the harmonicity hypothesis is not even need
ed for this direction.

---

### Proof

Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ be an open neighborhood of the origin, and let $u
 = (u_1, u_2) : U \to \mathbb{R}^2$ be a map whose component functions $u_1, u_2
$ are harmonic on $U$. Suppose the Jacobian

$$J(x,y) = \det\!\begin{pmatrix} \partial u_1/\partial x & \partial u_1/\partial
 y \\ \partial u_2/\partial x & \partial u_2/\partial y \end{pmatrix}$$

satisfies $J(0,0) \neq 0$.

**Step 1: $u$ is $C^1$ (in fact $C^\infty$).** Each $u_i$ is harmonic, i.e., $\D
elta u_i = 0$ on $U$. By elliptic regularity (or the classical mean-value charac
terization of harmonic functions), every harmonic function is $C^\infty$; indeed
 harmonic functions are real analytic. Therefore $u = (u_1, u_2)$ is a $C^\infty
$ map, hence in particular a $C^1$ map.

**Step 2: Apply the inverse function theorem.** The classical inverse function t
heorem states: if $f : \Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ is a $C^1
$ map on an open set $\Omega$ and $\det Df(a) \neq 0$ at some point $a \in \Omeg
a$, then there exists an open neighborhood $V \subseteq \Omega$ of $a$ such that
:

- $f(V)$ is open in $\mathbb{R}^n$,
- $f\big|_V : V \to f(V)$ is a $C^1$-diffeomorphism (bijective, with $C^1$ inver
se).

Applying this to $f = u$, $a = (0,0)$, and $n = 2$: since $u$ is $C^1$ and $\det
 Du(0,0) = J(0,0) \neq 0$, there exists an open neighborhood $V \subseteq U$ of
the origin such that $u\big|_V : V \to u(V)$ is a diffeomorphism.

**Step 3: Conclude one-to-one.** A diffeomorphism is, by definition, a bijection
. Hence $u\big|_V$ is one-to-one on $V$, a neighborhood of the origin. $\blacksq
uare$

---

### Remark on the asymmetry

The two directions of the implication are of fundamentally different character:

- **(Converse, proved above):** $\det Du \neq 0 \Rightarrow u$ one-to-one locall
y. This is **pure differential topology** — the inverse function theorem, valid
for *any* $C^1$ map $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$, with no harmonic hypothesis
 required. The non-vanishing of the Jacobian is an *open condition* (the determi
nant is continuous), so it persists in a whole neighborhood, and the inverse fun
ction theorem applies at every point of that neighborhood — guaranteeing not jus
t local injectivity at the origin, but throughout a neighborhood.

- **(Lewy's theorem, the forward direction):** $u$ one-to-one $+$ harmonic $\Rig
htarrow \det Du \neq 0$. This is the **substantive, non-trivial** direction. A o
ne-to-one $C^1$ map can in general have a vanishing Jacobian at a point (e.g., $
t \mapsto t^3$ in one dimension, or $(x,y) \mapsto (x^3, y)$ in two dimensions).
 What Lewy's theorem says is that the *additional structure* of harmonicity rule
s out this pathology: a harmonic map that is globally one-to-one on a domain can
not have its Jacobian collapse to zero. The proof exploits the rigid structure i
mposed by the Laplace equation (equivalently, by the Cauchy–Riemann equations wh
en one passes to the conjugate harmonic function).

Thus the converse is true, and its proof is a one-line application of the invers
e function theorem — the depth lies entirely in Lewy's original direction.
