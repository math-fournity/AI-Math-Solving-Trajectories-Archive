Looking at this problem, I need to find all functions $f: 2^{[n]} \to 2^{[n]}$ s
atisfying $|A \cap f(B)| = |B \cap f(A)|$ for all $A, B \subseteq [n]$.

## Setting Up the Algebraic Framework

Let $\chi_S \in \{0,1\}^n$ denote the characteristic vector of subset $S$. The c
ondition becomes:

$$\chi_A \cdot \chi_{f(B)} = \chi_B \cdot \chi_{f(A)} \quad \text{for all } A, B
$$

Let $W$ be the $2^n \times n$ matrix whose rows are all characteristic vectors $
\chi_A$, and $V$ the $2^n \times n$ matrix whose row $A$ is $\chi_{f(A)}$. The c
ondition is that $M = WV^T$ is symmetric, i.e., $WV^T = VW^T$.

## Reducing to a Symmetric Matrix

Since $W$ has full column rank $n$ (it contains the identity matrix among its ro
ws), every row of $V$ can be uniquely written as $\chi_{f(A)} = \chi_A \cdot C$
for a unique $n \times n$ matrix $C = (W^TW)^{-1}W^TV$. Thus $V = WC$.

Substituting: $WC^TW^T = WCW^T$, so $W(C^T - C)W^T = 0$. Since the rows of $W$ s
pan $\mathbb{R}^n$ (they include all standard basis vectors), this forces $C^T =
 C$.

**So $f$ satisfies the balance condition if and only if $C$ is symmetric**, wher
e $C$ is defined by $\chi_{f(A)} = \chi_A \cdot C$.

## Deriving the Structure of $C$

The constraint that $f(A) \subseteq [n]$ (i.e., $\chi_A \cdot C \in \{0,1\}^n$ f
or all $A$) imposes:

- **$A = \{i\}$**: $C_{ij} \in \{0,1\}$ for all $i,j$. So $C$ is a $\{0,1\}$-mat
rix.
- **$A = \{i,k\}$**: $C_{ij} + C_{kj} \in \{0,1\}$, so each column has **at most
 one** entry equal to 1.
- **Larger $A$**: automatically satisfied once each column has at most one 1.

Combined with **symmetry** ($C_{ij} = C_{ji}$):
- If $C_{ij} = 1$ with $i \neq j$, then $C_{ji} = 1$, forming a **transposition*
* $(i,j)$.
- If $C_{ii} = 1$, it's a **fixed point**.
- If column $j$ is all zeros, row $j$ is all zeros too (by symmetry), so $j$ is
**unmapped**.

This is exactly a **partial involution** $\sigma$ on $[n]$: a set of disjoint fi
xed points and transpositions, with remaining elements unmapped.

## Counting Partial Involutions

Each partial involution corresponds to: choosing a subset of $k$ "active" elemen
ts from $[n]$, then choosing an involution on those $k$ elements. The number of
involutions on $[k]$ is the telephone number:

$$T(k) = \sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor} \frac{k!}{j!\,(k-2j)!\,2^j}$$

Computing: $T(0)=1,\; T(1)=1,\; T(2)=2,\; T(3)=4,\; T(4)=10$.

The number of partial involutions on $[n]$ is:

$$N(n) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} T(k)$$

## Computing $N(3)$ and $N(4)$

**$N(3)$:**
| $k$ | $\binom{3}{k}$ | $T(k)$ | product |
|-----|----------------|--------|---------|
| 0   | 1              | 1      | 1       |
| 1   | 3              | 1      | 3       |
| 2   | 3              | 2      | 6       |
| 3   | 1              | 4      | 4       |

$N(3) = 1 + 3 + 6 + 4 = 14$

**$N(4)$:**
| $k$ | $\binom{4}{k}$ | $T(k)$ | product |
|-----|----------------|--------|---------|
| 0   | 1              | 1      | 1       |
| 1   | 4              | 1      | 4       |
| 2   | 6              | 2      | 12      |
| 3   | 4              | 4      | 16      |
| 4   | 1              | 10     | 10      |

$N(4) = 1 + 4 + 12 + 16 + 10 = 43$

## Verification

- **$n=1$**: $N(1) = 1 + 1 = 2$. Direct check: only constraint is $f(\emptyset)=
\emptyset$ (from $A=\emptyset$), and $f(\{1\})$ can be $\emptyset$ or $\{1\}$. ✓
- **$n=2$**: $N(2) = 1 + 2 + 2 = 5$. Verified by enumerating all 5 partial invol
utions and checking the balance condition. ✓

## Result

$$N(3) + N(4) = 14 + 43 = \boxed{57}$$
