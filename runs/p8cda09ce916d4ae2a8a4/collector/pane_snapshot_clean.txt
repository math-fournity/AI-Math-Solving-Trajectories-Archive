## Analysis

**Pointwise limit.** For $x \in [0,\infty)$, the ratio $\frac{x}{1+x} \in [0,1)$
. Hence $g_n(x) = \left(\frac{x}{1+x}\right)^n \to 0$ as $n\to\infty$ for every
$x \geq 0$ (with $g_n(0)=0$ trivially). So the pointwise limit is $g \equiv 0$.

**Checking the DCT hypotheses — dominating function.** Since $0 \leq \frac{x}{1+
x} < 1$ for $x \geq 0$, the sequence $g_n(x)$ is **non-increasing** in $n$ for e
ach fixed $x > 0$. Therefore
$$\sup_{n \geq 1} g_n(x) = g_1(x) = \frac{x}{1+x}.$$
Any dominating function $h$ with $|g_n| \leq h$ for all $n$ must satisfy $h(x) \
geq \frac{x}{1+x}$. But
$$\frac{x}{1+x} \to 1 \quad \text{as } x\to\infty,$$
so $\frac{x}{1+x} \notin L^1([0,\infty))$, and consequently **no** $h \in L^1([0
,\infty))$ can dominate every $g_n$. The DCT **cannot** be applied.

**The integrals are all infinite.** Substituting $u = \frac{x}{1+x}$ (so $x = \f
rac{u}{1-u}$, $dx = \frac{du}{(1-u)^2}$):
$$\int_0^\infty \left(\frac{x}{1+x}\right)^n dx = \int_0^1 \frac{u^n}{(1-u)^2}\,
du.$$
Near $u = 1$, $\frac{u^n}{(1-u)^2} \sim \frac{1}{(1-u)^2}$, which is not integra
ble. Hence
$$\int_0^\infty g_n(x)\,dx = +\infty \quad \text{for every } n.$$

Therefore
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^\infty g_n(x)\,dx = +\infty.$$

**Conclusion.** The Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem **cannot** be applied
 to $(g_n)$ on $[0,\infty)$, because no integrable dominating function exists (t
he pointwise supremum $\sup_n g_n = \frac{x}{1+x}$ is not in $L^1$). Each integr
al equals $+\infty$, so the limit of the integrals is $+\infty$ — which differs
from $\int_0^\infty (\lim g_n)\,dx = 0$, illustrating exactly why the DCT hypoth
esis is indispensable.
