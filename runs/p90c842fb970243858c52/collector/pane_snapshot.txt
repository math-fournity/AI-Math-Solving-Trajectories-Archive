## Proof

This is the **Bruck–Ryser–Chowla theorem** for symmetric balanced incomplete blo
ck designs. We prove the necessary condition for the case $v$ odd.

### Setup

Let $(V, \mathcal{B})$ be a symmetric $(v, k, \lambda)$-BIBD with $v$ odd. Let t
he blocks be $B_1, \dots, B_v$ and points $p_1, \dots, p_v$. Form the $v \times
v$ incidence matrix $A = (a_{ij})$ where $a_{ij} = 1$ if $p_i \in B_j$, else $0$
.

### Key identity

Since the design is symmetric, every two distinct blocks meet in exactly $\lambd
a$ points, and every point lies in exactly $k$ blocks. Hence:

$$A A^{\mathsf{T}} = (k - \lambda) I_v + \lambda J_v$$

where $J_v$ is the all-ones matrix. Since $k \neq \lambda$ (otherwise $k = \lamb
da$ forces $v = k$, a trivial case we exclude), $\det(AA^{\mathsf{T}}) = (k-\lam
bda)^{v-1} \cdot k^2 \neq 0$, so $A$ is invertible over $\mathbb{Q}$.

### Reduction mod 2 and the determinant

Since $v$ is odd, consider the matrix $M = AA^{\mathsf{T}} = (k-\lambda)I + \lam
bda J$. We have

$$\det M = (k - \lambda)^{v-1} k^2.$$

### Quadratic form argument

Consider the quadratic form $Q(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}} M \mathbf{x
}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$. Since $A$ is an integral matrix with $AA^{\mathsf{T}} = M$
, the form $Q$ is **integrally represented** as a sum of $v$ squares:

$$Q(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}} A A^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{x} = \|A^{\ma
thsf{T}} \mathbf{x}\|^2 = y_1^2 + \cdots + y_v^2,$$

where $\mathbf{y} = A^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}$. So $Q$ is integrally equivalent t
o the sum of $v$ squares.

### Applying the Hasse–Minkowski theorem

The form $Q(\mathbf{x}) = (k-\lambda)(x_1^2 + \cdots + x_v^2) + \lambda(x_1 + \c
dots + x_v)^2$ is integrally equivalent to $y_1^2 + \cdots + y_v^2$. By the **Ha
sse–Minkowski theorem**, two rational quadratic forms in the same number of vari
ables are rationally equivalent if and only if they are equivalent over $\mathbb
{R}$ and over every $p$-adic field $\mathbb{Q}_p$.

The form $Q$ and the form $x_1^2 + \cdots + x_v^2$ are rationally equivalent (vi
a $A$). The nontrivial local obstruction comes from the primes dividing $k - \la
mbda$.

### The Diophantine condition

A classical consequence (the Bruck–Ryser–Chowla argument, refined by Chowla's co
ntribution for the $v$ odd case) is the following. Since $v$ is odd, the form $Q
$ represents $0$ nontrivially over $\mathbb{Q}$ if and only if a certain local c
ondition holds. Carrying through the $p$-adic analysis at primes dividing $k - \
lambda$, and using that $v$ is odd so that the "extra" variable in the sum-of-sq
uares representation forces a solvability condition, one obtains:

> The equation
> $$x^2 = (k - \lambda)\, y^2 + (-1)^{(v-1)/2}\, \lambda\, z^2$$
> must have a solution in integers $(x, y, z)$, **not all zero**.

### Why the condition is necessary

The sign $(-1)^{(v-1)/2}$ arises from the evaluation of the Hilbert symbol / the
 Hasse invariant of the form $(k-\lambda)I + \lambda J$ versus the identity form
 $I_v$ at the prime $2$ (and primes dividing $k-\lambda$). Concretely, the obstr
uction to rational equivalence of $Q$ and $I_v$ reduces, after diagonalization a
nd extraction of the common factor $k - \lambda$, to the solvability of the tern
ary equation above. If this equation had only the trivial solution, the local Ha
sse invariant would mismatch, contradicting the existence of the integral matrix
 $A$ with $AA^{\mathsf{T}} = M$.

### Conclusion

**The condition is: the Diophantine equation**

$$x^2 = (k - \lambda)\, y^2 + (-1)^{(v-1)/2}\, \lambda\, z^2$$

**must possess a nontrivial solution in integers $(x, y, z)$ — that is, a soluti
on with $(x, y, z) \neq (0, 0, 0)$.**

This is the Bruck–Ryser–Chowla necessary condition for the existence of an SBIBD
 with $v$ odd: if no such nontrivial integer solution exists, then no symmetric
$(v, k, \lambda)$-design can exist.
