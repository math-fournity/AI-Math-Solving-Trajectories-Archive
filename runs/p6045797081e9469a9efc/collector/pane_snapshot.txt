## Proof

By the Riesz representation theorem, since $f: H \to \mathbb{C}$ is a continuous
 linear functional, there exists a unique vector $u \in H$ such that
$$f(v) = \langle v, u \rangle \quad \text{for all } v \in H.$$
Since $f \neq 0$, we have $u \neq 0$.

**Claim:** Yes, it is possible that $\|v_n\| \to \infty$ while $f(v_n) \to 0$.

**Construction.** Consider the closed subspace $u^\perp = \{w \in H : \langle w,
 u \rangle = 0\}$, the orthogonal complement of $\operatorname{span}\{u\}$. Sinc
e $H$ is infinite-dimensional and $\operatorname{span}\{u\}$ is one-dimensional,
 the orthogonal complement $u^\perp$ is infinite-dimensional (in particular, non
trivial). Hence there exists a vector $w \in u^\perp$ with $w \neq 0$.

Define the sequence
$$v_n = n \cdot w, \quad n \in \mathbb{N}.$$

Then:
- $\|v_n\| = n\|w\| \to \infty$ since $\|w\| > 0$.
- $f(v_n) = \langle v_n, u \rangle = \langle n w, u \rangle = n\langle w, u\rang
le = n \cdot 0 = 0$ for every $n$.

Therefore $f(v_n) \to 0$ (in fact $f(v_n) = 0$ for all $n$), while $\|v_n\| \to
\infty$.

**Remark on the mechanism.** The key point is that a continuous linear functiona
l on a Hilbert space "measures" only the component of a vector along a single di
rection $u$. In an infinite-dimensional space, there is always an orthogonal com
plement of any finite-dimensional subspace, so one can find vectors of arbitrari
ly large norm that lie entirely in the kernel of $f$. This is fundamentally an i
nfinite-dimensional phenomenon: in a finite-dimensional space, the kernel of a n
onzero functional is a proper subspace, and a sequence escaping to infinity cann
ot remain confined to it while the functional values still tend to $0$ — but in
infinite dimensions the kernel itself is unbounded and rich enough to contain se
quences of arbitrarily large norm.
