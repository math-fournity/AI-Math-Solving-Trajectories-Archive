Looking at this problem, I need to evaluate the limit of an integral over a regi
on $A$ defined using the angle with $e_1 = (0,1)$, using Lebesgue's dominated co
nvergence theorem.

## Setup and Key Observations

**The region $A$.** Let $w = (x,y) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$ in polar coordi
nates. Since $e_1 = (0,1)$, the angle $\alpha := \angle(w, e_1) \in [0,\pi]$ sat
isfies $\cos\alpha = \frac{w \cdot e_1}{|w|} = \frac{y}{r} = \sin\theta$, so $\a
lpha = \arccos(\sin\theta)$.

The condition defining $A$ is $r \le \sin(3\alpha)$. This requires $\sin(3\alpha
) \ge 0$, which holds for $\alpha \in [0, \pi/3] \cup [2\pi/3, \pi]$. In all cas
es $\sin(3\alpha) \le 1$, so **$A$ is contained in the closed unit disk** ($r \l
e 1$).

**Consequence:** On $A$, $|x| \le r \le 1$, so $|x| \in [0,1]$ and $|x|(1-|x|) \
ge 0$. The expression $(|x|(1-|x|))^{1/n}$ is well-defined and real-valued.

## Pointwise Limit of the Integrand

Define $f_n(x,y) = \frac{(|x|(1-|x|))^{1/n}\,\arctan(ny)}{1+x^2+y^2}$.

- **First factor:** For $|x| \in (0,1)$, $|x|(1-|x|) \in (0, 1/4]$, so $(|x|(1-|
x|))^{1/n} \to 1$. For $|x| \in \{0, 1\}$, $(|x|(1-|x|))^{1/n} = 0 \to 0$.
- **Second factor:** $\arctan(ny) \to \frac{\pi}{2}\operatorname{sgn}(y)$ for $y
 \neq 0$, and $\arctan(0) = 0$.

Thus the pointwise limit is:
$$f(x,y) = \frac{\pi\,\operatorname{sgn}(y)}{2(1+x^2+y^2)}$$
on the set $\{(x,y) \in A : 0 < |x| < 1,\; y \neq 0\}$, and $f = 0$ elsewhere. T
he exceptional set $\{|x|=0\}\cup\{|x|=1\}\cup\{y=0\}$ has Lebesgue measure zero
, so it does not affect the integral.

## Dominating Function (Lebesgue's DCT)

On $A$:
- $(|x|(1-|x|))^{1/n} \le 1$ (since $|x|(1-|x|) \in [0, 1/4]$),
- $|\arctan(ny)| \le \pi/2$.

Therefore:
$$|f_n(x,y)| \le \frac{\pi/2}{1+x^2+y^2} \le \frac{\pi}{2}.$$

Since $A$ is bounded (contained in the unit disk, which has finite measure), the
 constant $g \equiv \pi/2$ is integrable over $A$. By **Lebesgue's Dominated Con
vergence Theorem**:

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\iint_A f_n\,dx\,dy = \iint_A f\,dx\,dy = \frac{\pi}{2}\iint_
A \frac{\operatorname{sgn}(y)}{1+x^2+y^2}\,dx\,dy.$$

## Symmetry Argument: The Integral Vanishes

I claim $A$ is symmetric under the reflection $y \mapsto -y$.

**Proof of symmetry:** Under $w = (x,y) \mapsto \bar{w} = (x,-y)$:
- $|\bar{w}| = |w|$ (unchanged).
- $\cos\angle(\bar{w}, e_1) = \frac{\bar{w}\cdot e_1}{|\bar{w}|} = \frac{-y}{|w|
} = -\cos\angle(w, e_1)$, so $\angle(\bar{w}, e_1) = \pi - \angle(w, e_1) = \pi
- \alpha$.
- $\sin(3\angle(\bar{w},e_1)) = \sin(3(\pi-\alpha)) = \sin(3\pi - 3\alpha) = \si
n(3\alpha)$ (using $\sin(3\pi - 3\alpha) = \sin(3\alpha)$).

Thus the condition $|w| \le \sin(3\angle(w,e_1))$ is invariant under $y \mapsto
-y$, confirming $A$ is symmetric about the $x$-axis. $\checkmark$

**Oddness of the integrand:** The limit integrand $\frac{\operatorname{sgn}(y)}{
1+x^2+y^2}$ is **odd in $y$**: under $y \mapsto -y$, the numerator changes sign
while the denominator is unchanged.

Since $A$ is symmetric under $y \mapsto -y$ and the integrand is odd in $y$, the
 integral over the upper half of $A$ cancels the integral over the lower half:

$$\frac{\pi}{2}\iint_A \frac{\operatorname{sgn}(y)}{1+x^2+y^2}\,dx\,dy = 0.$$

## Conclusion

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\iint_A \frac{(|x|(1-|x|))^{1/n}\arctan(ny)}{1+x^2+y^2}\,dx\,
dy = \boxed{0}$$
