## Proof

**Yes**, $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1^-} \sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k x^k = +\infty$.

Let $S_n = \sum_{k=0}^n a_k$. By hypothesis, $S_n \to +\infty$ as $n \to \infty$
.

**Step 1: Abel summation identity.** For $x \in [0,1)$, the series $A(x) = \sum_
{k=0}^\infty a_k x^k$ converges since the radius of convergence is 1. We claim:

$$A(x) = (1-x)\sum_{n=0}^\infty S_n x^n.$$

Indeed, telescoping gives:

$$(1-x)\sum_{n=0}^\infty S_n x^n = \sum_{n=0}^\infty S_n x^n - \sum_{n=1}^\infty
 S_{n-1} x^n = S_0 + \sum_{n=1}^\infty (S_n - S_{n-1})x^n = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_
n x^n.$$

**Convergence of $\sum S_n x^n$ for $x \in [0,1)$:** Since $\limsup |a_k|^{1/k}
= 1$ (radius of convergence 1), for any $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $C_\varep
silon$ with $|a_k| \le C_\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon)^k$. Then $|S_n| \le \sum_{k=
0}^n C_\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon)^k \le C_\varepsilon' (1+\varepsilon)^n$, so $\
limsup |S_n|^{1/n} \le 1$, and $\sum S_n x^n$ converges for $|x|<1$.

**Step 2: Lower bound via truncation.** Fix any $M > 0$. Since $S_n \to +\infty$
, there exists $N$ such that $S_n \ge M$ for all $n \ge N$. Split:

$$A(x) = \underbrace{(1-x)\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} S_n x^n}_{T_1(x)} + \underbrace{(1-x)
\sum_{n=N}^\infty S_n x^n}_{T_2(x)}.$$

**First term:** $|T_1(x)| \le (1-x)\cdot N \cdot \max_{0\le n < N}|S_n| \to 0$ a
s $x \to 1^-$, since it is a fixed finite quantity multiplied by $(1-x)$.

**Second term:** Since $S_n \ge M$ for $n \ge N$ and $x \in [0,1)$:

$$T_2(x) \ge M(1-x)\sum_{n=N}^\infty x^n = M(1-x)\cdot\frac{x^N}{1-x} = M\,x^N.$
$

As $x \to 1^-$, $M\,x^N \to M$.

**Step 3: Conclusion.** Combining:

$$\liminf_{x\to 1^-} A(x) \ge \lim_{x\to 1^-}\bigl(T_1(x) + M\,x^N\bigr) = 0 + M
 = M.$$

Since $M > 0$ was arbitrary, $\liminf_{x\to 1^-} A(x) = +\infty$, which forces

$$\lim_{x\to 1^-} \sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k x^k = +\infty. \qquad\blacksquare$$
