Proof: Under RH, at least 40% of Riemann zeros are simple

Setup and notation

Let $N(T)$ denote the number of zeros of $\zeta(s)$ with $0 < \text{Im}(s) < T$
(counted with multiplicity), and let $N_0(T)$ denote the number of zeros on the
critical line $\text{Re}(s) = 1/2$ with $0 < \text{Im}(s) < T$ (counted with
multiplicity). Let $N_s(T)$ denote the number of simple zeros on the critical
line.

Under the Riemann Hypothesis, every zero lies on the critical line $\text{Re}(s)
= 1/2$, so $N_0(T) = N(T)$ for all $T$.

Levinson's method and the key insight

The central tool is Levinson's method (1974), later refined by Conrey (1989).
Levinson's approach does not merely count zeros on the critical line — it counts
zeros that are detected via a specific analytic mechanism that, under RH, forces
them to be simple.

The mechanism

Levinson's method works by studying the function $$\zeta^*(s) = \zeta(s) +
\lambda \zeta'(s)/\zeta(s) \cdot (\text{correction terms})$$ on the critical
line, and showing that a positive proportion of zeros of $\zeta(s)$ are captured
by a counting argument based on the argument principle applied to a transformed
function. The crucial point is:

Levinson's counting detects zeros where $\zeta'(s) \neq 0$ at the zero of $
\zeta$. Specifically, the method shows that the number of zeros on the critical
line where $\zeta$ vanishes but $\zeta'$ does not simultaneously vanish is at
least a fixed fraction of $N(T)$.

A zero $\rho = 1/2 + i\gamma$ of $\zeta$ where $\zeta'(\rho) \neq 0$ is
precisely a simple zero. Therefore, Levinson's method counts simple zeros (or at
least zeros of odd multiplicity, but the method is stronger).

The quantitative bound

Levinson (1974)

Levinson proved: $$\liminf_{T \to \infty} \frac{N_0(T)}{N(T)} \geq \frac{1}{3}.$
$

His method, when examined carefully, shows that the zeros it counts satisfy $
\zeta'(\rho) \neq 0$, i.e., they are simple. Unconditionally, this gives: at
least $1/3$ of all zeros are on the critical line and simple.

Conrey (1989)

Conrey refined Levinson's method by using a more flexible family of auxiliary
functions (incorporating higher derivatives and more general linear
combinations). He proved: $$\liminf_{T \to \infty} \frac{N_0(T)}{N(T)} \geq
\frac{2}{5} = 40%.$$

Again, the zeros captured by Conrey's refinement are zeros where $\zeta'(\rho)
\neq 0$, hence simple.

Why RH makes this a statement about simplicity

Unconditionally, Levinson–Conrey shows that at least $40%$ of all zeros (counted
with multiplicity) lie on the critical line and are simple. The fraction is
measured against $N(T)$, the total count with multiplicity.

Under RH, every zero is on the critical line, so $N_0(T) = N(T)$. The Levinson–
Conrey bound then says: of the $N(T)$ zeros (all on the critical line), at least
$\frac{2}{5} N(T)$ are simple. Since under RH the total number of zeros (with
multiplicity) equals $N(T)$, and the simple zeros account for at least $\frac{2}
{5}$ of them, we conclude:

$$\boxed{\text{Under RH, at least } 40% \text{ of Riemann zeros are simple.}}$$
