## Proof

**Claim.** Yes. If $\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n$ is $C^1$ and its Jac
obian $\mathbf{J}=\dfrac{\partial \mathbf{f}}{\partial \mathbf{x}}$ is real and
symmetric, then there exists a scalar function $y:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ (of
 class $C^2$) such that
$$\mathbf{J}=\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial \mathbf{x}\,\partial \mathbf{x}},$$
i.e. $\mathbf{J}$ is the Hessian of $y$.

---

### 1. Reformulation as a gradient-field question

Writing $\mathbf{f}=(f_1,\dots,f_n)^\top$ and $\mathbf{x}=(x_1,\dots,x_n)^\top$,
 the Jacobian has entries
$$J_{ij}=\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}.$$
Symmetry of $\mathbf{J}$ means
$$\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}=\frac{\partial f_j}{\partial x_i}\qquad\fora
ll\,i,j.\tag{1}$$

If we can find $y$ with $\mathbf{f}=\nabla y$, i.e. $f_i=\dfrac{\partial y}{\par
tial x_i}$, then by Schwarz's theorem (equality of mixed partials for $C^2$ func
tions)
$$\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}=\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x
_j\,\partial x_i},$$
so $\mathbf{J}=\nabla(\nabla y)=\operatorname{Hess}(y)$ is automatically symmetr
ic. Conversely, the question reduces to:

> **Reduced question.** Given a $C^1$ vector field $\mathbf{f}$ on $\mathbb{R}^n
$ whose Jacobian is symmetric, is $\mathbf{f}$ a gradient field?

### 2. The symmetry condition is the exact integrability condition

Consider the differential $1$-form associated to $\mathbf{f}$:
$$\omega=\sum_{i=1}^n f_i(\mathbf{x})\,dx_i.$$
A function $y$ with $\mathbf{f}=\nabla y$ is exactly a function with $dy=\omega$
, i.e. $\omega$ is **exact**. A form is exact iff it is **closed** on a simply c
onnected domain (Poincaré's lemma). The closedness condition is
$$\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}=\frac{\partial f_j}{\partial x_i}\qquad\fora
ll\,i,j,$$
which is precisely condition (1), i.e. precisely the symmetry of $\mathbf{J}$.

### 3. $\mathbb{R}^n$ is simply connected, so Poincaré's lemma applies

The domain $\mathbb{R}^n$ is contractible (hence simply connected, with trivial
first de Rham cohomology $H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(\mathbb{R}^n)=0$). Therefore every c
losed $1$-form on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is exact. Concretely, an explicit primitive is
given by integrating along rays from the origin:
$$y(\mathbf{x})=\int_0^1 \sum_{i=1}^n f_i(t\mathbf{x})\,x_i\,dt.\tag{2}$$

### 4. Verification that (2) yields $\nabla y=\mathbf{f}$

Differentiate (2) with respect to $x_k$:
$$\frac{\partial y}{\partial x_k}=\int_0^1\!\bigg(f_k(t\mathbf{x})\,t+\sum_{i=1}
^n\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_k}(t\mathbf{x})\,t\,x_i\bigg)dt.$$
Using the symmetry $\dfrac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_k}=\dfrac{\partial f_k}{\par
tial x_i}$,
$$\frac{\partial y}{\partial x_k}=\int_0^1\!\bigg(f_k(t\mathbf{x})\,t+\sum_{i=1}
^n\frac{\partial f_k}{\partial x_i}(t\mathbf{x})\,t\,x_i\bigg)dt=\int_0^1\frac{d
}{dt}\Big[t\,f_k(t\mathbf{x})\Big]dt.$$
By the fundamental theorem of calculus,
$$\frac{\partial y}{\partial x_k}=\Big[t\,f_k(t\mathbf{x})\Big]_{0}^{1}=f_k(\mat
hbf{x}).$$
Hence $\nabla y=\mathbf{f}$, and consequently
$$\operatorname{Hess}(y)=\nabla(\nabla y)=\nabla\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{J}.$$

### 5. Smoothness

Since $\mathbf{f}$ is $C^1$ with continuous symmetric partials, the primitive $y
$ constructed in (2) is $C^2$, so its Hessian is well defined and equals $\mathb
f{J}$.

---

### Conclusion

The symmetry of the real Jacobian $\mathbf{J}$ is *exactly* the closedness (inte
grability) condition for the associated $1$-form. Because $\mathbb{R}^n$ is simp
ly connected, Poincaré's lemma guarantees the form is exact, i.e. there exists a
 $C^2$ scalar potential $y$ with $\mathbf{f}=\nabla y$. Taking one more gradient
 gives $\mathbf{J}=\operatorname{Hess}(y)$.

**Therefore, yes — such a scalar function $y$ exists, and the symmetry of $\math
bf{J}$ is both necessary and sufficient (on $\mathbb{R}^n$) for $\mathbf{J}$ to
be the Hessian of some scalar function $y$.**
