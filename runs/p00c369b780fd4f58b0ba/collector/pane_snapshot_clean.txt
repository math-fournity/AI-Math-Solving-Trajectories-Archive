## Proof

Let $A = \{f \in K[x,y] : f(x,y) = f(y,x)\}$ be the subring of symmetric polynom
ials.

**Claim.** $K[x,y]$ is generated as an $A$-module by $\{1, x\}$.

**Step 1: Identify $A$.** The elementary symmetric polynomials in $x, y$ are
$$\sigma_1 = x + y, \qquad \sigma_2 = xy.$$
By the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials, every symmetric polynomial
in $x, y$ is a polynomial in $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$. Hence
$$A = K[\sigma_1, \sigma_2] = K[x+y, xy].$$

**Step 2: Show $\{1, x\}$ spans $K[x,y]$ over $A$.** It suffices to show that ev
ery monomial $x^i y^j$ ($i, j \geq 0$) is an $A$-linear combination of $1$ and $
x$.

Note that $y = \sigma_1 - x$, so every polynomial in $x, y$ can be rewritten as
a polynomial in $x$ with coefficients that are polynomials in $\sigma_1, \sigma_
2$. Concretely, consider the monomial $x^i y^j$. We prove by induction on $j$ th
at $x^i y^j \in A\cdot 1 + A\cdot x$.

- **Base case $j = 0$:** $x^i = x^i$. If $i$ is even, $x^i = (x^2)^{i/2}$, and $
x^2 = \sigma_1 x - \sigma_2 \in A\cdot x + A\cdot 1$, so by induction on $i$, $x
^{2k} \in A\cdot 1 + A\cdot x$. If $i$ is odd, $x^i = x \cdot x^{i-1}$ with $i-1
$ even, so $x^i \in A\cdot x + A\cdot 1$.

  More directly: $x^2 = \sigma_1 x - \sigma_2 \in A + Ax$. Thus every power $x^n
$ reduces (using $x^2 = \sigma_1 x - \sigma_2$) to an $A$-linear combination of
$1$ and $x$. So $x^i \in A + Ax$ for all $i \geq 0$.

- **Inductive step:** Suppose $x^i y^j \in A + Ax$ for all $i$ and all $j \leq m
$. For $j = m+1$:
$$x^i y^{m+1} = x^i y^m \cdot y = x^i y^m (\sigma_1 - x) = \sigma_1 (x^i y^m) -
x^{i+1} y^m.$$
By the inductive hypothesis, both $x^i y^m$ and $x^{i+1} y^m$ lie in $A + Ax$. S
ince $A + Ax$ is closed under multiplication by elements of $A$ (and $\sigma_1 \
in A$), and is an $A$-module, we get $x^i y^{m+1} \in A + Ax$.

Hence every monomial, and therefore every polynomial in $K[x,y]$, lies in $A\cdo
t 1 + A\cdot x$.

**Step 3: Show $\{1, x\}$ is $A$-linearly independent.** Suppose $a \cdot 1 + b
\cdot x = 0$ with $a, b \in A$. We must show $a = b = 0$.

Regard $K[x,y]$ as $K[x][y]$ or equivalently use the fact that $K[x,y]$ is a fre
e $K[x+y, xy]$-module. Consider the evaluation map: $K[x,y]$ is integral over $A
$ (since $x$ satisfies $t^2 - \sigma_1 t + \sigma_2 = 0$), and $(x,y) : \opera
torname{Frac}(A)] = 2$ (the field extension degree equals $2! = 2$, since $\oper
atorname{Frac}(A) = K(x+y, xy)$ is the fixed field of the $\mathbb{Z}/2$-action
swapping $x \leftrightarrow y$, and $K(x,y) / K(x,y)^{S_2}$ has degree $|S_2| =
2$).

If $a + bx = 0$ with $a, b \in A$ and $b \neq 0$, then $x = -a/b \in \operatorna
me{Frac}(A)$, which would make $K(x,y) = \operatorname{Frac}(A)(x) = \operatorna
me{Frac}(A)$, contradicting $(x,y):\operatorname{Frac}(A)] = 2$. Hence $b = 0$
, and then $a = 0$.

**Conclusion.** $\{1, x\}$ is an $A$-basis of $K[x,y]$. In particular, $K[x,y]$
is a **free $A$-module of rank $2$**, generated by
$$\boxed{\{1,\, x\}}.$$

(Equivalently, $\{1, y\}$ is also a basis, since $y = \sigma_1 - x$.)
