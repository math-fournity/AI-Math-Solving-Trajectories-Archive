## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, $\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = 0$.

**Setup.** The hypothesis $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ and $f' \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ (
with $f'$ the weak derivative) means $f \in W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})$. A standard Sob
olev embedding fact gives that every $W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})$ function has a repres
entative that is absolutely continuous on every compact interval. We work with t
his representative, so the fundamental theorem of calculus applies: for all $x \
geq 0$,
$$f(x) = f(0) + \int_0^x f'(t)\,dt.$$

**Step 1 — The limit exists.** Since $f' \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, the improper inte
gral $\int_0^\infty f'(t)\,dt$ converges absolutely. Hence
$$\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = f(0) + \int_0^\infty f'(t)\,dt =: L$$
exists and is finite.

**Step 2 — The limit must be zero.** Suppose for contradiction that $L \neq 0$.
Set $\varepsilon = |L|/2 > 0$. By convergence of the limit, there exists $M > 0$
 such that for all $x \geq M$,
$$|f(x) - L| < \frac{|L|}{2}, \quad\text{so}\quad |f(x)| \geq \frac{|L|}{2}.$$
Then
$$\int_{\mathbb{R}} |f(x)|\,dx \;\geq\; \int_M^\infty |f(x)|\,dx \;\geq\; \int_M
^\infty \frac{|L|}{2}\,dx \;=\; +\infty,$$
contradicting $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$. Therefore $L = 0$.

By the symmetric argument ($f(x) = f(0) - \int_x^0 f'(t)\,dt$ for $x \leq 0$), t
he same holds at $-\infty$.

**Conclusion.** Under the hypotheses $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ and $f' \in L^1(\ma
thbb{R})$, the limit $\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x)$ exists and equals $0$ (and likewis
e $\lim_{x\to-\infty} f(x) = 0$).
