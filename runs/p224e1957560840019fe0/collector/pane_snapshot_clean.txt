## Proof

**Setup.** Let $P: SO(n) \to SO(n)$ be the square map $P(A) = A^2$. We determine
 for which $n$ the differential $dP_A$ fails to be an isomorphism at some point
$A \in SO(n)$.

**Step 1: Compute the differential.**

The tangent space at $A \in SO(n)$ is $T_A SO(n) = A \cdot \mathfrak{so}(n)$, si
nce $A^T V$ must be skew-symmetric. Write a tangent vector as $V = AX$ with $X \
in \mathfrak{so}(n)$. Taking the curve $A(t) = A + tAX$:

$$dP_A(AX) = \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0} A(t)^2 = AXA + A^2X.$$

Since $T_{A^2}SO(n) = A^2 \cdot \mathfrak{so}(n)$, we write $AXA + A^2X = A^2 Y$
, giving

$$Y = A^{-1}XA + X = (\operatorname{Ad}_{A^{-1}} + I)(X).$$

One checks $Y \in \mathfrak{so}(n)$: $Y^T = A^{-1}X^T A + X^T = -A^{-1}XA - X =
-Y$. Thus $dP_A$ corresponds to the linear map

$$L_A = \operatorname{Ad}_{A^{-1}} + I : \mathfrak{so}(n) \to \mathfrak{so}(n).$
$

**Step 2: Criterion for failure.**

$dP_A$ is an isomorphism $\iff$ $L_A$ is invertible $\iff$ $-1$ is **not** an ei
genvalue of $\operatorname{Ad}_{A^{-1}}$ on $\mathfrak{so}(n)$.

Now $\mathfrak{so}(n) \cong \Lambda^2(\mathbb{R}^n)$. If $A$ has (complex) eigen
values $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ (on the unit circle, in conjugate pairs), t
hen $\operatorname{Ad}_{A^{-1}}$ on $\Lambda^2(\mathbb{C}^n)$ has eigenvalues $(
\lambda_a \lambda_b)^{-1}$ for $1 \le a < b \le n$. Thus $-1$ is an eigenvalue o
f $\operatorname{Ad}_{A^{-1}}$ $\iff$ $\lambda_a \lambda_b = -1$ for some pair $
a < b$.

**Step 3: Case $n = 1$.** $SO(1) = \{1\}$ and $\mathfrak{so}(1) = \{0\}$. The di
fferential is trivially an isomorphism (of zero-dimensional spaces).

**Step 4: Case $n = 2$.** $SO(2)$ is abelian, so $\operatorname{Ad}$ is trivial:
 $\operatorname{Ad}_{A^{-1}} = \operatorname{Id}$. Then $L_A = 2\operatorname{Id
}$, always invertible. Equivalently, the eigenvalues of $A \in SO(2)$ are $e^{\p
m i\theta}$, and the only product is $e^{i\theta} \cdot e^{-i\theta} = 1 \neq -1
$. So $dP_A$ is an isomorphism for every $A$.

**Step 5: Case $n \ge 3$.** We exhibit $A \in SO(n)$ with $\lambda_a \lambda_b =
 -1$.

- **$n = 3$:** Take $A$ to be rotation by $\pi$ about any axis. Its eigenvalues
are $1, -1, -1$. The product $1 \cdot (-1) = -1$, so $-1$ is an eigenvalue of $\
operatorname{Ad}_{A^{-1}}$, and $dP_A$ is not an isomorphism.

- **$n \ge 4$:** Take $A = \operatorname{diag}(-1, -1, 1, \dots, 1) \in SO(n)$ (
determinant $= (-1)^2 = 1$). Its eigenvalues are $-1, -1, 1, \dots, 1$. The prod
uct $(-1)\cdot 1 = -1$, so again $dP_A$ is not an isomorphism.

**Step 6: $dP$ is an isomorphism at the identity for all $n$.** At $A = I$, $\op
eratorname{Ad}_I = \operatorname{Id}$, so $L_I = 2\operatorname{Id}$ is invertib
le. Thus for $n \ge 3$, the differential is an isomorphism at some points but no
t at others — it is "not an isomorphism" in the sense that it fails to be an iso
morphism everywhere.

**Conclusion.** The differential of the square map $P: SO(n) \to SO(n)$ fails to
 be an isomorphism (at some point) precisely for

$$\boxed{n \ge 3.}$$

For $n = 1$ and $n = 2$, the differential is an isomorphism at every point of $S
O(n)$.

### PROOF COMPLETE
