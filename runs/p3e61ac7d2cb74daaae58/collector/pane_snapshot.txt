## Proof

**Answer: Yes. Each $X_i$ must be normally distributed.**

The key tool is **Cramér's decomposition theorem**:

> **Cramér's Theorem.** Let $Y_1, Y_2, \ldots, Y_n$ be independent random variab
les. If their sum $Y_1 + \cdots + Y_n$ has a normal distribution, then each $Y_i
$ has a normal distribution (possibly degenerate, i.e., with zero variance).

*Brief justification of the theorem (two-summand case, the general case followin
g by induction).* Suppose $Y = Y_1 + Y_2$ with $Y_1, Y_2$ independent and $Y \si
m \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. Taking characteristic functions,
$$\varphi_{Y_1}(t)\,\varphi_{Y_2}(t) = \varphi_Y(t) = \exp\!\Big(it\mu - \tfrac{
\sigma^2 t^2}{2}\Big).$$
The right-hand side is an **entire** (analytic on all of $\mathbb{C}$) function
of $t$ and **has no zeros** on $\mathbb{C}$. A classical theorem of Cramér (prov
ed via the Lévy–Cramér analytic continuation argument: since the product is enti
re and zero-free, each factor, being a characteristic function that divides an e
ntire zero-free function, extends to an entire function; writing $\varphi_{Y_1}(
t) = \exp(h_1(t))$, $\varphi_{Y_2}(t)=\exp(h_2(t))$ with $h_1+h_2 = it\mu - \sig
ma^2 t^2/2$, and using that each $\varphi_{Y_i}$ is bounded by $1$ on the real a
xis and satisfies $|\varphi_{Y_i}(t)|\leq \varphi_{Y_i}(0)=1$, one shows $h_i$ m
ust be a polynomial of degree at most $2$ with non-positive quadratic coefficien
t — hence each $\varphi_{Y_i}$ is a normal characteristic function). Thus $Y_1,
Y_2$ are normal. Induction gives the $n$-summand statement. $\square$

---

**Application to the problem.**

Set $Y_i := a_i X_i$ for $i = 1, \ldots, n$. Since the $X_i$ are independent, th
e $Y_i$ are independent as well. By hypothesis,
$$Y_1 + Y_2 + \cdots + Y_n = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1),$$
which is a normal distribution. Applying Cramér's theorem, **each $Y_i = a_i X_i
$ is normally distributed** (possibly degenerate).

Now consider two cases for each index $i$:

- **If $a_i \neq 0$:** $Y_i = a_i X_i$ is normal, so $X_i = Y_i / a_i$ is also n
ormal (scaling preserves normality).

- **If $a_i = 0$:** $Y_i = 0$ is degenerate normal (variance $0$), which is cons
istent, but this does not directly tell us the distribution of $X_i$. However, s
ince the $X_i$ are **i.i.d.**, all $X_i$ share the same distribution. So it suff
ices to find a single index $j$ with $a_j \neq 0$; then $X_j$ is normal, and by
identical distribution **every** $X_i$ is normal.

It remains only to confirm that at least one $a_j$ is nonzero. If all $a_i = 0$,
 then $\sum a_i X_i = 0$ almost surely, which is **not** $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ (a s
tandard normal is non-degenerate). This contradicts the hypothesis. Hence some $
a_j \neq 0$ exists.

Therefore **every $X_i$ is normally distributed**. $\blacksquare$
