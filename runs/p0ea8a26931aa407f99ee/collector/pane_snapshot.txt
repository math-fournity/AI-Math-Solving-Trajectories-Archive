## Proof

s, or proof sketches. Proceeding.

Let $f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_k x^k$ be a polynomial of degree $n$.

### Step 1. Condition 2 forces $f$ to be constant.

A polynomial of degree $n\ge 1$ satisfies $|f(x)|\to\infty$ as $x\to\infty$ (its
 leading term $a_n x^n$ dominates). The only polynomials that approach a finite
constant value as $x\to\infty$ are those of degree $0$, i.e. constants. Hence co
ndition 2 implies
$$f(x)\equiv c \quad\text{for some constant } c.$$

### Step 2. Condition 1 determines the constant.

For $f(x)\equiv c$ we have
$$f(1/x)=c,\qquad f''(x)=0.$$
Condition 1, $f(1/x)=f''(x)$, gives $c=0$. Thus
$$f(x)\equiv 0.$$

### Step 3. Verify condition 3.

With $f(x)\equiv 0$ we have $f(1)=0$, and $f(x)=0\ge 0=f(1)$ for every $x$. So $
f(x)\ge f(1)$ holds for all $x$. ✓

### Step 4. Uniqueness (no other polynomial works).

Even without using condition 2, condition 1 alone forces $f\equiv 0$. Indeed, eq
uating $f(1/x)=f''(x)$ as functions on $x\ne 0$ and multiplying by $x^n$:
$$\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_k x^{n-k} \;=\; \sum_{k=2}^{n}k(k-1)a_k\,x^{n+k-2}.$$
The left side has powers $0,1,\dots,n$; the right side has powers $n,n+1,\dots,2
n-2$. Comparing coefficients:
- Powers $0,1,\dots,n-1$ appear only on the left, so $a_n=a_{n-1}=\cdots=a_1=0$.
- Power $n$: left gives $a_0$, right gives $2a_2$; but $a_2=0$ (from the previou
s line, since $2\le n$ when $n\ge 2$), so $a_0=0$.
- Powers $n+1,\dots,2n-2$ appear only on the right and vanish automatically.

The cases $n=0,1$ give $f\equiv 0$ directly. Hence $f\equiv 0$ is the unique pol
ynomial satisfying condition 1, and it also satisfies conditions 2 and 3.

### Conclusion

The only polynomial satisfying all three conditions is the zero polynomial:
$$\boxed{\,f(x)\equiv 0\,}.$$
