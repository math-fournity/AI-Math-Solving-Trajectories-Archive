## Proof

**Claim.** For $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ and $\beta \in \mathbb
{R}$, the set $\{\alpha n^2 + \beta \bmod 1 : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$ is dense in $[
0,1]$.

We prove the stronger statement that the sequence $\{x_n\}_{n\geq 1}$ with $x_n
= \{\alpha n^2 + \beta\}$ (fractional part) is **equidistributed** mod $1$, whic
h implies density.

---

### Step 1. Weyl's equidistribution criterion

A sequence $\{x_n\}$ in $[0,1)$ is equidistributed mod $1$ if and only if for ev
ery positive integer $k$,
$$
\lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} e^{2\pi i k x_n} = 0. \tag{W}
$$

We verify (W) for $x_n = \alpha n^2 + \beta$. Since $e^{2\pi i k\{\alpha n^2+\be
ta\}} = e^{2\pi i k(\alpha n^2+\beta)}$,
$$
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} e^{2\pi i k x_n} = e^{2\pi i k\beta}\cdot \frac{1}{N}\
underbrace{\sum_{n=1}^{N} e^{2\pi i k\alpha n^2}}_{=: S_N(k)}.
$$
Since $|e^{2\pi i k\beta}|=1$, it suffices to show $S_N(k) = o(N)$ for every $k\
geq 1$. Setting $\theta = k\alpha$ (irrational since $\alpha\notin\mathbb{Q}$),
we must show
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{N} e^{2\pi i \theta n^2} = o(N) \quad\text{for irrational } \theta.
\tag{$\star$}
$$

---

### Step 2. Van der Corput's inequality

**Lemma (van der Corput).** For any complex numbers $z_1,\dots,z_N$ and any inte
ger $H\geq 1$,
$$
\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N} z_n\right|^2 \le \frac{N+H}{H}\sum_{n=1}^{N}|z_n|^2 + \frac
{2(N+H)}{H^2}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}(H-h)\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N-h} z_{n+h}\overline{z_n}\r
ight|. \tag{vdC}
$$

*Proof of Lemma.* Write $H\sum_{n} z_n = \sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\sum_{n} z_{n+h}$ (exte
nding $z_n=0$ for $n>N$). By Cauchy–Schwarz,
$$
H^2|S_N|^2 \le (N+H)\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}z_{n+h}\right|^2.
$$
Expanding $\left|\sum_n z_{n+h}\right|^2 = \sum_{n,m} z_{n+h}\overline{z_{m+h}}$
 and summing over $h$ gives
$$
\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\left|\sum_n z_{n+h}\right|^2 = H\sum_n|z_n|^2 + 2\operatorname{
Re}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}(H-h)\sum_{n} z_{n+h}\overline{z_n},
$$
from which (vdC) follows by taking real parts and the triangle inequality. $\squ
are$

---

### Step 3. Bounding the correlation sums

Apply (vdC) with $z_n = e^{2\pi i\theta n^2}$, so $|z_n|=1$ and $\sum|z_n|^2 = N
$. For the correlation:
$$
z_{n+h}\overline{z_n} = e^{2\pi i\theta((n+h)^2 - n^2)} = e^{2\pi i\theta h^2}\c
dot e^{2\pi i(2h\theta)n}.
$$
Hence
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{N-h} z_{n+h}\overline{z_n} = e^{2\pi i\theta h^2}\sum_{n=1}^{N-h} e^
{2\pi i(2h\theta)n}.
$$
This is a geometric series with ratio $e^{2\pi i\cdot 2h\theta}\neq 1$ (since $\
theta$ irrational $\Rightarrow$ $2h\theta\notin\mathbb{Z}$). Therefore
$$
\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N-h} e^{2\pi i(2h\theta)n}\right| \le \frac{1}{|\sin(2\pi h\th
eta)|} =: C_h(\theta),
$$
which is a **finite constant depending on $h$ and $\theta$ but independent of $N
$**.

---

### Step 4. Completing the estimate

Substituting into (vdC):
$$
|S_N|^2 \le \frac{(N+H)\,N}{H} + \frac{2(N+H)}{H^2}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}(H-h)\,C_h(\t
heta). \tag{1}
$$
Divide (1) by $N^2$:
$$
\frac{|S_N|^2}{N^2} \le \underbrace{\frac{N+H}{NH}}_{\to\, 1/H} + \underbrace{\f
rac{2(N+H)}{N^2 H^2}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}(H-h)\,C_h(\theta)}_{\to\, 0 \text{ as } N\t
o\infty}.
$$
For **fixed** $H$, the sum $\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}(H-h)C_h(\theta)$ is a finite consta
nt (independent of $N$), so the second term vanishes as $N\to\infty$. Taking $\l
imsup_{N\to\infty}$:
$$
\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{|S_N|^2}{N^2} \le \frac{1}{H}. \tag{2}
$$
Since (2) holds for **every** $H\geq 1$, we let $H\to\infty$ to obtain
$$
\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{|S_N|^2}{N^2} = 0,
$$
hence $|S_N|/N \to 0$, proving $(\star)$.

---

### Step 5. Conclusion

We have shown $S_N(k) = \sum_{n=1}^N e^{2\pi i k\alpha n^2} = o(N)$ for every $k
\geq 1$, hence
$$
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} e^{2\pi i k(\alpha n^2 + \beta)} = e^{2\pi i k\beta}\c
dot\frac{S_N(k)}{N} \to 0
$$
for every $k\geq 1$. By Weyl's criterion, $\{x_n\} = \{\alpha n^2 + \beta \bmod
1\}$ is **equidistributed** in $[0,1]$. Equidistribution implies density: for an
y subinterval $(a,b)\subseteq[0,1]$, the proportion of the first $N$ terms falli
ng in $(a,b)$ tends to $b-a > 0$, so in particular infinitely many terms lie in
$(a,b)$.

Therefore $\{\alpha n^2 + \beta \bmod 1 : n\in\mathbb{N}\}$ is **dense** in $[0,
1]$. $\blacksquare$
