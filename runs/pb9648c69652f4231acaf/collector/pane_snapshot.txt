Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the natural homomorphism $\
Gamma(N) \to \Gamma(M)$ (induced by the inclusion $N \hookrightarrow M$) is alwa
ys injective.

**Answer: The statement is FALSE.**

## Proof

**Setup.** For a commutative monoid $M$, the Grothendieck group $\Gamma(M)$ is c
onstructed as $(M \times M)/{\sim_M}$, where $(a,b) \sim_M (c,d)$ iff there exis
ts $m \in M$ such that $a + d + m = b + c + m$. The class $[(a,b)]$ represents $
a - b$, and the natural map $\gamma_M: M \to \Gamma(M)$ sends $a \mapsto [(a,0)]
$.

Given a submonoid $N \subseteq M$, the inclusion $i: N \hookrightarrow M$ induce
s a group homomorphism $\phi: \Gamma(N) \to \Gamma(M)$ via $\phi([(n_1, n_2)]_N)
 = [(n_1, n_2)]_M$.

**Well-definedness of $\phi$.** If $(n_1, n_2) \sim_N (n_1', n_2')$, there exist
s $n \in N$ with $n_1 + n_2' + n = n_2 + n_1' + n$. Since $N \subseteq M$, this
equation also holds in $M$, so $(n_1, n_2) \sim_M (n_1', n_2')$. Thus $\phi$ is
well-defined.

**Injectivity analysis.** The map $\phi$ is injective iff for all $n_1, n_2, n_1
', n_2' \in N$:
$$\exists\, m \in M:\ n_1 + n_2' + m = n_2 + n_1' + m \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\exists\, n \in N:\ n_1 + n_2' + n = n_2 + n_1' + n.$$

The problem is that the witnessing element $m$ may lie in $M \setminus N$, and n
o witness in $N$ may exist. This is exactly what happens when $M$ has elements t
hat "absorb" differences.

**Counterexample.** Let $M = \mathbb{N} \cup \{a\}$, where $a$ is a new element
with:
- $n + m$ is usual addition for $n, m \in \mathbb{N}$,
- $a + n = a$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$ (including $n = 0$),
- $a + a = a$.

Then $M$ is a commutative monoid with identity $0$. Let $N = \mathbb{N} \subsete
q M$, which is a submonoid.

**Compute $\Gamma(N)$.** Since $\mathbb{N}$ is cancellative, $\Gamma(N) = \mathb
b{Z}$, the integers.

**Compute $\Gamma(M)$.** For any $x, y \in M$, we show $(x, y) \sim_M (0, 0)$. T
ake $m = a \in M$:
$$x + 0 + a = x + a = a, \qquad y + 0 + a = y + a = a.$$
So $x + a = y + a = a$, hence $(x,y) \sim_M (0,0)$. Every element of $\Gamma(M)$
 is zero, so $\Gamma(M) = \{0\}$.

**The induced map.** The natural map $\phi: \Gamma(N) = \mathbb{Z} \to \Gamma(M)
 = \{0\}$ is the zero homomorphism, which is **not injective** (its kernel is al
l of $\mathbb{Z}$).

**Conclusion.** There exists a commutative monoid $M$ and a submonoid $N \subset
eq M$ such that the natural homomorphism $\Gamma(N) \to \Gamma(M)$ is not inject
ive. Therefore, $\Gamma(N)$ does not always embed in $\Gamma(M)$.

**Remark.** The statement *is* true when $M$ is cancellative: in that case the e
quivalence relation simplifies to $a + d = b + c$ (no witness needed), and any s
uch equation among elements of $N$ holds in $N$ itself. The failure is specific
to non-cancellative monoids, where absorbing or non-cancellative elements in $M
\setminus N$ can collapse distinctions that persist in $\Gamma(N)$.
