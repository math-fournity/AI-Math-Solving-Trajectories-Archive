**No.** The norm map need not be differentiable (even away from the origin) when
 it is not induced by a scalar product. A single explicit counterexample suffice
s.

---

### Counterexample: the $\ell^1$-norm on $\mathbb{R}^2$

Consider $(\mathbb{R}^2, \|\cdot\|_1)$ with
$$
\|x\|_1 = |x_1| + |x_2|.
$$
This is a genuine norm. It is **not** induced by any scalar product: indeed, any
 norm coming from a scalar product satisfies the parallelogram law
$$
\|x+y\|^2 + \|x-y\|^2 = 2\|x\|^2 + 2\|y\|^2,
$$
but taking $x=(1,0)$, $y=(0,1)$ gives $\|x+y\|_1^2+\|x-y\|_1^2 = 4+4=8$ while $2
\|x\|_1^2+2\|y\|_1^2 = 2+2=4$. So the parallelogram law fails, confirming the no
rm is not induced by a scalar product.

### Non-differentiability at a non-zero point

Consider the point $p = (1,0) \neq 0$. We show $\|\cdot\|_1$ is not differentiab
le at $p$.

The map $x \mapsto |x_1|$ is not differentiable at $x_1 = 0$ (the left and right
 derivatives are $-1$ and $+1$). Since $p_1 = 1 \neq 0$, the term $|x_1|$ is smo
oth near $p$. The issue is the term $|x_2|$, which is not differentiable at $x_2
 = 0$, and $p_2 = 0$.

Concretely, compute the directional derivative of $\|\cdot\|_1$ at $p$ in the di
rection $v = (0,1)$:
$$
\lim_{t \to 0^+} \frac{\|p + tv\|_1 - \|p\|_1}{t} = \lim_{t\to 0^+}\frac{|1| + |
t| - 1}{t} = \lim_{t\to 0^+}\frac{t}{t} = 1,
$$
while
$$
\lim_{t \to 0^-} \frac{\|p + tv\|_1 - \|p\|_1}{t} = \lim_{t\to 0^-}\frac{|t|}{t}
 = \lim_{t\to 0^-}\frac{-t}{t} = -1.
$$
The two one-sided limits differ, so the directional derivative in direction $v=(
0,1)$ does not exist. Hence $\|\cdot\|_1$ is **not (Fréchet/total) differentiabl
e** at $p=(1,0)$, a non-zero point.

### Conclusion

The $\ell^1$-norm on $\mathbb{R}^2$ is a norm not induced by any scalar product,
 and it fails to be differentiable at the non-zero point $(1,0)$. Therefore the
norm map is **not** always differentiable when it is not induced by a scalar pro
duct.
