## Proof

Since $\mathbb{k}$ is real closed, every positive element of $\mathbb{k}$ is a s
quare, and the unique ordering on $\mathbb{k}$ makes the set of squares exactly
the non-negative elements.

The element $c \neq 0$ is not a square in $\mathbb{k}$. Because $c > 0$ would fo
rce $c$ to be a square (real closed fields contain square roots of all positive
elements), we must have $c < 0$. Hence $-c > 0$, so $-c$ is a square in $\mathbb
{k}$: there exists $d \in \mathbb{k}$, $d \neq 0$, with
$$d^2 = -c.$$

Now work in the extension $\mathbb{k}(\sqrt{c})$. Consider the single element $\
dfrac{\sqrt{c}}{d} \in \mathbb{k}(\sqrt{c})$. Its square is
$$\left(\frac{\sqrt{c}}{d}\right)^2 = \frac{c}{d^2} = \frac{c}{-c} = -1.$$

Writing $\dfrac{\sqrt{c}}{d} = a_1 + b_1\sqrt{c}$ with $a_1 = 0,\; b_1 = \dfrac{
1}{d} \in \mathbb{k}$, we obtain
$$-1 = \left(a_1 + b_1\sqrt{c}\right)^2 = \sum_{j=1}^{1}\left(a_j + b_j\sqrt{c}\
right)^2,$$
with $a_1, b_1 \in \mathbb{k}$.

Thus $-1$ is a sum of squares in $\mathbb{k}(\sqrt{c})$ (in fact a single square
). $\square$
