## Solution

The power series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n z^n$ with $c_n = \frac{1}{n!}f^{(n)}(0
)$ is precisely the Taylor series of $f$ centered at the origin. A standard theo
rem of complex analysis states that **the radius of convergence of the Taylor se
ries of a function $f$ about $z_0$ equals the distance from $z_0$ to the nearest
 singularity of $f$**.

### Identifying the singularities of $f$

We have
$$f(z) = (z - 1 - i)^{-2}\cdot \sin(z).$$

- $\sin(z)$ is an **entire** function (analytic on all of $\mathbb{C}$), so it c
ontributes no singularities.
- $(z-1-i)^{-2}$ has a **pole of order 2** at $z = 1+i$.

Therefore $f$ has a single singularity at $z_0^{\ast} = 1 + i$.

### Computing the radius of convergence

The radius of convergence $R$ is the distance from the expansion center $0$ to t
he nearest singularity:

$$R = |1+i - 0| = |1+i| = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}.$$

### Verification of the pole (not a removable singularity)

At $z = 1+i$, $\sin(z)$ is analytic and $\sin(1+i) \neq 0$ (since $\sin(1+i) = \
sin 1 \cosh 1 + i\cos 1 \sinh 1$, and $\sin 1 \neq 0$). Hence the factor $(z-1-i
)^{-2}$ is not cancelled, and $z=1+i$ is genuinely a pole of order 2 of $f$. The
 singularity is confirmed.

### Conclusion

The radius of convergence of the power series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n z^n$ is

$$\boxed{R = \sqrt{2}}.$$
