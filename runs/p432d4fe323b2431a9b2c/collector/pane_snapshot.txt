## Proof

We analyze the number of solutions to $x^2 \equiv a \pmod{2^L}$ for odd $a$ and
$L \geq 1$.

**Case $L = 1$:** Modulo 2, the only odd residue is $1$, and $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod
{2}$ has the unique solution $x \equiv 1 \pmod{2}$. So there is **1 solution**.

**Case $L = 2$:** Every odd square satisfies $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ (since $(2k
+1)^2 = 4k^2+4k+1 \equiv 1$). Thus solutions exist only when $a \equiv 1 \pmod{4
}$, and then $x \equiv 1, 3 \pmod{4}$ both work. So there are **2 solutions**.

**Case $L \geq 3$:** We use the standard lifting structure. First note every odd
 square satisfies $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{8}$, so solutions exist only when $a \equi
v 1 \pmod{8}$.

Assume $a \equiv 1 \pmod{8}$. We claim the congruence $x^2 \equiv a \pmod{2^L}$
has exactly **4 solutions** for every $L \geq 3$.

We prove this by induction on $L$. For $L=3$: the four odd residues $1, 3, 5, 7$
 all square to $1 \pmod{8}$, giving 4 solutions.

For the inductive step, suppose $x^2 \equiv a \pmod{2^L}$ has exactly 4 solution
s for some $L \geq 3$. Each solution $x_0 \pmod{2^L}$ lifts to solutions modulo
$2^{L+1}$ of the form $x = x_0 + t \cdot 2^L$ with $t \in \{0, 1\}$. We need
$$x^2 = x_0^2 + 2 x_0 t \cdot 2^L + t^2 \cdot 2^{2L} \equiv a \pmod{2^{L+1}}.$$
Since $2L \geq L+1$ (as $L \geq 1$), the last term vanishes mod $2^{L+1}$. The c
ondition becomes
$$x_0^2 - a + 2 x_0 t \cdot 2^L \equiv 0 \pmod{2^{L+1}}.$$
Writing $x_0^2 - a = c \cdot 2^L$ (valid since $x_0^2 \equiv a \pmod{2^L}$), thi
s becomes
$$c + 2 x_0 t \equiv 0 \pmod{2}.$$
Since $x_0$ is odd, $2x_0$ is even, so $2x_0 t \equiv 0 \pmod{2}$. Thus the cond
ition reduces to $c \equiv 0 \pmod{2}$, i.e., $x_0^2 \equiv a \pmod{2^{L+1}}$. T
his is either satisfied by both lifts ($t=0$ and $t=1$) or by neither.

We must show it is satisfied by exactly one value of $c$-parity among the 4 solu
tions. In fact, since $a \equiv 1 \pmod{8}$, the 4 solutions mod $2^L$ come in t
wo pairs: $\{x_0, -x_0\}$ and $\{x_0 + 2^{L-1}, -(x_0 + 2^{L-1})\}$ (the two dis
tinct square roots of $a$ mod $2^{L-1}$, each lifting to two roots mod $2^L$). A
 standard Hensel-lifting argument for odd primes of $2$-adic squares shows that
among the 4 solutions mod $2^L$, exactly **2 lift** to solutions mod $2^{L+1}$ (
one from each pair), giving $2 \times 1 + 2 \times 1 = 4$ solutions mod $2^{L+1}
$.

More concretely: the two solutions $\{x_0, -x_0\}$ satisfy $x_0^2 \equiv -x_0^2
\pmod{2^{L+1}}$ is impossible since they're equal; rather $x_0^2$ and $(-x_0)^2
= x_0^2$ are the same value. The key point is that $x_0$ and $x_0 + 2^{L-1}$ sat
isfy $(x_0 + 2^{L-1})^2 = x_0^2 + x_0 \cdot 2^L + 2^{2L-2}$. For $L \geq 3$, $2L
-2 \geq L+1$, so $(x_0 + 2^{L-1})^2 \equiv x_0^2 + x_0 \cdot 2^L \pmod{2^{L+1}}$
. Since $x_0$ is odd, $x_0 \cdot 2^L \equiv 2^L \pmod{2^{L+1}}$, so $(x_0 + 2^{L
-1})^2 \equiv x_0^2 + 2^L \pmod{2^{L+1}}$. This means $x_0$ and $x_0 + 2^{L-1}$
have squares differing by exactly $2^L \pmod{2^{L+1}}$, so exactly one of them i
s $\equiv a \pmod{2^{L+1}}$. The same holds for $-x_0$ and $-(x_0 + 2^{L-1})$.

Therefore, from the 4 solutions mod $2^L$, exactly 2 lift (one from each pair of
 the form $\{x_0, x_0 + 2^{L-1}\}$), and each lifting solution gives exactly 2 l
ifts via $t \in \{0,1\}$...

Let me restate cleanly: The 4 solutions mod $2^L$ form two pairs $\{x_0, x_0 + 2
^{L-1}\}$ and $\{-x_0, -x_0 - 2^{L-1}\}$. Within each pair, exactly one member h
as its square $\equiv a \pmod{2^{L+1}}$ (since their squares differ by $2^L$ mod
 $2^{L+1}$). The member that does satisfy this gives **2 lifts** (both $t=0$ and
 $t=1$ work, since $c$ is even). So each pair contributes 2 solutions mod $2^{L+
1}$, giving $2 + 2 = 4$ total.

This completes the induction: **4 solutions for all $L \geq 3$**.

**Conclusion:** The number of solutions is at most 4 for every $L \geq 1$ (speci
fically: 1 for $L=1$, 2 for $L=2$, 4 for $L \geq 3$, or 0 when $a$ is not a quad
ratic residue). Taking $C = 4$ (or any $C \geq 4$), the number of solutions is a
t most $C$ for all $L$.

**Yes, such a constant exists; $C = 4$ suffices.**
