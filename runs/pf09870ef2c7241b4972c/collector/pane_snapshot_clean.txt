Looking at this problem, I need to find the minimum number of questions to guara
ntee identifying the prize among 3 doors, where I can only ask "Is it behind the
 left door?" or "Is it behind the right door?", and the quizmaster may lie at mo
st 10 times total.

## Proof

**Setup.** Let the three hypotheses be $L$, $M$, $R$ (prize behind left, middle,
 right). The truthful answers are:
- "Left?": $L \to$ yes, $M \to$ no, $R \to$ no
- "Right?": $L \to$ no, $M \to$ no, $R \to$ yes

Note that $M$ answers "no" to **both** questions. This is the key structural con
straint.

**Tracking lie counts.** Suppose we ask $p$ "left?" questions and $q$ "right?" q
uestions ($p+q=N$). Let:
- $a$ = number of "yes" answers to "left?", $b$ = "no" to "left?" ($a+b=p$)
- $c$ = number of "yes" answers to "right?", $d$ = "no" to "right?" ($c+d=q$)

The lie count for each hypothesis (if it were true) is:
$$\text{lies}(L) = b+c, \quad \text{lies}(M) = a+c, \quad \text{lies}(R) = a+d$$

A hypothesis remains **viable** if its lie count $\le 10$. We need exactly one v
iable hypothesis at the end.

**Upper bound: $N=42$ suffices.** Ask 21 "left?" questions and 21 "right?" quest
ions. Then $p=21, q=21$, and:

$$\text{lies}(L)+\text{lies}(R) = (b+c)+(a+d) = (a+b)+(c+d) = 42$$

So at most one of $L,R$ has $\le 10$ lies (if both did, their sum $\le 20 < 42$)
.

$$\text{lies}(L)+\text{lies}(M) = (b+c)+(a+c) = 21+2c \ge 21$$

So at most one of $L,M$ has $\le 10$ lies.

$$\text{lies}(M)+\text{lies}(R) = (a+c)+(a+d) = 2a+21 \ge 21$$

So at most one of $M,R$ has $\le 10$ lies.

Since at most one from **each pair** is viable, at most one hypothesis total is
viable. The true hypothesis always has $\le 10$ lies, so **exactly one** is viab
le. We identify the prize. $\checkmark$

**Lower bound: $N=41$ is impossible.** With $N=41$, by pigeonhole either $p \le
20$ or $q \le 20$.

**Case $p \le 20$:** The quizmaster (with truth $= M$) uses this strategy:
- Answer "no" to **all** "right?" questions (truthful for $M$, so $c=0$, zero li
es).
- Answer "yes" to $\min(10, p)$ "left?" questions and "no" to the rest.

Then $a = \min(10,p) \le 10$ and $b = p - a \le 10$ (since $p \le 20$). We get:
$$\text{lies}(M) = a + 0 = a \le 10 \quad \checkmark \text{ (viable)}$$
$$\text{lies}(L) = b + 0 = b \le 10 \quad \checkmark \text{ (viable)}$$

Both $L$ and $M$ are viable — we cannot distinguish them. The "right?" questions
 are **useless** here because $M$ truthfully answers "no" to "right?", so the qu
izmaster simply answers truthfully and saves all lies for "left?" questions.

**Case $q \le 20$:** By symmetry, the quizmaster (truth $= M$) answers "no" to a
ll "left?" questions ($a=0$) and balances "right?" answers: $c = \min(10,q) \le
10$, $d = q-c \le 10$. Then:
$$\text{lies}(M) = 0 + c \le 10, \quad \text{lies}(R) = 0 + d \le 10$$

Both $M$ and $R$ viable — we cannot distinguish them.

In either case, the quizmaster keeps two hypotheses viable, so $N=41$ fails.

**Why adaptivity doesn't help.** The quizmaster's strategy above is **non-adapti
ve** — it works regardless of the order or adaptivity of our questions. The fund
amental issue is:
- "Left?" questions cannot separate $M$ from $R$ (both truthfully answer "no").
- "Right?" questions cannot separate $L$ from $M$ (both truthfully answer "no" t
o "right?").

So we need enough "left?" questions to separate $L$ from $M$, and enough "right?
" questions to separate $M$ from $R$. The quizmaster can always answer the "usel
ess" question type truthfully (zero lies), concentrating all 10 lies on the "use
ful" type. To overcome 10 lies in a binary distinguishing task requires $2(10)+1
 = 21$ questions (majority vote / repetition code). Since we need 21 of **each**
 type:

$$N \ge 21 + 21 = 42$$

**Conclusion.** The smallest number of questions that guarantees identifying the
 prize is $\boxed{42}$.
