**Answer: Yes, such a function exists.**

## Proof

We first establish a necessary condition, then exhibit a construction satisfying
 it.

**Key observation.** If $|f|$ is continuous at a point $x_0$ and $f(x_0)=0$, the
n $f$ is continuous at $x_0$.

*Proof of observation.* Continuity of $|f|$ at $x_0$ gives $|f(x)|\to |f(x_0)|=0
$ as $x\to x_0$. But $|f(x)|\to 0$ forces $f(x)\to 0 = f(x_0)$, so $f$ is contin
uous at $x_0$. $\square$

Consequently, if $f$ is to be discontinuous at **every** point while $|f|$ is co
ntinuous, we must have $f(x)\neq 0$ (equivalently $|f(x)|>0$) for all $x\in\math
bb{R}$.

**Construction.** Define
$$
f(x)=\begin{cases}+1,& x\in\mathbb{Q},\\ -1,& x\notin\mathbb{Q}.\end{cases}
$$

*Discontinuity of $f$ everywhere.* Let $x_0\in\mathbb{R}$ and let $U$ be any nei
ghborhood of $x_0$. Both $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ are de
nse in $\mathbb{R}$, so $U$ contains a rational $r$ with $f(r)=+1$ and an irrati
onal $s$ with $f(s)=-1$. Thus in every neighborhood of $x_0$, $f$ takes both val
ues $+1$ and $-1$. Since $f(x_0)\in\{-1,+1\}$, one of these values differs from
$f(x_0)$, so $f(x)\not\to f(x_0)$. Hence $f$ is discontinuous at $x_0$. Since $x
_0$ was arbitrary, $f$ is discontinuous at every point of $\mathbb{R}$.

*Continuity of $|f|$.* For every $x\in\mathbb{R}$, $|f(x)|=1$. The constant func
tion $1$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.

Therefore $f$ is discontinuous at every point of $\mathbb{R}$, yet $|f|$ is cont
inuous on $\mathbb{R}$. $\blacksquare$
