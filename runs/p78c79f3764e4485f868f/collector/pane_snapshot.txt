## Proof

**Claim:** The sum of the absolute values of the coefficients can be made arbitr
arily large. No finite maximum exists; the supremum is $+\infty$.

### Construction via Chebyshev Polynomials

For each integer $n \geq 1$, the **Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind** is d
efined by:
$$T_n(x) = \cos(n \arccos x).$$

Two key properties:

1. **Boundedness:** $|T_n(x)| \leq 1$ for all $x \in [-1, 1]$, since $T_n(x) = \
cos(n \arccos x)$ and $|\cos(\cdot)| \leq 1$.

2. **Leading coefficient:** $T_n$ has degree $n$ with leading coefficient $2^{n-
1}$ for $n \geq 1$. (Verified: $T_1 = x$, $T_2 = 2x^2 - 1$, $T_3 = 4x^3 - 3x$, $
T_4 = 8x^4 - 8x^2 + 1$, etc.)

### Scaled Polynomials Satisfying the Constraint

For any $\epsilon \in (0, 1)$ and any $n \geq 1$, define:
$$P(x) = (1 - \epsilon)\, T_n(x).$$

Then for all $x \in [-1, 1]$:
$$|P(x)| = (1 - \epsilon)\,|T_n(x)| \leq 1 - \epsilon < 1.$$

In particular, $|P(x)| < 1$ for all $|x| < 1$. ✓

### The Coefficient Sum Grows Without Bound

Write $T_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} c_k^{(n)} x^k$. The sum of absolute values of the
 coefficients of $P$ is:
$$S(P) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} |(1-\epsilon)\, c_k^{(n)}| = (1 - \epsilon) \sum_{k=0}^{
n} |c_k^{(n)}|.$$

Since the leading coefficient satisfies $|c_n^{(n)}| = 2^{n-1}$:
$$S(P) \geq (1 - \epsilon) \cdot 2^{n-1}.$$

### Exceeding Any Prescribed Bound

Given any $M > 0$, choose:
- $n$ such that $2^{n-1} > 2M$ (i.e., $n \geq 2 + \lceil \log_2 M \rceil$),
- $\epsilon = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

Then:
$$S(P) \geq \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2^{n-1} = 2^{n-2} > M.$$

The polynomial $P(x) = \frac{1}{2} T_n(x)$ satisfies $|P(x)| \leq \frac{1}{2} <
1$ for all $|x| \leq 1$, yet its coefficient sum exceeds $M$.

### Conclusion

Since $M$ was arbitrary, for every positive real number $M$ there exists a polyn
omial $P$ with $|P(x)| < 1$ for $|x| < 1$ whose coefficient sum $\sum |a_k|$ exc
eeds $M$. Therefore:

$$\sup_{P:\, |P(x)|<1 \text{ for } |x|<1} \sum_k |a_k| = +\infty.$$

**The maximum value does not exist as a finite number; the supremum is $+\infty$
.** $\blacksquare$
