## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, such a function exists.

**Construction.** Let $c: [0,1] \to [0,1]$ be the **Cantor function** (devil's s
taircase), which satisfies:
- $c$ is continuous on $[0,1]$,
- $c$ is monotonically non-decreasing,
- $c(0) = 0$, $\;c(1) = 1$,
- $c'(x) = 0$ for almost every $x \in [0,1]$.

**Key fact:** $c(x) < 1$ for every $x \in [0,1)$.

*Proof of key fact.* The Cantor function maps the Cantor set $\mathcal{C}$ onto
$[0,1]$ via the base-2-to-base-3 correspondence: a point $x = 0.\epsilon_1\epsil
on_2\epsilon_3\ldots_3 \in \mathcal{C}$ (with each $\epsilon_i \in \{0,2\}$) is
sent to $c(x) = 0.\frac{\epsilon_1}{2}\frac{\epsilon_2}{2}\frac{\epsilon_3}{2}\l
dots_2$. The value $c = 1$ corresponds to the binary expansion $0.111\ldots_2 =
1$, which maps back to the ternary expansion $0.222\ldots_3 = 1$, i.e., the sing
le point $x = 1$. On each removed middle-third interval, $c$ is constant at a dy
adic value $k/2^n$ with $0 < k < 2^n$, hence strictly less than $1$. Therefore $
c(x) < 1$ for all $x \in [0,1)$. $\square$

Since $c(x) \to 1$ as $x \to 1^-$ (by continuity of $c$ and $c(1)=1$), the funct
ion
$$f(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 - c(x)}, \qquad x \in [0,1),$$
is well-defined with $1 - c(x) \in (0,1]$, so $f(x) \in [1,\infty) \subset [0,\i
nfty)$.

**Continuity.** The function $f$ is the composition $h \circ c$ where $h(t) = \f
rac{1}{1-t}$ is continuous on $[0,1)$ and $c$ is continuous on $[0,1]$. Hence $f
$ is continuous on $[0,1)$.

**Monotonicity.** The Cantor function $c$ is non-decreasing, and $h(t) = \frac{1
}{1-t}$ is strictly increasing on $[0,1)$. The composition of a non-decreasing f
unction with a strictly increasing function is non-decreasing. Hence $f$ is mono
tonically (non-decreasingly) increasing.

**Divergence at $1^-$.** As $x \to 1^-$, continuity gives $c(x) \to c(1) = 1$, s
o $1 - c(x) \to 0^+$, and therefore
$$f(x) = \frac{1}{1 - c(x)} \;\longrightarrow\; \infty.$$

**Derivative vanishes a.e.** The function $h(t) = \frac{1}{1-t}$ is differentiab
le at every $t \in [0,1)$ with $h'(t) = \frac{1}{(1-t)^2}$. The Cantor function
$c$ is differentiable at almost every $x \in [0,1)$, with $c'(x) = 0$ a.e. By th
e **pointwise chain rule** (which requires only that $c$ be differentiable at $x
$ and $h$ differentiable at $c(x)$ — no absolute continuity needed), at every su
ch point $x$:
$$f'(x) = h'(c(x)) \cdot c'(x) = \frac{1}{(1 - c(x))^2} \cdot c'(x) = \frac{1}{(
1-c(x))^2} \cdot 0 = 0.$$
Since this holds at almost every $x \in [0,1)$, we conclude $f' = 0$ almost ever
ywhere on $[0,1)$.

**Conclusion.** The function $f(x) = \frac{1}{1 - c(x)}$ is continuous on $[0,1)
$, monotonically increasing, maps $[0,1)$ into $[0,\infty)$, satisfies $\lim_{x
\to 1^-} f(x) = \infty$, and has $f' = 0$ almost everywhere. Therefore, such a f
unction **exists**. $\blacksquare$
