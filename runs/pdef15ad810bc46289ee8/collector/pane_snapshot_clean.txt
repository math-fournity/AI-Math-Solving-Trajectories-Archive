## Proof

Let $B$ be an $n \times n$ matrix and let $p(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - B)$ be
its characteristic polynomial.

**Key theorem (Cayley–Hamilton).** Every square matrix satisfies its own charact
eristic polynomial. That is,

$$p(B) = 0,$$

where $0$ denotes the $n \times n$ zero matrix.

**Computing $e^{p(B)}$.** By the definition

$$e^{p(B)} = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{(p(B))^j}{j!},$$

we substitute $p(B) = 0$:

- For $j = 0$: $\frac{(p(B))^0}{0!} = I$ (the identity matrix, since any matrix
to the power $0$ is the identity).
- For $j \geq 1$: $(p(B))^j = 0^j = 0$.

Therefore,

$$e^{p(B)} = I + 0 + 0 + \cdots = I.$$

**Conclusion.** By the Cayley–Hamilton theorem, $p(B)$ is the zero matrix, so $e
^{p(B)} = e^0 = I_n$, the $n \times n$ identity matrix. This holds for every $n
\times n$ matrix $B$, regardless of its eigenvalues, diagonalizability, or any o
ther property.

$$\boxed{e^{p(B)} = I_n}$$
