## Proof

We determine the profinite completion of $S^1 = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| = 1\}$.

**Key fact: $S^1$ is a divisible abelian group.**

Recall that an abelian group $A$ is *divisible* if for every $a \in A$ and every
 positive integer $n$, there exists $b \in A$ with $nb = a$. For $S^1$ (written
multiplicatively), this means: for every $z \in S^1$ and every $n \geq 1$, there
 exists $w \in S^1$ with $w^n = z$. Indeed, writing $z = e^{i\theta}$, we may ta
ke $w = e^{i\theta/n} \in S^1$. Hence $S^1$ is divisible.

**Quotients of divisible groups are divisible.**

If $A$ is divisible and $\pi: A \to A/N$ is the quotient map, then for any $\bar
{a} \in A/N$ and any $n \geq 1$, pick a lift $a \in A$ with $\pi(a) = \bar{a}$.
By divisibility of $A$, there exists $b \in A$ with $nb = a$. Then $n\bar{b} = \
overline{nb} = \bar{a}$, where $\bar{b} = \pi(b)$. So $A/N$ is divisible.

**Finite divisible abelian groups are trivial.**

Let $F$ be a finite divisible abelian group. Suppose $|F| > 1$. Then there exist
s a prime $p$ dividing $|F|$. By Cauchy's theorem, $F$ contains an element of or
der $p$, i.e., $\ker(p: F \to F) \neq \{0\}$, where $p: F \to F$ denotes multipl
ication by $p$. But divisibility of $F$ says $p: F \to F$ is surjective; since $
F$ is finite, surjectivity implies injectivity, so $\ker(p) = \{0\}$—a contradic
tion. Hence $|F| = 1$.

**Conclusion.**

The profinite completion of a group $G$ is
$$\widehat{G} = \varprojlim_{N \trianglelefteq G,\; [G:N] < \infty} G/N.$$
Since $S^1$ is abelian, every subgroup is normal. Every finite-index subgroup $N
 \leq S^1$ yields a finite quotient $S^1/N$, which is divisible (being a quotien
t of a divisible group) and finite, hence trivial by the argument above. Therefo
re $S^1/N = \{1\}$ for every finite-index subgroup $N$, i.e., the only finite qu
otient is the trivial group.

The inverse limit over a system consisting of only the trivial group is the triv
ial group. Therefore
$$\widehat{S^1} = \{1\}.$$

**Remark (topological perspective).** The same conclusion follows from connected
ness: $S^1$ is connected (it is the continuous image of $\mathbb{R}$ under $t \m
apsto e^{2\pi i t}$). In a connected topological group, every open subgroup is a
lso closed (its complement is a union of cosets, each open), hence clopen; by co
nnectedness the only open subgroups are $\{1\}$-coset... in fact the only open s
ubgroup is $S^1$ itself. Thus the topological profinite completion (using open f
inite-index normal subgroups) is also trivial.

Both the algebraic and topological notions of profinite completion yield the sam
e answer.

$$\boxed{\widehat{S^1} \cong \{1\}}$$
