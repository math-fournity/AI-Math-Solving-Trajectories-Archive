Looking at this problem, I need to analyze when the largest of $n$ consecutive p
ositive integers divides the LCM of the other $n-1$.

## Setup and Reduction

Let the sequence be $a, a+1, \ldots, a+n-1$ with maximum $M = a+n-1$. Property P
 requires:
$$M \mid \operatorname{lcm}(a, a+1, \ldots, a+n-2)$$

**Key Lemma.** For each prime power $p^k \| M$ (exactly dividing $M$), the condi
tion $p^k \mid \operatorname{lcm}(a, \ldots, M-1)$ holds if and only if $p^k \le
q n-1$.

*Proof.* Since $p^k \mid M$, the largest multiple of $p^k$ strictly less than $M
$ is $M - p^k$. This falls in $\{a, \ldots, M-1\} = \{M-(n-1), \ldots, M-1\}$ if
f $M - p^k \geq M - (n-1)$, i.e., $p^k \leq n-1$. ∎

**Corollary.** Property P holds iff every prime power in the factorization of $M
$ is $\leq n-1$, equivalently, $M \mid L(n-1)$ where $L(n-1) = \operatorname{lcm
}(1, 2, \ldots, n-1)$.

Since $a \geq 1$, we need $M \geq n$. So the valid sequences correspond to divis
ors of $L(n-1)$ that are $\geq n$.

Define $f(n) = |\{d \mid L(n-1) : d \geq n\}|$. Then:
- $n \in S$ iff $f(n) \geq 1$ (i.e., $L(n-1) \geq n$)
- $n \in U$ iff $f(n) = 1$ (exactly one valid sequence)

## Finding $n_0$

- $n=3$: $L(2) = 2 < 3$. No valid $M$. So $3 \notin S$.
- $n=4$: $L(3) = \operatorname{lcm}(1,2,3) = 6 \geq 4$. Valid $M$ exists. So $4
\in S$.

Thus $n_0 = 4$.

## Finding $U$

For $n \in U$, we need exactly one divisor of $L(n-1)$ that is $\geq n$. Since $
2 \mid L(n-1)$ for $n \geq 3$, the largest proper divisor of $L(n-1)$ is $L(n-1)
/2$. So uniqueness requires:
$$n \leq L(n-1) < 2n$$

**Checking small values:**
- $n=4$: $L(3) = 6$. Is $4 \leq 6 < 8$? **Yes.** ✓ So $4 \in U$.
  - Verify: only valid $M=6$, giving sequence $3,4,5,6$. Check: $\operatorname{l
cm}(3,4,5)=60$, and $6 \mid 60$. ✓
- $n=5$: $L(4) = 12$. Is $5 \leq 12 < 10$? No ($12 \geq 10$). Divisors of 12 tha

- $n=6$: $L(5) = 60$. Is $6 \leq 60 < 12$? No. Not in $U$.
- $n=7$: $L(6) = 60$. Is $7 \leq 60 < 14$? No. Not in $U$.
- $n=8$: $L(7) = 420$. Is $8 \leq 420 < 16$? No. Not in $U$.

**For all $n \geq 5$:** $L(n-1)$ grows exponentially ($\log L(n-1) \sim n-1$ by
Chebyshev's theorem), while $2n$ grows linearly. Since $L(4) = 12 > 10 = 2 \cdot
 5$ and $L$ is non-decreasing with the gap only widening, $L(n-1) \geq 2n$ for a
ll $n \geq 5$.

Therefore $U = \{4\}$.

## Final Computation

$$n_0 + \sum_{n \in U} n = 4 + 4 = \boxed{8}$$
