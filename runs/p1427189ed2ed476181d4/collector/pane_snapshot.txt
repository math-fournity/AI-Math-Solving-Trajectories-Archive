No. Almost uniform convergence does **not** imply $L^1$ convergence, even in a f
inite measure space. Here is a counterexample.

---

**Setup.** Let $X = [0,1]$ equipped with Lebesgue measure $\mu$, so $\mu(X) = 1
< \infty$ (a finite measure space). Define
$$f_n = n\,\mathbf{1}_{[0,\,1/n]}, \qquad f \equiv 0.$$
Each $f_n$ is measurable and integrable with $\|f_n\|_1 = \int_0^{1/n} n\,dx = 1
$.

**Claim 1: $f_n \to f$ almost uniformly.**

Recall that $f_n \to f$ *almost uniformly* means: for every $\delta > 0$ there e
xists a measurable set $E$ with $\mu(E) < \delta$ such that $f_n \to f$ uniforml
y on $X \setminus E$.

Given $\delta > 0$, set $E = [0, \delta/2)$, so $\mu(E) = \delta/2 < \delta$. On
 $X \setminus E = [\delta/2, 1]$, whenever $n > 2/\delta$ we have $1/n < \delta/
2$, hence $[0,1/n] \subseteq E$ and therefore
$$f_n(x) = 0 = f(x) \quad \text{for all } x \in X \setminus E.$$
In particular $\sup_{x \in X\setminus E} |f_n(x) - f(x)| = 0$ for all $n > 2/\de
lta$, so the convergence is uniform on $X \setminus E$. Since $\delta > 0$ was a
rbitrary, $f_n \to f$ almost uniformly. $\checkmark$

**Claim 2: $f_n \not\to f$ in $L^1$.**

For every $n$,
$$\|f_n - f\|_1 = \int_0^1 |f_n(x) - 0|\,dx = \int_0^{1/n} n\,dx = 1.$$
Thus $\|f_n - f\|_1 \equiv 1 \not\to 0$, so $f_n$ does **not** converge to $f$ i
n $L^1$. $\checkmark$

---

**Conclusion.** The sequence $(f_n)$ converges to $f$ almost uniformly in the fi
nite measure space $([0,1], \mu)$, yet fails to converge in $L^1$. Therefore, al
most uniform convergence does **not** imply $L^1$ convergence in a finite measur
e space.

The obstruction is integrability: almost uniform convergence controls the *sup-n
orm* on a large set, but it places no bound on the *mass* $\int |f_n|\,d\mu$ tha
t can concentrate on the small exceptional set $E$. In the example, all the $L^1
$-mass ($=1$) is concentrated on the shrinking interval $[0,1/n]$, which is exac
tly the set removed in the almost-uniform convergence argument.
