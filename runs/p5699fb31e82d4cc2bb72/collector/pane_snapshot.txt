## Proof

We find the residue of $f(z) = e^{1/z}$ at the isolated singularity $z = 0$.

**Step 1: Identify the singularity.** The function $e^{1/z}$ is undefined at $z
= 0$. Since $e^w$ is entire and $1/z$ has a pole of order $1$ at $z = 0$, the co
mposition $e^{1/z}$ has an essential singularity at $z = 0$. To find the residue
, we expand $f$ in a Laurent series about $z = 0$ and read off the coefficient o
f $z^{-1}$.

**Step 2: Laurent expansion.** The exponential function has the everywhere-conve
rgent Taylor series
$$e^{w} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{w^{n}}{n!}, \qquad w \in \mathbb{C}.$$

Substituting $w = 1/z$ (valid for every $z \neq 0$) gives
$$e^{1/z} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n!}\, z^{-n} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \fra
c{1}{n!}\, \frac{1}{z^{n}}.$$

Writing out the first few terms explicitly:
$$e^{1/z} = 1 + \frac{1}{z} + \frac{1}{2!\,z^{2}} + \frac{1}{3!\,z^{3}} + \cdots
$$

This is the Laurent series of $f$ about $z = 0$, converging for all $z \neq 0$ (
i.e., on $0 < |z| < \infty$).

**Step 3: Extract the residue.** By definition, the residue of $f$ at an isolate
d singularity $z_0$ is the coefficient of $(z - z_0)^{-1}$ in the Laurent expans
ion of $f$ about $z_0$:
$$\operatorname{Res}(f, z_0) = a_{-1}.$$

In the expansion above, the term $\dfrac{1}{z} = z^{-1}$ occurs when $n = 1$, wi
th coefficient $\dfrac{1}{1!} = 1$. All other powers $z^{-n}$ with $n \neq 1$ co
ntribute to different Laurent coefficients. Therefore
$$a_{-1} = \frac{1}{1!} = 1.$$

**Conclusion.**
$$\boxed{\operatorname{Res}\!\left(e^{1/z},\, 0\right) = 1}.$$
