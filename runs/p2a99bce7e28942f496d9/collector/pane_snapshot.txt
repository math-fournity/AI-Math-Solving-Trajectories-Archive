## Proof

**Notation.** Let $p_n$ denote the $n$-th prime, $\pi(x)$ the prime-counting fun
ction, and $g(p) = p^+(p) - p$ the prime gap after $p$ (where $p^+(p)$ is the ne
xt prime after $p$).

**Step 1: Simplify the inequality.**

Since $x$ and $y$ are prime, $p_{\pi(x)} = x$ and $p_{\pi(y)} = y$ (the $\pi(x)$
-th prime is $x$ itself). Also $p_{\pi(x)+1} = p^+(x) = x + g(x)$ and $p_{\pi(y)
+1} = p^+(y) = y + g(y)$. Substituting:

$$x + y < \frac{x + y + (x + g(x)) + (y + g(y)) - 2}{2} = x + y + \frac{g(x) + g
(y) - 2}{2}.$$

Subtracting $x+y$ from both sides and multiplying by 2:

$$g(x) + g(y) > 2. \tag{$\star$}$$

So the original inequality is equivalent to $(\star)$.

**Step 2: Key observation about the constraint $x+y$ prime.**

If $x+y$ is prime and $x, y \geq 2$, then $x+y \geq 4$, so $x+y$ is an odd prime
 (the only even prime is 2, and $x+y=2$ is impossible). Since $x+y$ is odd, exac
tly one of $x, y$ is even. The only even prime is 2, so **one of $x, y$ must equ
al 2**.

This means the set $\{(x,y) : x,y \text{ prime},\; x+y \text{ prime}\}$ consists
 entirely of pairs where at least one coordinate is 2. In particular, there are
**no** pairs with both $x > 2$ and $y > 2$.

**Step 3: Verify $(\star)$ for all admissible pairs.**

- **Case $x = y = 2$:** Then $x+y = 4$, which is not prime. This case is exclude
d.

- **Case $x = 2$, $y > 2$ (or symmetrically $y = 2$, $x > 2$):** Since $y > 2$ i
s prime, $y$ is odd, so $y+1$ is even and $> 2$, hence not prime. Therefore $g(y
) \geq 2$. Also $g(2) = 3 - 2 = 1$. Thus:
$$g(x) + g(y) = g(2) + g(y) \geq 1 + 2 = 3 > 2. \quad\checkmark$$

Since these are the only admissible cases (by Step 2), the inequality $(\star)$
— and hence the original inequality — holds for **every** pair of primes $(x, y)
$ with $x + y$ prime.

**Step 4: Conclusion regarding "sufficiently large."**

The inequality holds for *all* primes $x, y$ with $x+y$ prime (not merely suffic
iently large ones). In particular, it holds for all sufficiently large such prim
es — the statement is true, and in fact it is true unconditionally.

**Remark.** The qualifier "sufficiently large" is vacuous in a strong sense: the
 constraint $x+y$ prime forces one of $x, y$ to be 2, so no pair with both coord
inates large exists. The inequality nevertheless holds for every admissible pair
, including those where the non-2 coordinate is arbitrarily large (i.e., twin-pr
ime-type pairs $(2, p)$ with $p+2$ prime).
