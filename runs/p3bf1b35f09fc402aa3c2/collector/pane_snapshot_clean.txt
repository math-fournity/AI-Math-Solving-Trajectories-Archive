## Proof

We work in the space $V$ of real-valued $C^\infty$ functions on $\mathbb{R}$ wit
h period 1, equipped with
$$\langle f, g\rangle = \int_0^1 f(x)\,g(x)\,dx.$$

We seek $D^*: V \to V$ such that $\langle Df, g\rangle = \langle f, D^*g\rangle$
 for all $f, g \in V$.

**Compute the left-hand side.** By definition $Df = f'$, so
$$\langle Df, g\rangle = \int_0^1 f'(x)\,g(x)\,dx.$$

**Integrate by parts.** Let $u = g(x)$, $dv = f'(x)\,dx$, so $du = g'(x)\,dx$, $
v = f(x)$:
$$\int_0^1 f'(x)\,g(x)\,dx = \Big[f(x)\,g(x)\Big]_0^1 - \int_0^1 f(x)\,g'(x)\,dx
.$$

**Evaluate the boundary term.** Since $f$ and $g$ are periodic with period 1,
$$f(1) = f(0), \qquad g(1) = g(0).$$
Therefore
$$\Big[f(x)\,g(x)\Big]_0^1 = f(1)g(1) - f(0)g(0) = f(0)g(0) - f(0)g(0) = 0.$$

**Conclude.** The boundary term vanishes, leaving
$$\langle Df, g\rangle = -\int_0^1 f(x)\,g'(x)\,dx = \int_0^1 f(x)\bigl(-g'(x)\b
igr)\,dx = \big\langle f,\, -g'\bigr\rangle = \big\langle f,\, -Dg\bigr\rangle.$
$

Since this holds for every $f, g \in V$, comparing with $\langle Df, g\rangle =
\langle f, D^*g\rangle$ gives
$$D^*g = -g' = -Dg.$$

The map $-D: g \mapsto -g'$ sends $V$ into $V$ (the derivative of a period-1 $C^
\infty$ function is again period-1 $C^\infty$), so it is a valid operator on $V$
.

$$\boxed{D^* = -D, \quad \text{i.e., } (D^*g)(x) = -g'(x).}$$
