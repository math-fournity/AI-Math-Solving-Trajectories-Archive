## Proof

Assume for contradiction that a real-valued function $u$ on $[0,1]$ satisfies
$$u(x) = 1 + \lambda \int_x^1 u(y)\,u(y-x)\,dy \qquad \text{for all } x \in [0,1
],$$
with sufficient regularity (e.g., $u \in L^2([0,1])$) so that all integrals belo
w are well-defined and Fubini's theorem applies.

---

**Step 1. Integrate the equation over $[0,1]$.**

Set $I = \int_0^1 u(x)\,dx$. Integrating both sides over $x \in [0,1]$:

$$I = \int_0^1 1\,dx + \lambda \int_0^1\!\int_x^1 u(y)\,u(y-x)\,dy\,dx = 1 + \la
mbda \int_0^1\!\int_x^1 u(y)\,u(y-x)\,dy\,dx.$$

---

**Step 2. Apply Fubini's theorem to the double integral.**

The region of integration is $\{(x,y): 0 \le x \le 1,\; x \le y \le 1\} = \{(x,y
): 0 \le x \le y \le 1\}$.

*Fubini justification:* Since $u \in L^2([0,1])$, by Cauchy–Schwarz,
$$\int_0^1\!\int_0^y |u(y)|\,|u(y-x)|\,dx\,dy \le \int_0^1 |u(y)|\cdot\|u\|_{L^1
}\,dy = \|u\|_{L^1}^2 \le \|u\|_{L^2}^2 < \infty,$$
so the double integral is absolutely convergent and Fubini applies. Reversing th
e order:

$$\int_0^1\!\int_x^1 u(y)\,u(y-x)\,dy\,dx = \int_0^1 u(y)\left(\int_0^y u(y-x)\,
dx\right)dy.$$

---

**Step 3. Evaluate the inner integral.**

Substitute $t = y - x$ ($dx = -dt$; $x=0 \Rightarrow t=y$; $x=y \Rightarrow t=0$
):

$$\int_0^y u(y-x)\,dx = \int_0^y u(t)\,dt.$$

---

**Step 4. Recognize a perfect derivative.**

Define $F(y) = \int_0^y u(t)\,dt$, so $F'(y) = u(y)$, $F(0) = 0$, and $F(1) = I$
. Then:

$$\int_0^1 u(y)\left(\int_0^y u(t)\,dt\right)dy = \int_0^1 F'(y)\,F(y)\,dy = \tf
rac{1}{2}\bigl[F(y)^2\bigr]_0^1 = \tfrac{1}{2}F(1)^2 = \tfrac{I^2}{2}.$$

---

**Step 5. Derive the quadratic constraint on $I$.**

Substituting back into Step 1:

$$I = 1 + \frac{\lambda}{2}\,I^2,$$

equivalently,

$$\lambda\, I^2 - 2I + 2 = 0. \tag{$\star$}$$

---

**Step 6. Analyze the discriminant.**

Equation $(\star)$ is a quadratic in $I$ with real coefficients. For it to admit
 a real solution, its discriminant must be non-negative:

$$\Delta = (-2)^2 - 4\cdot\lambda\cdot 2 = 4 - 8\lambda \ge 0 \iff \lambda \le \
frac{1}{2}.$$

When $\lambda > \frac{1}{2}$, we have $\Delta = 4 - 8\lambda < 0$, so $(\star)$
has **no real solution**.

---

**Conclusion.**

If a real-valued function $u$ satisfying the integral equation existed, then $I
= \int_0^1 u(x)\,dx$ would be a real number satisfying $(\star)$. But for $\lamb
da > \frac{1}{2}$, the quadratic $(\star)$ has no real roots—a contradiction.

Therefore, **for $\lambda > \frac{1}{2}$, no real-valued function $u$ satisfies
the given integral equation.** $\blacksquare$
