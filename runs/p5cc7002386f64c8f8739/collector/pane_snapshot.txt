## Proof

We compute $L = \lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{\int_0^1 f(x)^n\,\ln(x+2)\,dx}{\int_0^1
f(x)^n\,dx}$ where $f(x)=1-x^2+x^3$.

**Step 1: Locate the maxima of $f$ on $[0,1]$.**

$f'(x)=-2x+3x^2=x(3x-2)$, with critical points $x=0$ and $x=2/3$. Evaluating:
$$f(0)=1,\quad f(2/3)=1-\tfrac{4}{9}+\tfrac{8}{27}=\tfrac{23}{27}<1,\quad f(1)=1
.$$
On $[0,2/3]$, $f'<0$ (so $f$ decreases $1\to 23/27$); on $[2/3,1]$, $f'>0$ (so $
f$ increases $23/27\to 1$). Hence $\max_{[0,1]}f=1$, attained **only** at $x=0$
and $x=1$.

**Step 2: Nature of each maximum.**

*Near $x=0$:* $f(x)=1-x^2+x^3=1-x^2(1-x)$. For $x\in[0,\tfrac12]$: $\tfrac12 x^2
\le x^2(1-x)\le x^2$, so
$$1-x^2\le f(x)\le 1-\tfrac{x^2}{2}.$$

*Near $x=1$:* set $t=1-x$; $f(1-t)=1-t+2t^2-t^3=1-t(1-t)^2$. For $t\in[0,\tfrac1
2]$: $\tfrac14 t\le t(1-t)^2\le t$, so
$$1-t\le f(1-t)\le 1-\tfrac{t}{4}.$$

**Step 3: The $x=0$ contribution dominates.**

Denominator $D_n=\int_0^1 f(x)^n\,dx$. Split at $x=\tfrac12$.

*Upper bound on the $x=1$ side:* $\displaystyle\int_{1/2}^{1}f(x)^n\,dx=\int_0^{
1/2}f(1-t)^n\,dt\le\int_0^{1/2}\!\bigl(1-\tfrac{t}{4}\bigr)^n dt\le\int_0^\infty
 e^{-nt/4}dt=\frac{4}{n}.$

*Asymptotics of the $x=0$ side:* $\displaystyle\int_0^{1/2}f(x)^n\,dx\ge\int_0^{
1/2}(1-x^2)^n dx$. With $u=x\sqrt{n}$:
$$\int_0^{1/2}(1-x^2)^n dx=\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\int_0^{\sqrt n/2}\!\Bigl(1-\frac{u^
2}{n}\Bigr)^{\!n}du\;\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\;\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\int_0^\infty e^
{-u^2}du=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2\sqrt n}.$$
Likewise $\int_0^{1/2}f(x)^n\,dx\le\int_0^{1/2}(1-x^2/2)^n\,dx\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt
 n}\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2/2}\,du=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}$...
— in any case $\int_0^{1/2}f(x)^n\,dx=\Theta(n^{-1/2})$.

Since $n^{-1/2}\gg n^{-1}$, the $x=0$ region dominates: $D_n\sim\frac{\sqrt\pi}{
2\sqrt n}$, and the $x=1$ region contributes only $O(n^{-1})$.

**Step 4: Evaluate the limit.**

Let $g(x)=\ln(x+2)$, continuous with $g(0)=\ln 2$, $g(1)=\ln 3$.

*Numerator $x=1$ side:* $\displaystyle\Bigl|\int_{1/2}^{1}f(x)^n g(x)\,dx\Bigr|\
le\ln 3\cdot\frac{4}{n}=O(n^{-1}).$

*Numerator $x=0$ side:* On $[0,\tfrac12]$, $f$ has a **unique** maximum at $x=0$
 and $g$ is continuous there. By the standard Laplace-method argument (the proba
bility measures $\mu_n$ with density $\propto f(x)^n\mathbf 1_{[0,1/2]}$ concent
rate at $0$):
$$\frac{\int_0^{1/2}f(x)^n\,g(x)\,dx}{\int_0^{1/2}f(x)^n\,dx}\;\xrightarrow{n\to
\infty}\;g(0)=\ln 2.$$

Combining:
$$\frac{N_n}{D_n}=\frac{\int_0^{1/2}f^n g\,dx}{\int_0^{1/2}f^n\,dx+O(n^{-1})}\cd
ot\frac{\int_0^{1/2}f^n\,dx}{D_n}+\frac{O(n^{-1})}{D_n}\;\xrightarrow{n\to\infty
}\;(\ln 2)\cdot 1+0=\ln 2.$$

$$\boxed{L=\ln 2}$$
