## Proof

**Claim.** There exists an infinitely differentiable function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\m
athbb{R}$ such that $f(n)=|n|$ for every $n\in\mathbb{Z}$.

**Construction.** Let $\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be the standard smooth bump
$$
\psi(x)=\begin{cases}\exp\!\Big(\dfrac{-1}{1-x^{2}}\Big), & |x|<1,\\[4pt] 0, & |
x|\ge 1.\end{cases}
$$
This is $C^\infty$, supported on $[-1,1]$, and $\psi(0)=e^{-1}\neq 0$. Set
$$
\phi(x)=\frac{\psi(2x)}{\psi(0)}.
$$
Then $\phi\in C^\infty_c(\mathbb{R})$, $\operatorname{supp}\phi\subseteq[-\tfrac
12,\tfrac12]$, and $\phi(0)=1$.

Define
$$
f(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} |n|\,\phi(x-n).
$$

**The sum is locally finite.** The support of $\phi(x-n)$ lies in $(n-\tfrac12,\
,n+\tfrac12)$. These intervals are pairwise disjoint for distinct integers $n$.
Hence on any compact set $K\subset\mathbb{R}$, at most one term of the sum is no
nzero (in fact at most two near half-integer boundaries, but never more than a b
ounded number). More precisely, for any $x$, at most one $n$ satisfies $|x-n|<\t
frac12$, so the sum has at most one nonzero term at each point. Thus $f$ is well
-defined everywhere.

**Smoothness.** Since the supports of $\phi(\cdot-n)$ are mutually disjoint, eve
ry point $x$ has a neighborhood on which at most one summand $|n|\phi(x-n)$ is n
onzero. Therefore $f$ agrees locally with a single $C^\infty$ function $|n|\phi(
x-n)$, so $f\in C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$. (Equivalently, all derivatives $f^{(k)}(x
)=\sum_n |n|\,\phi^{(k)}(x-n)$ are locally finite sums of $C^\infty$ functions,
hence $C^\infty$.)

**Interpolation property.** For any integer $m\in\mathbb{Z}$:
$$
f(m)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|n|\,\phi(m-n).
$$
The term $\phi(m-n)$ is nonzero only when $|m-n|<\tfrac12$, i.e. only when $n=m$
. Thus
$$
f(m)=|m|\,\phi(0)=|m|\cdot 1=|m|.
$$

This is exactly the required condition $f(n)=|n|$ for all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$.

**Conclusion.** The function $f$ constructed above is infinitely differentiable
on $\mathbb{R}$ and satisfies $f(n)=|n|$ for every integer $n$. Therefore such a
 function exists. $\blacksquare$
