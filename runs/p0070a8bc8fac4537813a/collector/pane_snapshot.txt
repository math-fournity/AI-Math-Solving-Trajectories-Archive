**Answer: Yes.** For every $m \in [0,1]$ there exists a dense (Lebesgue-)measura
ble subset $A \subseteq [0,1]$ with $m(A)=m$.

---

**Proof.**

We use two standard facts and then assemble them.

**Fact 1 (Fat Cantor sets of arbitrary measure).** For every $\beta \in [0,1]$ t
here exists a closed, nowhere-dense set $C_\beta \subseteq [0,1]$ with Lebesgue
measure $m(C_\beta)=\beta$.

*Construction.* Start with $[0,1]$. At step $n\geq 1$ we have $2^{n-1}$ closed i
ntervals; from the middle of each remove an open interval of length $c_n:=\dfrac
{1-\beta}{3^n}$. The total length removed is

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{n-1}c_n=(1-\beta)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{n-1}}{3^n}=
(1-\beta)\cdot\frac{1/3}{1-2/3}=1-\beta.$$

Since $c_n\to 0$ geometrically, at each stage the pieces removed fit inside the
available intervals, so the construction is well-defined. Let $C_\beta$ be the i
ntersection of all the closed sets remaining after each step. Then $C_\beta$ is
closed, contains no interval (at step $n$ every remaining interval has length $\
leq$ something tending to $0$), hence is nowhere dense, and

$$m(C_\beta)=1-(1-\beta)=\beta. \qquad\checkmark$$

(For $\beta=0$ this gives the usual middle-thirds Cantor set; for $\beta=1$ we m
ay simply take $C_1=[0,1]$—closed, and "nowhere dense" is not needed in that end
point case since we handle $m=1$ separately below.)

**Fact 2.** If $C\subseteq[0,1]$ is closed and nowhere dense, then $[0,1]\setmin
us C$ is open and dense in $[0,1]$.

*Proof.* $C$ closed $\Rightarrow$ $[0,1]\setminus C$ is relatively open. Nowhere
 dense (and closed) means $C$ has empty interior, so its complement is dense. $\
checkmark$

---

**Main construction.** Fix $m\in[0,1]$.

- **$m=0$:** Take $A=\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$. It is countable (hence $m(A)=0$) and
dense. $\checkmark$

- **$m=1$:** Take $A=[0,1]$. It is dense in itself and $m(A)=1$. $\checkmark$

- **$0<m<1$:** Set $\beta:=1-m\in(0,1)$ and let $C:=C_\beta$ be the closed nowhe
re-dense set from Fact 1 with $m(C)=\beta=1-m$. Define

$$A:=\bigl([0,1]\setminus C\bigr)\;\cup\;\bigl(\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]\bigr).$$

  *Density.* $[0,1]\setminus C$ is dense by Fact 2, and $A\supseteq[0,1]\setminu
s C$, so $A$ is dense in $[0,1]$.

  *Measurability.* $A$ is a union of a Borel set and a Borel set, hence measurab
le.

  *Measure.* Since $\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$ has measure $0$,

$$m(A)=m\bigl([0,1]\setminus C\bigr)+m\bigl((\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1])\setminus([0,1]
\setminus C)\bigr)=\bigl(1-m(C)\bigr)+0=1-(1-m)=m.$$

Thus $A$ is dense and $m(A)=m$. $\checkmark$

---

All three cases are covered, so for **every** $m\in[0,1]$ there exists a dense m
easurable subset $A\subseteq[0,1]$ with Lebesgue measure exactly $m$. $\blacksqu
are$
