## Proof

We start from the two-dimensional Navier equation of motion for an isotropic ela
stic medium, which governs the displacement field **U** = (u_x, u_y):

$$\rho\,\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{U}}{\partial t^{2}}=(\lambda+\mu)\,\nabla(\nab
la\cdot\mathbf{U})+\mu\,\nabla^{2}\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{f},$$

where **f** = (f_x, f_y) is the density of the volume (body) forces, and λ, μ ar
e the Lamé constants.

---

### Step 1. Helmholtz decomposition of the displacement

The problem gives

$$\mathbf{U}=i\Big(\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial\psi}{\parti
al y}\Big)+j\Big(\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial
 x}\Big)=\nabla\varphi+\nabla\times(\psi\,\hat{k}),$$

since in 2D, ∇×(ψ k̂) = (ψ_y, −ψ_x). This is the standard scalar/vector (Helmholt
z) decomposition of the displacement into an irrotational part ∇φ and a solenoid
al part ∇×(ψ k̂).

Key identities:

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{U}=\nabla^{2}\varphi,\qquad \nabla\times\mathbf{U}=-\nabla^
{2}\psi\,\hat{k},$$

because ∇·(∇×(ψ k̂)) = 0 and (∇×∇φ) = 0.

---

### Step 2. Helmholtz decomposition of the body force

We similarly decompose the body force density:

$$\mathbf{f}=\nabla\Phi+\nabla\times(\Psi\,\hat{k}),$$

where Φ(x,y,t) is the scalar potential and Ψ(x,y,t) is the (single-component) ve
ctor potential of **f**. Taking the divergence and the z-curl gives the Poisson
equations that determine them:

$$\nabla^{2}\Phi=\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}=\frac{\partial f_x}{\partial x}+\frac{\pa
rtial f_y}{\partial y},$$

$$\nabla^{2}\Psi=-\,(\nabla\times\mathbf{f})_z=\frac{\partial f_x}{\partial y}-\
frac{\partial f_y}{\partial x}.$$

---

### Step 3. Substitute the decomposition into the Navier equation

Using ∇²U = ∇(∇²φ) + ∇×(∇²ψ k̂) (Laplacian commutes with both ∇ and ∇×), and ∇·U
= ∇²φ, the right-hand side of the Navier equation becomes

$$(\lambda+\mu)\nabla(\nabla^{2}\varphi)+\mu\big[\nabla(\nabla^{2}\varphi)+\nabl
a\times(\nabla^{2}\psi\,\hat{k})\big]+\nabla\Phi+\nabla\times(\Psi\,\hat{k})$$

$$=(\lambda+2\mu)\nabla(\nabla^{2}\varphi)+\mu\,\nabla\times(\nabla^{2}\psi\,\ha
t{k})+\nabla\Phi+\nabla\times(\Psi\,\hat{k}).$$

The left-hand side is

$$\rho\,\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{U}}{\partial t^{2}}=\rho\,\nabla\frac{\partial
^{2}\varphi}{\partial t^{2}}+\rho\,\nabla\times\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\par
tial t^{2}}\,\hat{k}\Big).$$

---

### Step 4. Separate irrotational and solenoidal parts

Equating the two sides and collecting the gradient (irrotational) terms and the
curl (solenoidal) terms separately — which is legitimate because a vector field
that is both irrotational and solenoidal in a simply connected region is determi
ned only up to a harmonic function that we absorb into the potentials — we obtai
n two independent scalar equations:

**Irrotational part:**

$$\rho\,\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial t^{2}}=(\lambda+2\mu)\,\nabla^{2}\va
rphi+\Phi.$$

**Solenoidal part:**

$$\rho\,\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial t^{2}}=\mu\,\nabla^{2}\psi+\Psi.$$

---

### Step 5. Identify F₁ and F₂

Comparing with the equations stated in the problem,

$$\rho\,\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial t^{2}}=(\lambda+2\mu)\Big(\frac{\par
tial^{2}\varphi}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial y^{2}}\Big)
+F_1,$$

$$\rho\,\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial t^{2}}=\mu\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{
\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial y^{2}}\Big)+F_2,$$

we identify

$$\boxed{\,F_1=\Phi,\qquad F_2=\Psi\,}$$

where Φ and Ψ are the scalar and vector potentials of the body-force density **f
** = (f_x, f_y), determined by

$$\nabla^{2}F_1=\frac{\partial f_x}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial f_y}{\partial y},
\qquad \nabla^{2}F_2=\frac{\partial f_x}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial f_y}{\partia
l x}.$$

---

### Interpretation

The Helmholtz decomposition splits the displacement **U** into a compressional (
P-wave) part ∇φ propagating with speed √((λ+2μ)/ρ) and a rotational (S-wave) par
t ∇×(ψ k̂) propagating with speed √(μ/ρ). The same decomposition applied to the b
ody force **f** yields the source terms F₁ = Φ (driving the P-wave equation) and
 F₂ = Ψ (driving the S-wave equation). Thus the two inhomogeneous wave equations
 are precisely the decoupled P- and S-wave equations sourced by the scalar and v
ector potentials of the volume-force density, respectively. ∎
