## Solution

**Answer:** Not necessarily — it fails for the zero ring. However, for every *no
nzero* commutative ring with $1$, the implication $R^m \cong R^n \Rightarrow m =
 n$ does hold. So the answer to the question as stated (without assuming $1 \neq
 0$) is **no**.

---

### Counterexample: the zero ring

Let $R = \{0\}$ be the zero ring, where $1 = 0$. This is a commutative ring with
 $1$ under the only possible operations. For every non-negative integer $m$, the
 free module $R^m$ is the singleton $\{0\}$, i.e. the zero module. Hence for any
 $m, n \geq 0$ (in particular $m \neq n$, say $m = 1, n = 2$), we have

$$R^m = \{0\} = R^n,$$

so $R^m \cong R^n$ as $R$-modules, yet $m \neq n$. Thus the implication does **n
ot** hold in general.

---

### The positive result for nonzero commutative rings

Suppose now that $R$ is a commutative ring with $1$ and $1 \neq 0$ (equivalently
, $R$ is not the zero ring). We show $R^m \cong R^n \Rightarrow m = n$.

**Step 1: Existence of a maximal ideal.** Since $R \neq 0$ is commutative with $
1$, the set of proper ideals of $R$ is nonempty (it contains $\{0\}$, which is p
roper because $1 \neq 0$) and partially ordered by inclusion. Every chain of pro
per ideals has an upper bound (its union), which is again proper (it does not co
ntain $1$). By Zorn's Lemma, $R$ possesses a maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$.

**Step 2: Pass to the residue field.** The quotient $k := R/\mathfrak{m}$ is a f
ield (maximality of $\mathfrak{m}$ makes the quotient a commutative ring with no
 nonzero proper ideals, hence a field).

**Step 3: Tensor the isomorphism.** The functor $(-) \otimes_R k$ is right-exact
; applied to an isomorphism it yields an isomorphism. Concretely, for any $r$,

$$R^r \otimes_R k \;\cong\; (R \otimes_R k)^r \;\cong\; k^r,$$

as $k$-vector spaces (equivalently as $R$-modules, where $R$ acts through the qu
otient map $R \to k$). Applying this to the given isomorphism $R^m \xrightarrow{
\;\sim\;} R^n$ gives a $k$-linear isomorphism

$$k^m \;\cong\; k^n.$$

**Step 4: Dimension of vector spaces.** The modules $k^m$ and $k^n$ are finite-d
imensional vector spaces over the field $k$, of dimensions $m$ and $n$ respectiv
ely. An isomorphism of finite-dimensional vector spaces preserves dimension, so

$$m = \dim_k k^m = \dim_k k^n = n. \qquad \blacksquare$$

---

### Summary

The statement "$R^m \cong R^n \Rightarrow m = n$" is the **Invariant Basis Numbe
r (IBN)** property. It holds for every nonzero commutative ring with $1$ (via re
duction modulo a maximal ideal to a field, where dimension is well-defined). The
 single obstruction is the zero ring $R = \{0\}$ with $1 = 0$, for which $R^m =
0$ for all $m$ and the implication fails. Therefore, as the problem is phrased —
 assuming only "commutative ring with $1$" — the answer is **no, it does not nec
essarily follow that $m = n$**, with the zero ring furnishing the counterexample
.
