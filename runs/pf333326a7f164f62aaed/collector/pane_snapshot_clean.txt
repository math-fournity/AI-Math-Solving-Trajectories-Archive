**Proof.** Yes, $f$ is necessarily analytic on all of $\mathbb{C}$.

We use Morera's theorem. Recall: if $f$ is continuous on a domain $\Omega$ and $
\int_{\partial T} f(z)\,dz = 0$ for every closed triangle $T \subseteq \Omega$,
then $f$ is analytic on $\Omega$.

Here $f$ is continuous on $\mathbb{C}$ by hypothesis, so it suffices to show tha
t for every closed triangle $T \subset \mathbb{C}$,
$$\int_{\partial T} f(z)\,dz = 0.$$

Let $\Gamma = \partial B_1(0) = \{z : |z|=1\}$ be the unit circle, and let $D =
\{z : |z|<1\}$. By hypothesis $f$ is analytic on $D$ and on $\mathbb{C}\setminus
\overline{D}\supseteq \{|z|>1\}$, and continuous on all of $\mathbb{C}$.

Fix a closed triangle $T$.

**Case 1: $T \cap \Gamma = \varnothing$.** Then $T$ lies entirely in $D$ or enti
rely in $\{|z|>1\}$ (since $T$ is connected and $\Gamma$ separates these two reg
ions). In either region $f$ is analytic, so by Cauchy–Goursat $\int_{\partial T}
 f = 0$.

**Case 2: $T \cap \Gamma \neq \varnothing$.** The circle $\Gamma$ is a smooth (r
eal-analytic) curve, so it intersects the piecewise-linear boundary $\partial T$
 in finitely many points and transversally except possibly at finitely many tang
encies; in any case $\Gamma$ cuts $T$ into finitely many pieces, each of which l
ies either in $\overline{D}$ or in $\mathbb{C}\setminus D$, with piecewise-smoot
h boundary consisting of sub-arcs of $\partial T$ and sub-arcs of $\Gamma$.

Concretely, $T \cap D$ is a finite union of regions $R_1^{\mathrm{in}},\dots,R_m
^{\mathrm{in}}$, each bounded by sub-arcs of $\partial T$ (lying in $D$) and sub
-arcs of $\Gamma$ (lying in $\operatorname{int} T$). Likewise $T \cap \{|z|>1\}$
 is a finite union of regions $R_1^{\mathrm{out}},\dots,R_n^{\mathrm{out}}$ boun
ded by sub-arcs of $\partial T$ (lying in $\{|z|>1\}$) and sub-arcs of $\Gamma$.

On each $R_j^{\mathrm{in}}$, the function $f$ is analytic on a neighbourhood of
$\overline{R_j^{\mathrm{in}}}\subseteq \overline{D}$ except possibly along the $
\Gamma$–arcs, but $f$ is continuous on $\overline{R_j^{\mathrm{in}}}$ and analyt
ic in its interior (which lies in $D$). By the standard Cauchy theorem for regio
ns with piecewise-smooth boundary (applied to $f$ on $D$, which is analytic thro
ughout $D$),
$$\int_{\partial R_j^{\mathrm{in}}} f(z)\,dz = 0.$$
The integral over $\partial R_j^{\mathrm{in}}$ is well-defined because $f$ is co
ntinuous on $\overline{R_j^{\mathrm{in}}}\subset\mathbb{C}$, so in particular on
 the $\Gamma$–arcs.

Similarly, on each $R_j^{\mathrm{out}}\subseteq \{|z|>1\}$, $f$ is analytic in t
he interior and continuous on the closure, so
$$\int_{\partial R_j^{\mathrm{out}}} f(z)\,dz = 0.$$

Now sum these equalities over all pieces. The sub-arcs of $\partial T$ appear ex
actly once each, with the orientation induced by $\partial T$, because the piece
s tile $T$. The sub-arcs of $\Gamma$ appear twice—once as part of the boundary o
f an "in" piece and once as part of the boundary of an "out" piece—with opposite
 orientations. Since $f$ is single-valued and continuous, these paired contribut
ions cancel. Therefore
$$\int_{\partial T} f(z)\,dz = \sum_j \int_{\partial R_j^{\mathrm{in}}} f + \sum
_j \int_{\partial R_j^{\mathrm{out}}} f = 0 + 0 = 0.$$

(If one is uneasy about the tangency / degenerate cases, approximate $\Gamma$ by
 a slightly perturbed circle $\Gamma_\varepsilon$ that meets $\partial T$ transv
ersally, apply the above, and let $\varepsilon\to 0$ using the uniform continuit
y of $f$ on the compact set $T$; the limit recovers the same conclusion.)

**Conclusion.** In both cases $\int_{\partial T} f = 0$ for every triangle $T\su
bset\mathbb{C}$. Since $f$ is continuous on $\mathbb{C}$, Morera's theorem impli
es $f$ is analytic on all of $\mathbb{C}$, i.e. $f$ is entire.

Hence $f$ is necessarily analytic on $\mathbb{C}$. $\blacksquare$
