## Proof

**Claim.** Yes, such a function exists.

**Construction.** Let $\{r_1, r_2, r_3, \dots\}$ be an enumeration of the ration
al numbers in $[0,1]$ (which is countable). Define $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ by

$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} n, & \text{if } x = r_n \text{ for some } n,\\ 0, & \text{i
f } x \text{ is irrational.} \end{cases}
$$

**$f$ is finite at every point.** For each $x\in[0,1]$: if $x$ is irrational, $f
(x)=0$; if $x$ is rational, then $x=r_n$ for a unique $n$ (since the enumeration
 assigns each rational exactly one index), and $f(x)=n<\infty$. So $f$ takes a f
inite value at every point of $[0,1]$.

**$f$ is unbounded on every neighborhood of every point.** Let $x\in[0,1]$ and l
et $U$ be any neighborhood of $x$ (relative to $[0,1]$), so $U$ contains some op
en interval $(a,b)\cap[0,1]$ with $a<b$. The interval $(a,b)$ contains infinitel
y many rationals, hence contains $r_n$ for infinitely many indices $n$. In parti
cular, for any $M>0$, there exists $n>M$ with $r_n\in(a,b)\subset U$, and at tha
t point $f(r_n)=n>M$. Therefore

$$
\sup_{y\in U} f(y) = +\infty,
$$

so $f$ is unbounded on $U$.

Since $x$ and its neighborhood $U$ were arbitrary, $f$ is unbounded on every nei
ghborhood of every point of $[0,1]$, while remaining finite at every point. ∎
