## Proof

**Claim:** Such a bijection exists if and only if $n \geq 2$.

### Part 1: $n = 1$ does not work

Every norm on $\mathbb{R}^1$ has the form $\|x\| = c|x|$ for some constant $c >
0$. The condition $\|f(k+1)-f(k)\|=1$ becomes $|f(k+1)-f(k)| = 1/c$. Since $f(k+
1)-f(k) \in \mathbb{Q}$, the value $r := 1/c$ must be a positive rational.

Each step satisfies $f(k+1) - f(k) = \pm r$, so by induction:
$$f(k) = f(1) + r\sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \varepsilon_i, \quad \varepsilon_i \in \{-1,+1
\}.$$
The range of $f$ is contained in $\{f(1) + rm : m \in \mathbb{Z}\} = f(1) + r\ma
thbb{Z}$, a coset of $r\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{Q}$. Since $r\mathbb{Z} \neq \mat
hbb{Q}$ (for instance, $r/2 \notin r\mathbb{Z}$), this coset is a proper subset
of $\mathbb{Q}$. Thus $f$ cannot be surjective. $\square$

### Part 2: $n \geq 2$ works

We use the $\ell^\infty$ norm $\|(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\|_\infty = \max_i |x_i|$ on $\
mathbb{R}^n$.

**Key Lemma.** For $n \geq 2$, given any $p, q \in \mathbb{Q}^n$ and any finite
set $S \subset \mathbb{Q}^n$ with $p, q \notin S$, there exists a path $p = u_0,
 u_1, \ldots, u_L = q$ in $\mathbb{Q}^n$ with $\|u_{i+1}-u_i\|_\infty = 1$ and $
u_i \notin S$ for all $0 < i < L$.

*Proof of Key Lemma.* Write $p = (p_1, \ldots, p_n)$ and $q = (q_1, \ldots, q_n)
$.

**Step A (reach a safe $x_1$-value).** From $p$, step to $p' = (p_1 + t,\; p_2 +
 1,\; p_3, \ldots, p_n)$ where $t \in [-1,1] \cap \mathbb{Q}$ is chosen so that
$p' \notin S$ and $a := p_1 + t \notin \pi_1(S)$ (the projection of $S$ onto the
 first coordinate). This is possible since $[-1,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}$ is infinite wh
ile at most $2|S|$ values of $t$ are excluded.

**Step B (align coordinates $x_3,\ldots,x_n$ with $q$).** For each $j \in \{3,\l
dots,n\}$, change $x_j$ from $p_j$ to $q_j$ by steps of $\pm 1$ in the $j$-th co
ordinate, keeping all other coordinates fixed (so each step has $\ell^\infty$-no
rm 1). Every intermediate point has first coordinate $a \notin \pi_1(S)$, hence
avoids $S$. After this, we are at $(a,\; p_2+1,\; q_3, \ldots, q_n)$.

**Step C (navigate in the $(x_1,x_2)$-plane to reach $q$).** Keep $x_3,\ldots,x_
n$ fixed at $q_3,\ldots,q_n$ throughout.

*C1.* From $(a, p_2+1)$, climb to $(a, M)$ for a large integer $M$ via steps $(0
,+1)$. All intermediate points have $x_1 = a \notin \pi_1(S)$, so they avoid $S$
.

*C2.* From $(a, M)$, descend to $(c, q_2+1)$ via $M - q_2 - 1$ steps of the form
 $(s_i, -1)$ with $s_i \in [-1,1] \cap \mathbb{Q}$. At each step, the landing po
int $(a + \sum_{j \leq i} s_j,\; M-i,\; q_3,\ldots,q_n)$ belongs to $S$ for at m
ost $|S|$ values of $s_i$. Since $[-1,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}$ is infinite, we can alwa
ys choose $s_i$ avoiding these finitely many bad values. We choose the $s_i$ to
steer the running sum $\sum s_i$ toward $q_1 - a$: use the first $\lceil |q_1 -
a| \rceil$ steps to move $x_1$ close to $q_1$ (each $s_i = \pm 1$ or a fractiona
l correction), then use remaining steps alternating $\pm\delta$ (for small ratio
nal $\delta$ avoiding bad values) to keep $x_1$ near $q_1$. For $M$ large enough
, the target $q_1 - a$ lies in $[-(M-q_2-1),\, M-q_2-1]$, so this is achievable.
 The final point $(c, q_2+1, q_3,\ldots,q_n)$ satisfies $|c - q_1| \leq 1$.

*C3.* Step from $(c, q_2+1, q_3,\ldots,q_n)$ to $q = (q_1, q_2, q_3,\ldots,q_n)$
. The displacement is $(q_1 - c,\, -1,\, 0,\ldots, 0)$ with $\ell^\infty$-norm $
\max(|q_1-c|, 1) = 1$ since $|q_1 - c| \leq 1$.

All intermediate vertices avoid $S$. If any vertex repeats, we shortcut the loop
: replacing $u_i, \ldots, u_j$ (where $u_i = u_j$) by the single step $u_i \to u
_{j+1}$ preserves the unit-norm property (since $u_i = u_j$ and $\|u_{j+1} - u_j
\|_\infty = 1$) and the avoidance of $S$. Repeating yields a simple path. $\squa
re$

**Greedy construction of the Hamiltonian path.** Enumerate $\mathbb{Q}^n = \{q_1
, q_2, q_3, \ldots\}$. We build finite simple paths $P_1, P_2, \ldots$ such that
:
1. $P_k$ is a prefix of $P_{k+1}$ (extension from the right end only),
2. $\{q_1, \ldots, q_k\} \subseteq V(P_k)$.

*Base:* $P_1 = (q_1)$.

*Inductive step:* Given $P_k = (v_1, \ldots, v_m)$ with $\{q_1,\ldots,q_k\} \sub
seteq \{v_1,\ldots,v_m\}$:
- If $q_{k+1} \in V(P_k)$, set $P_{k+1} = P_k$.
- Otherwise, apply the Key Lemma with $p = v_m$, $q = q_{k+1}$, and $S = V(P_k)
\setminus \{v_m\}$ (which is finite). This yields a simple path $v_m, w_1, \ldot
s, w_\ell, q_{k+1}$ with all $w_i \notin V(P_k) \setminus \{v_m\}$ and all $w_i$
 distinct. Set $P_{k+1} = (v_1, \ldots, v_m, w_1, \ldots, w_\ell, q_{k+1})$.

This is a simple path (the new vertices are outside $V(P_k)$ and distinct), $P_k
$ is a prefix of $P_{k+1}$, and $\{q_1,\ldots,q_{k+1}\} \subseteq V(P_{k+1})$.

*Limit:* Since each $P_{k+1}$ extends $P_k$ from the right, the sequence stabili
zes pointwise: the $i$-th vertex $f(i)$ is well-defined for all $i \in \mathbb{N
}$. Every $q_j$ enters $V(P_j)$ and remains in all subsequent paths, so every po
int of $\mathbb{Q}^n$ appears in the limit. No vertex is ever repeated (each ext
ension adds only new vertices). Thus $f: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{Q}^n$ is a bijec
tion with $\|f(k+1) - f(k)\|_\infty = 1$ for all $k$. $\square$

### Conclusion

The required bijection and norm exist if and only if $\boxed{n \geq 2}$.
