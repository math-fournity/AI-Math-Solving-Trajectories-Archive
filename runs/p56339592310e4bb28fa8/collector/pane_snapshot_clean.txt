## Proof

**Yes**, $S(z)$ can have a root inside the unit circle.

**Counterexample.** Consider the rational function
$$R(z) = z - 2,$$
whose unique root is $z = 2$. Since $|2| = 2 > 1$, this root lies **outside** th
e unit circle, so $R$ has no roots inside the unit circle.

Take $a = 2$. Then $aR(a) = 2 \cdot (2 - 2) = 0$, and
$$
S(z) = \frac{zR(z) - aR(a)}{z - a} = \frac{z(z-2) - 0}{z - 2} = \frac{z(z-2)}{z-
2} = z.
$$
(The singularity at $z = 2$ is removable, and the simplified form holds everywhe
re.)

Thus $S(z) = z$, which has a root at $z = 0$. Since $|0| = 0 < 1$, this root lie
s **inside** the unit circle.

**General observation.** Whenever $R(a) = 0$ for some $a$ with $|a| \geq 1$ (i.e
., $a$ is a root of $R$ not inside the unit circle), we have $aR(a) = 0$, so
$$
S(z) = \frac{zR(z)}{z - a} = z \cdot \frac{R(z)}{z - a}.
$$
Since $R(a) = 0$, the factor $(z - a)$ divides $R(z)$, making $S$ a rational fun
ction that always carries the factor $z$. Hence $z = 0$ is always a root of $S$
in this situation — and $0$ is always inside the unit circle.

This shows that the condition "$R$ has no roots inside the unit circle" does **n
ot** prevent $S$ from having roots inside the unit circle. $\blacksquare$
