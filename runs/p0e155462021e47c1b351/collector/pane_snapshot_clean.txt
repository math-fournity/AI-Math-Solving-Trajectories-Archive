**Answer: No.**

A countable linearly independent set in a separable Hilbert space does **not** n
ecessarily form a Schauder basis. A Schauder basis requires more than linear ind
ependence: it requires that **every** vector in the space admit a *unique* expan
sion as a convergent series $\sum_{n} a_n x_n$, which in particular forces the c
losed linear span of the basis to be the entire space. Mere (algebraic) linear i
ndependence guarantees neither completeness of the span nor uniqueness/unconditi
onal convergence of expansions.

**Counterexample.** Let $H = \ell^2(\mathbb{N})$, the canonical separable Hilber
t space, with standard orthonormal basis $\{e_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$. Consider the
set
$$S = \{e_2, e_3, e_4, \ldots\}.$$

- **$S$ is countable:** it is indexed by $\{2, 3, 4, \ldots\} \subset \mathbb{N}
$.
- **$S$ is linearly independent:** any finite subcollection $\{e_{n_1}, \ldots,
e_{n_k}\}$ with distinct $n_i \geq 2$ is linearly independent, since the $e_n$ a
re orthonormal.
- **$S$ is not a Schauder basis for $\ell^2$:** The closed linear span of $S$ is
$$\overline{\operatorname{span}}\, S = \{x = (x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots) \in \ell^2
: x_1 = 0\},$$
which is a **proper** closed subspace of $\ell^2$. In particular, $e_1 \in \ell^
2$ does not lie in $\overline{\operatorname{span}}\, S$ (it is orthogonal to eve
ry element of $S$), so $e_1$ admits no expansion $\sum_{n=2}^{\infty} a_n e_n$ a
t all. Hence $S$ fails the defining property of a Schauder basis.

Thus a countable linearly independent set can fail to be a Schauder basis even i
n the most standard separable Hilbert space.

**Remark (a stronger failure).** Even requiring the closed span to be all of $H$
 is insufficient. There exist countable linearly independent sets whose linear s
pan is dense in $H$ yet which are still not Schauder bases, because the expansio
n of some vector either fails to converge or is not unique — the associated coor
dinate functionals need not be continuous. This shows that the gap between "coun
table linearly independent set" and "Schauder basis" is substantial: one needs b
oth **totality** (dense span) **and** the **basis property** (unique, convergent
 expansions with continuous coordinate functionals), neither of which is implied
 by linear independence alone.

Therefore, a countable linearly independent set in a separable Hilbert space doe
s **not** necessarily form a Schauder basis.
