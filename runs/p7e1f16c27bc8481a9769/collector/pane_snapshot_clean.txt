## Necessary and Sufficient Condition for an Orthogonal Coordinate System

**Claim.** A coordinate system $(x^1, \dots, x^n)$ is orthogonal if and only if
its metric tensor $(g_{ij})$ is **diagonal**, i.e.

$$g_{ij} = 0 \quad \text{for all } i \neq j.$$

---

### Preliminaries

Let the coordinate system be defined on a region of an $n$-dimensional (pseudo-)
Riemannian manifold with metric tensor $g$. In coordinates $(x^1,\dots,x^n)$, th
e coordinate basis vectors are

$$\mathbf{e}_i = \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial x^i}, \qquad i = 1,\dots,n,
$$

where $\mathbf{r}$ is the position vector. The components of the metric tensor a
re the inner products of these basis vectors:

$$g_{ij} = \mathbf{e}_i \cdot \mathbf{e}_j.$$

A coordinate system is called **orthogonal** when, at every point, the coordinat
e basis vectors $\mathbf{e}_1, \dots, \mathbf{e}_n$ are mutually perpendicular,
i.e.

$$\mathbf{e}_i \cdot \mathbf{e}_j = 0 \quad \text{for all } i \neq j.$$

---

### Proof

**(⇒) Orthogonal coordinate system ⇒ diagonal metric tensor.**

Assume the coordinate system is orthogonal. By definition, the coordinate basis
vectors satisfy $\mathbf{e}_i \cdot \mathbf{e}_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$. Since $g_{
ij} = \mathbf{e}_i \cdot \mathbf{e}_j$, we immediately obtain

$$g_{ij} = 0 \quad \text{for all } i \neq j.$$

Thus the matrix $(g_{ij})$ has no off-diagonal entries; it is diagonal.

**(⇐) Diagonal metric tensor ⇒ orthogonal coordinate system.**

Assume the metric tensor is diagonal: $g_{ij} = 0$ for all $i \neq j$. By defini
tion of the metric components,

$$g_{ij} = \mathbf{e}_i \cdot \mathbf{e}_j = 0 \quad \text{for all } i \neq j.$$

This says precisely that every pair of distinct coordinate basis vectors is perp
endicular. Hence the coordinate basis is mutually orthogonal at every point, whi
ch is the definition of an orthogonal coordinate system.

---

### Reasoning Behind the Condition

The key insight is that the metric tensor **encodes all pairwise inner products
of the coordinate basis vectors**. Its diagonal entries $g_{ii} = \|\mathbf{e}_i
\|^2$ give the squared lengths (scale factors) of each coordinate direction, whi
le its off-diagonal entries $g_{ij}$ ($i\neq j$) measure the **cosine of the ang
le** between coordinate directions, weighted by their lengths:

$$g_{ij} = \|\mathbf{e}_i\|\,\|\mathbf{e}_j\|\cos\theta_{ij}.$$

- **Geometric meaning of $g_{ij}=0$:** Since $\|\mathbf{e}_i\|, \|\mathbf{e}_j\|
 > 0$ (the basis is non-degenerate), $g_{ij} = 0$ forces $\cos\theta_{ij} = 0$,
i.e. $\theta_{ij} = 90°$. So vanishing off-diagonal entries are *exactly* the st
atement that coordinate directions meet at right angles.
- **Why diagonal is both necessary and sufficient:** Orthogonality is *defined*
by vanishing pairwise inner products, and the off-diagonal metric entries *are*
those pairwise inner products. The two conditions are therefore the same stateme
nt in different languages — one geometric, one algebraic. No additional hypothes
is (such as the metric being Euclidean, or the coordinates being Cartesian) is r
equired; the equivalence holds for any Riemannian metric in any coordinate chart
.

**Consequence (scale factors / line element).** When the metric is diagonal, the
 line element simplifies to

$$ds^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} g_{ii}\,(dx^i)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(h_i\bigr)^2 (dx^
i)^2,$$

where $h_i = \sqrt{g_{ii}}$ are the **Lamé scale factors**. This is the familiar
 form used for orthogonal curvilinear coordinates (Cartesian, cylindrical, spher
ical, etc.), and it is precisely the diagonal form of the metric that makes this
 decomposition possible — confirming that diagonality of $(g_{ij})$ is the exact
 algebraic signature of geometric orthogonality.
