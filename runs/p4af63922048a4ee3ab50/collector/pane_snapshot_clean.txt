## Proof

**Claim.** There exists a bijection between the set of prime numbers and the set
 of positive integers that are not divisible by any perfect square other than 1
(the squarefree positive integers).

**Definitions.** Let $\mathbb{P} = \{2, 3, 5, 7, 11, \dots\}$ be the set of prim
es. Let $S = \{n \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0} : \text{no } d^2 \text{ with } d > 1 \text{
 divides } n\}$ be the set of squarefree positive integers.

A positive integer $n$ is squarefree if and only if in its prime factorization $
n = p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_k^{a_k}$ every exponent satisfies $a_i \in \{0, 1\}$. Equ
ivalently, $n$ is squarefree iff $n$ is a (finite) product of *distinct* primes,
 including the empty product $n = 1$.

**Step 1: Both sets are infinite.**

$\mathbb{P}$ is infinite (Euclid's theorem).

$S$ is infinite: every prime $p$ is squarefree (its only prime factor appears to
 exponent $1$), so $\mathbb{P} \subseteq S$, hence $S$ is infinite.

**Step 2: $S$ is countable.**

$S \subseteq \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$, and $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ is countable, so $S$ is cou
ntable.

**Step 3: An explicit bijection is unnecessary — countability suffices.**

A standard theorem of set theory states:

> *If $A$ and $B$ are both countably infinite sets, then there exists a bijectio
n $A \to B$.*

*Proof of the theorem.* Since $A$ is countably infinite, there is a bijection $f
: \mathbb{Z}_{>0} \to A$. Since $B$ is countably infinite, there is a bijection
$g: \mathbb{Z}_{>0} \to B$. Then $g \circ f^{-1}: A \to B$ is a composition of b
ijections, hence a bijection. $\square$

Applying this with $A = \mathbb{P}$ and $B = S$ (both countably infinite by Step
s 1–2), we conclude:

$$\exists\text{ a bijection } \mathbb{P} \to S.$$

**Step 4 (optional, for concreteness): an explicit bijection.**

For those who prefer an explicit construction, enumerate the primes in increasin
g order $p_1 = 2, p_2 = 3, p_3 = 5, \dots$. Every squarefree $n > 1$ has a uniqu
e representation $n = p_{i_1} p_{i_2} \cdots p_{i_k}$ with $i_1 < i_2 < \cdots <
 i_k$. Map each nonempty finite subset $\{i_1, \dots, i_k\} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}
_{>0}$ to the squarefree number $p_{i_1}\cdots p_{i_k}$, and map the empty subse
t to $1$. This gives a bijection between the set of all finite subsets of $\math
bb{Z}_{>0}$ and $S$.

The set of all finite subsets of $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ is countably infinite (it is
a countable union, over $k$, of the countable sets of $k$-element subsets). Sinc
e $\mathbb{P}$ is also countably infinite, composing the enumeration of finite s
ubsets with the above map and then with any enumeration of $\mathbb{P}$ yields a
n explicit bijection $\mathbb{P} \to S$.

**Conclusion.** The set of primes and the set of squarefree positive integers ar
e both countably infinite, and any two countably infinite sets are in bijection.
 Therefore such a bijection exists. $\blacksquare$
