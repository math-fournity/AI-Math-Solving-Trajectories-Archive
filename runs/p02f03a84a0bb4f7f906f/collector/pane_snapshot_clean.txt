The statement is **false**. Here is a counterexample and proof.

## Counterexample

Let $R = \mathbb{Z}$ (a PID), let $r = s = 2$ (both prime elements of $\mathbb{Z
}$), and let $M = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ (a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-mod
ule).

**Compute $\operatorname{Ann}_M(sr)$:** Here $sr = 2 \cdot 2 = 4$. We have
$$\operatorname{Ann}_M(4) = \{m \in \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} : 4m \equiv 0 \pmod{4
}\} = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} = M.$$

**Compute $\operatorname{Ann}_M(s)$ and $\operatorname{Ann}_M(r)$:** Since $r =
s = 2$,
$$\operatorname{Ann}_M(s) = \operatorname{Ann}_M(r) = \{m \in \mathbb{Z}/4\mathb
b{Z} : 2m \equiv 0 \pmod{4}\} = \{0, 2\} = 2\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb
{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}.$$

**Check the claimed equality:** The right-hand side $\operatorname{Ann}_M(s) \op
lus \operatorname{Ann}_M(r)$ is interpreted as an internal direct sum of submodu
les of $M$. For an internal direct sum to be well-defined, we need $\operatornam
e{Ann}_M(s) \cap \operatorname{Ann}_M(r) = \{0\}$. But since $r = s$,
$$\operatorname{Ann}_M(s) \cap \operatorname{Ann}_M(r) = 2\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}
 \neq \{0\}.$$
So the internal direct sum is not even well-defined. Moreover, even if we interp
ret $\oplus$ as an external direct sum, we get
$$\operatorname{Ann}_M(s) \oplus \operatorname{Ann}_M(r) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\math
bb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z},$$
which has exponent $2$, whereas $\operatorname{Ann}_M(sr) = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{
Z}$ has exponent $4$. These are not isomorphic as $\mathbb{Z}$-modules. In eithe
r interpretation,
$$\operatorname{Ann}_M(sr) \neq \operatorname{Ann}_M(s) \oplus \operatorname{Ann
}_M(r). \qquad \blacksquare$$

## Remark

The statement **is** true when $r$ and $s$ are **non-associate** primes (i.e., $
(r) \neq (s)$). In that case, by the structure theorem, each cyclic summand $R/(
p^e)$ of $M$ contributes to $\operatorname{Ann}_M(s)$ only when $p \sim s$ and t
o $\operatorname{Ann}_M(r)$ only when $p \sim r$, so the two annihilators live o
n disjoint sets of summands, their intersection is trivial, and one verifies on
each summand that $\operatorname{Ann}_{R/(p^e)}(sr) = \operatorname{Ann}_{R/(p^e
)}(s) + \operatorname{Ann}_{R/(p^e)}(r)$. The coprimality of distinct primes in
a PID is what makes this work. The statement as written fails because it does no
t require $r$ and $s$ to be distinct (non-associate).
