# Proof

**Setup.** Let $\Omega = C([0,1],\mathbb{R})$ be the path space, $B_t(\omega)=\o
mega(t)$ the coordinate process, and $\nu$ the Wiener measure (the law of standa
rd Brownian motion starting at $0$). Let $\mu = (B_1)_\# \nu$ be the law of the
terminal value $B_1$, which is the standard Gaussian $N(0,1)$ with density $\var
phi(y) = (2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-y^2/2}$ with respect to Lebesgue measure. Since $\mu \
sim \mathrm{Leb}$ (mutually absolutely continuous), the notions "$\mu$-a.e." and
 "Lebesgue-a.e." coincide.

By the disintegration theorem, there exists a family $\{\nu_y\}_{y\in\mathbb{R}}
$ of probability measures on $\Omega$ (defined $\mu$-a.e., hence Lebesgue-a.e.)
such that
$$\nu = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \nu_y \, \mu(dy), \qquad \text{and} \qquad \nu_y(\{\om
ega : \omega(1)=y\})=1 \text{ for } \mu\text{-a.e. } y.$$

**Claim.** For **every** $y\in\mathbb{R}$ (hence in particular for Lebesgue-a.e.
 $y$), $\nu_y$ is the law of the Brownian bridge from $0$ to $y$.

Recall the Brownian bridge from $0$ to $y$ is the centered Gaussian process $\be
ta^{(y)}$ on $[0,1]$ with
$$\beta^{(y)}_0 = 0,\quad \beta^{(y)}_1 = y, \quad \mathbb{E}[\beta^{(y)}_t] = t
y, \quad \mathrm{Cov}(\beta^{(y)}_s,\beta^{(y)}_t) = \min(s,t)-st.$$

**Proof of the claim.** We exhibit an explicit version of the disintegration tha
t is the Brownian bridge law for every $y$, and then invoke uniqueness of the di
sintegration up to $\mu$-null sets.

*Step 1: Finite-dimensional conditional distributions are Gaussian.* Fix $0 < t_
1 < \cdots < t_n < 1$. Under $\nu$, the vector $(B_{t_1},\dots,B_{t_n},B_1)$ is
jointly Gaussian with
$$\mathbb{E}[B_{t_i}] = 0,\quad \mathrm{Cov}(B_{t_i},B_{t_j}) = \min(t_i,t_j),\q
uad \mathrm{Cov}(B_{t_i},B_1)=t_i,\quad \mathrm{Var}(B_1)=1.$$

The conditional distribution of $(B_{t_1},\dots,B_{t_n})$ given $B_1 = y$ is Gau
ssian (conditional of a joint Gaussian is Gaussian) with:

- **Conditional mean:** $\displaystyle \mathbb{E}[B_{t_i}\mid B_1=y] = \frac{\ma
thrm{Cov}(B_{t_i},B_1)}{\mathrm{Var}(B_1)}\,y = t_i\, y.$

- **Conditional covariance:**
$$\mathrm{Cov}(B_{t_i},B_{t_j}\mid B_1) = \min(t_i,t_j) - \frac{t_i\, t_j}{1} =
\min(t_i,t_j) - t_i t_j.$$

These are exactly the mean $t_i y$ and covariance $\min(t_i,t_j)-t_i t_j$ of $(\
beta^{(y)}_{t_1},\dots,\beta^{(y)}_{t_n})$.

*Step 2: Equality of laws.* Both $\nu_y$ (the conditional law) and the Brownian-
bridge law $\pi_y$ are probability measures on $C([0,1],\mathbb{R})$ under which
 the coordinate process is Gaussian. They agree on all finite-dimensional margin
als at times $0<t_1<\cdots<t_n<1$ (and trivially at $t=0$ where both give $\delt
a_0$, and at $t=1$ where both give $\delta_y$). By the Kolmogorov extension theo
rem (or equivalently by equality of all cylinder-set probabilities), $\nu_y = \p
i_y$ as measures on path space.

*Step 3: This version works for every $y$.* The computation above is valid for *
*every** $y\in\mathbb{R}$ — no division by zero or singularity occurs, since $\m
athrm{Var}(B_1)=1>0$. Thus $\{\pi_y\}_{y\in\mathbb{R}}$ is a family of probabili
ty measures, defined for all $y$, satisfying $\pi_y(\omega(1)=y)=1$, and
$$\nu = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \pi_y\,\mu(dy).$$
Indeed, for any bounded measurable $F$ on $\Omega$:
$$\int_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{E}_{\pi_y}[F]\,\mu(dy) = \mathbb{E}_\nu\!\big[\mathb
b{E}[F\mid B_1]\big] = \mathbb{E}_\nu[F],$$
where the first equality uses Step 2 (the regular conditional distribution of $F
$ given $B_1=y$ equals $\mathbb{E}_{\pi_y}[F]$) and the second is the tower prop
erty.

*Step 4: Uniqueness.* The disintegration of $\nu$ over $\mu=(B_1)_\#\nu$ is uniq
ue up to $\mu$-null sets: if $\{\nu_y\}$ and $\{\nu'_y\}$ are two disintegration
s, then $\nu_y = \nu'_y$ for $\mu$-a.e. $y$. Since $\{\pi_y\}$ is a valid disint
egration defined for **all** $y$, any disintegration $\{\nu_y\}$ satisfies $\nu_
y = \pi_y$ for $\mu$-a.e. $y$, i.e. for Lebesgue-a.e. $y$ (as $\mu\sim\mathrm{Le
b}$).

**Conclusion.** Yes. For Lebesgue almost every $y$, the conditional law $\nu_y$
is exactly the law of the Brownian bridge starting at $0$ and ending at $y$. In
fact, the natural version of the disintegration gives this for **every** $y\in\m
athbb{R}$.
