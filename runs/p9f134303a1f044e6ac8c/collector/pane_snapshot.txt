## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, such a set $B$ always exists.

**Step 1: $A$ contains a perfect set.**

Since $A$ is an uncountable Borel subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$, the perfect set theo
rem (valid in ZFC) implies $A$ contains a nonempty perfect subset $P$. A perfect
 set is closed and has no isolated points; consequently $P$ is uncountable with
$|P| = \mathfrak{c}$.

**Step 2: $P$ contains a Cantor set of Lebesgue measure zero.**

We construct a Cantor set $C \subseteq P$ with $\lambda(C) = 0$. Since $P$ is pe
rfect, every point of $P$ is a limit point of $P$, so every neighborhood of a po
int in $P$ contains infinitely many points of $P$.

Build a binary tree of closed balls $\{\overline{B}_s : s \in \{0,1\}^{<\omega}\
}$:
- **Level 0:** Choose $\overline{B}_\emptyset$, a closed ball whose interior mee
ts $P$.
- **Level $k+1$ from level $k$:** Given $\overline{B}_s$ (radius $r_k$) with $P
\cap \overline{B}_s$ containing a point in the interior, pick two distinct point
s $x_{s0}, x_{s1} \in P$ in the interior of $\overline{B}_s$. Choose disjoint cl
osed balls $\overline{B}_{s0}, \overline{B}_{s1}$ centered near $x_{s0}, x_{s1}$
, contained in $\overline{B}_s$, each with interior meeting $P$, and with common
 radius $r_{k+1}$ satisfying
$$2^{k+1}\,\omega_n\, r_{k+1}^n < 2^{-k},$$
where $\omega_n$ is the volume of the unit ball in $\mathbb{R}^n$. This is achie
vable because $P$ is perfect, allowing us to find points of $P$ arbitrarily clos
e together and thus choose arbitrarily small balls.

Set $C = \bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty} \bigcup_{s \in \{0,1\}^k} \overline{B}_s$. Then:
- **$C \subseteq P$:** Each $\overline{B}_s$ was chosen to meet $P$, and since $
P$ is closed, all limit points remain in $P$.
- **$C$ is a Cantor set:** The construction yields a space homeomorphic to $\{0,
1\}^{\mathbb{N}}$, so $|C| = \mathfrak{c}$.
- **$\lambda(C) = 0$:** For every $k$, $C \subseteq \bigcup_{s \in \{0,1\}^k} \o
verline{B}_s$, so $\lambda(C) \leq 2^k \omega_n r_k^n < 2^{-(k-1)}$. Letting $k
\to \infty$ gives $\lambda(C) = 0$.
- **$C$ is closed** (intersection of closed sets), hence Borel.

**Step 3: There exists a non-Borel subset of $C$.**

Since $|C| = \mathfrak{c}$, the set $C$ has $2^{\mathfrak{c}}$ subsets. The Bore
l $\sigma$-algebra of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is generated by a countable basis, so the n
umber of Borel subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is $\mathfrak{c}$. Thus the number of B
orel subsets of $C$ is at most $\mathfrak{c}$. By Cantor's theorem, $2^{\mathfra
k{c}} > \mathfrak{c}$, so there exists $B \subseteq C$ that is **not** Borel.

**Step 4: Verification.**

- $B \subseteq C \subseteq P \subseteq A$, so $B \subset A$. ✓
- $C$ has Lebesgue measure zero and $B \subseteq C$. By **completeness** of Lebe
sgue measure, every subset of a measure-zero measurable set is Lebesgue measurab
le with measure zero. Hence $B$ is Lebesgue measurable and $\lambda(B) = 0$. ✓
- $B$ is not Borel by construction. ✓
