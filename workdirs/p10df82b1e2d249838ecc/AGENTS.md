# Problem

In the coastal village of Tritonia, there are exactly 3 distinct offshore fishing zones that form the local territory $S$. 

A "Management Strategy" $T$ is defined as a specific collection of various conservation plans. Each individual conservation plan within a strategy identifies a specific set of fishing zones (subsets of $S$) that are to be protected simultaneously. 

For a Management Strategy $T$ to be considered "Ecologically Stable," it must satisfy a dual-balance requirement:
1. For any two conservation plans $U$ and $V$ belonging to the strategy $T$, the plan that protects only the zones common to both (the intersection $U \cap V$) must also be included in the strategy $T$.
2. For those same two plans $U$ and $V$, the plan that protects all zones covered by either plan (the union $U \cup V$) must also be included in the strategy $T$.

How many different Ecologically Stable Management Strategies $T$ can be formed from the possible subsets of the 3 fishing zones?
