## Solution

**Domain note.** The integrand $x^{-1/2}$ is real-valued only for $x>0$, and $(1
+x)^2$ vanishes at $x=-1$ (a double pole on the negative real axis). Hence the i
ntegral over $(-\infty,\infty)$ is not convergent in the ordinary sense; the mea
ningful, convergent integral is

$$I = \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{x^{-1/2}}{(1+x)^2}\,dx.$$

We evaluate it by two independent methods.

---

### Method 1: Residue Calculus (Keyhole Contour)

Take $f(z)=\dfrac{z^{-1/2}}{(1+z)^2}$ with the branch $z^{-1/2}=e^{-\frac12\log
z}$, $\arg z\in(0,2\pi)$, so the branch cut lies along the **positive** real axi
s. The only singularity inside the keyhole contour is the double pole at $z=-1=e
^{i\pi}$.

**Residue at $z=-1$ (pole of order 2):**

$$\operatorname{Res}_{z=-1} f(z)=\frac{d}{dz}\bigl[z^{-1/2}\bigr]_{z=-1}=-\frac1
2\,z^{-3/2}\Big|_{z=-1}.$$

Since $-1=e^{i\pi}$, we have $(-1)^{-3/2}=e^{-3i\pi/2}=e^{i\pi/2}=i$, so

$$\operatorname{Res}_{z=-1} f(z)=-\frac{i}{2}.$$

**Contour integral.** By the residue theorem,

$$\oint f(z)\,dz = 2\pi i\left(-\frac{i}{2}\right)=\pi.$$

**Contributions from the two sides of the cut.** On the upper edge ($\arg z\to 0
^+$): $z^{-1/2}=x^{-1/2}$. On the lower edge ($\arg z\to 2\pi^-$): $z^{-1/2}=x^{
-1/2}e^{-i\pi}=-x^{-1/2}$. Traversed in opposite directions, they combine to

$$\int_{\epsilon}^{R}\frac{x^{-1/2}}{(1+x)^2}\,dx+\int_{R}^{\epsilon}\frac{-x^{-
1/2}}{(1+x)^2}\,dx=2\int_{\epsilon}^{R}\frac{x^{-1/2}}{(1+x)^2}\,dx.$$

The large circle ($|f|\sim R^{-5/2}$, length $\sim 2\pi R$) contributes $O(R^{-3
/2})\to0$; the small circle ($|f|\sim\epsilon^{-1/2}$, length $\sim 2\pi\epsilon
$) contributes $O(\epsilon^{1/2})\to0$. Therefore

$$2I = \pi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I=\frac{\pi}{2}.$$

---

### Method 2: Beta Function (Real Method)

Substitute $x=\dfrac{t}{1-t}$, $\,dx=\dfrac{dt}{(1-t)^2}$, with $t:0\to1$:

$$x^{-1/2}=\left(\frac{1-t}{t}\right)^{1/2},\qquad (1+x)^2=\frac{1}{(1-t)^2}.$$

Hence

$$I=\int_0^1 \left(\frac{1-t}{t}\right)^{1/2}(1-t)^2\cdot\frac{dt}{(1-t)^2}=\int
_0^1 t^{-1/2}(1-t)^{1/2}\,dt=B\!\left(\tfrac12,\tfrac32\right).$$

Using $\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt\pi$, $\Gamma(3/2)=\tfrac12\sqrt\pi$, $\Gamma(2)=1$:

$$B\!\left(\tfrac12,\tfrac32\right)=\frac{\Gamma(1/2)\,\Gamma(3/2)}{\Gamma(2)}=\
frac{\sqrt\pi\cdot\frac12\sqrt\pi}{1}=\frac{\pi}{2}.$$

---

Both methods agree:

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{-1/2}}{(1+x)^2}\,dx=\frac{\pi}{2
}.}$$
