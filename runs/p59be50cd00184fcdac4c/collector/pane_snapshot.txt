## Answer

**Yes.** The spectrum of a densely defined unbounded self-adjoint operator on a
Hilbert space is necessarily an unbounded subset of the real line.

---

## Proof

Let $A$ be a densely defined self-adjoint operator on a Hilbert space $H$. We es
tablish two facts:

**Fact 1: $\sigma(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.**

Since $A$ is self-adjoint ($A = A^*$), for every $z \in \mathbb{C} \setminus \ma
thbb{R}$, the operator $A - zI$ is bounded below. Indeed, for $x \in D(A)$:

$$\|(A - zI)x\|^2 = \|(A - \operatorname{Re} z\, I)x - i\,(\operatorname{Im} z)\
, x\|^2.$$

Using self-adjointness, the cross terms vanish:

$$\|(A - zI)x\|^2 = \|(A - \operatorname{Re} z\, I)x\|^2 + (\operatorname{Im} z)
^2 \|x\|^2 \ge (\operatorname{Im} z)^2 \|x\|^2.$$

Since $A$ is self-adjoint (hence closed), $A - zI$ has closed range. The estimat
e shows $\ker(A - zI) = \{0\}$ and the range is closed; self-adjointness also gi
ves $\operatorname{ran}(A - zI)^\perp = \ker(A^* - \bar z I) = \ker(A - \bar z I
) = \{0\}$. Thus $A - zI$ is bijective from $D(A)$ onto $H$ with bounded inverse
, so $z \in \rho(A)$. Hence $\sigma(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.

**Fact 2: If $A$ is unbounded, then $\sigma(A)$ is unbounded.**

We prove the contrapositive. Suppose $\sigma(A)$ is bounded, i.e., $\sigma(A) \s
ubseteq [-M, M]$ for some $M > 0$.

By the spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators, there exists a projection-va
lued measure $E$ supported on $\sigma(A)$ such that

$$A = \int_{\sigma(A)} \lambda\, dE(\lambda),$$

with domain

$$D(A) = \left\{x \in H : \int_{\sigma(A)} \lambda^2\, d\langle E(\lambda)x, x\r
angle < \infty\right\}.$$

Since $\sigma(A) \subseteq [-M, M]$, for every $x \in H$:

$$\int_{\sigma(A)} \lambda^2\, d\langle E(\lambda)x, x\rangle \le M^2 \int_{\sig
ma(A)} d\langle E(\lambda)x, x\rangle = M^2 \|x\|^2 < \infty.$$

Therefore $D(A) = H$, and for all $x \in H$:

$$\|Ax\|^2 = \int_{\sigma(A)} \lambda^2\, d\langle E(\lambda)x, x\rangle \le M^2
 \|x\|^2,$$

so $\|A\| \le M$. This means $A$ is a bounded operator defined on all of $H$—con
tradicting the hypothesis that $A$ is unbounded.

**Conclusion.** By contraposition, if $A$ is self-adjoint and unbounded, its spe
ctrum cannot be bounded. Combined with Fact 1, $\sigma(A)$ is an **unbounded sub
set of $\mathbb{R}$**. $\blacksquare$
