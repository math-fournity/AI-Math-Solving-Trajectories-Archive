## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, such a function exists.

**Step 1: Reduce to an additive equation.**

Suppose $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ satisfies $f(x)f(y)=f(x+y)$ and $f$ is not i
dentically zero. Pick $a$ with $f(a)\neq 0$. Setting $y=0$:
$$f(x)f(0)=f(x)\implies f(0)=1.$$
Setting $y=-x$:
$$f(x)f(-x)=f(0)=1,$$
so $f(x)\neq 0$ for every $x$. In particular $f(x)=f(x/2)^2>0$ for all $x$, so $
f$ is strictly positive. Hence we may write $f(x)=e^{g(x)}$ where $g(x)=\ln f(x)
:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$. The functional equation becomes
$$e^{g(x)+g(y)}=e^{g(x+y)},$$
so $g$ is **additive**: $g(x+y)=g(x)+g(y)$ for all $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$.

Conversely, any additive $g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ gives a solution $f(x)=e^{g
(x)}$ of the original equation. Moreover, since $\ln$ is continuous, $f$ is cont
inuous if and only if $g$ is continuous.

Thus the problem reduces to: **does there exist a discontinuous additive functio
n $g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$?**

**Step 2: Construct a discontinuous additive function via a Hamel basis.**

View $\mathbb{R}$ as a vector space over $\mathbb{Q}$. By the Axiom of Choice, $
\mathbb{R}$ admits a Hamel basis $B$ (a $\mathbb{Q}$-basis). Since $\mathbb{R}$
is uncountable and $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable, $B$ is uncountable; in particular
$|B|\geq 2$, so pick two distinct elements $b_1, b_2\in B$.

Define a $\mathbb{Q}$-linear map $g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ by specifying its v
alues on the basis:
$$g(b_1)=0,\qquad g(b)=b\ \text{for all }b\in B\setminus\{b_1\},$$
and extending $\mathbb{Q}$-linearly: every $x\in\mathbb{R}$ has a unique represe
ntation $x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_i b_i$ with $q_i\in\mathbb{Q}$, $b_i\in B$, and we se
t $g(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_i\,g(b_i)$.

Being $\mathbb{Q}$-linear, $g$ is additive: $g(x+y)=g(x)+g(y)$.

**Step 3: Show $g$ is discontinuous.**

Every continuous additive function on $\mathbb{R}$ is of the form $g(x)=cx$ for
some constant $c\in\mathbb{R}$ (this is the classical result: additivity gives $
g(q)=cq$ on $\mathbb{Q}$ with $c=g(1)$, and continuity extends to all of $\mathb
b{R}$).

Suppose for contradiction that $g(x)=cx$. Then $g(b_1)=c\,b_1=0$ implies $c=0$ (
since $b_1\neq 0$). But then $g(b_2)=c\,b_2=0$, contradicting $g(b_2)=b_2\neq 0$
. Hence $g$ is not of the form $cx$, and therefore $g$ is **discontinuous**.

**Step 4: Conclude.**

Set $f(x)=e^{g(x)}$. Then:
- $f(x)f(y)=e^{g(x)+g(y)}=e^{g(x+y)}=f(x+y)$ for all $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$. ✓
- $f$ is not everywhere continuous: if $f$ were continuous, then $g=\ln\circ\, f
$ would be continuous (composition of continuous functions, valid since $f>0$),
contradicting Step 3. ✓

Therefore, **there exists a function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ satisfying $f(x
)f(y)=f(x+y)$ for all $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$ that is not everywhere continuous.** $\
blacksquare$
