## Proof

**Scaling.** Multiply all charges by 4 (this does not change any probability). T
he batteries then hold **4, 8, 16, 32** (total 60). If all four are equal, each
holds 60/4 = **15**. Averaging a pair sets both to their common average, so a pa
ir is *good* if its two charges sum to **30** (then averaging gives 15, 15). The
re are 6 pairs and 4 independent cycles, hence **6⁴ = 1296** equally likely sequ
ences of pair-choices.

---

**Step 1 — State after one cycle.** The six possible states (averaging the chose
n pair) are:

| pair chosen | resulting state |
|---|---|
| (1,2) | (6, 6, 16, 32) |
| (1,3) | (10, 8, 10, 32) |
| (1,4) | (18, 8, 16, 18) |
| (2,3) | (4, 12, 12, 32) |
| (2,4) | (4, 20, 16, 20) |
| (3,4) | (4, 8, 24, 24) |

None of these contains the value 15, and **no pair in any of them sums to 30**.

---

**Step 2 — No battery equals 15 after two cycles.** Any charge after cycle 2 is
either an untouched cycle-1 value (none is 15) or the average of a cycle-1 pair
(which would be 15 only if that pair summed to 30 — impossible by Step 1). Hence
 **after two cycles, no battery is at 15**.

Consequently, **all-15 after three cycles is impossible**: to make every battery
 15 in cycle 3, the two batteries *not* selected in cycle 3 must already be 15 a
fter cycle 2, yet none is.

---

**Step 3 — What is required before cycle 4.** Suppose the state before cycle 4 i
s (a,b,c,d) and pair Q is selected. After cycle 4 the two batteries *not* in Q a
re unchanged, so they must already be 15; the two in Q must sum to 30. Since all
-15 after three cycles is impossible, the only feasible pre-cycle-4 state is:

> **exactly two batteries equal 15** (these are the two *not* selected in cycle
4), and the other two sum to 30.

For such a state, Q is forced (it must be the two non-15 batteries), giving **ex
actly 1** valid choice for cycle 4. Therefore

$$\#\{\text{valid 4-sequences}\} \;=\; \#\{\text{3-sequences ending with exactly
 two batteries }=15\}.$$

---

**Step 4 — Counting 3-sequences ending with exactly two 15's.** Let S be the sta
te after two cycles and let P be the pair selected in cycle 3. After cycle 3, th
e two batteries in P become their average m; the other two are unchanged. For ex
actly two batteries to equal 15:

- *Both 15's outside P:* they were 15 after cycle 2 — impossible (Step 2).
- *One 15 in P, one outside:* both batteries in P equal m, so one being 15 force
s m = 15, making *both* in P equal 15; then the outside one being 15 too gives ≥
3 fifteens, which (sum = 60) forces the fourth to 15 — i.e. all-15, ruled out.
- *Both 15's in P:* then m = 15, i.e. **P summed to 30 after cycle 2**, and the
unchanged pair is not 15 (automatic by Step 2). ✓

So a 3-sequence ends with exactly two 15's **iff** the 2-cycle state S has a goo
d pair (summing to 30) and cycle 3 selects it. Thus

$$\#\{\text{3-seq ending exactly two 15's}\} \;=\; \sum_S \mathrm{mult}(S)\cdot
g(S),$$

where mult(S) = number of 2-cycle sequences reaching S, and g(S) = number of goo
d pairs in S.

---

**Step 5 — Two-cycle states that contain a good pair.** Applying each of the 6 p
airs to each of the 6 cycle-1 states (36 cases) and checking which results conta
in two values summing to 30, the only hits are:

| 2-cycle state | how it arises | good pairs g |
|---|---|---|
| (6, 6, 24, 24) | from (6,6,16,32) choosing (3,4); from (4,8,24,24) choosing (1
,2) | 4 |
| (10, 20, 10, 20) | from (10,8,10,32) choosing (2,4); from (4,20,16,20) choosin
g (1,3) | 4 |
| (18, 12, 12, 18) | from (18,8,16,18) choosing (2,3); from (4,12,12,32) choosin
g (1,4) | 4 |

Each of these three states is of the form (a, a, b, b) with a + b = 30, so its f
our *cross*-pairs (one a with one b) all sum to 30, while its two same-value pai
rs sum to 2a and 2b (12 & 48, 20 & 40, 36 & 24 — none is 30). Hence g = 4 for ea
ch. Every other cycle-2 outcome (the remaining 30 of the 36) contains no pair su
mming to 30, so g = 0 there.

Each of the three states is reached by exactly **2** two-cycle sequences, so mul
t = 2 for each.

---

**Step 6 — Total.**

$$\#\{\text{valid 4-sequences}\} \;=\; 3\;\text{states}\;\times\;2\;(\text{mult}
)\;\times\;4\;(\text{good pairs}) \;=\; 24.$$

Therefore

$$\Pr(\text{all four equal after 4 cycles}) \;=\; \frac{24}{6^{4}} \;=\; \frac{2
4}{1296} \;=\; \boxed{\dfrac{1}{54}}.$$
