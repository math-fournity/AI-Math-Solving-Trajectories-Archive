## Solution

We solve the nonhomogeneous heat equation on $0 < x < L$, $t > 0$:

$$u_t = k\,u_{xx} + g(x), \qquad u(x,0)=f(x), \qquad u(0,t)=u(L,t)=0.$$

### 1. Eigenfunction basis

The operator $-\partial_{xx}$ with Dirichlet boundary conditions on $(0,L)$ has
eigenfunctions

$$X_n(x) = \sin\!\Big(\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\Big), \qquad n=1,2,\dots$$

with eigenvalues $\lambda_n = \big(\tfrac{n\pi}{L}\big)^2$. These form a complet
e orthogonal basis of $L^2(0,L)$, and every member individually satisfies $X_n(0
)=X_n(L)=0$. Hence any sufficiently regular function vanishing at the endpoints
can be expanded in a sine series. We expand the unknown solution, the source, an
d the initial data all in this basis:

$$u(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u_n(t)\,\sin\!\Big(\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\Big),$$
$$g(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} g_n\,\sin\!\Big(\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\Big), \qquad g_n=\t
frac{2}{L}\int_0^L g(x)\sin\!\Big(\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\Big)\,dx,$$
$$f(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f_n\,\sin\!\Big(\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\Big), \qquad f_n=\t
frac{2}{L}\int_0^L f(x)\sin\!\Big(\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\Big)\,dx.$$

### 2. Justification of term-by-term differentiation in $x$

We must justify substituting the series into the PDE, in particular differentiat
ing twice in $x$ term by term. Suppose the solution $u$ is continuous on $[0,L]\
times[0,\infty)$ with continuous derivatives up to the order required, and $u(\c
dot,t)$, $u_{xx}(\cdot,t)$ are continuous on $[0,L]$ with $u(0,t)=u(L,t)=0$. The
n for each fixed $t$, $u_{xx}(\cdot,t)$ is continuous on $[0,L]$, so its sine se
ries converges uniformly to $u_{xx}$ on $[0,L]$ (standard convergence theorem fo
r piecewise-$C^1$ functions vanishing at the endpoints). Likewise $u(\cdot,t)$ h
as a uniformly convergent sine series. Because $u_{xx}(\cdot,t)$ is continuous a
nd vanishes compatibly at the boundary (obtained by extending $u$ oddly), we may
 integrate by parts twice without boundary terms:

$$\tfrac{2}{L}\int_0^L u_{xx}(x,t)\sin\!\Big(\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\Big)dx = -\lambda
_n\,u_n(t).$$

Thus the Fourier-sine coefficient of $u_{xx}$ equals $-\lambda_n u_n(t)$, i.e. t
erm-by-term differentiation is legitimate: $u_{xx}\;\widehat{=}\;\sum_n u_n(t)(-
\lambda_n)\sin(\cdot)$. The same argument gives the coefficient of $u_t$ as $\do
t u_n(t)$ (differentiation in $t$ is justified by differentiating the coefficien
t formula $\dot u_n = \tfrac{2}{L}\int_0^L u_t\sin\,dx$, which is valid since $u
_t$ is continuous). Substituting the three series into the PDE and matching coef
ficients (orthogonality) yields, for every $n\ge 1$,

$$\dot u_n(t) = -k\lambda_n\,u_n(t) + g_n, \qquad u_n(0)=f_n.$$

### 3. Solving the ODE for each mode

This is a first-order linear ODE with constant coefficients. Multiplying by the
integrating factor $e^{k\lambda_n t}$:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\big[e^{k\lambda_n t}u_n(t)\big] = g_n\,e^{k\lambda_n t}.$$

Integrating from $0$ to $t$ and using $u_n(0)=f_n$:

$$u_n(t) = f_n\,e^{-k\lambda_n t} + \frac{g_n}{k\lambda_n}\big(1-e^{-k\lambda_n
t}\big).$$

### 4. The solution

Substituting back and writing $\lambda_n = (n\pi/L)^2$:

$$\boxed{\;u(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[f_n\,e^{-k(n\pi/L)^2 t}+\frac{g_n}{k(
n\pi/L)^2}\Big(1-e^{-k(n\pi/L)^2 t}\Big)\right]\sin\!\Big(\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\Big)
\;}$$

with

$$f_n=\frac{2}{L}\int_0^L f(x)\sin\!\Big(\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\Big)dx, \qquad g_n=\f
rac{2}{L}\int_0^L g(x)\sin\!\Big(\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\Big)dx.$$

### 5. Verification of all conditions

- **PDE.** For each $t>0$ the factor $e^{-k\lambda_n t}$ makes the series and it
s $x$-derivatives of every order converge uniformly (exponential decay beats any
 polynomial growth in $n$), so term-by-term $x$-differentiation is justified a p
osteriori; the $t$-derivative is likewise justified by uniform convergence of $\
dot u_n\sin$. Substitution gives $\dot u_n = -k\lambda_n u_n + g_n$, hence $u_t
= k\,u_{xx}+g(x)$ pointwise.
- **Initial condition.** At $t=0$ the exponential factors become $1$, so $u_n(0)
=f_n+0=f_n$ and $u(x,0)=\sum f_n\sin(\cdot)=f(x)$ (the sine series of the contin
uous, endpoint-vanishing $f$).
- **Boundary conditions.** Each $\sin(n\pi x/L)$ vanishes at $x=0,L$, so $u(0,t)
=u(L,t)=0$.
- **Steady state.** As $t\to\infty$, $u_n(t)\to g_n/(k\lambda_n)$, recovering th
e steady solution $u_\infty(x)=\sum_n \tfrac{g_n}{k\lambda_n}\sin(\cdot)$, which
 solves $k\,u_\infty''+g=0$ with $u_\infty(0)=u_\infty(L)=0$ — the expected long
-time equilibrium driven by the steady source.

This completes the solution by the eigenfunction (Fourier sine) expansion method
, with all $x$-differentiations justified by the uniform convergence of the sine
 series of the continuous, boundary-compatible functions $u$, $u_{xx}$, and $g$.
