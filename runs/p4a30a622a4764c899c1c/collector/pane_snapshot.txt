**Answer: Yes, such an uncountable set exists.**

**Proof.**

Since the rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ are countable, fix a bijection $f : \mat
hbb{N} \to \mathbb{Q}$. This lets us regard each subset of $\mathbb{Q}$ as a sub
set of $\mathbb{N}$ (via $f^{-1}$), so it suffices to build an uncountable chain
 inside $\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Q})$ under inclusion.

For each real number $r \in \mathbb{R}$, define

$$A_r = \{q \in \mathbb{Q} : q < r\}.$$

This is a subset of $\mathbb{Q}$, hence (transported through $f$) a subset of $\
mathbb{N}$.

**The family is uncountable.** The map $r \mapsto A_r$ is injective: if $r \neq
s$, say $r < s$, then by the density of $\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{R}$ there exist
s a rational $q$ with $r < q < s$. Then $q \in A_s$ but $q \notin A_r$, so $A_r
\neq A_s$. Thus $\{A_r : r \in \mathbb{R}\}$ is indexed injectively by $\mathbb{
R}$ and is therefore uncountable.

**The family is totally ordered by inclusion.** Take any two members $A_r, A_s$
with $r \leq s$. Then every rational $q < r$ also satisfies $q < s$, so $A_r \su
bseteq A_s$. Hence for any pair, either $A_r \subseteq A_s$ or $A_s \subseteq A_
r$ (depending on which of $r, s$ is smaller). Moreover, when $r < s$ the inclusi
on is strict, as shown above.

Therefore $\{A_r : r \in \mathbb{R}\}$ is an uncountable set of subsets of $\mat
hbb{N}$ (via the bijection $f$) such that any two of its members are comparable
under inclusion. $\blacksquare$
