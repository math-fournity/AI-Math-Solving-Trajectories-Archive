## Proof

Let $\Sigma X = S(X)$ denote the suspension. We use two standard facts:

**Fact 1 (Suspension isomorphism).** For any based space $X$ and all $k$,
$$\widetilde{H}_k(\Sigma X) \cong \widetilde{H}_{k-1}(X), \qquad \widetilde{H}^k
(\Sigma X) \cong \widetilde{H}^{k-1}(X).$$

**Fact 2 (Künneth theorem).** Since $S^n$ and $S^m$ have free abelian homology,
the Künneth theorem gives (with no Tor terms):
$$H_k(S^n \times S^m) \cong \bigoplus_{i+j=k} H_i(S^n)\otimes H_j(S^m).$$

---

### Step 1. Homology of $S^n \times S^m$

The only nonzero homology groups of $S^n$ are $H_0 \cong \mathbb{Z}$ and $H_n \c
ong \mathbb{Z}$, and similarly for $S^m$. Hence the nonzero tensor products $H_i
(S^n)\otimes H_j(S^m)$ are:

| $(i,j)$ | degree $k=i+j$ | group |
|---|---|---|
| $(0,0)$ | $0$ | $\mathbb{Z}$ |
| $(n,0)$ | $n$ | $\mathbb{Z}$ |
| $(0,m)$ | $m$ | $\mathbb{Z}$ |
| $(n,m)$ | $n+m$ | $\mathbb{Z}$ |

**Case $n \neq m$:** these four degrees are distinct, so
$$\widetilde{H}_k(S^n \times S^m) \cong \begin{cases}\mathbb{Z} & k \in \{n,\, m
,\, n+m\},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

**Case $n = m$:** the degrees $n$ and $m$ coincide, so the two summands in degre
e $n$ combine:
$$\widetilde{H}_k(S^n \times S^n) \cong \begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}
& k = n,\\ \mathbb{Z} & k = 2n,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

---

### Step 2. Homology of $S^n \vee S^m \vee S^{n+m}$

For a wedge of based spaces, reduced homology splits as a direct sum:
$$\widetilde{H}_k\!\left(\bigvee_\alpha X_\alpha\right) \cong \bigoplus_\alpha \
widetilde{H}_k(X_\alpha).$$

Applying this to $S^n \vee S^m \vee S^{n+m}$:

**Case $n \neq m$:** the three summands contribute in distinct degrees $n$, $m$,
 $n+m$:
$$\widetilde{H}_k(S^n \vee S^m \vee S^{n+m}) \cong \begin{cases}\mathbb{Z} & k \
in \{n,\, m,\, n+m\},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

**Case $n = m$:** the wedge is $S^n \vee S^n \vee S^{2n}$, so the two copies of
$S^n$ contribute in degree $n$:
$$\widetilde{H}_k(S^n \vee S^n \vee S^{2n}) \cong \begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\
mathbb{Z} & k = n,\\ \mathbb{Z} & k = 2n,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

---

### Step 3. Comparison of reduced homology

In both cases ($n = m$ and $n \neq m$), the reduced homology groups of $S^n \tim
es S^m$ and $S^n \vee S^m \vee S^{n+m}$ are **isomorphic** (identical ranks in e
very degree).

---

### Step 4. Cohomology groups

Since all the homology groups involved are free abelian, the Universal Coefficie
nt Theorem for cohomology gives
$$H^k(X) \cong \operatorname{Hom}(H_k(X),\,\mathbb{Z}) \oplus \operatorname{Ext}
(H_{k-1}(X),\,\mathbb{Z}),$$
and the $\operatorname{Ext}$ term vanishes because $H_{k-1}$ is free. Thus $H^k(
X) \cong \operatorname{Hom}(H_k(X),\mathbb{Z})$, which is isomorphic to $H_k(X)$
 as an abelian group (both being $\mathbb{Z}^r$ in each degree). Therefore the r
educed cohomology groups of $S^n \times S^m$ and $S^n \vee S^m \vee S^{n+m}$ are
 isomorphic in every degree as well.

---

### Step 5. Suspension

By the suspension isomorphism (Fact 1),
$$\widetilde{H}_k\bigl(\Sigma(S^n \times S^m)\bigr) \cong \widetilde{H}_{k-1}(S^
n \times S^m), \qquad \widetilde{H}_k\bigl(\Sigma(S^n \vee S^m \vee S^{n+m})\big
r) \cong \widetilde{H}_{k-1}(S^n \vee S^m \vee S^{n+m}).$$

Since the right-hand sides are isomorphic in every degree (Steps 3 and 4), the l
eft-hand sides are isomorphic in every degree. The same argument applies to redu
ced cohomology via the cohomological suspension isomorphism. Adding back the unr
educed part ($H_0 \cong \mathbb{Z}$ for both suspensions, which are path-connect
ed), the full (unreduced) homology and cohomology groups are isomorphic.

---

### Conclusion

**Yes**, the spaces $S(S^n \times S^m)$ and $S(S^n \vee S^m \vee S^{n+m})$ have
isomorphic homology and cohomology groups for all $n, m \geq 1$.

The essential reason is that $S^n \times S^m$ and $S^n \vee S^m \vee S^{n+m}$ ha
ve identical reduced homology and cohomology *groups* (they differ only in the m
ultiplicative/cup-product structure, which is irrelevant here since (i) the ques
tion asks only about groups, and (ii) suspension kills cup products anyway). The
 suspension isomorphism then shifts both sets of groups by one degree, preservin
g the isomorphism.
