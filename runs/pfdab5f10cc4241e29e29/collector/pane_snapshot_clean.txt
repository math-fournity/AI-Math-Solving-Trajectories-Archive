## Answer

**No**, not in general. The statement fails for two arbitrary closed disjoint se
ts. It becomes true if one of them (say $K$) is **compact** — that is the standa
rd form of the theorem. Below I give a counterexample showing the general statem
ent is false, and then prove the positive result under the compactness hypothesi
s.

---

### Counterexample (both sets merely closed)

Take the topological vector space $X = \mathbb{R}^2$ with its usual topology. De
fine

$$K = \{(x,0) : x \in \mathbb{R}\}, \qquad C = \{(x, e^x) : x \in \mathbb{R}\}.$
$

- $K$ is closed: it is the zero set of the continuous map $(x,y)\mapsto y$.
- $C$ is closed: it is the graph of the continuous function $x\mapsto e^x$, and
the graph of a continuous $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ function is closed in $\math
bb{R}^2$.
- $K\cap C=\emptyset$, since $e^x>0$ for every $x$, so no point $(x,e^x)$ lies o
n the $x$-axis.

Let $V$ be **any** neighborhood of $0$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Then $V$ contains some
 open ball $B(0,\varepsilon)$ with $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $x_0<\ln\varepsilon$,
 so that $e^{x_0}<\varepsilon$, and set $p=(x_0,e^{x_0})\in C$.

- $p\in C\subseteq C+V$.
- The point $(x_0,0)\in K$, and $\|p-(x_0,0)\|=e^{x_0}<\varepsilon$, hence $p\in
 (x_0,0)+B(0,\varepsilon)\subseteq K+V$.

Therefore $p\in (K+V)\cap(C+V)$, so $(K+V)\cap(C+V)\neq\emptyset$. Since $V$ was
 arbitrary, **no** neighborhood $V$ of $0$ satisfies $(K+V)\cap(C+V)=\emptyset$.

---

### Positive result: the statement holds when $K$ is compact

**Theorem.** Let $X$ be a topological vector space, $K\subseteq X$ compact, $C\s
ubseteq X$ closed, with $K\cap C=\emptyset$. Then there exists a neighborhood $V
$ of $0$ such that $(K+V)\cap(C+V)=\emptyset$.

**Proof.** For each $x\in K$, since $x\notin C$ and $C$ is closed, there is a ne
ighborhood $U_x$ of $0$ with $(x+U_x)\cap C=\emptyset$. By continuity of additio
n (applied twice), choose a **balanced** neighborhood $W_x$ of $0$ with

$$W_x+W_x+W_x \subseteq U_x.$$

The family $\{x+W_x\}_{x\in K}$ is an open cover of $K$; by compactness there ex
ist $x_1,\dots,x_n\in K$ with

$$K\subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^n (x_i+W_{x_i}).$$

Set $V=\bigcap_{i=1}^n W_{x_i}$; this is a neighborhood of $0$ (finite intersect
ion of neighborhoods of $0$).

Suppose, for contradiction, that $(K+V)\cap(C+V)\neq\emptyset$, and pick $p$ in
the intersection. Then

$$p = k+v_1 = c+v_2, \qquad k\in K,\; c\in C,\; v_1,v_2\in V.$$

Since $k\in K$, there is some $i$ with $k\in x_i+W_{x_i}$, i.e. $k=x_i+w$ for so
me $w\in W_{x_i}$. Then

$$c = p-v_2 = k+v_1-v_2 = x_i + \underbrace{(w+v_1-v_2)}_{\in\, W_{x_i}+W_{x_i}+
W_{x_i}\,\subseteq\, U_{x_i}}.$$

Here we used $v_1,v_2\in V\subseteq W_{x_i}$ and that $W_{x_i}$ is balanced, so
$-v_2\in W_{x_i}$. Thus $c\in x_i+U_{x_i}$, contradicting $(x_i+U_{x_i})\cap C=\
emptyset$.

Hence $(K+V)\cap(C+V)=\emptyset$. $\blacksquare$

---

**Conclusion.** The answer to the question as stated (with $K$ and $C$ merely cl
osed) is **no**; the counterexample above shows the assertion can fail. The asse
rtion is true in the standard, stronger form where one of the two closed sets is
 **compact**.
