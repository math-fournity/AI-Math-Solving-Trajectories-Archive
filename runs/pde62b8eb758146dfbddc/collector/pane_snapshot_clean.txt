## Proof

Let $S=\{1,2,\dots,n\}$ with $n>1$, and let $\pi$ be a uniformly random permutat
ion of $S$. For each $k\in S$, define the indicator random variable

$$
X_k=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\pi\text{ has a local maximum at }k,\\0,&\text{oth
erwise.}\end{cases}
$$

The total number of local maxima is $X=\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_k$. By **linearity of exp
ectation**,

$$
\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_k]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Pr(X_k=1).
$$

We compute $\Pr(X_k=1)$ for the three cases.

**Case 1: $k=1$.** Here $X_1=1$ iff $\pi(1)>\pi(2)$. The pair $(\pi(1),\pi(2))$
is uniformly distributed over all ordered pairs of distinct elements of $S$, so
by symmetry $\Pr(\pi(1)>\pi(2))=\tfrac12$. Thus

$$
\mathbb{E}[X_1]=\frac12.
$$

**Case 2: $k=n$.** Here $X_n=1$ iff $\pi(n-1)<\pi(n)$. By the same symmetry argu
ment applied to the pair $(\pi(n-1),\pi(n))$,

$$
\mathbb{E}[X_n]=\frac12.
$$

**Case 3: $1<k<n$.** Here $X_k=1$ iff $\pi(k-1)<\pi(k)$ and $\pi(k)>\pi(k+1)$, i
.e., $\pi(k)$ is strictly larger than both of its neighbors. Consider the triple
 of values $(\pi(k-1),\pi(k),\pi(k+1))$. Since $\pi$ is a uniformly random permu
tation, this triple is uniformly distributed over all ordered triples of distinc
t elements of $S$. In particular, each of the three positions in the triple is e
qually likely to hold the **largest** of the three values. The event $X_k=1$ occ
urs exactly when the largest value sits in the middle position, so

$$
\Pr(X_k=1)=\frac13,\qquad 1<k<n.
$$

**Summing.** There are $n-2$ interior positions ($k=2,\dots,n-1$), plus the two
boundary positions. Therefore

$$
\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[X_1]+\sum_{k=2}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[X_k]+\mathbb{E}[X_n]=\f
rac12+(n-2)\cdot\frac13+\frac12.
$$

Simplifying,

$$
\mathbb{E}[X]=1+\frac{n-2}{3}=\frac{3+n-2}{3}=\boxed{\dfrac{n+1}{3}}.
$$

**Sanity check.** For $n=5$ this gives $\frac{6}{3}=2$. The example permutation
$(2,1,4,5,3)$ has local maxima at $k=1$ (value $2$) and $k=4$ (value $5$), i.e.,
 exactly $2$ local maxima, consistent with the expected value. For $n=2$ the for
mula gives $1$, and indeed every permutation of $\{1,2\}$ has exactly one local
maximum (at $k=1$ if $\pi=(2,1)$, at $k=2$ if $\pi=(1,2)$).

Hence the average number of local maxima of a permutation of $S=\{1,2,\dots,n\}$
 is

$$
\frac{n+1}{3}.
$$
