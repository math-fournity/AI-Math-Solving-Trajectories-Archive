**Answer: Yes.**

**Proof.**

Since $P \le Z(G)$, every element of $G$ commutes with every element of $P$, so
in particular every element of $G$ normalizes $P$. Hence $P \trianglelefteq G$.
Since $P$ is a Sylow $p$-subgroup, writing $|G| = p^a \cdot m$ with $\gcd(p,m)=1
$ and $|P|=p^a$, we have $\gcd(|P|,\,|G:P|) = \gcd(p^a, m) = 1$. Thus $P$ is a *
*normal Hall subgroup** of $G$.

By the **Schur–Zassenhaus theorem**, since $P \trianglelefteq G$ and $\gcd(|P|,|
G:P|)=1$, there exists a subgroup $N \le G$ with
$$G = PN, \qquad P \cap N = \{e\}.$$
Moreover, since $P$ is a $p$-group (hence solvable), the theorem guarantees that
 **all complements of $P$ in $G$ are conjugate**.

We now show $N \trianglelefteq G$. Let $g \in G$. Then $gNg^{-1}$ is again a com
plement of $P$:
- $P \cdot gNg^{-1} = g(PN)g^{-1} = gGg^{-1} = G$ (using $P\trianglelefteq G$),
- $P \cap gNg^{-1} = g(P\cap N)g^{-1} = \{e\}$ (using $P\trianglelefteq G$).

By conjugacy of complements, there exists $x \in G$ with $gNg^{-1} = xNx^{-1}$.
Write $x = pn$ with $p \in P$, $n \in N$ (possible since $G = PN$). Then
$$xNx^{-1} = pn\,N\,n^{-1}p^{-1} = pNp^{-1}.$$
Since $p \in P \le Z(G)$, the element $p$ commutes with every element of $G$, so
 $pNp^{-1} = N$. Therefore
$$gNg^{-1} = N.$$

Since $g$ was arbitrary, $N \trianglelefteq G$. We have produced a normal subgro
up $N$ with $P \cap N = \{e\}$ and $PN = G$. $\blacksquare$
