The answer is **false**. Here is the proof.

---

**Polar decomposition setup.** Let $H$ be a Hilbert space and $T \in \mathcal{B}
(H)$. The polar decomposition writes $T = UP$ where $P = (T^*T)^{1/2} \geq 0$ an
d $U$ is a partial isometry with $\ker U = \ker P$. The positive part $P$ is *al
ways* uniquely determined (it is defined directly from $T$). The only question i
s uniqueness of the partial isometry $U$.

**Key fact.** $U$ is uniquely determined on $\overline{\operatorname{ran} P}$ by
 the relation $U(Px) = Tx$ (well-defined and isometric since $\|Px\|^2 = \langle
 T^*T x, x\rangle = \|Tx\|^2$), and extended by continuity. On $(\overline{\oper
atorname{ran} P})^\perp = \ker P = \ker T$, the value of $U$ is *not* constraine
d by $T = UP$, because $P$ annihilates that subspace.

**Lemma.** The polar decomposition of $T$ is unique if and only if $\ker T = \{0
\}$.

*Proof of Lemma.*
$(\Leftarrow)$ If $\ker T = \{0\}$, then $\ker P = \{0\}$, so $\overline{\operat
orname{ran} P} = H$. Thus $U$ is determined on all of $H$ by continuity, hence u
nique.

$(\Rightarrow)$ If $\ker T \neq \{0\}$, then $\ker P \neq \{0\}$. Let $U_0$ be t
he standard partial isometry (with $U_0 = 0$ on $\ker P$). Pick a nonzero partia
l isometry $V$ whose initial space lies in $\ker P$ and whose final space lies i
n $(\operatorname{ran} U_0)^\perp$ (possible since $\ker P \neq \{0\}$). Set $U'
 = U_0 + V$. Then $U'$ is still a partial isometry (its initial projection is th
e sum of two orthogonal projections), and $T = U'P$ since $VP = 0$. Yet $U' \neq
 U_0$. Hence the decomposition is not unique. $\square$

**Applying the lemma to the question.** The question claims uniqueness $\iff$ in
vertibility. We test both directions.

- *Invertible $\Rightarrow$ unique:* Holds, since invertible $\Rightarrow \ker T
 = \{0\}$ $\Rightarrow$ unique by the Lemma.
- *Unique $\Rightarrow$ invertible:* **Fails.** Consider the **unilateral shift*
* $S$ on $\ell^2(\mathbb{N})$:
$$S(x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots) = (0, x_1, x_2, \dots).$$
Then $S^*S = I$, so $P = (S^*S)^{1/2} = I$, and $T = UP$ forces $U = S$. The dec
omposition is **unique**. Yet $S$ is **not invertible**: it is not surjective (t
he vector $e_1 = (1,0,0,\dots)$ is not in $\operatorname{ran} S$). Equivalently,
 $SS^* = I - e_1 e_1^* \neq I$.

Thus $S$ is a non-invertible operator with a unique polar decomposition, contrad
icting the "only if" direction.

**Conclusion.** The polar decomposition is unique if and only if the operator is
 **injective** ($\ker T = \{0\}$), which is strictly weaker than being invertibl
e. The unilateral shift provides an explicit counterexample to "unique $\Rightar
row$ invertible."

The statement is **false**.
