**Find all real roots of** $\sqrt{x^2-p}+2\sqrt{x^2-1}=x$, **with real parameter
** $p$.

### Domain restrictions

The left-hand side is a sum of (principal) square roots, hence $\ge 0$; therefor
e $x\ge 0$. We also need $x^2-1\ge 0$, i.e. $|x|\ge 1$. Combined with $x\ge 0$ t
his gives

$$x\ge 1.$$

Finally $x^2-p\ge 0$ is required.

### Bounding $x$

Rewrite the equation as
$$\sqrt{x^2-p}=x-2\sqrt{x^2-1}.$$
Since the left side is $\ge 0$, we need $x\ge 2\sqrt{x^2-1}$. Squaring (both sid
es non-negative):
$$x^2\ge 4(x^2-1)\;\Longrightarrow\;4\ge 3x^2\;\Longrightarrow\;x^2\le \tfrac{4}
{3}.$$
Together with $x\ge 1$:
$$\boxed{1\le x\le \tfrac{2}{\sqrt 3}}.\tag{1}$$

### Deriving the relation between $p$ and $x$

Square $\sqrt{x^2-p}=x-2\sqrt{x^2-1}$:
$$x^2-p=x^2-4x\sqrt{x^2-1}+4(x^2-1)=5x^2-4-4x\sqrt{x^2-1},$$
so
$$p=4+4x\sqrt{x^2-1}-4x^2=4\bigl(1-x^2+x\sqrt{x^2-1}\bigr).\tag{2}$$

### Solving for $x$ in terms of $p$

From (2): $x\sqrt{x^2-1}=\tfrac{p}{4}-1+x^2$. Set $q=\tfrac p4-1$; then $x\sqrt{
x^2-1}=q+x^2$. Squaring:
$$x^2(x^2-1)=(q+x^2)^2=q^2+2qx^2+x^4,$$
$$x^4-x^2=q^2+2qx^2+x^4\;\Longrightarrow\;-x^2(1+2q)=q^2,$$
$$x^2=\frac{-q^2}{1+2q}=\frac{-(p/4-1)^2}{(p-2)/2}=\frac{(p-4)^2}{8(2-p)}.\tag{3
}$$

(We need $1+2q<0$, i.e. $p<2$, which is guaranteed in the range found below.)

Since $x\ge 1>0$,
$$x=\frac{4-p}{2\sqrt{\,2(2-p)\,}}=\frac{4-p}{2\sqrt{4-2p}}.\tag{4}$$

### Determining the admissible range of $p$

Let $v=\sqrt{x^2-1}\in\bigl[0,\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\bigr]$ (from (1)). Then $x=\sqrt{v
^2+1}$ and (2) becomes
$$p=4v\bigl(\sqrt{v^2+1}-v\bigr)=:f(v).$$

Compute $f'(v)=\dfrac{4(2v^2+1)}{\sqrt{v^2+1}}-8v$. Setting $f'(v)=0$ gives $2v^
2+1=2v\sqrt{v^2+1}$; squaring yields $(2v^2+1)^2=4v^2(v^2+1)$, i.e. $4v^4+4v^2+1
=4v^4+4v^2$, i.e. $1=0$ — a contradiction. So $f'$ never vanishes; $f'(0)=4>0$ m
eans $f$ is **strictly increasing** on $\bigl[0,\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\bigr]$.

Endpoint values:
$$f(0)=0,\qquad f\!\left(\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\right)=\tfrac4{\sqrt3}\!\left(\tfrac2{\
sqrt3}-\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\right)=\tfrac43.$$

Hence $f$ is a bijection $\bigl[0,\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\bigr]\to\bigl[0,\tfrac43\bigr]
$. Therefore a real root exists **if and only if**
$$0\le p\le \tfrac43,$$
and in that range it is **unique**.

### Verification of the formula

- $p=0$: $x=\dfrac{4}{2\sqrt4}=1$. Check: $\sqrt1+2\sqrt0=1=x$. ✓
- $p=\tfrac43$: $x=\dfrac{8/3}{2\sqrt{2/3}}=\dfrac{8/3}{4/\sqrt3}=\dfrac2{\sqrt3
}$. Check: $\sqrt0+2\sqrt{1/3}=\dfrac2{\sqrt3}=x$. ✓

The squaring steps introduced no extraneous roots: the first squaring required $
x-2\sqrt{x^2-1}\ge0$ (enforced by (1)); the second required $q+x^2\ge0$, which h
olds throughout $[0,\tfrac43]$ by continuity and is verified at both endpoints.
The bijection argument confirms exactly one solution per admissible $p$.

### Answer

For the equation $\sqrt{x^2-p}+2\sqrt{x^2-1}=x$:

- **If $0\le p\le \dfrac43$**, there is exactly one real root:
$$\boxed{\,x=\dfrac{4-p}{2\sqrt{\,4-2p\,}}\,}.$$

- **If $p<0$ or $p>\dfrac43$**, there are **no real roots**.
