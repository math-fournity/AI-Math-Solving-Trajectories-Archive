## Answer

**Yes.** Every uncountable subset $S \subseteq [0,1]$ contains at least one of i
ts own accumulation points.

### Proof

Recall that $x$ is an *accumulation point* (limit point) of $S$ if every open ne
ighborhood of $x$ contains some point of $S \setminus \{x\}$.

Suppose, for contradiction, that **no** accumulation point of $S$ lies in $S$. T
hen every point $s \in S$ is *not* an accumulation point of $S$, i.e., each $s \
in S$ is an **isolated point** of $S$: there exists an open set $U_s \subseteq [
0,1]$ with
$$U_s \cap S = \{s\}.$$

The unit interval $[0,1]$ (with its subspace topology from $\mathbb{R}$) is **se
cond-countable**: it admits a countable base $\mathcal{B} = \{B_1, B_2, B_3, \do
ts\}$, for instance the collection of all sets of the form $(a,b) \cap [0,1]$ wi
th $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$, together with the half-open neighborhoods $\{0\} \cup
[0, \varepsilon) \cap [0,1]$ and $(1-\varepsilon, 1] \cap [0,1]$ at the endpoint
s (all with rational $\varepsilon$).

For each $s \in S$, since $U_s$ is open and $s \in U_s$, the base property gives
 some $B_{n(s)} \in \mathcal{B}$ with
$$s \in B_{n(s)} \subseteq U_s.$$
Consequently,
$$B_{n(s)} \cap S \subseteq U_s \cap S = \{s\},$$
and since $s \in B_{n(s)} \cap S$, we get $B_{n(s)} \cap S = \{s\}$.

**The map $s \mapsto n(s)$ is injective.** Indeed, if $s \neq t$ but $n(s) = n(t
) = n$, then $B_n \cap S$ would contain both $s$ and $t$, contradicting $B_n \ca
p S = \{s\}$ (a singleton).

Thus we have constructed an injection
$$S \hookrightarrow \mathbb{N}, \qquad s \mapsto n(s).$$

Since $\mathbb{N}$ is countable, $S$ must be **countable**. This contradicts the
 hypothesis that $S$ is uncountable.

Therefore our assumption was false: $S$ must contain at least one of its own acc
umulation points. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The key topological fact used is that every discrete subspace of a s
econd-countable space is countable (each isolated point "consumes" a distinct ba
sic open set from the countable base). Since $[0,1]$ is second-countable, no unc
ountable subset of it can be discrete, which is exactly the statement proved abo
ve.
