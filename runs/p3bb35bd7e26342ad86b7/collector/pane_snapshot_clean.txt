## Proof

**Yes**, such a sequence exists. We construct an explicit example.

Let $A$ be the set of positive integers whose **leading (most significant) decim
al digit is 1**:

$$A = \{1, 10, 11, 12, \ldots, 19, 100, 101, \ldots, 199, 1000, \ldots, 1999, \l
dots\}.$$

This is a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers. We show that $\delt
a(A) = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{A(n)}{n}$ does not exist by exhibiting two subseq
uences of $n$ along which $\frac{A(n)}{n}$ converges to **different** limits.

---

**Subsequence 1: $n = 10^k - 1$.**

The elements of $A$ not exceeding $10^k - 1$ are exactly those with $j$ digits (
$1 \le j \le k$) and leading digit 1, namely the integers in $[10^{j-1},\, 2\cdo
t 10^{j-1} - 1]$ for $j = 1, 2, \ldots, k$. Each such block has $10^{j-1}$ eleme
nts, so

$$A(10^k - 1) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} 10^{j-1} = \frac{10^k - 1}{9}.$$

Therefore

$$\frac{A(10^k - 1)}{10^k - 1} = \frac{1}{9} \xrightarrow{k \to \infty} \frac{1}
{9}.$$

---

**Subsequence 2: $n = 2\cdot 10^{k-1} - 1$.**

The elements of $A$ not exceeding $2\cdot 10^{k-1} - 1$ are the same blocks for
$j = 1, \ldots, k$ (since $2\cdot 10^{k-1} - 1 \ge 2\cdot 10^{k-1} - 1$ and the
$k$-digit block $[10^{k-1}, 2\cdot 10^{k-1}-1]$ is fully included, while no $(k{
+}1)$-digit elements appear). Thus

$$A(2\cdot 10^{k-1} - 1) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} 10^{j-1} = \frac{10^k - 1}{9}.$$

Hence

$$\frac{A(2\cdot 10^{k-1} - 1)}{2\cdot 10^{k-1} - 1} = \frac{(10^k - 1)/9}{2\cdo
t 10^{k-1} - 1} \xrightarrow{k \to \infty} \frac{10^k/9}{2\cdot 10^{k-1}} = \fra
c{10}{18} = \frac{5}{9}.$$

---

**Conclusion.**

Along $n = 10^k - 1$ the ratio $\frac{A(n)}{n} \to \frac{1}{9}$, while along $n
= 2\cdot 10^{k-1} - 1$ the ratio $\frac{A(n)}{n} \to \frac{5}{9}$. Since $\frac{
1}{9} \neq \frac{5}{9}$, the limit $\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{A(n)}{n}$ does not e
xist.

Therefore there exists a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers whose
 natural density does not exist. $\blacksquare$
