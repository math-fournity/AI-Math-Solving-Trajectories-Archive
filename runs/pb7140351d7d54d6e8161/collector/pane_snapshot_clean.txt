## Solution

**Contour analysis.** The integrand $f(z) = \dfrac{\cos(2z)}{(z-\pi/4)^2}$ has a
 single singularity at $z_0 = \pi/4$. I first verify that $z_0$ lies inside the
triangle $\Gamma$ with vertices
$$A = i=(0,1),\quad B=-1-i=(-1,-1),\quad C=\pi-i=(\pi,-1).$$

Since $\pi/4 \approx 0.785$, the point $z_0 = (0.785,\, 0)$. Check each edge:

- **Bottom edge** $BC$: horizontal at $y=-1$. The point has $y=0>-1$; it lies on
 the interior (upper) side. ✓
- **Left edge** $AB$: line $y = 2x+1$. At $x=0.785$, $y_{\text{line}}=2.57$; the
 point has $y=0<2.57$, which is the interior side (confirmed by the centroid $(\
tfrac{\pi-1}{3},-\tfrac13)\approx(0.714,-0.333)$, also below this line). ✓
- **Right edge** $AC$: line $y = 1-\tfrac{2x}{\pi}$. At $x=0.785=\pi/4$, $y_{\te
xt{line}}=1-\tfrac{2\cdot\pi/4}{\pi}=1-\tfrac12=\tfrac12$; the point has $y=0<\t
frac12$, the interior side (centroid also below). ✓

Hence $z_0=\pi/4$ is inside $\Gamma$.

**Orientation.** Traversing $A\to B\to C\to A$ (top → bottom-left → bottom-right
 → top) is counterclockwise, so $\Gamma$ is positively oriented.

**Applying Cauchy's integral formula for derivatives.** With $g(z)=\cos(2z)$ (en
tire) and $a=\pi/4$,
$$\oint_\Gamma \frac{g(z)}{(z-a)^2}\,dz = 2\pi i\, g'(a).$$
Compute $g'(z) = -2\sin(2z)$, so
$$g'(\pi/4) = -2\sin(\pi/2) = -2\cdot 1 = -2.$$

Therefore
$$\int_\Gamma \frac{\cos(2z)}{(z-\pi/4)^2}\,dz = 2\pi i\cdot(-2) = \boxed{-4\pi
i}.$$
