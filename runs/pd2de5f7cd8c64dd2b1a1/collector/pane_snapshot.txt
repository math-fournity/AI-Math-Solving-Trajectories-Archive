## Solution

Let $N = \sin(A) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2011 & 2012 \\ 0 & 1 & 2013 \\ 0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix} = I + M$, where $M = N - I = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2011 & 2012 \\ 0
& 0 & 2013 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Step 1: Jordan structure of $N$.** Compute $M^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 2011
\cdot 2013 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \neq 0$ and $M^3 = 0$. Since
$M^2 \neq 0$, $N$ is a single Jordan block $J_3(1)$.

**Step 2: $A$ commutes with $N$.** Since $A$ commutes with every function of $A$
, in particular $A$ commutes with $\sin(A) = N$. The commutant of a single Jorda
n block $J_3(1)$ consists exactly of polynomials in $N$, i.e. matrices of the fo
rm $\alpha I + \beta M + \gamma M^2$. Hence
$$A = \alpha I + B, \quad B = \beta M + \gamma M^2, \quad B^3 = 0.$$
In particular $A$ has the single eigenvalue $\alpha$ (with multiplicity 3) and $
\operatorname{tr}(A) = 3\alpha$.

**Step 3: Eigenvalue constraint.** The eigenvalues of $\sin(A)$ are $\sin(\alpha
) = 1$, so $\cos(\alpha) = 0$.

**Step 4: Compute $\sin(A)$ via the commuting split $A = \alpha I + B$.** Using
$\sin(x+y) = \sin x \cos y + \cos x \sin y$ (valid since $\alpha I$ and $B$ comm
ute):
$$\sin(A) = \sin(\alpha)\cos(B) + \cos(\alpha)\sin(B) = 1\cdot\cos(B) + 0\cdot\s
in(B) = \cos(B).$$
Since $B^3 = 0$, we have $\cos(B) = I - \tfrac{1}{2}B^2$. Also $B^2 = \beta^2 M^
2$ (as $M^3 = 0$ kills the cross terms). Therefore
$$\sin(A) = I - \tfrac{\beta^2}{2} M^2.$$

**Step 5: Compare with $N = I + M$.** We would need
$$-\tfrac{\beta^2}{2} M^2 = M.$$
But $M$ has nonzero entries in positions $(1,2), (1,3), (2,3)$, while $M^2$ has
a nonzero entry only in position $(1,3)$. They are linearly independent (e.g. th
e $(1,2)$-entry gives $0 = -2\cdot 2011$, a contradiction). So no real $\beta$ c
an satisfy this.

**Step 6: Exclude the degenerate case.** If $\beta = \gamma = 0$, then $A = \alp
ha I$ and $\sin(A) = \sin(\alpha) I = I \neq N$. So this also fails.

**Conclusion.** No real $3\times 3$ matrix $A$ satisfies $\sin(A) = N$. The requ
ired answer is $0$.
