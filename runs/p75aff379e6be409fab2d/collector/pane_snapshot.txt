## Evaluation of $I = \int_{0}^{\infty} J_{0}(x)\,dx$

**Answer: $I = 1$.**

### Step 1. An integral representation of $J_0$

The Bessel function of order zero admits the representation
$$
J_0(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos(x\sin\theta)\,d\theta .
$$
(This is the standard real-part form of the Schlömilch / Poisson representation;
 it follows directly from the series $J_0(x)=\sum_{k\ge 0}(-1)^k\frac{(x/2)^{2k}
}{(k!)^2}$ together with $\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\pi \sin^{2k}\theta\,d\theta=\frac
{(2k)!}{2^{2k}(k!)^2}$.)

### Step 2. Regularize with a Laplace factor

For $s>0$ define
$$
I(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sx}J_0(x)\,dx .
$$
Substituting the representation and exchanging the order of integration (justifi
ed by absolute convergence for $s>0$):
$$
I(s)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sx}\cos(x\sin\theta)\
,dx\right)d\theta .
$$
The inner integral is elementary:
$$
\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sx}\cos(ax)\,dx=\frac{s}{s^{2}+a^{2}},\qquad s>0.
$$
With $a=\sin\theta$,
$$
I(s)=\frac{s}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{d\theta}{s^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta}.
$$

### Step 3. Evaluate the $\theta$–integral

Use the standard identity (valid for $a>0$)
$$
\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{d\theta}{a^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta}=\frac{\pi}{a\sqrt{a^{2}+1}}.
$$
*Proof of the identity:* by symmetry it is $2\int_0^{\pi/2}\!$. Set $t=\tan\thet
a$, so $\sin^2\theta=\frac{t^2}{1+t^2}$, $d\theta=\frac{dt}{1+t^2}$:
$$
\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{a^2+\sin^2\theta}
=\int_0^\infty \frac{dt}{a^2(1+t^2)+t^2}
=\int_0^\infty \frac{dt}{a^2+(a^2+1)t^2}
=\frac{1}{a\sqrt{a^2+1}}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}.
$$
Doubling gives $\pi/(a\sqrt{a^2+1})$. $\checkmark$

Applying it with $a=s$:
$$
I(s)=\frac{s}{\pi}\cdot\frac{\pi}{s\sqrt{s^{2}+1}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{s^{2}+1}}.
$$

### Step 4. Convergence of the original integral and Abel's theorem

As $x\to\infty$,
$$
J_0(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\cos\!\Bigl(x-\frac{\pi}{4}\Bigr)+O(x^{-3/2}).
$$
The leading term is $x^{-1/2}$ times an oscillating function of constant amplitu
de, so $\int_1^\infty J_0(x)\,dx$ converges by **Dirichlet's test** (the amplitu
de $x^{-1/2}$ decreases monotonically to $0$, and $\int \cos(x-\pi/4)\,dx$ has u
niformly bounded partial integrals). The $O(x^{-3/2})$ remainder is absolutely i
ntegrable. Hence $\int_0^\infty J_0(x)\,dx$ converges as an improper Riemann int
egral.

By **Abel's theorem** for Laplace transforms: if $\int_0^\infty f(x)\,dx$ conver
ges, then
$$
\int_0^\infty f(x)\,dx=\lim_{s\to 0^+}\int_0^\infty e^{-sx}f(x)\,dx .
$$
Therefore
$$
I=\lim_{s\to 0^+}I(s)=\lim_{s\to 0^+}\frac{1}{\sqrt{s^{2}+1}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{0+1
}}=1.
$$

### Conclusion

$$
\boxed{\,I=\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{0}(x)\,dx=1\, .}
$$
