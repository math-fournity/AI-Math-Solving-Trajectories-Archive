# EGF of Permutations with All Cycles of Distinct Lengths

## Setup

We work in the framework of analytic combinatorics (Flajolet–Sedgewick). A permu
tation decomposes uniquely as an unordered set of cycles. In the symbolic method
, the class $\mathcal{P}$ of all permutations is

$$\mathcal{P} = \text{SET}(\text{CYC}(\mathcal{Z})),$$

with EGF $P(z) = \exp\!\bigl(\log\frac{1}{1-z}\bigr) = \frac{1}{1-z}$, since a c
ycle of length $k$ has EGF $z^k/k$ and $\text{SET}$ corresponds to exponentiatio
n.

## Constraining to distinct cycle lengths

We now restrict to the subclass $\mathcal{D}$ of permutations in which **no two
cycles share the same length**. The key observation is:

> The choice of cycles decomposes **independently by length**. For each length $
k \ge 1$, we either include **zero** cycles of length $k$, or **exactly one** cy
cle of length $k$ (we may never include two or more, since that would violate di
stinctness).

This is precisely the "labelled product with at-most-one constraint per componen
t type," which converts the $\text{SET}$ construction (an unrestricted product $
\prod_k \exp(z^k/k)$) into a **restricted product** where each factor's exponent
ial is truncated to its first two terms:

$$\exp\!\left(\frac{z^k}{k}\right) \;\longrightarrow\; 1 + \frac{z^k}{k} \qquad(
\text{keep only orders } 0 \text{ and } 1).$$

## Deriving the EGF

Since the cycle-length choices are independent across $k$, the EGF of $\mathcal{
D}$ is the infinite product of the truncated factors:

$$\boxed{\,D(z) \;=\; \prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(1 + \frac{z^k}{k}\right).\,}$$

## Verification by coefficient extraction

We confirm the formula against small cases by computing $[z^n]\,D(z)$ and checki
ng that $n!\,[z^n]\,D(z)$ equals the count of permutations of $[n]$ with all cyc
le lengths distinct.

**$n=1$.** $[z^1]D = 1$ (from the $k=1$ factor), giving $1!\cdot 1 = 1$. The onl
y permutation $(1)$ has a single cycle of length $1$. ✓

**$n=2$.** From $(1+z)(1+z^2/2)\cdots$, the $z^2$ term is $z^2/2$, so $[z^2]D =
1/2$, giving $2!\cdot\tfrac12 = 1$. The valid permutation is $(1\,2)$ (one 2-cyc
le); $(1)(2)$ has two 1-cycles and is excluded. ✓

**$n=3$.** From $(1+z)(1+z^2/2)(1+z^3/3)$, the $z^3$ coefficient is $\tfrac12 +
\tfrac13 = \tfrac56$, giving $6\cdot\tfrac56 = 5$. The five valid permutations a
re $(1\,2)(3),\,(1\,3)(2),\,(2\,3)(1),\,(1\,2\,3),\,(1\,3\,2)$; the identity $(1
)(2)(3)$ is excluded (three 1-cycles). ✓

**$n=4$.** The $z^4$ coefficient of $\prod_{k\le 4}(1+z^k/k)$ comes from: $z^4/4
$ (the $k=4$ factor alone), $z\cdot z^3/3$ (lengths $1,3$), and $z^2/2\cdot z^2/
2$ — but the last would require **two** 2-cycles, which the truncated factor $1+
z^2/2$ forbids (it contributes at most one $z^2/2$). So $[z^4]D = \tfrac14 + \tf
rac13 = \tfrac{7}{12}$, giving $24\cdot\tfrac{7}{12}=14$. One checks directly: v
alid cycle-type partitions of $4$ with distinct parts are $\{4\},\{3,1\}$, contr
ibuting $\binom{4}{4}\cdot 3! + \binom{4}{3}\cdot 2! = 6 + 8 = 14$. ✓ (The parti
tion $\{2,1,1\}$ is excluded by distinctness, and $\{2,2\}$ is excluded both by
distinctness and by the truncation.)

All checks agree, confirming the formula.

## Conclusion

The exponential generating function of the class of permutations whose cycles al
l have distinct lengths is

$$D(z) \;=\; \prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(1 + \frac{z^k}{k}\right).$$
