## Proof

The answer is **Yes**.

We use the **Vitali Covering Theorem** (a standard result in measure theory).

> **Vitali Covering Theorem.** *Let $E \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be a measurable set
 with $m(E) < \infty$, and let $\mathcal{V}$ be a collection of closed balls for
ming a Vitali cover of $E$—i.e., for every $x \in E$ and every $\delta > 0$, the
re exists $B \in \mathcal{V}$ with $x \in B$ and $\operatorname{rad}(B) < \delta
$. Then there exists a countable pairwise-disjoint subcollection $\{B_i\} \subse
t \mathcal{V}$ with $m\!\left(E \setminus \bigcup_i B_i\right) = 0$.*

*(Reference: W. Rudin, *Real and Complex Analysis*, Ch. 8; or L.C. Evans & R.F.
Gariepy, *Measure Theory and Fine Properties of Functions*, §1.5. The greedy-sel
ection proof—choosing at each stage a largest remaining ball, then observing the
 5-fold enlargements cover $E$—works in every dimension.)*

---

### Constructing the cover

Let $S = (0,1)^2$ be the interior of the unit square, so $m(S) = 1$. Define

$$\mathcal{V} = \bigl\{\,\overline{D}(p,\,1/k) \;:\; k \in \mathbb{Z}^+,\;\; p \
in \mathbb{R}^2,\;\; \overline{D}(p,\,1/k) \subset [0,1]^2\,\bigr\},$$

the family of all closed discs of radius $1/k$ (for positive integers $k$) that
are entirely contained in the closed unit square.

**Claim.** $\mathcal{V}$ is a Vitali cover of $S$.

*Proof of claim.* Let $x \in S = (0,1)^2$ and $\delta > 0$. Since $x$ lies in th
e interior of $[0,1]^2$, the distance

$$d(x) = \min\{x_1,\; x_2,\; 1-x_1,\; 1-x_2\} > 0.$$

Choose $k \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ with $1/k < \min\{d(x),\, \delta\}$. Then the closed
 disc $\overline{D}(x,\,1/k)$ has radius $1/k < d(x)$, so it is contained in $[0
,1]^2$, and its radius is $< \delta$. Hence $\overline{D}(x,1/k) \in \mathcal{V}
$, it contains $x$, and its radius is $< \delta$. $\square$

---

### Applying the Vitali Covering Theorem

Since $S$ is measurable with $m(S)=1<\infty$ and $\mathcal{V}$ is a Vitali cover
 of $S$, the theorem yields a countable pairwise-disjoint subcollection

$$\{D_i = \overline{D}(p_i,\, 1/k_i)\}_{i=1}^{\infty} \subset \mathcal{V}$$

with $m\!\left(S \setminus \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} D_i\right) = 0$.

Because the $D_i$ are pairwise disjoint and all lie inside $[0,1]^2$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \operatorname{area}(D_i) = m\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}
 D_i\right) = m(S) = 1.$$

(The last equality holds since $\bigcup D_i \subset [0,1]^2$, the $D_i$ are disj
oint, and they cover $S$ up to measure zero.)

---

### Extracting a finite subcollection

Since the series $\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \operatorname{area}(D_i) = 1$ converges, f
or any $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N$ such that

$$\sum_{i=1}^{N} \operatorname{area}(D_i) > 1 - \varepsilon.$$

The uncovered area is then:

$$m\!\left([0,1]^2 \setminus \bigcup_{i=1}^{N} D_i\right) = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{N} \
operatorname{area}(D_i) < \varepsilon.$$

Setting $\varepsilon = 10^{-4}$, the finite collection $\{D_1, \dots, D_N\}$ sat
isfies all requirements:

| Requirement | Satisfied because |
|---|---|
| **Finite** number of discs | $N < \infty$ (truncation of a convergent series)
|
| **Pairwise disjoint** | Inherited from the countable disjoint subcollection |
| Each radius is $1/k$, $k \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ | Each $D_i \in \mathcal{V}$ has ra
dius $1/k_i$ |
| **Inside** the unit square | Each $D_i \subset [0,1]^2$ by definition of $\mat
hcal{V}$ |
| **Uncovered area** $< 10^{-4}$ | $1 - \sum_{i=1}^N \operatorname{area}(D_i) <
10^{-4}$ |

---

### Conclusion

Such a finite collection of disjoint discs exists. The key ingredients are:

1. **The radii $\{1/k : k \in \mathbb{Z}^+\}$ are dense near $0$**, so discs of
these radii can be made arbitrarily small—this is what makes $\mathcal{V}$ a Vit
ali cover.

2. **The Vitali Covering Theorem** guarantees that a countable disjoint subcolle
ction covers the square up to measure zero.

3. **Finite measure** ($m(S)=1$) allows truncating the countable collection to a
 finite one while losing an arbitrarily small area.

Therefore, a finite number of disjoint discs with radii of the form $1/k$ can be
 placed inside a unit square so that the uncovered area is less than $10^{-4}$.
$\blacksquare$
