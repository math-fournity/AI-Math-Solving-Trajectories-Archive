**Problem.** Which regular polygons can be obtained (and how) by cutting a cube
with a plane?

---

## Setup and a key structural fact

Place the cube as $[-1,1]^3$ (or $[0,1]^3$; the choice is immaterial). Let $\Pi$
 be a plane and $P = \Pi \cap \text{cube}$. Since the cube is convex, $P$ is a c
onvex polygon. Each side of $P$ lies in a face of the cube, and each face of the
 cube is a square, so $\Pi$ meets each face in either $\emptyset$, a point, or a
 segment. Hence **each face contributes at most one side** of $P$, and distinct
sides lie in distinct faces. The cube has 6 faces, so $P$ has **at most 6 sides*
*: $P$ is a triangle, quadrilateral, pentagon, or hexagon.

The six faces come in **three pairs of parallel faces**: $\{x=\pm1\}$, $\{y=\pm1
\}$, $\{z=\pm1\}$. A crucial observation:

> **If $\Pi$ meets both faces of a parallel pair, the two corresponding sides of
 $P$ are parallel.**

Indeed, each such side is the intersection of $\Pi$ with a plane $x=\pm1$ (say);
 two parallel planes cut by $\Pi$ yield parallel lines.

We also need a small fact about regular $n$-gons. In a regular $n$-gon, two side
s separated by $k$ steps have directions differing by $k\cdot 360^\circ/n$; they
 are parallel iff $2k/n\in\mathbb{Z}$.

| $n$ | parallel-side pairs |
|-----|---------------------|
| 3 | none |
| 4 | 2 (opposite sides) |
| 5 | none |
| 6 | 3 (opposite sides) |

---

## Ruling out the regular pentagon

A pentagonal section uses 5 of the 6 faces. Drawing 5 faces from 3 pairs, by the
 pigeonhole principle at least two pairs must be **fully** used (using at most o
ne full pair gives at most $1\cdot 2 + 2\cdot 1 = 4$ faces $< 5$). Hence a penta
gonal section has **at least two pairs of parallel sides**.

But a regular pentagon has **no** pair of parallel sides (from the table above:
$2k/5\notin\mathbb{Z}$ for $1\le k\le 4$). Contradiction.

> **A regular pentagon cannot be obtained.**

---

## The equilateral triangle (yes)

Cut the cube $[0,1]^3$ by the plane $x+y+z=1$, which passes through the three ve
rtices adjacent to the origin: $(1,0,0),(0,1,0),(0,0,1)$. The section is the tri
angle with these vertices. Each side has length $\sqrt{2}$, so it is **equilater
al**. (It uses one face from each parallel pair — consistent with a triangle hav
ing no parallel sides.)

---

## The square (yes)

Cut the cube by a plane parallel to a face, e.g. $z=0$ in $[-1,1]^3$. The sectio
n is the square $[-1,1]^2\times\{0\}$. (One may verify this is essentially the o
nly type: a tilted plane meeting four faces as two complete parallel pairs force
s the section to be a rectangle; equality of adjacent sides and perpendicularity
 then force the plane to be face-parallel.)

---

## The regular hexagon (yes)

Cut the cube $[-1,1]^3$ by the plane $x+y+z=0$, which passes through the center
and is perpendicular to a body diagonal. It meets the six edges

$$(0,1,-1),\ (1,0,-1),\ (1,-1,0),\ (0,-1,1),\ (-1,0,1),\ (-1,1,0).$$

Each vertex is at distance $\sqrt{2}$ from the origin, and consecutive vertices
subtend $60^\circ$ at the origin (e.g. $(0,1,-1)\cdot(1,0,-1)=1=(\sqrt2)(\sqrt2)
\cos\theta$, so $\theta=60^\circ$). Six equal chords of $60^\circ$ on a circle f
orm a **regular hexagon** with side $\sqrt{2}$. (It uses all six faces — three c
omplete parallel pairs — matching the three pairs of parallel opposite sides of
a regular hexagon.)

---

## Completeness

A plane section has at most 6 sides, so only $n=3,4,5,6$ are candidates. We have
 shown:
- $n=3$: equilateral triangle — **possible** (plane through three vertices adjac
ent to a cube vertex).
- $n=4$: square — **possible** (plane parallel to a face).
- $n=5$: regular pentagon — **impossible** (would force two pairs of parallel si
des, but a regular pentagon has none).
- $n=6$: regular hexagon — **possible** (plane through the center perpendicular
to a body diagonal).

No other values of $n$ can occur.

---

**Answer.** The regular polygons obtainable as plane sections of a cube are exac
tly the **equilateral triangle**, the **square**, and the **regular hexagon**, o
btained respectively by:
1. a plane through the three vertices adjacent to one vertex of the cube;
2. a plane parallel to a face of the cube;
3. a plane through the center of the cube perpendicular to a body diagonal.

The regular pentagon is impossible, because any pentagonal section must use both
 faces of at least two parallel pairs (giving two pairs of parallel sides), wher
eas a regular pentagon has no parallel sides.
