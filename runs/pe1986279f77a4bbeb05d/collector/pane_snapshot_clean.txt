## Theorem

Let $(X, \|\cdot\|)$ be a normed vector space. The following are equivalent:

1. $X$ is finite-dimensional.
2. The closed unit ball $\overline{B}(0,1) = \{x : \|x\| \le 1\}$ is compact.
3. Every closed and bounded subset of $X$ is compact (the Heine–Borel property).

In particular, in any infinite-dimensional normed space there exist closed bound
ed sets that are **not** compact, so "compact = closed + bounded" holds *only* i
n finite dimension.

## Proof

### (1) $\Rightarrow$ (2): Finite-dimensional $\Rightarrow$ unit ball compact.

If $\dim X = n < \infty$, choose a basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$ and the coordinate is
omorphism $T \colon \mathbb{R}^n \to X$, $T(\alpha) = \sum_i \alpha_i e_i$. On t
he finite-dimensional space $X$ all norms are equivalent, so $\|\cdot\|$ is equi
valent to the max-norm $\|x\|_\infty = \max_i |\alpha_i|$ pulled back via $T^{-1
}$. The unit ball of $\|\cdot\|_\infty$ is the cube $[-1,1]^n$, which is compact
 in $\mathbb{R}^n$ by the classical Heine–Borel theorem. Norm equivalence gives
a continuous inclusion both ways between the two unit balls, so $\overline{B}(0,
1)$ is the continuous image (under $T$) of a compact set, hence compact.

### (2) $\Rightarrow$ (3): Unit ball compact $\Rightarrow$ Heine–Borel property.

Let $C \subseteq X$ be closed and bounded, say $C \subseteq \overline{B}(0,R)$.
The closed ball $\overline{B}(0,R) = R\,\overline{B}(0,1)$ is compact (scaling i
s a homeomorphism). A closed subset of a compact set is compact, so $C$ is compa
ct.

### (3) $\Rightarrow$ (1): Heine–Borel property $\Rightarrow$ finite-dimensional
 (contrapositive).

We prove the contrapositive: **if $X$ is infinite-dimensional, then $\overline{B
}(0,1)$ is not compact.** The tool is Riesz's lemma.

**Riesz's Lemma.** *Let $X$ be a normed space, $Y \subsetneq X$ a proper closed
subspace, and $0 < \theta < 1$. Then there exists $x \in X$ with $\|x\| = 1$ and
 $\operatorname{dist}(x, Y) \ge \theta$.*

*Proof of the lemma.* Pick $u \in X \setminus Y$. Since $Y$ is closed, $d = \ope
ratorname{dist}(u, Y) > 0$. Choose $y_0 \in Y$ with $\|u - y_0\| \le d/\theta$ (
possible since $d = \inf_{y \in Y}\|u - y\|$ and $\theta < 1$ gives $d/\theta >
d$). Set
$$x = \frac{u - y_0}{\|u - y_0\|}, \qquad \text{so } \|x\| = 1.$$
For any $y \in Y$,
$$\|x - y\| = \frac{\|u - (y_0 + \|u-y_0\|\,y)\|}{\|u - y_0\|} \ge \frac{d}{\|u
- y_0\|} \ge \frac{d}{d/\theta} = \theta,$$
since $y_0 + \|u - y_0\|\,y \in Y$. Hence $\operatorname{dist}(x, Y) \ge \theta$
. $\square$

- Pick $x_1$ with $\|x_1\| = 1$. Let $Y_1 = \operatorname{span}\{x_1\}$.
- $Y_1$ is finite-dimensional, hence closed (every finite-dimensional subspace o

$\|x_2\| = 1$ and $\operatorname{dist}(x_2, Y_1) \ge \tfrac12$. In particular $\
|x_2 - x_1\| \ge \tfrac12$.

For all $i < j$, since $x_i \in Y_{j-1}$,

Thus $(x_n)$ is a sequence in $\overline{B}(0,1)$ whose terms are pairwise separ

In a metric space (every normed space is metric), compactness $\iff$ sequential
