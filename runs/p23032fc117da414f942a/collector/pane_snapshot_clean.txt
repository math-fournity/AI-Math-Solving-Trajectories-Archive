## Proof

Consider the (discrete) Heisenberg group $H$ consisting of all upper-triangular
$3\times 3$ matrices with integer entries and $1$'s on the diagonal:

$$
H = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & a & c \\ 0 & 1 & b \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} :
a, b, c \in \mathbb{Z} \right\}.
$$

We denote such a matrix by $(a, b, c)$. The group law is

$$
(a, b, c)(a', b', c') = (a+a',\; b+b',\; c+c' + ab').
$$

The inverse is $(a, b, c)^{-1} = (-a, -b, -c+ab)$.

---

### Step 1. The center $Z(H)$

A central element $(a,b,c)$ must commute with every $(x,y,z)$. Computing both pr
oducts:

$$
(a,b,c)(x,y,z) = (a+x,\; b+y,\; c+z+ay),
$$
$$
(x,y,z)(a,b,c) = (x+a,\; y+b,\; z+c+xb).
$$

Equality for all $x, y$ forces $ay = xb$ for all $x, y \in \mathbb{Z}$, which ho
lds iff $a = 0$ and $b = 0$. Hence

$$
Z(H) = \{(0, 0, c) : c \in \mathbb{Z}\}.
$$

---

### Step 2. The commutator subgroup $[H, H]$

For $g = (a,b,c)$ and $h = (a',b',c')$, a direct computation gives

$$
[g, h] = g^{-1}h^{-1}gh = (0,\; 0,\; ab' - a'b).
$$

So every commutator lies in $Z(H)$, and in particular $[H, H] \subseteq Z(H)$ (s
ince $Z(H)$ is a subgroup containing all commutators).

Conversely, for any $c \in \mathbb{Z}$, take $g = (1, 0, 0)$ and $h = (0, c, 0)$
. Then

$$
[g, h] = (1\cdot c - 0\cdot 0) = (0, 0, c).
$$

Thus every element $(0, 0, c) \in Z(H)$ is itself a single commutator. Therefore
 $Z(H) \subseteq [H, H]$.

---

### Conclusion

Combining both inclusions,

$$
Z(H) = [H, H].
$$

**Yes**, the center of the Heisenberg group is also its commutator subgroup. (Th
e same argument works verbatim over $\mathbb{R}$ or any commutative ring in plac
e of $\mathbb{Z}$.)
