## Proof

**Answer: No.** A non-polynomial analytic function can map $\mathbb{Q}$ into $\m
athbb{Q}$.

**Counterexample.** Consider
$$f(x) = \frac{1}{1+x^2}.$$

**Claim 1: $f$ is real-analytic on all of $\mathbb{R}$.**

The function $f(x) = 1/(1+x^2)$ is a rational function whose only singularities
in the complex plane are simple poles at $x = \pm i$. For any $x_0 \in \mathbb{R
}$, the distance from $x_0$ to the nearest singularity $\pm i$ is $\sqrt{1 + x_0
^2} > 0$. Hence the Taylor series of $f$ about $x_0$ converges to $f$ in a neigh
borhood of $x_0$ of positive radius. Since this holds for every $x_0 \in \mathbb
{R}$, $f$ is real-analytic on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

**Claim 2: $f(\mathbb{Q}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}$.**

Let $x = a/b \in \mathbb{Q}$ with $a \in \mathbb{Z}$, $b \in \mathbb{Z}\setminus
\{0\}$. Then
$$1 + x^2 = 1 + \frac{a^2}{b^2} = \frac{b^2 + a^2}{b^2},$$
so
$$f(x) = \frac{1}{1+x^2} = \frac{b^2}{a^2 + b^2}.$$
Since $b \neq 0$, we have $b^2 \geq 1$ and $a^2 + b^2 \geq 1$, so $f(x)$ is a ra
tio of two positive integers, hence $f(x) \in \mathbb{Q}$.

**Claim 3: $f$ is not a polynomial.**

If $f$ were a polynomial, then $1 = (1+x^2) f(x)$ would imply $1+x^2$ divides $1
$ in the polynomial ring, which is impossible since $\deg(1+x^2) = 2 > 0 = \deg(
1)$. Equivalently, the Taylor expansion $f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n x^{2n
}$ (valid for $|x|<1$) has infinitely many nonzero terms, so $f$ cannot be a pol
ynomial.

**Conclusion.** The function $f(x) = 1/(1+x^2)$ is real-analytic on $\mathbb{R}$
, satisfies $f(\mathbb{Q}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}$, and is not a polynomial. There
fore, the answer to the question is **no**: the condition $f(\mathbb{Q}) \subset
eq \mathbb{Q}$ does not force an analytic function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ t
o be a polynomial.
