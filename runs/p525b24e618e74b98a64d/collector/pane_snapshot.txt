explicit prior definitions. I'll interpret the standard setting: S is the feasib

**Claim.** There exist matrices $M$ and $N$ such that the solution set $S = \{x
: Mx \leq N\}$ is not a simplex.

**Construction.** Take
$$M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad N
 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Then
$$S = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x_1 \leq 1,\ x_2 \leq 1,\ -x_1 \leq 1,\ -x_2 \leq 1
\} = [-1,1]\times[-1,1],$$
the closed square of side length 2 centered at the origin.

**Why $S$ is not a simplex.** A $d$-simplex is the convex hull of exactly $d+1$
affinely independent points; in particular it has exactly $d+1$ vertices. In $\m
athbb{R}^2$, a simplex is a triangle and has exactly $3$ vertices. The square $S
 = [-1,1]^2$ has **four** vertices, namely $(\pm1,\pm1)$, and these four points
are not affinely independent in $\mathbb{R}^2$ (any affine subspace of $\mathbb{
R}^2$ has affine dimension at most $2$, so at most $3$ affinely independent poin
ts). Hence $S$ cannot be the convex hull of $3$ affinely independent points, i.e
. $S$ is not a simplex.

**Robustness of the answer.** The conclusion does not depend on the particular i
nterpretation:
- Under $Mx \leq N$: the square above is a non-simplex polyhedron.
- Under $Mx = N$: taking $M = (0\ 0)$ and $N = 0$ gives $S = \mathbb{R}^2$, an u
nbounded affine subspace, which is not a simplex (every non-degenerate simplex i
s bounded).

In every natural reading, matrices $M, N$ yielding a non-simplex-shaped solution
 set exist. $\blacksquare$
