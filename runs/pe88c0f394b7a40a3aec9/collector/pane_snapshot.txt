## Proof

**Claim.** Yes. There exists a metric space $(X, d)$ and a sequence $(x_n)$ in $
X$ such that no subsequence of $(x_n)$ converges in $X$, yet
$$\inf\{d(x_m, x_n) \mid m, n \in \mathbb{N},\; m \neq n\} = 0.$$

**Construction.** Take $X = \mathbb{R}$ with the usual metric $d(s, t) = |s - t|
$. Define the sequence $(x_n)_{n \geq 1}$ by
$$x_{2k-1} = k, \qquad x_{2k} = k + \frac{1}{k}, \qquad k = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

Explicitly: $x_1 = 1,\; x_2 = 2,\; x_3 = 2,\; x_4 = \tfrac{5}{2},\; x_5 = 3,\; x
_6 = \tfrac{10}{3},\; \dots$

**Step 1: The infimum of pairwise distances is $0$.**

For each $k \geq 1$, the two consecutive terms $x_{2k-1}$ and $x_{2k}$ are disti
nct, and
$$d(x_{2k-1},\, x_{2k}) = \left|k - \left(k + \tfrac{1}{k}\right)\right| = \frac
{1}{k}.$$
Since $\frac{1}{k} \to 0$ as $k \to \infty$, the set $\{d(x_m, x_n) : m \neq n\}
$ contains values arbitrarily close to $0$. All pairwise distances are strictly
positive (the terms are distinct points of $\mathbb{R}$, a metric space), so the
 infimum is non-negative. Combining these two facts:
$$\inf\{d(x_m, x_n) \mid m \neq n\} = 0.$$

**Step 2: No subsequence of $(x_n)$ converges in $\mathbb{R}$.**

Observe that both "tracks" of the sequence diverge to $+\infty$:
$$x_{2k-1} = k \xrightarrow{k \to \infty} +\infty, \qquad x_{2k} = k + \frac{1}{
k} \xrightarrow{k \to \infty} +\infty.$$

Let $(x_{n_j})_{j \geq 1}$ be any subsequence. The index set $\{n_j\}$ is an inf
inite subset of $\mathbb{N}$, so by the pigeonhole principle it contains infinit
ely many odd indices, or infinitely many even indices (or both).

- **Case A:** $\{n_j\}$ contains infinitely many odd indices. Then there is a su
b-subsequence with $n_j = 2k_j - 1$ and $k_j \to \infty$, giving $x_{n_j} = k_j
\to +\infty$.
- **Case B:** $\{n_j\}$ contains infinitely many even indices. Then there is a s
ub-subsequence with $n_j = 2k_j$ and $k_j \to \infty$, giving $x_{n_j} = k_j + 1
/k_j \to +\infty$.

In either case, $(x_{n_j})$ has a sub-subsequence tending to $+\infty$, which me
ans $(x_{n_j})$ itself is **unbounded**. (If a sequence is bounded, every sub-su
bsequence is bounded; contrapositively, an unbounded sub-subsequence forces the
original subsequence to be unbounded.)

A fundamental theorem of real analysis states that every convergent sequence in
$\mathbb{R}$ is bounded. Since every subsequence $(x_{n_j})$ is unbounded, **no
subsequence converges** in $\mathbb{R}$.

**Conclusion.** The metric space $X = \mathbb{R}$ with the standard metric, toge
ther with the sequence $x_{2k-1} = k$, $x_{2k} = k + 1/k$, satisfies both requir
ed properties simultaneously:

1. $\inf\{d(x_m, x_n) : m \neq n\} = 0$ (Step 1), and
2. no subsequence of $(x_n)$ converges in $X$ (Step 2).

The key mechanism is that the sequence "escapes to infinity" in $\mathbb{R}$ (wh
ich is non-compact), so no subsequence can converge, while consecutive paired te
rms $(k,\; k+1/k)$ approach each other arbitrarily closely, driving the infimum
of pairwise distances down to $0$ without ever attaining it. $\blacksquare$
