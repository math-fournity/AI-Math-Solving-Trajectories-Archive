## Proof

**Yes, it is possible.**

**Construction.** For each real number $x \in \mathbb{R}$, define the degenerate
 rectangle
$$R_x = [x, x] \times [0, 1],$$
which has width $0$ and height $1$ — a vertical line segment from $(x, 0)$ to $(
x, 1)$. Consider the collection
$$\mathcal{R} = \{R_x : x \in \mathbb{R}\}.$$

**Uncountability.** The map $x \mapsto R_x$ is injective (distinct $x$-values yi
eld distinct segments), so $|\mathcal{R}| = |\mathbb{R}|$, which is uncountable.

**Non-overlapping.** For $x_1 \neq x_2$, the sets $R_{x_1} = \{x_1\} \times [0,1
]$ and $R_{x_2} = \{x_2\} \times [0,1]$ are disjoint (they lie on different vert
ical lines), so in particular their interiors are disjoint. Thus the rectangles
are pairwise non-overlapping.

**Why degenerate rectangles are essential.** If we restrict to non-degenerate re
ctangles (each having positive area), then at most **countably** many non-overla
pping rectangles can exist. The argument: every non-degenerate rectangle has a n
onempty open interior, which contains a point with rational coordinates (by dens
ity of $\mathbb{Q}^2$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$). Since the interiors are pairwise disjo
int, the rational points selected from different rectangles are distinct. As $\m
athbb{Q}^2$ is countable, at most countably many such rectangles can exist.

The key insight is that degenerate rectangles have **empty interiors**, so the r
ational-point counting argument breaks down — there is no rational point guarant
eed to lie in the interior of a degenerate rectangle. This is precisely what per
mits an uncountable family.
