## Proof

**Claim.** $A(\mathbb{T}) \subseteq C(\mathbb{T})$: every function whose Fourier
 series converges absolutely is continuous, and the Fourier series converges uni
formly to the function itself.

---

**Setup.** Recall that a function $f:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}$ belongs to the Wie
ner algebra $A(\mathbb{T})$ precisely when its Fourier coefficients
$$\hat{f}(n)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(t)\,e^{-int}\,dt,\qquad n\in\math
bb{Z},$$
satisfy
$$\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|\hat{f}(n)|<\infty. \tag{$\star$}$$

We must show that under $(\star)$ the Fourier series
$$S(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{f}(n)\,e^{inx}$$
defines a continuous function on $\mathbb{T}$ and that $S=f$ (so the limit of th
e Fourier series coincides with the original function).

---

**Step 1 — Uniform convergence via the Weierstrass $M$-test.**

For every $x\in\mathbb{T}$ and every $n\in\mathbb{Z}$,
$$|\hat{f}(n)\,e^{inx}|=|\hat{f}(n)|.$$
By $(\star)$ the numerical series $\sum_{n}|\hat{f}(n)|$ converges. Hence, by th
e **Weierstrass $M$-test**, the trigonometric series $\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{
f}(n)\,e^{inx}$ converges **uniformly** on $\mathbb{T}$ to a function $S:\mathbb
{T}\to\mathbb{C}$.

---

**Step 2 — Continuity of the limit.**

Each partial sum
$$S_N(x)=\sum_{|n|\le N}\hat{f}(n)\,e^{inx}$$
is a trigonometric polynomial, hence continuous on $\mathbb{T}$. A **uniform lim
it of continuous functions is continuous** (the standard $\varepsilon/3$ argumen
t: given $\varepsilon>0$, pick $N$ with $\|S-S_N\|_\infty<\varepsilon/3$, then $
\delta$ with $|S_N(x)-S_N(y)|<\varepsilon/3$ for $|x-y|<\delta$, giving $|S(x)-S
(y)|<\varepsilon$). Therefore
$$S\in C(\mathbb{T}).$$

---

**Step 3 — The limit coincides with $f$.**

Because the convergence is uniform, we may interchange summation and integration
. For each $k\in\mathbb{Z}$,
$$\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} S(x)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{f
}(n)\,\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{i(n-k)x}\,dx=\hat{f}(k),$$
using the orthogonality relation $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{i(n-k)x}\,dx
=\delta_{nk}$. Thus the Fourier coefficients of the continuous function $S$ are
exactly $\hat{f}(k)$, the same as those of $f$.

Since $f\in L^1(\mathbb{T})$ (it has Fourier coefficients), the **uniqueness the
orem for Fourier coefficients** of $L^1$ functions states: two $L^1$ functions w
ith identical Fourier coefficients are equal almost everywhere. Hence
$$S=f\quad\text{a.e. on }\mathbb{T}.$$

But $S$ is continuous. Therefore $f$ admits a continuous representative — namely
 $S$ — and identifying $f$ with this representative (as is standard when we spea
k of $A(\mathbb{T})$ as a subalgebra of $C(\mathbb{T})$), we obtain
$$f=S\in C(\mathbb{T}),$$
and the Fourier series converges **uniformly** to $f$ itself.

---

**Conclusion.** Absolute summability $(\star)$ of the Fourier coefficients force
s the Fourier series to converge uniformly; the uniform limit is continuous; and
 its Fourier coefficients match those of $f$, so by uniqueness it equals $f$ (a.
e., hence everywhere after identifying $f$ with its continuous representative).
Therefore

$$\boxed{A(\mathbb{T})\subseteq C(\mathbb{T}),}$$

i.e., absolute convergence of the Fourier series implies that the limit function
 is continuous and coincides with the original function. $\blacksquare$
