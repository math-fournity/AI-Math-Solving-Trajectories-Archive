# Theory of the Singular Eigenvalue Problem $L(x) = -\lambda t x$

## 1. Reduction to Sturm–Liouville Form

The eigenvalue equation is
$$
(tx')' + \frac{m^2}{t}\,x + \lambda\, t\, x = 0, \qquad 0 < t < 1.
$$
Writing $\mathcal{L}(x) := -L(x) = -(tx')' - \frac{m^2}{t}\,x$, the equation bec
omes
$$
\mathcal{L}(x) = \lambda\, w(t)\, x, \qquad w(t) = t,
$$
which is the standard Sturm–Liouville form $-(p\,x')' + q\,x = \lambda\, w\, x$
with
$$
p(t) = t, \qquad q(t) = -\frac{m^2}{t}, \qquad w(t) = t.
$$

**Singularity analysis.** The endpoint $t = 1$ is *regular*: $p, q, w$ are conti
nuous and $p, w > 0$ there. The endpoint $t = 0$ is *singular* for three indepen
dent reasons:

1. $p(0) = 0$ — the leading coefficient vanishes.
2. $q(t) = -m^2/t \to -\infty$ as $t \to 0^+$ — the potential is unbounded.
3. $1/p(t) = 1/t$ is not integrable on $(0, \epsilon)$.

Hence the classical regular Sturm–Liouville theory does not apply directly, and
we must rebuild the theory using the singular endpoint analysis of Weyl.

## 2. Local Analysis at the Singular Endpoint $t = 0$

Setting $\lambda = 0$ in the ODE gives the *indicial equation*. Near $t = 0$ the
 dominant balance is
$$
(tx')' + \frac{m^2}{t}\,x = 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; t^2 x'' + t\,x' + m^2 x =
0,
$$
a Cauchy–Euler equation. Seeking $x = t^\alpha$ yields
$$
\alpha(\alpha - 1) + \alpha + m^2 = \alpha^2 + m^2 = 0,
$$
so $\alpha = \pm\, i m$ (for $m \neq 0$). Two linearly independent solutions nea
r $t = 0$ are
$$
\phi_1(t) = t^{im} = e^{im \ln t}, \qquad \phi_2(t) = t^{-im} = e^{-im \ln t}.
$$

**$L^2$-classification (Weyl).** With weight $w(t) = t$, we test:
$$
\int_0^{\epsilon} |t^{\pm im}|^2\, t\, dt = \int_0^{\epsilon} t\, dt = \frac{\ep
silon^2}{2} < \infty.
$$
Both solutions are in $L^2\!\big((0,\epsilon),\, t\,dt\big)$. By Weyl's alternat
ive, the endpoint $t = 0$ is **limit-circle**: *every* solution of the ODE (for
any $\lambda$) is square-integrable near $0$ with weight $t$. Consequently, a bo
undary condition must be imposed at $t = 0$ to obtain a self-adjoint operator —
exactly as at a regular endpoint.

**Case $m = 0$.** The indicial root $\alpha = 0$ is double; the two solutions ar
e $\phi_1 = 1$ and $\phi_2 = \ln t$. Both satisfy $\int_0^\epsilon |\phi_j|^2 t\
,dt < \infty$, so the endpoint remains limit-circle. A boundary condition is sti
ll required.

## 3. Boundary Conditions

### At $t = 1$ (regular endpoint)

We impose a standard separated boundary condition
$$
\cos\beta\; x(1) + \sin\beta\; x'(1) = 0, \qquad \beta \in [0, \pi),
$$
with the Dirichlet condition $x(1) = 0$ (i.e., $\beta = 0$) being the most commo
n choice.

### At $t = 0$ (singular, limit-circle endpoint)

Since both local solutions are $L^2$, we must specify a boundary condition to se
lect a self-adjoint realization. Any solution has the asymptotic form
$$
x(t) \sim A\, t^{im} + B\, t^{-im} \quad (t \to 0^+),
$$
(with $x \sim A + B\ln t$ when $m = 0$). A **self-adjoint boundary condition** a
t $t = 0$ is specified by a real parameter $\alpha \in [0, 2\pi)$:
$$
B = e^{i\alpha}\, A,
$$
i.e., the ratio of the two asymptotic coefficients is a fixed phase. Equivalentl
y, one requires
$$
\lim_{t \to 0^+} t\big(x\,\overline{\psi_\alpha}' - \overline{\psi_\alpha}\, x'\
big) = 0,
$$
where $\psi_\alpha(t) = t^{im} + e^{i\alpha}\, t^{-im}$ is a fixed reference sol
ution. The parameter $\alpha$ plays the same role as the phase $\beta$ at the re
gular endpoint.

**Special cases:**
- $\alpha = \pi$ (i.e., $B = -A$): $x(t) \sim A(t^{im} - t^{-im}) = 2iA\sin(m\ln
 t) \to 0$.
- $\alpha = 0$ (i.e., $B = A$): $x(t) \sim 2A\cos(m\ln t)$, bounded but non-vani
shing.
- For $m = 0$: the conditions reduce to $B = 0$ (i.e., $x$ bounded at $0$) or $A
 = 0$ (i.e., $x(t) \sim B\ln t \to -\infty$, but still $L^2$), or a Robin-type c
ombination $\cos\theta\, A + \sin\theta\, B = 0$.

## 4. Lagrange Identity and Green's Formula

For any twice-differentiable $x, y$ on $(0, 1)$, direct computation gives the **
Lagrange identity**:
$$
\bar{y}\,\mathcal{L}(x) - x\,\overline{\mathcal{L}(y)} = \frac{d}{dt}\Big[t\big(
x\,\bar{y}' - \bar{y}\,x'\big)\Big].
$$

Integrating from $\epsilon$ to $1$ and letting $\epsilon \to 0^+$ yields **Green
's formula**:
$$
\int_0^1 \big[\bar{y}\,\mathcal{L}(x) - x\,\overline{\mathcal{L}(y)}\big]\,dt =
\Big[t\big(x\,\bar{y}' - \bar{y}\,x'\big)\Big]_{t=0}^{t=1}.
$$

The boundary term at $t = 1$ vanishes by the regular boundary condition. At $t =
 0$, using the asymptotic expansions $x \sim A_x t^{im} + B_x t^{-im}$, $y \sim
A_y t^{im} + B_y t^{-im}$:
$$
\lim_{t \to 0^+} t\big(x\,\bar{y}' - \bar{y}\,x'\big) = 2im\big(A_x \overline{A_
y} - B_x \overline{B_y}\big).
$$
(For $m = 0$ the analogous expression is $A_x \overline{B_y} - B_x \overline{A_y
}$ where $A$ is the constant term and $B$ the $\ln t$ coefficient.)

When both $x$ and $y$ satisfy the same self-adjoint boundary condition $B = e^{i
\alpha}A$, we get $B_x \overline{B_y} = e^{i\alpha}\overline{e^{i\alpha}} A_x \o
verline{A_y} = A_x \overline{A_y}$ (since $|e^{i\alpha}| = 1$), so the limit van
ishes. **Green's formula reduces to**
$$
\boxed{\int_0^1 \bar{y}\,\mathcal{L}(x)\,dt = \int_0^1 x\,\overline{\mathcal{L}(
y)}\,dt,}
$$
which is the **self-adjointness (symmetry)** of $\mathcal{L}$ on the domain $D_\
alpha$ of functions satisfying both boundary conditions.

## 5. Reality of Eigenvalues

**Theorem.** *Every eigenvalue of the self-adjoint problem $\mathcal{L}(x) = \la
mbda\, t\, x$ with boundary conditions $(\alpha, \beta)$ is real.*

*Proof.* Let $\mathcal{L}(x) = \lambda\, t\, x$ with $x \not\equiv 0$. Taking th
e complex conjugate equation $\mathcal{L}(\bar{x}) = \bar{\lambda}\, t\, \bar{x}
$ (valid because $p, q, w$ are real), apply Green's formula with $y = \bar{x}$:
$$
(\lambda - \bar{\lambda})\int_0^1 |x|^2\, t\, dt = \int_0^1 \big[\bar{x}\,\mathc
al{L}(x) - x\,\overline{\mathcal{L}(\bar{x})}\big]\,dt = 0.
$$
Since $x \not\equiv 0$ and $w = t > 0$ on $(0,1)$, the integral is positive, so
$\lambda = \bar{\lambda}$. $\quad\blacksquare$

## 6. Orthogonality of Eigenfunctions

**Theorem.** *Eigenfunctions corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogona
l with respect to the weight $w(t) = t$:*
$$
\int_0^1 x_j(t)\,\overline{x_k(t)}\; t\, dt = 0, \qquad \lambda_j \neq \lambda_k
.
$$

*Proof.* Apply Green's formula to $x_j, x_k$:
$$
\lambda_j \int_0^1 \bar{x}_k\, x_j\, t\,dt - \bar{\lambda}_k \int_0^1 x_j\, \bar
{x}_k\, t\,dt = 0.
$$
Since $\lambda_k$ is real, $\bar{\lambda}_k = \lambda_k$, giving $(\lambda_j - \
lambda_k)\int_0^1 x_j \bar{x}_k\, t\,dt = 0$. For $\lambda_j \neq \lambda_k$ the
 integral vanishes. $\quad\blacksquare$

## 7. Simplicity of Eigenvalues

**Theorem.** *Each eigenvalue is simple (has geometric multiplicity one).*

*Proof.* The ODE is second-order, so the solution space for a given $\lambda$ is
 two-dimensional. One boundary condition (at $t = 0$) reduces to a one-dimension
al subspace; the second (at $t = 1$) then either eliminates it entirely (not an
eigenvalue) or selects a unique (up to scalar) eigenfunction. $\quad\blacksquare
$

This parallels the regular Sturm–Liouville theory exactly.

## 8. Existence of a Discrete Spectrum

**Theorem.** *The self-adjoint realization $\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,\beta}$ has a co
untably infinite sequence of real eigenvalues*
$$
\lambda_1 < \lambda_2 < \lambda_3 < \cdots, \qquad \lambda_n \to +\infty,
$$
*with corresponding eigenfunctions $\{x_n\}$ forming a complete orthogonal syste
m in $L^2\big((0,1),\, t\,dt\big)$.*

*Proof sketch.* Because the endpoint $t = 0$ is limit-circle (not limit-point),
the singular problem is **essentially regular** in the sense of Weyl: the resolv
ent $(\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,\beta} - \lambda)^{-1}$ is a compact (Hilbert–Schmidt)
 integral operator on $L^2\big((0,1), t\,dt\big)$. Compactness of the resolvent
implies:

1. The spectrum is purely discrete: a sequence of eigenvalues $\lambda_n \to +\i
nfty$ with no finite accumulation point.
2. Each eigenvalue has finite multiplicity (here, simple by §7).
3. The eigenfunctions form a complete orthonormal system.

The compactness of the resolvent follows from the construction of the **Green's
function** $G(t, s; \lambda)$: for non-real $\lambda$, the unique solution satis
fying the boundary condition at $t = 0$ and the condition at $t = 1$ yields a co
ntinuous kernel on $[0,1] \times [0,1]$, so the integral operator
$$
(T_\lambda f)(t) = \int_0^1 G(t, s; \lambda)\, f(s)\, s\, ds
$$
is Hilbert–Schmidt, hence compact. $\quad\blacksquare$

## 9. Explicit Eigenfunctions and Eigenvalue Equation

The general solution of $(tx')' + \frac{m^2}{t}x + \lambda t x = 0$ with $\lambd

The boundary condition at $t = 0$ fixes the ratio $c_2/c_1$ (a specific linear c

## 10. Asymptotic Distribution of Eigenvalues
