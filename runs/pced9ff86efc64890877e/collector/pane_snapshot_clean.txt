## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, there exists a bijective differentiable function $f:\mathbb{R}^+
\to\mathbb{R}^+$ whose derivative is not continuous.

**Construction.** Let $c=1$. Choose a smooth bump function $\eta:\mathbb{R}\to[0
,1]$ with
- $\eta\equiv 1$ on $\bigl[\tfrac34,\tfrac54\bigr]$,
- $\operatorname{supp}\eta\subset\bigl(\tfrac12,\tfrac32\bigr)$,

so in particular $\eta(0)=0$. Define
$$
\phi(x)=\eta(x)\,(x-1)^{2}\sin\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{x-1}\Bigr)\ \ (x\neq 1),\qquad \
phi(1)=0.
$$

**Step 1. $\phi$ is differentiable everywhere and $\phi(0)=0$.**

Away from $x=1$, $\phi$ is a product of smooth functions, hence smooth. At $x=1$
, since $\eta\equiv 1$ near $1$,
$$
|\phi(x)|\le (x-1)^{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \phi'(1)=\lim_{x\to 1}\frac{(x-1
)^{2}\sin(1/(x-1))}{x-1}=\lim_{x\to 1}(x-1)\sin\!\Bigl(\tfrac1{x-1}\Bigr)=0.
$$
So $\phi$ is differentiable on all of $\mathbb{R}$, with $\phi'(1)=0$. Since $\o
peratorname{supp}\eta\subset(\tfrac12,\tfrac32)$, we have $\eta(0)=0$, hence $\p
hi(0)=0$.

**Step 2. $\phi'$ is bounded but discontinuous at $1$.**

For $x\neq 1$,
$$
\phi'(x)=\eta'(x)(x-1)^{2}\sin\!\Bigl(\tfrac1{x-1}\Bigr)+\eta(x)\Bigl[2(x-1)\sin
\!\Bigl(\tfrac1{x-1}\Bigr)-\cos\!\Bigl(\tfrac1{x-1}\Bigr)\Bigr].
$$
Both terms are bounded (the first because $\eta'$ is bounded and $(x-1)^2\le \tf
rac14$ on the support; the second because $|2(x-1)|\le 1$ on the support and $|\
cos|\le 1$). Thus $\phi'$ is bounded: $|\phi'(x)|\le M$ for some constant $M$.

Near $x=1$ (where $\eta\equiv 1$, $\eta'\equiv 0$),
$$
\phi'(x)=2(x-1)\sin\!\Bigl(\tfrac1{x-1}\Bigr)-\cos\!\Bigl(\tfrac1{x-1}\Bigr)\xri
ghtarrow[x\to 1]{}\ \text{oscillates in }[-1,1],
$$
since the first term tends to $0$ while $-\cos(1/(x-1))$ attains every value in
$[-1,1]$ along sequences $x_n\to 1$. Hence $\lim_{x\to 1}\phi'(x)$ does **not**
exist, so $\phi'$ is discontinuous at $1$, even though $\phi'(1)=0$.

**Step 3. Define $f$ and verify it is a differentiable bijection $\mathbb{R}^+\t
o\mathbb{R}^+$.**

Pick $\varepsilon>0$ with $\varepsilon M<1$ and set
$$
f(x)=x+\varepsilon\,\phi(x),\qquad x>0.
$$
Then $f$ is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}^+$ with
$$
f'(x)=1+\varepsilon\,\phi'(x)\ge 1-\varepsilon M>0,
$$
so $f$ is **strictly increasing**, hence injective. Being continuous (even diffe
rentiable), $f$ maps intervals to intervals. Moreover
$$
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}f(x)=0+\varepsilon\,\phi(0)=0,\qquad \lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=\inf
ty
$$
(the latter because $\phi$ is compactly supported, hence bounded, while $x\to\in
fty$). By the Intermediate Value Theorem, $f\bigl((0,\infty)\bigr)=(0,\infty)=\m
athbb{R}^+$, so $f$ is **surjective**. Thus $f$ is a bijection $\mathbb{R}^+\to\
mathbb{R}^+$.

**Step 4. $f'$ is not continuous.**

Since $f'(x)=1+\varepsilon\,\phi'(x)$ and $\phi'$ is discontinuous at $1$, $f'$
is also discontinuous at $1\in\mathbb{R}^+$.

**Conclusion.** The function $f(x)=x+\varepsilon\,\eta(x)(x-1)^{2}\sin(1/(x-1))$
 (with the value at $x=1$ set by continuity) is a bijective differentiable map $
\mathbb{R}^+\to\mathbb{R}^+$ whose derivative fails to be continuous at $x=1$. T
herefore such a function **exists**. $\blacksquare$
