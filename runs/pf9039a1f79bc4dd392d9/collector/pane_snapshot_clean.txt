## Evaluation of $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty \frac{\cos x}{1+x^2}\,dx$

**Claim.** $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty \frac{\cos x}{1+x^2}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{2e}
.$

### Step 1. Laplace representation of $\frac{1}{1+x^2}$

For $x\geq 0$,
$$\frac{1}{1+x^2}=\int_0^\infty e^{-(1+x^2)t}\,dt.$$

Hence
$$I:=\int_0^\infty\frac{\cos x}{1+x^2}\,dx=\int_0^\infty\cos x\int_0^\infty e^{-
(1+x^2)t}\,dt\,dx.$$

**Justification of exchanging order.** Since
$$\int_0^\infty\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-(1+x^2)t}|\cos x|\,dt\,dx=\int_0^\infty\fra
c{|\cos x|}{1+x^2}\,dx\leq\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\frac{\pi}{2}<\infty,$$
Fubini's theorem applies. Therefore
$$I=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\left(\int_0^\infty e^{-tx^2}\cos x\,dx\right)dt.$$

### Step 2. The inner Gaussian–cosine integral

Completing the square in the standard Fourier transform of a Gaussian gives, for
 $t>0$,
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-tx^2}e^{ibx}\,dx=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{t}}\,e^{-b^2/(4t)
}.$$
Taking real parts and using evenness,
$$\int_0^\infty e^{-tx^2}\cos(bx)\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{t}}\,e^{-b^2/(
4t)}.$$
With $b=1$:
$$\int_0^\infty e^{-tx^2}\cos x\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{t}}\,e^{-1/(4t)}
.$$

Substituting back,
$$I=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\int_0^\infty t^{-1/2}\,e^{-t-1/(4t)}\,dt.$$

### Step 3. A lemma on the remaining integral

**Lemma.** For $a>0$, $\displaystyle J(a):=\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2-a^2/u^2}\,du=\f
rac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\,e^{-2a}.$

*Proof.* Substitute $u=a/v$ ($du=-a/v^2\,dv$):
$$J(a)=a\int_0^\infty v^{-2}\,e^{-v^2-a^2/v^2}\,dv.$$
Renaming $v\to u$,
$$\int_0^\infty u^{-2}\,e^{-u^2-a^2/u^2}\,du=\frac{J(a)}{a}.\tag{$\ast$}$$

Differentiating under the integral sign (justified for $a>0$ by domination: $e^{
-u^2-a^2/u^2}$ and $\frac{2a}{u^2}e^{-u^2-a^2/u^2}$ decay super-polynomially at
$0$ and like $e^{-u^2}$ at $\infty$, uniformly on compact $a$-intervals bounded
away from $0$):
$$J'(a)=\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2-a^2/u^2}\!\left(-\frac{2a}{u^2}\right)du=-2a\int_0
^\infty u^{-2}\,e^{-u^2-a^2/u^2}\,du\overset{(\ast)}{=}-2a\cdot\frac{J(a)}{a}=-2
J(a).$$

Thus $J'(a)=-2J(a)$, so $J(a)=Ce^{-2a}$. Letting $a\to 0^+$ (dominated convergen
ce, since $e^{-a^2/u^2}\to 1$ and the integrand is dominated by $e^{-u^2}$):
$$J(0)=\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2}\,du=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}=C.$$
Hence $J(a)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\,e^{-2a}$. $\square$

### Step 4. Reducing the remaining integral to the lemma

In $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty t^{-1/2}\,e^{-t-1/(4t)}\,dt$, set $t=u^2$ ($dt=2u
\,du$, $t^{-1/2}=1/u$):
$$\int_0^\infty t^{-1/2}\,e^{-t-1/(4t)}\,dt=2\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2-1/(4u^2)}\,du
=2\,J\!\left(\tfrac12\right).$$

By the lemma with $a=\tfrac12$:
$$2\,J\!\left(\tfrac12\right)=2\cdot\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\,e^{-1}=\sqrt\pi\,e^{-1}.
$$

### Step 5. Conclusion

$$I=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\cdot\sqrt\pi\,e^{-1}=\frac{\pi}{2e}.$$

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\int_0^\infty \frac{\cos x}{1+x^2}\,dx=\frac{\pi}{2e}.}$$
