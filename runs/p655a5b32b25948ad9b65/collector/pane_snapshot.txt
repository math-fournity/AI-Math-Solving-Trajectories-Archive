**Answer: No.** A continuous function on $\mathbb{R}^n$ that vanishes at infinit
y need not be integrable.

**Counterexample.** Define $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ by
$$f(x)=\frac{1}{1+|x|},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^n.$$

*Continuity.* The map $x\mapsto |x|$ is continuous and $t\mapsto 1/(1+t)$ is con
tinuous on $[0,\infty)$, so $f$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}^n$.

*Vanishing at infinity.* As $|x|\to\infty$, $1+|x|\to\infty$, hence $f(x)=1/(1+|
x|)\to 0$. Thus $f$ vanishes at infinity.

*Non-integrability.* Using polar coordinates $x=r\omega$ with $r=|x|$, $\omega\i
n\mathbb{S}^{n-1}$, and $dx=r^{n-1}\,dr\,d\sigma(\omega)$:
$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}|f(x)|\,dm_n=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{
1+r}\,r^{n-1}\,dr\,d\sigma(\omega)=\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})\int_0^\infty \frac{r
^{n-1}}{1+r}\,dr.$$
For $r\ge 1$, $\frac{r^{n-1}}{1+r}\ge \frac{r^{n-1}}{2r}=\frac{1}{2}r^{n-2}$. Si
nce $n\ge 1$, the exponent $n-2\ge -1$, so
$$\int_1^\infty \frac{r^{n-1}}{1+r}\,dr\ge \frac12\int_1^\infty r^{n-2}\,dr=+\in
fty \qquad(\text{as }n-2\ge -1).$$
Therefore $\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}|f|\,dm_n=+\infty$; $f$ is not integrable.

**Remark.** The key distinction is that "vanishing at infinity" only controls th
e *pointwise* decay $f(x)\to 0$, whereas integrability requires the *volume-weig
hted* decay $\int |f|\,dm_n<\infty$. In dimension $n$, the volume of the shell $
\{r\le|x|\le r+1\}$ grows like $r^{n-1}$, so a function decaying merely as $1/(1
+|x|)$ is overwhelmed by the growth of volume. Integrability demands decay faste
r than $|x|^{-n}$ (up to logarithmic corrections), which is strictly stronger th
an merely tending to $0$.
