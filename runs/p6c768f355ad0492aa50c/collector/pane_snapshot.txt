**Answer: No.** The implication $[A, e^B] = 0 \Rightarrow [A, B] = 0$ is **false
** in general. A counterexample suffices.

---

**Counterexample.** Work over $\mathbb{C}$ with $n = 2$. Set

$$B = \begin{pmatrix} 2\pi i & 0 \\ 0 & -2\pi i \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A = \begin
{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

**Step 1: $[A, e^B] = 0$.** Since $B$ is diagonal,

$$e^B = \begin{pmatrix} e^{2\pi i} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{-2\pi i} \end{pmatrix} = \begin
{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = I.$$

Hence $[A, e^B] = [A, I] = 0$ for *every* matrix $A$, in particular for our chos
en $A$.

**Step 2: $[A, B] \neq 0$.** Compute directly:

$$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2\pi i & 0 \\
 0 & -2\pi i \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2\pi i \\ 2\pi i & 0 \end{pmat
rix},$$

$$BA = \begin{pmatrix} 2\pi i & 0 \\ 0 & -2\pi i \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0
& 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2\pi i \\ -2\pi i & 0 \end{pmat
rix}.$$

Thus

$$[A, B] = AB - BA = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -4\pi i \\ 4\pi i & 0 \end{pmatrix} \ne
q 0.$$

---

**Why this works.** The matrix exponential is not injective: distinct matrices c
an exponentiate to the same value. Here $B \neq 0$ yet $e^B = I$, because the ei
genvalues of $B$ are integer multiples of $2\pi i$. The commutativity $[A, e^B]
= 0$ only constrains $A$ to commute with $e^B = I$, which is vacuous, while $B$
itself (with eigenvalues $\pm 2\pi i$) need not commute with $A$.

More generally, whenever $e^B = I$ but $B \neq 0$, *any* $A$ failing to commute
with $B$ yields $[A, e^B] = 0$ yet $[A, B] \neq 0$. The implication would hold u
nder additional hypotheses (e.g. if the eigenvalues of $B$ lie in a strip where
$\exp$ is injective, such as $|\operatorname{Im}\lambda| < \pi$, or if $A$ and $
B$ are both real symmetric / Hermitian), but it fails in general.

**Conclusion:** $[A, e^B] = 0$ does **not** necessarily imply $[A, B] = 0$.
