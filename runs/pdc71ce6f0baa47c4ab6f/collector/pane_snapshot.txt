Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether every compact, closed, non-
convex body $K \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ with no holes can be written as a finite un
ion of compact convex closed bodies, each contained in $K$.

**Answer: No.** Such a finite decomposition does not always exist.

## Counterexample

Define $K \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ by:
$$K = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0 \le x \le 1,\; -x^2 \le y \le x^2\}.$$

**Verification of the hypotheses:**

- **Compact:** $K$ is closed (defined by non-strict inequalities) and bounded, h
ence compact.
- **Closed:** Immediate from the definition.
- **Body (non-empty interior):** The point $(\tfrac{1}{2}, 0)$ is interior since
 $|0| < (\tfrac{1}{2})^2 = \tfrac{1}{4}$.
- **Non-convex:** The points $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ are in $K$, but their midpoint
$(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2})$ is not, since $\tfrac{1}{2} > \tfrac{1}{4} = (\tf
rac{1}{2})^2$.
- **No holes:** The complement $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus K$ is connected. The enti
re region $\{x < 0\}$ lies in the complement, and any point outside $K$ can be c
onnected to this region without crossing $K$ (one can route around the cusp). Th
ere are no bounded components of the complement.

## Key Lemma

**Lemma.** Any convex set $C \subseteq K$ contains **at most one** point on the
upper boundary curve
$$\Gamma = \{(x, x^2) : x \in [0, 1]\} \subset \partial K.$$

**Proof of Lemma.** Suppose for contradiction that $C$ contains two distinct poi
nts $(a, a^2)$ and $(b, b^2)$ on $\Gamma$, with $a < b$. Since $C$ is convex, th
e entire line segment between them lies in $C \subseteq K$. Parametrize this seg
ment as:
$$\bigl(a + t(b-a),\; a^2 + t(b^2 - a^2)\bigr), \quad t \in [0,1].$$

Setting $x(t) = a + t(b-a)$ and $y(t) = a^2 + t(b^2 - a^2)$, we compute:
$$y(t) - x(t)^2 = t(1-t)(b-a)^2.$$

For $t \in (0,1)$ and $a \neq b$, this is **strictly positive**, meaning $y(t) >
 x(t)^2$. But membership in $K$ requires $y \le x^2$. Therefore the segment (exc
luding endpoints) lies **outside** $K$, contradicting $C \subseteq K$. $\square$

**Remark.** The identical computation (by symmetry $y \mapsto -y$) shows that an
y convex subset of $K$ also contains at most one point on the lower boundary $\{
(x, -x^2) : x \in [0,1]\}$.

## Conclusion

The upper boundary $\Gamma$ is an **uncountable** subset of $K$. By the Lemma, e
ach compact convex closed set $C_i \subseteq K$ contains at most **one** point o
f $\Gamma$. Therefore, for any finite collection $\{C_i\}_{i=1}^N$ with $C_i \su
bseteq K$:
$$\left|\Gamma \cap \bigcup_{i=1}^N C_i\right| \le N < \infty < |\Gamma|.$$

Since $\Gamma \subset K$ but $\Gamma \not\subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^N C_i$, we have
 $K \neq \bigcup_{i=1}^N C_i$.

Thus **no finite decomposition** of $K$ into compact convex closed bodies contai
ned in $K$ exists. $\blacksquare$

**Generalization to $\mathbb{R}^n$, $n \ge 3$:** The same construction extends b
y taking $K = \{(x_1, \ldots, x_n) : 0 \le x_1 \le 1,\; x_2^2 + \cdots + x_n^2 \
le x_1^4\}$, a solid of revolution with a cusp at the origin. The curve $\gamma
= \{(t, t^2, 0, \ldots, 0) : t \in [0,1]\}$ on the boundary satisfies the identi
cal chord-lies-outside property, so the same argument applies.

**For $n = 1$:** Any compact body in $\mathbb{R}$ with non-empty interior and no
 holes must be a single closed interval, which is convex—so no non-convex exampl
e exists (the premise is vacuous).
