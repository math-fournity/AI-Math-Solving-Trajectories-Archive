## Problem

Let $N$ be a positive integer whose digits add up to $23$. What is the greatest
possible product the digits of $N$ can have?

## Proof

We seek to maximize the product of the (decimal) digits of $N$, subject to the c
onstraint that the digits are positive integers between $1$ and $9$ and sum to $
23$.

**Observation 1 (no zeros, no large digits).** Any digit $0$ makes the entire pr
oduct $0$, so an optimal $N$ has no zero digits. We may also use any number of d
igits (more digits with the same total sum can only help, since splitting a digi
t $d \ge 5$ into smaller pieces increases the product — shown below).

**Observation 2 (splitting large digits increases the product).** For any digit
$d \ge 5$, replacing it by $3$ and $d-3$ keeps the sum fixed and changes the pro
duct by a factor of $\frac{3(d-3)}{d}$. For $d \ge 5$ this factor is $> 1$:
- $d=5$: $\frac{3\cdot 2}{5}=\frac65>1$,
- $d=6$: $\frac{3\cdot 3}{6}=\frac32>1$,
- $d=7$: $\frac{3\cdot 4}{7}=\frac{12}{7}>1$,
- $d=8$: $\frac{3\cdot 5}{8}=\frac{15}{8}>1$,
- $d=9$: $\frac{3\cdot 6}{9}=2>1$.

So in an optimal solution every digit is at most $4$.

**Observation 3 (prefer 3's over 2's and 4's).** Among digits $\le 4$, compare g
roupings with equal sum:
- $2+2 = 4$ but $2\cdot 2 = 4 = 4$, so two $2$'s tie one $4$; we may use $4$'s o
r pairs of $2$'s interchangeably for sum $4$.
- $3+3 = 6$ versus $2+4 = 6$: products $9$ vs $8$, so two $3$'s beat $\{2,4\}$.
- $3+3 = 6$ versus $2+2+2 = 6$: products $9$ vs $8$, so two $3$'s beat three $2$
's.
- $4+4 = 8$ versus $3+3+2 = 8$: products $16$ vs $18$, so $\{3,3,2\}$ beats $\{4
,4\}$.

Thus $3$'s are the most efficient building block. The only reason to use a non-$
3$ digit is to absorb the remainder when the sum is not divisible by $3$.

**Observation 4 (handling the remainder).** Write $23 = 3q + r$ with $r \in \{0,
1,2\}$:
$$23 = 3\cdot 7 + 2, \qquad q=7,\; r=2.$$

- If $r=0$: use $q$ threes.
- If $r=2$: use $q$ threes and one $2$. (A $2$ is better than leaving the remain
der as a single digit $2$ vs. any other split of $2$.)
- If $r=1$: use $q-1$ threes and one $4$ (since $3+1$ should be replaced by $2+2
=4$, as $3\cdot 1 < 2\cdot 2$).

Here $r=2$, so the optimal digit multiset is
$$\{3,3,3,3,3,3,3,2\} \quad\text{(seven 3's and one 2)}.$$

**Verification of optimality against nearby alternatives.**
- Seven $3$'s and one $2$: product $= 3^7 \cdot 2 = 2187 \cdot 2 = \mathbf{4374}
$.
- Six $3$'s and one $5$: $3^6\cdot 5 = 729\cdot 5 = 3645 < 4374$.
- Five $3$'s and two $4$'s: $3^5\cdot 4^2 = 243\cdot 16 = 3888 < 4374$.
- Five $3$'s, one $4$, one $2$: $3^5\cdot 4\cdot 2 = 243\cdot 8 = 1944 < 4374$.
- Four $3$'s and ... (sum $12$, remainder $11$ split as $4+4+3$): $3^5\cdot 4^2
= 3888 < 4374$.

All alternatives give strictly smaller products, confirming the $r=2$ rule.

**Constructing $N$.** The integer $N = 23333333$ (or any permutation, e.g. $3233
3333$) has digits $\{2,3,3,3,3,3,3,3\}$ summing to $2+7\cdot 3 = 23$, with digit
 product
$$2 \cdot 3^7 = 2 \cdot 2187 = 4374.$$

## Conclusion

The greatest possible product of the digits of $N$ is

$$\boxed{4374}.$$
