**Answer: TRUE** (for distinct $\alpha, \beta \in \Omega$; the case $\alpha = \b

## Proof

Let $G$ act primitively on $\Omega$, and let $\Gamma \subseteq \Omega$ be a subs
et that is not a block. We prove that for any **distinct** $\alpha, \beta \in \O
mega$, there exists $g \in G$ such that $\Gamma^g$ contains exactly one of $\alp
ha, \beta$.

**Step 1: Define a relation.** Define $\sim$ on $\Omega$ by:
$$\alpha \sim \beta \iff \bigl[\forall\, g \in G:\ \alpha \in \Gamma^g \Longleft
rightarrow \beta \in \Gamma^g\bigr].$$

It is immediate that $\sim$ is an equivalence relation (reflexivity, symmetry, a
nd transitivity all follow from the logical equivalence).

**Step 2: $\sim$ is a $G$-congruence.** We show that $\alpha \sim \beta$ implies
 $\alpha^h \sim \beta^h$ for every $h \in G$. For any $g \in G$:
$$\alpha^h \in \Gamma^g \iff \alpha \in \Gamma^{g h^{-1}} \iff \beta \in \Gamma^
{g h^{-1}} \iff \beta^h \in \Gamma^g,$$
where the middle equivalence uses $\alpha \sim \beta$. Hence $\alpha^h \sim \bet
a^h$.

**Step 3: Equivalence classes of a $G$-congruence are blocks.** Let $\Delta$ be
an equivalence class of $\sim$. For any $g \in G$, the set $\Delta^g$ is also an
 equivalence class (by Step 2, $\sim$ is $G$-invariant). Since distinct equivale
nce classes are disjoint, either $\Delta^g = \Delta$ or $\Delta^g \cap \Delta =
\emptyset$. Thus $\Delta$ is a block.

**Step 4: Apply primitivity.** Since $G$ is primitive, the only $G$-congruences
on $\Omega$ are the **universal relation** ($\alpha \sim \beta$ for all $\alpha,
 \beta$) and the **identity relation** ($\alpha \sim \beta$ only if $\alpha = \b
eta$). We analyze both cases.

**Case 1: $\sim$ is the identity relation.** Then for any distinct $\alpha, \bet
a$, we have $\alpha \not\sim \beta$, which means there exists $g \in G$ such tha
t $\alpha \in \Gamma^g$ and $\beta \notin \Gamma^g$ (or vice versa). This is exa
ctly the desired conclusion.

**Case 2: $\sim$ is the universal relation.** Then all elements of $\Omega$ are
equivalent, meaning for every $g \in G$, the set $\Gamma^g$ either contains all
elements or none. That is, $\Gamma^g \in \{\emptyset, \Omega\}$ for every $g$. B
ut $|\Gamma^g| = |\Gamma|$ for all $g$ (group actions are bijections), so this f
orces $|\Gamma| = 0$ or $|\Gamma| = |\Omega|$, i.e., $\Gamma = \emptyset$ or $\G
amma = \Omega$.

Both $\emptyset$ and $\Omega$ are trivial blocks ($\emptyset^g \cap \emptyset =
\emptyset$ for all $g$; $\Omega^g = \Omega$ for all $g$), contradicting the hypo
thesis that $\Gamma$ is **not** a block. Hence Case 2 is impossible.

**Conclusion.** Only Case 1 is possible, and it yields the desired result: for a
ny distinct $\alpha, \beta \in \Omega$, there exists $g \in G$ such that $\Gamma
^g$ contains exactly one of $\alpha, \beta$. $\blacksquare$
