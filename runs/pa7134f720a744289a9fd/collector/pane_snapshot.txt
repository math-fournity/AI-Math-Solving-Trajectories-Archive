## Proof

We show that for $0<\theta<\pi$,

$$S(\theta):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin((2k+1)\theta)}{2k+1}=\frac{\pi}{4}.$$

### Step 1. Identify the series as a Fourier series

Consider the $2\pi$-periodic odd function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ defined on
 $(0,\pi)$ by

$$f(\theta)=\frac{\pi}{4},\qquad 0<\theta<\pi,$$

and extended to $(-\pi,0)$ by oddness, $f(-\theta)=-f(\theta)=-\pi/4$. This is t
he standard **square wave** of amplitude $\pi/4$.

Since $f$ is odd, its Fourier series is a pure sine series $f(\theta)\sim\sum_{n
=1}^{\infty}b_n\sin(n\theta)$ with

$$b_n=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi}f(\theta)\sin(n\theta)\,d\theta=\frac{2}{\pi}\cdo
t\frac{\pi}{4}\int_0^{\pi}\sin(n\theta)\,d\theta=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[-\frac{\cos(n\
theta)}{n}\Bigr]_0^{\pi}.$$

Hence

$$b_n=\frac{1}{2n}\bigl(1-\cos(n\pi)\bigr)=\frac{1-(-1)^n}{2n}.$$

Therefore

$$b_n=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{n},& n\ \text{odd},\\[4pt]0,& n\ \text{even}.\end{c
ases}$$

Writing $n=2k+1$ for the odd indices,

$$f(\theta)\sim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin((2k+1)\theta)}{2k+1}=S(\theta).$$

So the given series is exactly the Fourier sine series of the square wave $f$.

### Step 2. Apply Dirichlet's convergence theorem

The function $f$ is piecewise constant, hence piecewise $C^1$ (in fact piecewise
 smooth) on every bounded interval, with finitely many jump discontinuities per
period (jumps at $0,\pi,2\pi,\dots$). By **Dirichlet's theorem** (equivalently,
the Fourier convergence theorem for piecewise smooth functions), the Fourier ser
ies of $f$ converges at every $\theta$ to

$$\frac{f(\theta^+)+f(\theta^-)}{2}.$$

### Step 3. Evaluate the limit on $(0,\pi)$

For any $\theta\in(0,\pi)$, the point $\theta$ is a **point of continuity** of $
f$: both one-sided limits exist and equal $f(\theta)=\pi/4$. Concretely, every i
nterior point of $(0,\pi)$ is bounded away from the jump points $0$ and $\pi$, s
o $f$ is locally constant equal to $\pi/4$ in a neighbourhood of $\theta$, givin
g

$$\frac{f(\theta^+)+f(\theta^-)}{2}=\frac{\pi/4+\pi/4}{2}=\frac{\pi}{4}.$$

Therefore, by Dirichlet's theorem,

$$S(\theta)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin((2k+1)\theta)}{2k+1}=\frac{\pi}{4},\qq
uad 0<\theta<\pi.$$

### Step 4. Independent verification via Abel summation (optional check)

For additional confidence, consider the Abel means. For $0<r<1$,

$$\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}r^{2k+1}\frac{\sin((2k+1)\theta)}{2k+1}=\operatorname{Im}\s

As $r\to1^-$, $\operatorname{artanh}(re^{i\theta})\to\operatorname{artanh}(e^{i\

$$\operatorname{artanh}(e^{i\theta})=\frac12\log\frac{1+e^{i\theta}}{1-e^{i\thet

Since $\cos(\theta/2)>0$ and $\sin(\theta/2)>0$ for $0<\theta<\pi$,

$$\frac{\cos(\theta/2)}{-i\sin(\theta/2)}=\frac{\cos(\theta/2)}{\sin(\theta/2)}\

$$\lim_{r\to1^-}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}r^{2k+1}\frac{\sin((2k+1)\theta)}{2k+1}=\frac

By Abel's theorem (the partial sums of $\sum\sin((2k+1)\theta)/(2k+1)$ are bound

$$S(\theta)=\frac{\pi}{4},\qquad 0<\theta<\pi.$$
