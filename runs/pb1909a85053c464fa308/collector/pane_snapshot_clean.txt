## Proof

**Claim.** Yes. Every non-measurable subset of $\mathbb{R}$ (Lebesgue) contains
a bounded non-measurable subset.

**Proof.** Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be Lebesgue non-measurable. Partition $\
mathbb{R}$ into the countable family of half-open unit intervals
$$\mathbb{R} = \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} [n, n+1),$$
and set
$$A_n := A \cap [n, n+1), \qquad n \in \mathbb{Z}.$$

Each $A_n$ is bounded (it is contained in $[n,n+1]$, a bounded interval), and
$$A = \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} A_n.$$

Suppose, for contradiction, that every $A_n$ were Lebesgue measurable. Then $A$,
 being a countable union of measurable sets, would itself be Lebesgue measurable
 — contradicting the hypothesis that $A$ is non-measurable.

Therefore at least one $A_{n_0}$ is non-measurable. This $A_{n_0}$ is a bounded
(it lies in $[n_0, n_0+1]$) non-measurable subset of $A$.

Hence every non-measurable set contains a bounded non-measurable subset. $\black
square$

**Remark.** The argument uses only countable additivity / closure of the Lebesgu
e $\sigma$-algebra under countable unions, together with the fact that $\mathbb{
R}$ is a countable union of bounded intervals. The same reasoning shows more gen
erally that in any measure space where the whole space is a countable union of s
ets from a prescribed bounded family, a non-measurable set must have a non-measu
rable piece in one of those pieces.
