# Bézout's Theorem

**Theorem (Bézout).** *The maximum number of isolated solutions to a system of $
n$ polynomial equations in $n$ variables, where the $i$-th polynomial has degree
 $d_i$, is*
$$\boxed{d_1 \cdot d_2 \cdots d_n}.$$

---

## Setup

Let $k$ be an algebraically closed field and let $f_1, \dots, f_n \in k[x_1, \do
ts, x_n]$ with $\deg f_i = d_i$. We work in affine space $\mathbb{A}^n_k$ and it
s projective compactification $\mathbb{P}^n_k$. Let $F_i$ denote the homogenizat
ion of $f_i$ to degree $d_i$:
$$F_i(X_0, X_1, \dots, X_n) = X_0^{d_i}\, f_i\!\left(\tfrac{X_1}{X_0}, \dots, \t
frac{X_n}{X_0}\right).$$

The affine solutions of $f_1 = \cdots = f_n = 0$ correspond to projective soluti
ons of $F_1 = \cdots = F_n = 0$ with $X_0 \neq 0$.

---

## Proof

### Step 1: The projective bound via intersection theory

We use the classical intersection-theoretic form of Bézout's theorem. In $\mathb
b{P}^n$, each hypersurface $V(F_i)$ defines a divisor whose class is $d_i H$, wh
ere $H$ is the hyperplane class. The intersection of $n$ hypersurfaces in genera
l position is a zero-dimensional scheme whose length equals the degree of the pr
oduct of their classes in the Chow ring:
$$\deg\!\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n} [V(F_i)]\right) = \deg\!\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n} d_i
H\right) = \left(\prod_{i=1}^{n} d_i\right) \deg(H^n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} d_i,$$
since $\deg(H^n) = 1$ (the $n$-fold self-intersection of the hyperplane class is
 a single point).

**Projective Bézout.** For *any* $n$ hypersurfaces $V(F_1), \dots, V(F_n) \subse
t \mathbb{P}^n$ with no common component of positive dimension, the scheme-theor
etic intersection $V(F_1) \cap \cdots \cap V(F_n)$ is zero-dimensional, and its
length (counting multiplicities) is exactly
$$\sum_{P} \operatorname{mult}(P) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} d_i.$$

This is the standard Bézout theorem for projective space; it follows from the fa
ct that the intersection product is well-defined on rational equivalence classes
 and equals the product of degrees when the intersection is proper (zero-dimensi
onal).

### Step 2: From projective to affine

If the affine system $f_1 = \cdots = f_n = 0$ has only isolated solutions, then
the projective system $F_1 = \cdots = F_n = 0$ has no common positive-dimensiona
l component *in the affine chart* $X_0 \neq 0$. However, there may be additional
 solutions at infinity ($X_0 = 0$).

**Case A: No solutions at infinity.** If $F_1 = \cdots = F_n = 0$ has no solutio
n with $X_0 = 0$, then the projective intersection is entirely in the affine cha
rt, and by Step 1:
$$\#\{\text{affine isolated solutions, with multiplicity}\} = \prod_{i=1}^{n} d_
i.$$
In particular, the number of *distinct* isolated solutions is at most $\prod d_i
$.

**Case B: Solutions at infinity exist.** The projective intersection still has t
otal length $\prod d_i$ (counting multiplicities), but some of this length is "u
sed up" by points at infinity. The affine isolated solutions therefore number at
 most
$$\prod_{i=1}^{n} d_i - (\text{length at infinity}) \;\leq\; \prod_{i=1}^{n} d_i
.$$

In either case, the number of distinct isolated affine solutions is at most $\pr
od_{i=1}^{n} d_i$.

### Step 3: The bound is achieved (sharpness)

It remains to show that $\prod d_i$ is actually attained. We exhibit a system wi
th exactly $\prod d_i$ distinct isolated solutions.

**Generic choice achieves the bound.** Consider the parameter space of all $n$-t
uples $(f_1, \dots, f_n)$ with $\deg f_i = d_i$. The condition that the projecti
ve system has a solution at infinity is a *proper closed condition* on this para
meter space: it requires the existence of a point $[0:X_1:\cdots:X_n]$ satisfyin
g all $F_i(0, X_1, \dots, X_n) = 0$, which imposes nontrivial algebraic conditio
ns. Therefore, for a **Zariski-open dense** set of choices, there are no solutio
ns at infinity.

Moreover, the condition that the intersection is *transverse* (all intersection
multiplicities equal to $1$) is also Zariski-open. On the open set where both co
nditions hold, the projective Bézout theorem gives exactly $\prod d_i$ distinct,
 multiplicity-one solutions, all in the affine chart.

**Explicit example (for concreteness).** Take $f_i(x_1, \dots, x_n) = x_i^{d_i}
- 1$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$. Then $\deg f_i = d_i$, and the system $x_i^{d_i} = 1
$ for all $i$ has exactly $d_i$ choices for each $x_i$ (over an algebraically cl
osed field, each $x_i^{d_i} - 1$ has $d_i$ distinct roots), giving
$$d_1 \cdot d_2 \cdots d_n$$
distinct isolated solutions. This confirms the bound is sharp.

---

## Conclusion

The maximum number of isolated solutions to a system of $n$ polynomial equations
 in $n$ variables with degrees $d_1, \dots, d_n$ is

$$\boxed{\prod_{i=1}^{n} d_i = d_1 \cdot d_2 \cdots d_n.}$$

This is **Bézout's theorem**: the bound follows from intersection theory in proj
ective space (the intersection product of $n$ hypersurfaces of degrees $d_1, \do
ts, d_n$ has total length $\prod d_i$), and the bound is achieved by generic sys
tems (e.g., $x_i^{d_i} - 1 = 0$).
