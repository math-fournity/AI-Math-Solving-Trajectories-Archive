## Proof

**Claim.** Up to $\mathbb{R}$-algebra isomorphism, $\mathbb{C}$ is the unique fi
eld extension of $\mathbb{R}$ of degree $2$.

**Proof.** Let $K$ be a field extension of $\mathbb{R}$ with $:\mathbb{R}]=2$.
 Then $K$ is a $2$-dimensional $\mathbb{R}$-vector space. Pick any $\alpha\in K\
setminus\mathbb{R}$; then $\{1,\alpha\}$ is an $\mathbb{R}$-basis of $K$. Since
$:\mathbb{R}]=2<\infty$, $\alpha$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{R}$ of degree (di
viding) $2$, and since $\alpha\notin\mathbb{R}$ its minimal polynomial has degre
e exactly $2$. So there exist $b,c\in\mathbb{R}$ with

$$\alpha^{2}+b\alpha+c=0.$$

Complete the square. Setting $\alpha'=\alpha+\tfrac{b}{2}\in K$, we get

$$(\alpha')^{2}=\frac{b^{2}}{4}-c=:d\in\mathbb{R}.$$

**Case $d\geq 0$.** Then $d$ has a real square root $s=\sqrt{d}\in\mathbb{R}$, g
iving $(\alpha')^{2}=s^{2}$, i.e. $(\alpha'-s)(\alpha'+s)=0$. Since $K$ is a fie
ld (an integral domain) and $\alpha'+s\neq 0$ would force $\alpha'=s\in\mathbb{R
}$ and hence $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}$, we reach $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}$ — contradicti
ng the choice of $\alpha$. (Indeed $\alpha'=\pm s\in\mathbb{R}$ in either case.)
 So this case is impossible.

**Case $d<0$.** Then $-d>0$, so $-d$ has a positive real square root $r=\sqrt{-d
}\in\mathbb{R}$, $r\neq 0$. Define

$$\beta:=\frac{\alpha'}{r}=\frac{\alpha+\tfrac{b}{2}}{r}\in K.$$

Then

$$\beta^{2}=\frac{(\alpha')^{2}}{r^{2}}=\frac{d}{-d}=-1.$$

So $K$ contains an element $\beta$ with $\beta^{2}=-1$, and $\{1,\beta\}$ is an
$\mathbb{R}$-basis of $K$ (since $\beta\notin\mathbb{R}$, as $-1$ has no real sq
uare root).

Now consider the $\mathbb{R}$-algebra homomorphism

$$\varphi:\mathbb{R}[X]\to K,\qquad X\mapsto\beta.$$

Its kernel contains the ideal $(X^{2}+1)$, and since $X^{2}+1$ is irreducible ov
er $\mathbb{R}$ (it has no real root), $(X^{2}+1)$ is maximal, so $\ker\varphi=(
X^{2}+1)$. Thus $\varphi$ descends to an injective $\mathbb{R}$-algebra homomorp
hism

$$\bar\varphi:\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^{2}+1)\hookrightarrow K.$$

Both sides are $2$-dimensional $\mathbb{R}$-vector spaces, so $\bar\varphi$ is a
n isomorphism of $\mathbb{R}$-algebras (hence of fields, and of extensions of $\
mathbb{R}$). Finally,

$$\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^{2}+1)\cong\mathbb{C},\qquad X+(X^{2}+1)\mapsto i.$$

Composing, $K\cong\mathbb{C}$ as an extension of $\mathbb{R}$.

Since $K$ was an arbitrary degree-$2$ extension of $\mathbb{R}$, every such exte
nsion is $\mathbb{R}$-isomorphic to $\mathbb{C}$. $\blacksquare$

y element is either a square or minus a square, so the discriminant $d=b^{2}/4-c
$ of any quadratic over $\mathbb{R}$ is forced to be negative (the non-square ca
se), and $-d$ is automatically a square. This is exactly what forces every quadr
atic extension to be obtained by adjoining a square root of $-1$, i.e. to be $\m
athbb{C}$.
