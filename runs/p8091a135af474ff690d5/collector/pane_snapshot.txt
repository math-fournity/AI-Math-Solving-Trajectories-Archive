**Answer: Yes.** $f$ must be a bijection from $\mathbb{R}$ onto $f(\mathbb{R})$.

---

**Proof.**

Let $I=\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ denote the irrationals. The hypothesis is
that $f|_I:I\to f(I)$ is a bijection, i.e. $f$ is injective on $I$.

**Step 1. $f|_I$ is strictly monotone.**

Suppose for contradiction that $f|_I$ is not monotone. Then it is neither non-de
creasing nor non-increasing, so there exist irrationals $a<b$ with $f(a)>f(b)$ a
nd irrationals $c<d$ with $f(c)<f(d)$. Among the four points $\{a,b,c,d\}$, writ
e them in increasing order $w<x<y<z$. The restriction of $f$ to $\{w,x,y,z\}$ is
 neither non-decreasing (it contains the descent $a\to b$) nor non-increasing (i
t contains the ascent $c\to d$). A finite sequence that is neither non-decreasin
g nor non-increasing contains a consecutive triple forming a peak or a valley. H
ence there exist irrationals $r<s<t$ with either

$$f(s)>\max\{f(r),f(t)\}\qquad\text{(peak)}$$

or $f(s)<\min\{f(r),f(t)\}$ (valley). The two cases are symmetric (replace $f$ b
y $-f$), so assume a peak: $f(s)>\max\{f(r),f(t)\}$.

Let $m\in[r,t]$ be a point where $f$ attains its maximum on $[r,t]$. Since $f(m)
\ge f(s)>\max\{f(r),f(t)\}$, we have $m\in(r,t)$ and $f(m)>\max\{f(r),f(t)\}$. S
et $\alpha=\max\{f(r),f(t)\}$ and $M=f(m)$, so $\alpha<M$.

For every $y\in(\alpha,M)$:
- Since $f(r)<y<M=f(m)$, the Intermediate Value Theorem applied to $f$ on $[r,m]
$ gives a point in $(r,m)$ where $f$ equals $y$. Thus $(\alpha,M)\subseteq f\big
((r,m)\big)$.
- Likewise $f(t)<y<M=f(m)$ and IVT on $[m,t]$ gives $(\alpha,M)\subseteq f\big((
m,t)\big)$.

Now decompose $(r,m)=(I\cap(r,m))\sqcup(\mathbb{Q}\cap(r,m))$. Since $\mathbb{Q}
\cap(r,m)$ is countable, $f(\mathbb{Q}\cap(r,m))$ is countable, and
$$f\big((r,m)\big)=f\big(I\cap(r,m)\big)\cup f\big(\mathbb{Q}\cap(r,m)\big).$$
Therefore
$$f\big(I\cap(r,m)\big)\supseteq f\big((r,m)\big)\setminus f\big(\mathbb{Q}\cap(
r,m)\big)\supseteq(\alpha,M)\setminus C_1,$$
where $C_1=f(\mathbb{Q}\cap(r,m))$ is countable. Similarly
$$f\big(I\cap(m,t)\big)\supseteq(\alpha,M)\setminus C_2,$$
with $C_2=f(\mathbb{Q}\cap(m,t))$ countable. Hence
$$f\big(I\cap(r,m)\big)\cap f\big(I\cap(m,t)\big)\cap(\alpha,M)\supseteq(\alpha,
M)\setminus(C_1\cup C_2),$$
which is an interval minus a countable set, hence nonempty. Pick $y$ in this int
ersection. Then there exist irrationals $u\in(r,m)$ and $v\in(m,t)$ with $f(u)=f
(v)=y$. Since $u<m<v$, we have $u\neq v$, yet both are irrational with the same
$f$-value. This contradicts the injectivity of $f$ on $I$.

Thus $f|_I$ is monotone, and being injective it is **strictly monotone**.

**Step 2. $f$ is strictly monotone on all of $\mathbb{R}$.**

Assume $f|_I$ is strictly increasing (the decreasing case is symmetric). Let $x<
y$ in $\mathbb{R}$. Choose irrationals $p,q$ with $x<p<q<y$ (possible since $I$
is dense). Take a sequence of irrationals $a_n\downarrow x$ with $a_n<p$ and a s
equence of irrationals $b_n\uparrow y$ with $b_n>q$. Then $a_n<p<q<b_n$, all irr
ational, so $f(a_n)<f(p)<f(q)<f(b_n)$. By continuity,
$$f(x)=\lim f(a_n)\le f(p)<f(q)\le \lim f(b_n)=f(y).$$
Hence $f(x)<f(y)$, so $f$ is strictly increasing on $\mathbb{R}$.

**Step 3. Conclusion.**

A strictly monotone function is injective. Therefore $f:\mathbb{R}\to f(\mathbb{
R})$ is a continuous injective map, i.e. a bijection onto its image. $\blacksqua
re$
