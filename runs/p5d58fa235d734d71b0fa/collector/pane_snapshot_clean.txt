## Proof

**Answer: Yes, such a submodule exists.**

**Example.** Let $R = \mathbb{Z}$ and let $M = \mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-mod
ule. Take $I = M = \mathbb{Z}$.

**Claim 1: $M$ is not semisimple.**

The submodules of $\mathbb{Z}$ are precisely $n\mathbb{Z}$ for $n \geq 0$. Every
 nonzero submodule $n\mathbb{Z}$ (with $n \geq 1$) is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$
 as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, hence is not simple (it contains the proper nonzero s
ubmodule $2n\mathbb{Z}$). Therefore $\mathbb{Z}$ has **no simple (irreducible) s
ubmodules at all**. A semisimple module is a direct sum of simple modules; since
 $\mathbb{Z}$ has no simple submodules, it cannot be semisimple.

**Claim 2: $I = \mathbb{Z}$ is nonzero.**

This is immediate.

**Claim 3: $I$ contains no irreducible submodules of $M$.**

An irreducible submodule of $M$ is a submodule that is simple (irreducible) as a
 module. As established in Claim 1, $\mathbb{Z}$ has no simple submodules whatso
ever: every nonzero submodule $n\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}$ properly contains $
2n\mathbb{Z} \neq 0$, so no nonzero submodule is simple. Since $I = M = \mathbb{
Z}$, the submodules of $M$ contained in $I$ are exactly all submodules of $M$, n
one of which are irreducible. Hence $I$ contains no irreducible submodules.

**Conclusion.** The $\mathbb{Z}$-module $M = \mathbb{Z}$ is not semisimple, and
the nonzero submodule $I = \mathbb{Z}$ contains no irreducible submodules. This
confirms that such a submodule exists. $\blacksquare$
