## Solution

Define the iterated sine sequence by $f_1(x) = \sin x$ and $f_{n+1}(x) = \sin(f_
n(x))$, so the integrand is $\lim_{n\to\infty} f_n(x)$.

**Claim:** For every $x \in [0, \pi]$, $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} f_n(x) =
0$.

**Proof of claim.** The case $x = 0$ is immediate since $f_n(0) = 0$ for all $n$
. For $x \in (0, \pi]$, we have $f_1(x) = \sin x \in (0, 1] \subset (0, \pi/2)$.
 On the interval $(0, \pi/2)$ the elementary inequality $\sin t < t$ holds stric
tly. Hence
$$0 < f_{n+1}(x) = \sin(f_n(x)) < f_n(x)$$
for every $n \ge 1$, so $(f_n(x))$ is a strictly decreasing sequence bounded bel
ow by $0$. It therefore converges to some limit $L \ge 0$. Passing to the limit
in the recursion $f_{n+1}(x) = \sin(f_n(x))$ (using continuity of $\sin$) gives
$$L = \sin L.$$
The only nonnegative solution of $L = \sin L$ is $L = 0$ (since $\sin L < L$ for
 all $L > 0$). Thus $f_n(x) \to 0$.

**Evaluating the integral.** Since $f_n(x) \to 0$ pointwise on $[0, \pi]$, the i
ntegrand is identically $0$:
$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \sin(\sin(\cdots\sin(x)\cdots)) = 0, \qquad \forall\, x \in
[0,\pi].$$
Therefore
$$I = \int_0^{\pi} \lim_{n\to\infty} f_n(x)\,dx = \int_0^{\pi} 0\,dx = 0.$$

(No interchange of limit and integral is needed here — the pointwise limit is co
mputed first, yielding the constant function $0$, and the integral of $0$ over $
[0,\pi]$ is simply $0$.)

$$\boxed{I = 0}$$
