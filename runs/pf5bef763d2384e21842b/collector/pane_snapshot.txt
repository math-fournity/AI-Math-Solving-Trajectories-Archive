## Proof

Let
$$F(x,y,z)=\bigl(xz-x\cos z,\;-yz+y\cos z,\;-4-(x^{2}+y^{2})\bigr),$$
and let $S_R=\{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=R^{2},\,z\ge 0\}$ be the upper hemisphere of ra
dius $R$, oriented by the **outward** unit normal (the normal pointing away from
 the origin, which on the upper hemisphere has positive $z$‑component).

---

### Step 1. Compute the divergence of $F$

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(xz-x\cos z)=z-\cos z,$$
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(-yz+y\cos z)=-z+\cos z,$$
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\bigl(-4-(x^{2}+y^{2})\bigr)=0.$$

Adding,
$$\nabla\cdot F=(z-\cos z)+(-z+\cos z)+0=0.$$

So $F$ is **divergence‑free** on all of $\mathbb R^{3}$.

---

### Step 2. Close the surface and apply the Divergence Theorem

Let $D_R=\{x^{2}+y^{2}\le R^{2},\,z=0\}$ be the equatorial disk, oriented by the
 outward normal of the closed upper half‑ball, i.e. by $\mathbf n_D=-\mathbf k$.
 The closed surface $S_R\cup D_R$ bounds the upper half‑ball $V_R$. By the Diver
gence Theorem,

$$\iint_{S_R}F\cdot\mathbf n\,dA+\iint_{D_R}F\cdot(-\mathbf k)\,dA=\iiint_{V_R}\
nabla\cdot F\,dV=0.$$

Hence
$$\iint_{S_R}F\cdot\mathbf n\,dA=-\iint_{D_R}F\cdot(-\mathbf k)\,dA.$$

---

### Step 3. Evaluate the flux through the disk

On $D_R$ we have $z=0$, so
$$F(x,y,0)=\bigl(-x,\;y,\;-4-(x^{2}+y^{2})\bigr).$$
Thus
$$F\cdot(-\mathbf k)=-F_z=4+(x^{2}+y^{2}).$$

Therefore, using polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$ on the disk,
$$\iint_{D_R}F\cdot(-\mathbf k)\,dA=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\int_{0}^{R}\bigl(4+r^{2}
\bigr)\,r\,dr\,d\theta
=2\pi\Bigl[2r^{2}+\tfrac{r^{4}}{4}\Bigr]_{0}^{R}
=4\pi R^{2}+\frac{\pi}{2}R^{4}.$$

---

### Step 4. Flux through $S_R$ as a function of $R$

$$\Phi(R):=\iint_{S_R}F\cdot\mathbf n\,dA=-\Bigl(4\pi R^{2}+\frac{\pi}{2}R^{4}\B
igr).$$

---

### Step 5. Maximize over $R\in\mathbb R$

For every real $R$ we have $R^{2}\ge 0$ and $R^{4}\ge 0$, hence
$$4\pi R^{2}+\frac{\pi}{2}R^{4}\ge 0,$$
with equality **if and only if** $R=0$. Consequently
$$\Phi(R)=-\Bigl(4\pi R^{2}+\frac{\pi}{2}R^{4}\Bigr)\le 0=\Phi(0),$$
and equality holds only at $R=0$.

Thus $\Phi$ attains its global maximum uniquely at

$$\boxed{R=0}.$$

(At $R=0$ the surface degenerates to the origin and the flux is $0$, which is th
e largest value the flux ever takes; for every $R>0$ the flux is strictly negati
ve.)
