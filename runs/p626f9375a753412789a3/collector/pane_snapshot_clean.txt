Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the binomial property $(A+B
)^n = \sum_{j=0}^n \binom{n}{j} A^j B^{n-j}$ for a single integer $n$ forces $AB
 = BA$.

**Answer: No.** The binomial property for a single $n$ does not generally imply
commutativity. While $n = 2$ does force $AB = BA$, for $n = 3$ there exist non-c
ommuting matrices satisfying the binomial formula.

---

## Proof

### Case $n = 1$ (trivial)

$(A+B)^1 = A + B$ and $\binom{1}{0}A^0B^1 + \binom{1}{1}A^1B^0 = B + A = A + B$.
 The binomial formula holds for **all** matrices, commuting or not. So $n=1$ imp
oses no constraint.

### Case $n = 2$: binomiality **does** imply $AB = BA$

Expanding directly:
$$(A+B)^2 = A^2 + AB + BA + B^2.$$

The binomial formula gives:
$$A^2 + 2AB + B^2.$$

Equating: $AB + BA = 2AB$, hence $BA = AB$. So for $n=2$, commutativity is force
d.

### Case $n = 3$: binomiality does **not** imply $AB = BA$

**General analysis.** Let $C = AB - BA$ (so $C = 0$ iff $A, B$ commute). Expandi
ng $(A+B)^3$ and comparing with $\sum_{j=0}^3 \binom{3}{j} A^j B^{3-j}$, the non
-trivial conditions reduce to:

$$2AC + CA = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad BC + 2CB = 0.$$

These are **weaker** than $C = 0$; non-trivial solutions exist.

**Explicit counterexample.** Take
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} -
2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

**Non-commutativity.** We compute:
$$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad BA = \begin{pmatrix}
 0 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Since $AB \neq BA$, the matrices do **not** commute.

**Verification of the binomial formula for $n=3$.** Since $A$ is nilpotent with
$A^2 = 0$ (and hence $A^3 = 0$), the binomial sum collapses to:
$$\sum_{j=0}^{3} \binom{3}{j} A^j B^{3-j} = B^3 + 3AB^2 + 3\underbrace{A^2}_{=0}
B + \underbrace{A^3}_{=0} = B^3 + 3AB^2.$$

Compute each term:
$$B^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad B^3 = \begin{pmatrix
} -8 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad AB^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \en
d{pmatrix}.$$

So:
$$B^3 + 3AB^2 = \begin{pmatrix} -8 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}
0 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -8 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Now compute $(A+B)^3$ directly. We have $A + B = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1
 \end{pmatrix}$, so:
$$(A+B)^2 = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4
& -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},$$
$$(A+B)^3 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} -2 & 1
\\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -8 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Therefore:
$$(A+B)^3 = \begin{pmatrix} -8 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = B^3 + 3AB^2 = \sum_{
j=0}^{3}\binom{3}{j}A^jB^{3-j},$$

yet $AB \neq BA$. $\square$

### Conclusion

The binomial property $(A+B)^n = \sum_{j=0}^n \binom{n}{j} A^j B^{n-j}$ for a si
ngle integer $n$ does **not** imply $AB = BA$ in general:

- **$n = 1$**: The formula holds trivially for all matrices — no constraint.
- **$n = 2$**: The formula **does** force $AB = BA$.
- **$n = 3$**: The formula does **not** force commutativity, as demonstrated by
the explicit counterexample above (real $2 \times 2$ matrices with $AB \neq BA$
satisfying the binomial formula).

The key structural reason is that for $n = 2$, the single condition $AB + BA = 2
AB$ directly yields $BA = AB$. For $n \geq 3$, the conditions on the commutator
$C = AB - BA$ become linear constraints like $2AC + CA = 0$ and $BC + 2CB = 0$,
which admit non-zero solutions — the system is underdetermined relative to the r
equirement $C = 0$.
