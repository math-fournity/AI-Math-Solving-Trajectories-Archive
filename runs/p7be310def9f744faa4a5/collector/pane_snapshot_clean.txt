Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether arbitrary Taylor series dat
a can be simultaneously prescribed at two distinct points by a single smooth fun
ction. This is a two-point generalization of Borel's theorem.

**Answer: Yes, it is true.**

**Proof.**

Let $x_1 \neq x_2$ be distinct real numbers, and let $(a_n)_{n \geq 0}$, $(b_n)_
{n \geq 0}$ be arbitrary real sequences.

**Setup.** Let $\chi \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ satisfy $\chi(t) = 1$ for $|t| \l
eq 1$ and $\chi(t) = 0$ for $|t| \geq 2$. Set $D = |x_2 - x_1| > 0$. We choose a
 sequence $\epsilon_n > 0$ with $\epsilon_n \to 0$, $\epsilon_n < D/4$ for all $
n$, and satisfying a convergence condition specified below. Define:

$$f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{a_n}{n!}(x-x_1)^n \,\chi\!\Bigl(\frac{x-x_1}{
\epsilon_n}\Bigr) \;+\; \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{b_n}{n!}(x-x_2)^n \,\chi\!\Big
l(\frac{x-x_2}{\epsilon_n}\Bigr).$$

**Disjointness of supports.** The support of $\chi\bigl((x-x_i)/\epsilon_n\bigr)
$ is $\{x : |x - x_i| \leq 2\epsilon_n\}$. Since $4\epsilon_n < D = |x_2 - x_1|$
, by the triangle inequality no point can lie in both supports simultaneously. H
ence the first sum vanishes identically on a neighborhood of $x_2$, and the seco
nd sum vanishes identically on a neighborhood of $x_1$.

**Choice of $\epsilon_n$ ensuring convergence.** Write $P_n(x) = \frac{a_n}{n!}(
x-x_1)^n$ and $\chi_n(x) = \chi\bigl((x-x_1)/\epsilon_n\bigr)$. For the $k$-th d
erivative, by Leibniz:

$$(P_n \chi_n)^{(k)}(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{k} \binom{k}{j} P_n^{(j)}(x)\, \chi_n^{(k-
j)}(x).$$

Since $\chi_n^{(m)}(x) = \epsilon_n^{-m}\,\chi^{(m)}\bigl((x-x_1)/\epsilon_n\big
r)$, we have $\|\chi_n^{(m)}\|_\infty \leq C_m\, \epsilon_n^{-m}$ where $C_m = \
|\chi^{(m)}\|_\infty$, and $\chi_n^{(m)}$ is supported on $|x - x_1| \leq 2\epsi
lon_n$.

For $n > k$ and $0 \leq j \leq k$, on the support of $\chi_n$:

$$|P_n^{(j)}(x)| = \frac{|a_n|}{(n-j)!}|x-x_1|^{n-j} \leq \frac{|a_n|\, 2^{n-j}}
{(n-j)!}\,\epsilon_n^{n-j}.$$

Therefore:

$$\|P_n^{(j)}\,\chi_n^{(k-j)}\|_\infty \leq \frac{|a_n|\, 2^{n-j}\, C_{k-j}}{(n-
j)!}\,\epsilon_n^{n-k}.$$

Since $n > k$, the exponent $n - k \geq 1$, so this tends to $0$ as $\epsilon_n
\to 0$. We choose $\epsilon_n$ inductively (decreasing, with $\epsilon_n < D/4$)
 small enough that for every $k < n$ and every $0 \leq j \leq k$:

$$\frac{|a_n|\, 2^{n-j}\, C_{k-j}}{(n-j)!}\,\epsilon_n^{n-k} \leq 2^{-n}.$$

This is possible because for each $n$, only finitely many conditions (those with
 $k < n$) must be satisfied. Then for $n > k$:

$$\|(P_n \chi_n)^{(k)}\|_\infty \leq \sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\, 2^{-n} \leq 2^
k \cdot 2^{-n},$$

so $\sum_{n > k} \|(P_n\chi_n)^{(k)}\|_\infty \leq 2^k \sum_{n>k} 2^{-n} < \inft
y$. The finitely many terms with $n \leq k$ are each smooth. The identical argum
ent applies to the second sum. Hence both series converge in $C^\infty$, and $f$
 is smooth.

**Evaluation at $x_1$.** Since the second sum vanishes near $x_1$, we have $f^{(
k)}(x_1) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (P_n \chi_n)^{(k)}(x_1)$.

- **For $n > k$:** $P_n^{(j)}(x_1) = 0$ for all $j \leq k < n$ (since $P_n$ has
a zero of order $n$ at $x_1$), so every term in the Leibniz sum vanishes: $(P_n\
chi_n)^{(k)}(x_1) = 0$.

- **For $n \leq k$:** $P_n^{(j)}(x_1) = 0$ unless $j = n$ (and $P_n^{(n)}(x_1) =
 a_n$), so only the $j = n$ term survives:

$$(P_n \chi_n)^{(k)}(x_1) = \binom{k}{n}\, a_n\, \chi_n^{(k-n)}(x_1).$$

Now $\chi_n(x) = 1$ for $|x - x_1| \leq \epsilon_n$, so $\chi_n$ is identically
$1$ near $x_1$, giving $\chi_n^{(m)}(x_1) = 0$ for $m \geq 1$ and $\chi_n^{(0)}(
x_1) = 1$.

  - If $n = k$: $\binom{k}{k}\, a_k \cdot 1 = a_k$.
  - If $n < k$: $\binom{k}{n}\, a_n \cdot 0 = 0$.

Therefore $f^{(k)}(x_1) = a_k$ for all $k \geq 0$.

**Evaluation at $x_2$.** By the identical argument applied to the second sum (wh
ich vanishes near $x_1$ and is the sole contributor near $x_2$), we obtain $f^{(
k)}(x_2) = b_k$ for all $k \geq 0$.

**Conclusion.** The smooth function $f$ satisfies $f^{(n)}(x_1) = a_n$ and $f^{(
n)}(x_2) = b_n$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}_0$. $\blacksquare$
