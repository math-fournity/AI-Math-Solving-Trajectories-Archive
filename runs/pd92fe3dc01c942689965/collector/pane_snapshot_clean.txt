## Proof

We determine the cardinality of $P(P(\mathbb{N}))$ step by step.

**Step 1: Cardinality of $P(\mathbb{N})$.**

For any set $X$, there is a bijection between $P(X)$ and the set of functions $X
 \to \{0,1\}$, via the characteristic-function map $A \mapsto \chi_A$. Hence
$$|P(X)| = 2^{|X|}.$$

Applying this to $X = \mathbb{N}$, with $|\mathbb{N}| = \aleph_0$:
$$|P(\mathbb{N})| = 2^{\aleph_0}.$$

The cardinal $2^{\aleph_0}$ is, by definition, the cardinality of the continuum
$\mathfrak{c}$ (denoted $C$ in the problem). This is the standard identification
: $\mathbb{R}$, $P(\mathbb{N})$, and $\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}$ are all equipotent.
So
$$|P(\mathbb{N})| = C.$$

**Step 2: Cardinality of $P(P(\mathbb{N}))$.**

Apply the same identity $|P(X)| = 2^{|X|}$ again, now with $X = P(\mathbb{N})$:
$$|P(P(\mathbb{N}))| = 2^{|P(\mathbb{N})|} = 2^{C}.$$

**Step 3: $2^C \neq C$.**

By Cantor's theorem, for any set $X$ there is no surjection $X \to P(X)$, so $|P
(X)| > |X|$. Taking $X = P(\mathbb{N})$ (which has cardinality $C$):
$$|P(P(\mathbb{N}))| = 2^C > C.$$

Thus $2^C$ and $C$ are distinct cardinals, and the cardinality of $P(P(\mathbb{N
}))$ is strictly the larger one.

**Conclusion.**

$$\boxed{|P(P(\mathbb{N}))| = 2^{C}}$$

The cardinality of $P(P(\mathbb{N}))$ is $2^C$, **not** $C$.
