**Problem.** Determine whether there exists an infinite non-nilpotent group in w
hich every maximal subgroup is a normal subgroup.

**Answer.** Yes, such a group exists.

---

**Proof.**

We exhibit an explicit example. Let $\Omega$ be an infinite set (say $\Omega = \
mathbb{N}$), and let

$$G = A_{\mathrm{fin}}(\Omega)$$

be the **finitary alternating group** on $\Omega$, i.e. the group of all even pe
rmutations of $\Omega$ with finite support. We verify that $G$ has all the requi
red properties.

**Claim 1: $G$ is infinite.**
Since $\Omega$ is infinite, there are $3$-cycles supported on arbitrarily chosen
 triples, and there are infinitely many of them. Hence $G$ is infinite.

**Claim 2: $G$ is (non-abelian) simple.**
This is a standard theorem: the finitary alternating group on an infinite set is
 simple. (Sketch: any non-identity element of $A_{\mathrm{fin}}(\Omega)$, when c
onjugated by suitable elements, produces a $3$-cycle; the $3$-cycles generate $A
_{\mathrm{fin}}(\Omega)$; hence every nontrivial normal subgroup is all of $G$.)
 In particular $G$ is non-abelian, since it contains the non-commuting $3$-cycle
s $(1\,2\,3)$ and $(1\,2\,4)$.

**Claim 3: $G$ is perfect, i.e. $G = [G,G]$.**
Since $G$ is non-abelian and simple, the commutator subgroup $[G,G]$ is a nontri
vial normal subgroup of $G$ (nontrivial because $G$ is non-abelian). By simplici
ty, $[G,G] = G$.

**Claim 4: $G$ has no maximal subgroups.**
Suppose, for contradiction, that $M < G$ is a maximal subgroup. By the correspon
dence theorem, $G/M$ has no proper nontrivial subgroups, so $G/M \cong C_p$ for
some prime $p$. But $G/M$ is a quotient of the perfect group $G$, hence is itsel
f perfect:

$$[G/M,\, G/M] = [G,G]\,M/M = G/M.$$

Yet $[C_p, C_p] = \{e\} \neq C_p$ for every prime $p$, a contradiction. Therefor
e $G$ has **no** maximal subgroups.

Consequently, the statement "every maximal subgroup of $G$ is normal" holds **va
cuously**.

**Claim 5: $G$ is not nilpotent.**
A nilpotent group that is simple must be cyclic of prime order. Indeed, in a nil
potent group every proper subgroup is properly contained in its normalizer (the
normalizer condition); a simple nilpotent group has no proper nontrivial normal
subgroups, and combining these facts forces the group to be cyclic of prime orde
r. Since $G$ is infinite and non-abelian, it cannot be nilpotent.

**Conclusion.**
The group $G = A_{\mathrm{fin}}(\Omega)$ is infinite, non-nilpotent, and has no
maximal subgroups — so every maximal subgroup is (vacuously) normal. Hence an in
finite non-nilpotent group in which every maximal subgroup is normal **does exis
t**. $\blacksquare$
