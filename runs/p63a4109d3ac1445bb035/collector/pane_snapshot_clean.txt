Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the converse of "If $C_0(X)
$ is complete for a locally compact Hausdorff space $X$, then $X$ is compact" is
 true.

The converse is: **"If $X$ is compact, then $C_0(X)$ is complete."**

---

## Proof that the converse is TRUE

**Setup.** Let $X$ be a compact Hausdorff space (which is automatically locally
compact). We equip $C_0(X)$ with the supremum norm $\|f\|_\infty = \sup_{x\in X}
|f(x)|$.

**Step 1: Reduction to $C(X)$.** When $X$ is compact, every continuous function
$f:X\to\mathbb{C}$ vanishes at infinity: given $\varepsilon>0$, take $K=X$ (whic
h is compact), and $|f(x)|<\varepsilon$ for $x\notin K$ holds vacuously since $X
\setminus K=\emptyset$. Hence
$$C_0(X) = C(X).$$

**Step 2: $C(X)$ is complete under $\|\cdot\|_\infty$.** Let $(f_n)_{n=1}^\infty
$ be a Cauchy sequence in $(C(X),\|\cdot\|_\infty)$.

*Pointwise limit.* For each $x\in X$,
$$|f_n(x)-f_m(x)|\le \|f_n-f_m\|_\infty,$$
so $(f_n(x))$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb{C}$. By completeness of $\mathbb{C}$, define
 $f(x):=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_n(x)$.

*Uniform convergence.* Given $\varepsilon>0$, pick $N$ so that $\|f_n-f_m\|_\inf
ty<\varepsilon/2$ for all $n,m\ge N$. For any $x\in X$ and $n\ge N$, choose $m\g
e N$ large enough that $|f_m(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon/2$. Then
$$|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le |f_n(x)-f_m(x)|+|f_m(x)-f(x)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\va
repsilon}{2}=\varepsilon.$$
Taking the supremum over $x$: $\|f_n-f\|_\infty\le\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$. So
$f_n\to f$ uniformly.

*Continuity of the limit.* The uniform limit of continuous functions is continuo
us. Indeed, given $x_0\in X$ and $\varepsilon>0$, choose $n$ with $\|f_n-f\|_\in
fty<\varepsilon/3$. By continuity of $f_n$, there is an open neighborhood $U\ni
x_0$ with $|f_n(x)-f_n(x_0)|<\varepsilon/3$ for $x\in U$. Then for $x\in U$:
$$|f(x)-f(x_0)|\le|f(x)-f_n(x)|+|f_n(x)-f_n(x_0)|+|f_n(x_0)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon.
$$
Thus $f$ is continuous, i.e., $f\in C(X)=C_0(X)$.

Since $f_n\to f$ in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$ and $f\in C_0(X)$, the Cauchy sequence co
nverges in $C_0(X)$. Therefore $C_0(X)$ is complete. $\blacksquare$

---

**Remark on the original statement.** The original statement is actually *false*
: $C_0(X)$ is complete for **every** locally compact Hausdorff $X$, not just com
pact ones. The key fact is that $C_0(X)$ is a closed subspace of the Banach spac
e $C_b(X)$ (bounded continuous functions with sup norm)—closed because a uniform
 limit of functions vanishing at infinity also vanishes at infinity. For example
, $C_0(\mathbb{R})$ is a Banach space, yet $\mathbb{R}$ is not compact. So the o
riginal implication fails, but its converse holds.

**Conclusion:** The converse is **true**.
