Yes, such a sequence always exists. Here is the proof.

**Theorem.** Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence of positive reals with $\sum_{n=1}^{\inft
y} x_n = +\infty$. Then there exists a sequence $(y_n)$ of positive reals such t
hat $y_n/x_n \to 0$ and $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} y_n = +\infty$.

**Proof.** Define the partial sums $s_n := \sum_{k=1}^{n} x_k$, so $s_n \nearrow
 +\infty$. Set
$$
y_1 := x_1, \qquad y_n := \frac{x_n}{\sqrt{s_{n-1}}} \quad (n \ge 2).
$$
(Any positive choice for $y_1$ works; only the tail matters.)

*Ratio tending to zero.* For $n \ge 2$,
$$
\frac{y_n}{x_n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{n-1}}}.
$$
Since $s_{n-1} \to +\infty$, we have $1/\sqrt{s_{n-1}} \to 0$, hence $y_n/x_n \t
o 0$.

*Divergence of $\sum y_n$.* For $n \ge 2$ and $t \in [s_{n-1}, s_n]$, we have $t
 \ge s_{n-1}$, so $1/\sqrt{t} \le 1/\sqrt{s_{n-1}}$. Therefore
$$
\int_{s_{n-1}}^{s_n} \frac{dt}{\sqrt{t}} \;\le\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{n-1}}}\,(s_n
- s_{n-1}) \;=\; \frac{x_n}{\sqrt{s_{n-1}}} \;=\; y_n.
$$
Summing from $n=2$ to $N$ (a telescoping integral),
$$
\sum_{n=2}^{N} y_n \;\ge\; \sum_{n=2}^{N} \int_{s_{n-1}}^{s_n} \frac{dt}{\sqrt{t
}} \;=\; \int_{s_1}^{s_N} \frac{dt}{\sqrt{t}} \;=\; 2\bigl(\sqrt{s_N} - \sqrt{s_
1}\bigr).
$$
Since $s_N \to +\infty$, the right-hand side tends to $+\infty$, so $\sum_{n=1}^
{\infty} y_n = +\infty$.

Thus $(y_n)$ satisfies both required properties. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The same idea works with $\sqrt{\cdot}$ replaced by any increasing f
unction $g$ with $g(t)\to\infty$ and $\int^{\infty} dt/g(t) = +\infty$ (e.g. $g(
t)=\log t$ for $t$ large), giving $y_n = x_n/g(s_{n-1})$; the integral test argu
ment is identical. This shows the construction is robust: one can make $y_n/x_n
\to 0$ as slowly as desired while still forcing divergence.
