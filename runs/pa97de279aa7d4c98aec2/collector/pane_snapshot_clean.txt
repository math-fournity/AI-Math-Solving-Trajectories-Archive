**Answer: Yes.** The Cantor set is an example. Here is the proof.

---

Let $C \subseteq [0,1]$ denote the **middle-third Cantor set**, i.e. the set of
all $x\in[0,1]$ admitting a ternary expansion $x=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n 3^{-n}$
 with each $a_n\in\{0,2\}$.

**Claim.** $C$ is uncountable, is not dense in any open interval, and is not sca
ttered.

*Proof of the claim.*

**1. $C$ is uncountable.**

The map $\varphi:\{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}\to C$ defined by
$$\varphi\big((b_n)_{n\ge 1}\big)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2b_n}{3^{n}}$$
is a surjection: every $x\in C$ has a ternary expansion using only $0$ and $2$,
and setting $b_n=a_n/2$ gives a preimage. The only ambiguity is the usual one in
volving trailing $2$'s vs.\ a terminating expansion, which affects at most count
ably many points and does not change the cardinality. Since $\{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}
$ is uncountable (it bijects with $\mathcal P(\mathbb N)$), $C$ is uncountable.

**2. $C$ is not dense in any open interval (equivalently, $C$ is nowhere dense).
**

$C$ is closed, being the intersection of the closed sets $C_n$ (the union of $2^
n$ closed intervals of length $3^{-n}$ obtained at stage $n$). Hence $\overline
C=C$, and "$C$ is dense in the open interval $I$" would mean $I\subseteq C$.

We show no nonempty open interval is contained in $C$. At each stage $n\ge 1$ of
 the construction, an open middle-third interval $G_n$ (one of the $2^{n-1}$ gap
s of length $3^{-n}$) is removed. The collection of all removed gaps $\{G_n\}$ i
s dense in $[0,1]$: every nonempty open interval $I\subseteq[0,1]$ contains some
 gap $G_n$. (Indeed, choose $n$ large enough that $3^{-n}<|I|/3$; among the $2^n
$ intervals of $C_n$, at least one removed gap of stage $n+1$ lies inside $I$.)
Each $G_n$ is disjoint from $C$, so $I$ contains a point outside $C$. Thus $I\no
t\subseteq C=\overline C$ for every open interval $I$, meaning $C$ is not dense
in any open interval.

**3. $C$ is not scattered.**

Recall that a topological space is *scattered* iff every nonempty subspace has a
n isolated point; equivalently, it contains no nonempty dense-in-itself subspace
. We show $C$ is **perfect** (closed and has no isolated points), which immediat
ely implies it is dense-in-itself and hence not scattered.

$C$ is closed (shown above). To see $C$ has no isolated points, let $x\in C$ and
 let $U$ be any open neighborhood of $x$. Pick $\varepsilon>0$ with $(x-\varepsi
lon,x+\varepsilon)\subseteq U$. Choose $n$ large enough that $3^{-n}<\varepsilon
$. The point $x$ lies in one of the $2^n$ intervals $J$ of length $3^{-n}$ compr
ising $C_n$. This interval $J=[a,b]$ has both endpoints in $C$, and at least one
 endpoint $y\in\{a,b\}$ satisfies $y\neq x$ (if $x$ is an endpoint, take the oth
er endpoint; if $x$ is interior, take either). Then $y\in C\cap (x-\varepsilon,x
+\varepsilon)\subseteq C\cap U$ and $y\neq x$. Hence every neighborhood of $x$ c
ontains another point of $C$, so $x$ is not isolated. Since $x$ was arbitrary, $
C$ has no isolated points.

Therefore $C$ is perfect, hence dense-in-itself, hence **not scattered**.

---

**Conclusion.** The Cantor set $C$ is an uncountable subset of $[0,1]$ that is n
ot dense in any open interval (it is nowhere dense) yet is not scattered (it is
perfect). Hence such a set exists.
