## Proof

**Answer: Yes, it is possible.**

### Key Observation

The polar decomposition $A = UP$ with $P = \sqrt{A^*A}$ yields a **unique** unit
ary $U$ when $A$ is invertible. When $A$ is singular, $P$ is singular, and the u
nitary factor is **not** uniquely determined. We prove this constructively.

### Setup

Since $A$ is singular, $\det A = 0$, so $\det(A^*A) = |\det A|^2 = 0$, hence $P
= \sqrt{A^*A}$ is also singular. Therefore $\ker P \neq \{0\}$.

Let $V = \ker P$ with $\dim V = k \geq 1$, and $W = (\ker P)^\perp$ with $\dim W
 = n - k$. Since $P$ is positive semidefinite, $P$ maps $W \to W$ invertibly (as
 a positive definite operator on $W$) and $P$ maps $V \to 0$.

### Analyzing the Constraint

Suppose $A = U_1 P = U_2 P$ with $U_1, U_2$ unitary. Then:
$$(U_1 - U_2)\,P = 0.$$

This means $U_1 - U_2$ vanishes on $\operatorname{range}(P) = W$ (since $P$ is i
nvertible on $W$). Thus:
$$U_1\big|_W = U_2\big|_W.$$

Both $U_1$ and $U_2$ are unitary, so each maps $W$ isometrically onto $\operator
name{range}(A)$ and consequently maps $V$ isometrically onto $\operatorname{rang
e}(A)^\perp$. The two maps **agree on $W$** but are **free to differ on $V$**, p
rovided each remains a unitary isomorphism $V \to \operatorname{range}(A)^\perp$
.

Since $\dim V = k \geq 1$, there are infinitely many unitary isomorphisms $V \to
 \operatorname{range}(A)^\perp$. For instance, if $U_1\big|_V$ is one such isomo
rphism, then $e^{i\theta}\, U_1\big|_V$ (for any $\theta \not\equiv 0 \pmod{2\pi
}$) is another. Defining $U_2$ to agree with $U_1$ on $W$ and to equal $e^{i\the
ta} U_1$ on $V$ gives a **different** unitary matrix with $U_2 P = A$.

### Explicit Example

Take $n = 2$ and $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which is si
ngular. Then:
$$A^*A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad P = \sqrt{A^*A} =
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

We need $UP = A$. Computing:
$$UP = U\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} u_{11} &
0 \\ u_{21} & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Setting this equal to $A$ requires $u_{11} = 1$ and $u_{21} = 0$. Since $U$ is u
nitary, its first column $(1, 0)^T$ has norm $1$, and the second column must be
a unit vector orthogonal to it, hence of the form $(0, e^{i\theta})^T$. Thus:
$$U = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\theta} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \theta \in
\mathbb{R}.$$

Choosing:
- $U_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ (i.e., $\theta = 0$),
- $U_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ (i.e., $\theta = \pi$),

both are unitary, both satisfy $U_i P = A$, and $U_1 \neq U_2$. $\blacksquare$

### Conclusion

When $A$ is singular, the polar decomposition $A = UP$ does **not** determine $U
$ uniquely. The singularity of $P$ leaves a nontrivial kernel on which $U$ can b
e freely (unitarily) chosen, yielding distinct unitary factors $U_1 \neq U_2$ wi
th $A = U_1 P = U_2 P$.
