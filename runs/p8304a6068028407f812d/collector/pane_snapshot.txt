## Proof

Let $f_n(x) = (1-\sqrt{\sin x})^{n}\cos x$ for $x\in[0,\pi/2]$, $n\ge 0$.

**Step 1. Pointwise sum.** For $x\in(0,\pi/2]$, we have $\sin x\in(0,1]$, hence
$r(x):=1-\sqrt{\sin x}\in[0,1)$. Thus the geometric series converges:
$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} f_n(x)=\cos x\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r(x)^n=\frac{\cos x}{1-r(x
)}=\frac{\cos x}{\sqrt{\sin x}}.$$
At $x=0$, $r(0)=1$ and the series diverges, but $\{0\}$ has measure zero, so the
 pointwise limit a.e. is $g(x):=\dfrac{\cos x}{\sqrt{\sin x}}$.

**Step 2. Dominating function for LDCT.** Set $g_N(x):=\sum_{n=0}^{N}f_n(x)$. Si
nce $f_n\ge 0$ and $r(x)\in[0,1]$,
$$g_N(x)=\frac{1-r(x)^{N+1}}{1-r(x)}\cos x=\frac{1-r(x)^{N+1}}{\sqrt{\sin x}}\co
s x.$$
Because $0\le r(x)^{N+1}\le 1$, we get $0\le 1-r(x)^{N+1}\le 1$, hence
$$0\le g_N(x)\le \frac{\cos x}{\sqrt{\sin x}}=g(x).$$
The dominating function $g$ is integrable:
$$\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\cos x}{\sqrt{\sin x}}\,dx\;\stackrel{u=\sin x}{=}\;\int_0
^1 u^{-1/2}\,du=2\sqrt{u}\Big|_0^1=2<\infty.$$
So $g\in L^1([0,\pi/2])$, $g_N\to g$ a.e., and $|g_N|\le g$. By the **Lebesgue D
ominated Convergence Theorem**,
$$\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_0^{\pi/2}g_N(x)\,dx=\int_0^{\pi/2}g(x)\,dx.$$

**Step 3. Interchange summation and integration.** Since $g_N$ is a finite sum,
$$\int_0^{\pi/2}g_N(x)\,dx=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\int_0^{\pi/2}f_n(x)\,dx.$$
Taking $N\to\infty$ and applying Step 2,
$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_0^{\pi/2}(1-\sqrt{\sin x})^{n}\cos x\,dx=\int_0^{\pi/2
}\frac{\cos x}{\sqrt{\sin x}}\,dx=2.$$

**Justification of LDCT (recap).** The interchange $\sum_n\int f_n = \int\sum_n
f_n$ is justified by applying LDCT to the partial sums $g_N=\sum_{n=0}^N f_n$, w
hich converge a.e. to $g$ and are dominated by the integrable function $g(x)=\co
s x/\sqrt{\sin x}$ (whose integral equals $2$). The hypotheses of LDCT — pointwi
se a.e. convergence, measurability, and an integrable dominating function — are
all satisfied.

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}(1-\sqrt{\sin x})^{n}\c
os x\,dx=2}$$
