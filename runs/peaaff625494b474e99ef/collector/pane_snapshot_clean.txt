No. The size of the Galois group of a polynomial need not equal the factorial of
 its degree. Below is a complete justification.

## Statement

For a polynomial $f \in F[x]$ of degree $n$, its Galois group $\operatorname{Gal
}(f/F) := \operatorname{Gal}(E/F)$, where $E$ is the splitting field of $f$ over
 $F$, is a subgroup of the symmetric group $S_n$. Hence

$$|\operatorname{Gal}(f/F)| \mid n!$$

in the separable case, but equality $|\operatorname{Gal}(f/F)| = n!$ holds only
under special circumstances (e.g. $f$ irreducible and separable of degree $n$ wi
th "generic" roots). It fails in general, as the following counterexample shows.

## Counterexample

Take $F = \mathbb{Q}$ and

$$f(x) = (x-1)(x-2) = x^2 - 3x + 2.$$

- **Degree.** $\deg f = 2$, so $n! = 2! = 2$.
- **Splitting field.** The roots of $f$ are $1$ and $2$, both already in $\mathb
b{Q}$. Therefore the splitting field of $f$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $E = \mathbb{Q}
$ itself.
- **Galois group.** $\operatorname{Gal}(f/\mathbb{Q}) = \operatorname{Gal}(\math
bb{Q}/\mathbb{Q})$ is the trivial group, of order $1$.

Thus

$$|\operatorname{Gal}(f/\mathbb{Q})| = 1 \neq 2 = (\deg f)!.$$

So the size of the Galois group is **not** always the factorial of the degree.

## Why equality fails in general (structural explanation)

Let $f \in F[x]$ have degree $n$ and splitting field $E$.

1. **Separability.** If $f$ is not separable (e.g. in characteristic $p$ with re
peated roots), the splitting-field extension $E/F$ is not Galois in the usual se
nse, and the automorphism group can be strictly smaller than $n!$ — in fact smal
ler than $[E:F]$.

2. **Even when separable**, $|\operatorname{Gal}(f/F)| = [E:F]$, and $[E:F]$ div
ides $n!$ but need not equal it. Concretely, adjoining one root gives an interme
diate field $F(\alpha)$ with $[F(\alpha):F] \leq n$, and each subsequent root ad
joined contributes a factor of at most the number of remaining roots. The produc
t of these successive degrees is $[E:F]$, and it equals $n!$ only when every ste
p realizes the maximal possible extension — i.e. when the Galois group acts as t
he full symmetric group $S_n$ on the roots. This is a strong condition, not auto
matic.

3. **Reducibility shrinks the group.** If $f = g \cdot h$ with $\deg g = r$, $\d
eg h = s$, $r+s=n$, the Galois group permutes the roots of $g$ among themselves
and the roots of $h$ among themselves, so it embeds into $S_r \times S_s$, which
 has order $r!\,s! < n!$ whenever $r,s \geq 1$. The counterexample above is exac
tly this situation with $r=s=1$.

## A nontrivial (irreducible) counterexample

For completeness, equality can fail even for irreducible polynomials. Let $F = \
mathbb{Q}$ and

$$f(x) = x^3 - 3x - 1.$$

This cubic is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ (no rational root). Its discriminant
 is

$$\Delta = -4(-3)^3 - 27(-1)^2 = 108 - 27 = 81 = 9^2,$$

a perfect square. For an irreducible cubic, the Galois group is $A_3$ (cyclic of
 order $3$) when the discriminant is a square, and $S_3$ (order $6$) otherwise.
Hence

$$|\operatorname{Gal}(f/\mathbb{Q})| = 3 \neq 6 = 3! = (\deg f)!.$$

This shows the failure is not merely an artifact of reducibility.

## Conclusion

The size of the Galois group of a polynomial of degree $n$ is at most $n!$ (and
divides $n!$ when the polynomial is separable), but it is **not** always equal t
o $n!$. Equality holds only in special cases — most notably when the polynomial
is separable and its Galois group is the full symmetric group $S_n$. Reducibilit
y, repeated roots, and "small" splitting fields all provide counterexamples, as
demonstrated above.
