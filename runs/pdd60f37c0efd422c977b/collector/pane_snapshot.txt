# Applying the Classification Theorem to Distinguish the Two Families

## The Classification Theorem (statement)

Every compact connected 2-manifold (closed surface) is homeomorphic to exactly o
ne of the following:

- The sphere $\mathbb{S}^{2}$;
- A connected sum of $g$ tori, $\underbrace{\mathbb{T}^{2}\#\cdots\#\mathbb{T}^{
2}}_{g}$, for $g\geq 1$ (orientable, genus $g$);
- A connected sum of $k$ projective planes, $\underbrace{\mathbb{P}^{2}\#\cdots\
#\mathbb{P}^{2}}_{k}$, for $k\geq 1$ (non-orientable, genus $k$).

Moreover, no two surfaces on this list are homeomorphic to each other. The theor
em is made effective by two computable invariants: **orientability** and the **E
uler characteristic** $\chi$.

## The two invariants

**Orientability.** A surface is orientable if it admits a consistent choice of n
ormal direction (equivalently, it contains no Möbius band, or its first Stiefel–
Whitney class vanishes). The sphere and all connected sums of tori are orientabl
e; every connected sum involving at least one $\mathbb{P}^{2}$ is non-orientable
. Orientability is a topological invariant, so an orientable surface can never b
e homeomorphic to a non-orientable one. This already **separates the two familie
s** listed in the problem:

$$\{\mathbb{S}^{2},\,\mathbb{T}^{2},\,\mathbb{T}^{2}\#\mathbb{T}^{2},\,\dots\}\q
uad\text{(all orientable)}$$
$$\{\mathbb{P}^{2},\,\mathbb{P}^{2}\#\mathbb{P}^{2},\,\dots\}\quad\text{(all non
-orientable)}.$$

No member of the first family is homeomorphic to any member of the second, becau
se orientability is preserved by homeomorphism and the two families have opposit
e orientability type.

**Euler characteristic.** For a finite CW-structure with $V$ vertices, $E$ edges
, $F$ faces, $\chi = V - E + F$; this is independent of the chosen triangulation
 and hence a topological invariant. The standard models give:

| Surface | $\chi$ |
|---|---|
| $\mathbb{S}^{2}$ | $2$ |
| $\#_{g}\,\mathbb{T}^{2}$ (genus $g\geq 1$) | $2 - 2g$ |
| $\#_{k}\,\mathbb{P}^{2}$ (genus $k\geq 1$) | $2 - k$ |

These values are derived from the connected-sum rule $\chi(M\# N)=\chi(M)+\chi(N
)-2$ together with $\chi(\mathbb{S}^{2})=2$, $\chi(\mathbb{T}^{2})=0$, $\chi(\ma
thbb{P}^{2})=1$.

## Distinguishing within each family

**Within the orientable family** $\{\mathbb{S}^{2},\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{T}^{2}
\#\mathbb{T}^{2},\dots\}$: every member is orientable, so orientability does not
 separate them. But $\chi$ takes the distinct values $2,0,-2,-4,\dots$ (i.e. $2-
2g$ for $g=0,1,2,3,\dots$). Since $\chi$ is a homeomorphism invariant and these
values are all distinct, no two orientable surfaces in the list are homeomorphic
. The genus $g$ is recovered uniquely from $\chi$ by $g = (2-\chi)/2$.

**Within the non-orientable family** $\{\mathbb{P}^{2},\mathbb{P}^{2}\#\mathbb{P
}^{2},\dots\}$: again all are non-orientable, so orientability does not separate
 them. Here $\chi$ takes the distinct values $1,0,-1,-2,\dots$ (i.e. $2-k$ for $
k=1,2,3,\dots$). These are all distinct, so no two non-orientable surfaces in th
e list are homeomorphic. The genus $k$ is recovered uniquely by $k = 2 - \chi$.

## Why the two invariants together give a *complete* classification

The pair $(\text{orientability},\,\chi)$ pins down a unique surface on the class
ification list:

- **Orientable case.** $\chi = 2-2g$ is always **even**, and $g=(2-\chi)/2\geq 0
$ is a non-negative integer. This selects exactly $\#_{g}\mathbb{T}^{2}$ (with $
\mathbb{S}^{2}$ as the $g=0$ case).
- **Non-orientable case.** $\chi = 2-k$ ranges over all integers $\leq 1$, and $
k = 2-\chi\geq 1$ is a positive integer. This selects exactly $\#_{k}\mathbb{P}^
{2}$.

Crucially, the **parity** of $\chi$ also acts as an internal consistency check o
n orientability: an orientable surface always has **even** $\chi$, while a non-o
rientable surface can have either parity. Concretely:

- $\chi = 2$: only $\mathbb{S}^{2}$ (orientable, $g=0$).
- $\chi = 0$: could be $\mathbb{T}^{2}$ (orientable, $g=1$) **or** $\mathbb{P}^{
2}\#\mathbb{P}^{2}$ (non-orientable, $k=2$). Orientability breaks the tie.
- $\chi = 1$: only $\mathbb{P}^{2}$ (non-orientable, $k=1$); no orientable surfa
ce has odd $\chi$.
- $\chi = -2$: could be $\mathbb{T}^{2}\#\mathbb{T}^{2}$ (orientable, $g=2$) **o
r** $\mathbb{P}^{2}\#\mathbb{P}^{2}\#\mathbb{P}^{2}\#\mathbb{P}^{2}$ (non-orient
able, $k=4$). Again orientability decides.

So the value of $\chi$ alone narrows the candidates to at most one orientable an
d at most one non-orientable surface, and the orientability test then selects be
tween them. This is exactly the mechanism by which the two families in the probl
em are distinguished:

1. **Between the families**: orientability — the first family is entirely orient
able, the second entirely non-orientable, and the two types are mutually exclusi
ve.
2. **Within each family**: Euler characteristic — it takes distinct values on di
stinct members, so each member is uniquely identified.

## Conclusion

The Classification Theorem reduces the problem of identifying a compact 2-manifo
ld to computing two invariants. **Orientability** separates the orientable famil
y $\{\mathbb{S}^{2},\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{T}^{2}\#\mathbb{T}^{2},\dots\}$ from
the non-orientable family $\{\mathbb{P}^{2},\mathbb{P}^{2}\#\mathbb{P}^{2},\dots
\}$, since a homeomorphism preserves orientability and the two families lie on o
pposite sides of this dichotomy. **Euler characteristic** then separates the mem
bers *within* each family: it equals $2-2g$ on the $g$-fold torus (giving $2,0,-
2,-4,\dots$) and $2-k$ on the $k$-fold projective plane (giving $1,0,-1,-2,\dots
$), with all values distinct within each family. The pair $(\text{orientability}
,\chi)$ is therefore a complete set of invariants: it determines a unique surfac
e on the classification list, and in particular distinguishes every member of th
e first family from every member of the second.
