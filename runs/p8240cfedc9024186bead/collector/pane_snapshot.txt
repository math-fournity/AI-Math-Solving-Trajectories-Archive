Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether for every monic integer pol
ynomial of degree ≥ 2 with nonzero constant term, there exists a prime p making
it reducible over ℤ_p.

**Answer: Yes, such a prime always exists.**

**Proof.**

Let $f(x) = x^n + a_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n$ with $n \geq 2$ and $a_n \neq 0$.

**Key observation:** Since $f$ is a polynomial of degree $n \geq 2$, it has at m
ost $n$ roots in $\mathbb{Z}$ (indeed, at most $n$ roots in any field). Therefor
e, among the infinitely many integers, there are at most $n$ values $m$ with $f(
m) = 0$. In particular, there exists an integer $m$ such that $f(m) \neq 0$.

**Construction of the prime:** Fix such an integer $m$ with $f(m) \neq 0$. Since
 $f$ has integer coefficients and $m \in \mathbb{Z}$, the value $f(m)$ is a nonz
ero integer, so $|f(m)| \geq 1$. Hence $f(m)$ has at least one prime divisor $p$
.

**Reducibility mod $p$:** Since $p \mid f(m)$, we have $f(m) \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$.
 By the Factor Theorem applied in $\mathbb{Z}_p[x]$, the linear polynomial $(x -
 m)$ divides $f(x)$ in $\mathbb{Z}_p[x]$. That is,
$$f(x) \equiv (x - m)\, g(x) \pmod{p}$$
for some $g(x) \in \mathbb{Z}_p[x]$ of degree $n - 1$.

Since $n \geq 2$, we have $\deg(g) = n - 1 \geq 1$, so both factors $(x - m)$ an
d $g(x)$ are non-constant. Therefore $f(x)$ is reducible over $\mathbb{Z}_p$.

**Remark on the hypothesis $a_n \neq 0$:** The condition $a_n \neq 0$ (i.e., $f(
0) \neq 0$) is not essential for the argument — it merely rules out the trivial
case where $x \mid f(x)$ over $\mathbb{Z}$, which would make $f$ reducible over
every $\mathbb{Z}_p$ immediately. The argument above works for any monic integer
 polynomial of degree $\geq 2$: one simply needs *some* integer $m$ with $f(m) \
neq 0$, which always exists since a degree-$n$ polynomial has at most $n$ intege
r roots.

**Conclusion:** For every polynomial $f(x) = x^n + a_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n$ w
ith integer coefficients, $n \geq 2$, and $a_n \neq 0$, there exists a prime $p$
 such that $f(x)$ is reducible over $\mathbb{Z}_p$. $\blacksquare$
