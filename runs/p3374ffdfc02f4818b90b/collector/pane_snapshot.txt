## Proof

**Claim:** The conditions stated do not imply $H_n(X, \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{
Z}$.

**Key observation:** The condition $\partial S = 0$ (empty boundary) only requir
es that every $(n-1)$-simplex is a face of **at least two** $n$-simplices. For $
|S|$ to be a closed manifold, we would need **exactly two**. The given hypothese
s do not enforce the manifold property, and without it, the standard theorem "$H
_n \cong \mathbb{Z}$ for closed orientable $n$-manifolds" does not apply.

**Counterexample ($n = 2$):** Let $S_1$ and $S_2$ each be a triangulation of the
 2-sphere $S^2$ as an oriented simplicial complex. Each $S_i$ is 2-dimensional,
connected, oriented, and satisfies $\partial S_i = 0$ (every edge is a face of e
xactly two triangles, as required for a closed surface).

Choose a vertex $v_1 \in S_1$ and $v_2 \in S_2$. Form the **wedge sum** $S = S_1
 \vee S_2$ by identifying $v_1 \sim v_2$ to a single vertex $v$. This is a valid
 simplicial complex (we identify only a vertex, so no two distinct simplices col
lapse to the same simplex).

**Verification of all hypotheses:**

1. **Dimension $n = 2$:** $S$ is a 2-dimensional simplicial complex. ✓

2. **Connected:** $|S| = S^2 \vee S^2$ is the wedge of two connected spaces, hen
ce connected. ✓

3. **Oriented:** Each $S_i$ is independently oriented. Since $S_1$ and $S_2$ int
ersect only at the single vertex $v$, there is no compatibility conflict—the ori
entations on the two spheres do not interact. Thus $S$ is an oriented simplicial
 complex. ✓

4. **$\partial S = 0$:** Every edge of $S$ belongs to exactly one of $S_1$ or $S
_2$ (the identification is only at the vertex $v$; no edge is shared between the
 two spheres). Within each $S_i$, every edge is a face of exactly two 2-simplice
s. Therefore every edge of $S$ is a face of exactly two 2-simplices, so no edge
lies in $\partial S$. Hence $\partial S = 0$. ✓

**Computation of $H_2$:** The space $|S| = S^2 \vee S^2$ has homology given by t
he wedge sum formula. Since $S^2 \vee S^2$ is the one-point union, and using red
uced homology (which is additive under wedge sums):

$$\widetilde{H}_k(S^2 \vee S^2) \cong \widetilde{H}_k(S^2) \oplus \widetilde{H}_
k(S^2) \quad \text{for all } k.$$

For $k = 2$: $\widetilde{H}_2(S^2) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, so

$$H_2(S^2 \vee S^2) \cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}.$$

**Conclusion:** $H_2(X, \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z} \not\cong
 \mathbb{Z}$, yet all the stated hypotheses are satisfied. Therefore, the answer
 to the question is **No**: $H_n(X, \mathbb{Z})$ is not necessarily isomorphic t
o $\mathbb{Z}$.

**Remark on the root cause:** The standard result $H_n(M, \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mat
hbb{Z}$ holds for **closed orientable $n$-manifolds**. The hypotheses given ($\p
artial S = 0$, connected, oriented) are necessary conditions for $|S|$ to be a c
losed orientable manifold, but they are **not sufficient**: $\partial S = 0$ all
ows $(n-1)$-simplices to be incident to more than two $n$-simplices (as happens
at the wedge point in our example, where the link is $S^1 \sqcup S^1$ rather tha
n $S^1$). Without the manifold condition—or the stronger pseudomanifold conditio
n requiring exactly two $n$-simplices per $(n-1)$-face—the top homology group ca
n be a free abelian group of rank greater than 1.
