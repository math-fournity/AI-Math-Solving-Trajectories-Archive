## Proof

**Step 1. Solutions are real analytic.**

The operator $L = \Delta + \lambda$ has constant (hence real-analytic) coefficie
nts and is elliptic, since its principal symbol $\sigma_L(\xi) = -|\xi|^2$ vanis
hes only at $\xi = 0$. A classical theorem of Bernstein and Petrovsky (analytic
hypoellipticity of elliptic operators with analytic coefficients) states:

> If $L$ is an elliptic operator with real-analytic coefficients on an open set
$\Omega$, then every distributional solution $u$ of $Lu = 0$ is real-analytic on
 $\Omega$.

Applying this with $\Omega = \mathbb{R}^n$, the function $f$ is real-analytic on
 all of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

*(Sketch of why this holds for our constant-coefficient case: elliptic regularit
y bootstraps $f \in H^k_{\text{loc}}$ for every $k$, and the constant-coefficien
t fundamental solution $\Phi$ of $\Delta + \lambda$ is real-analytic away from t
he origin; convolving the local representation $f = \Phi * (\lambda f)$ against
the smooth data and iterating shows the Taylor series of $f$ at every point conv
erges to $f$ in a neighborhood, giving analyticity.)*

**Step 2. An analytic function vanishing on an open set vanishes identically.**

Define
$$A = \bigl\{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : \partial^\alpha f(x) = 0 \text{ for every mult
i-index } \alpha\bigr\}.$$

- $A$ is **closed**, since every partial derivative $\partial^\alpha f$ is conti
nuous.
- $A$ is **open**: if $x_0 \in A$, then the Taylor series of $f$ at $x_0$ is ide
ntically zero, and by real-analyticity this series converges to $f$ in some neig
hborhood of $x_0$, so $f \equiv 0$ in that neighborhood, hence every point of th
at neighborhood also lies in $A$.
- $A$ is **nonempty**: since $f \equiv 0$ on the nonempty open set $U$, all deri
vatives of $f$ vanish at every point of $U$, so $U \subseteq A$.

**Step 3. Conclusion.**

The set $A$ is nonempty, open, and closed in the connected space $\mathbb{R}^n$,
 so $A = \mathbb{R}^n$. In particular $f(x) = 0$ for every $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$.

Therefore $f$ is identically zero on $\mathbb{R}^n$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The hypothesis $\lambda > 0$ is not needed for unique continuation;
the argument works for any $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ (and indeed for any zeroth-o
rder analytic potential). With $\lambda > 0$ the equation is the Helmholtz equat
ion, which admits many nontrivial global solutions such as $f(x) = \cos(\sqrt{\l
ambda}\,x_1)$—but none of these can vanish on an open set, which is exactly the
content of the unique continuation principle proved above.
