## Proof

**Claim:** The statement is true: $\overline{I \setminus E} = I$.

We prove both inclusions.

**Step 1: $\overline{I \setminus E} \subseteq I$.**

The unit square $I = [0,1]\times[0,1]$ is closed in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Since $I \se
tminus E \subseteq I$, taking closures (which are monotone) gives
$$\overline{I \setminus E} \subseteq \overline{I} = I.$$

**Step 2: $I \subseteq \overline{I \setminus E}$.**

We show every point of $I$ lies in the closure of $I \setminus E$. Let $x \in I$
 and let $B(x, r)$ be any open ball of radius $r > 0$ centered at $x$. It suffic
es to show
$$B(x, r) \cap (I \setminus E) \neq \emptyset.$$

Consider the set $B(x, r) \cap I$. This is an open (in $I$) neighborhood of $x$
relative to $I$, and it has **positive two-dimensional Lebesgue measure**: indee
d, $x \in I$ and $B(x,r)$ is open, so $B(x,r) \cap I$ contains a non-degenerate
open region (even when $x$ is on the boundary of $I$, the intersection with the
interior of $I$ near $x$ has positive area; and when $x$ is in the interior, $B(
x,r) \cap I$ contains a small ball of positive area).

Since $E$ has Lebesgue measure zero, it cannot contain any set of positive measu
re. Therefore
$$B(x, r) \cap I \not\subseteq E,$$
which means there exists a point
$$y \in B(x, r) \cap I \setminus E = B(x, r) \cap (I \setminus E).$$

Thus every neighborhood of $x$ intersects $I \setminus E$, so $x \in \overline{I
 \setminus E}$. Since $x \in I$ was arbitrary,
$$I \subseteq \overline{I \setminus E}.$$

**Conclusion.**

Combining both inclusions,
$$\overline{I \setminus E} = I.$$

The statement is **true**. The essential reason is that a measure-zero set $E$ i
s too thin to create any "holes" in the closed square $I$: every neighborhood of
 every point of $I$ has positive area and therefore cannot be entirely swallowed
 by $E$.
