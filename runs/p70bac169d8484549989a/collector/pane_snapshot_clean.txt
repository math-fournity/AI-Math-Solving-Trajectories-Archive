## Proof

Let $B(t)$ be a standard Brownian motion. By definition, for each fixed $t \geq
0$, the random variable $B(t)$ is normally distributed with mean $0$ and varianc
e $t$:

$$B(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t).$$

We wish to compute $\mathbf{E}[\cos(B(t))]$.

**Method: Characteristic function of a Gaussian.**

The characteristic function of a random variable $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma
^2)$ is

$$\varphi_X(u) = \mathbf{E}[e^{iuX}] = e^{iu\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 u^2}.$$

Applying this to $B(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t)$ (so $\mu = 0$, $\sigma^2 = t$):

$$\mathbf{E}[e^{iuB(t)}] = e^{-\frac{1}{2}t\,u^2}.$$

By Euler's formula, $e^{iuB(t)} = \cos(uB(t)) + i\sin(uB(t))$. Taking the real p
art of both sides:

$$\mathbf{E}[\cos(uB(t))] = \operatorname{Re}\!\left(e^{-\frac{1}{2}t\,u^2}\righ
t) = e^{-\frac{1}{2}t\,u^2},$$

since $e^{-\frac{1}{2}t\,u^2}$ is real and positive. Setting $u = 1$:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{E}[\cos(B(t))] = e^{-t/2}.}$$

**Verification via direct integration.** As a consistency check, compute directl
y:

$$\mathbf{E}[\cos(B(t))] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \cos(x)\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi
t}}\,e^{-x^2/(2t)}\,dx.$$

Using the standard Gaussian integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{ax - x^2/(2t)}\
,dx = \sqrt{2\pi t}\,e^{a^2 t/2}$ (valid for all $a \in \mathbb{C}$ by analytic
continuation), set $a = i$:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{ix - x^2/(2t)}\,dx = \sqrt{2\pi t}\,e^{-t/2}.$$

Taking the real part (the imaginary part, the $\sin$ integral, vanishes by odd s
ymmetry):

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \cos(x)\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\,e^{-x^2/(2t)}\,dx =
e^{-t/2},$$

confirming the result.
