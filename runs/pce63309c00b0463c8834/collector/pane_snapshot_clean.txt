Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether there exists a positive int
eger $n$ such that none of $n, n+1, \ldots, n+2017$ is a sum of two squares.

**Answer: Yes, such $n$ exists.**

## Proof

**Key Theorem (Fermat–Euler).** A positive integer $m$ is expressible as the sum
 of two squares of integers if and only if every prime $p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$ app
ears to an **even** power in the prime factorization of $m$.

**Construction.** By Dirichlet's theorem, there are infinitely many primes congr
uent to $3 \pmod{4}$. Select $2018$ distinct such primes
$$q_0,\; q_1,\; q_2,\; \ldots,\; q_{2017}, \qquad q_i \equiv 3 \pmod{4}.$$

Since the $q_i$ are distinct primes, the moduli $q_0^2, q_1^2, \ldots, q_{2017}^
2$ are pairwise coprime. By the **Chinese Remainder Theorem**, there exists an i
nteger $n$ satisfying the simultaneous congruences:
$$n + i \;\equiv\; q_i \pmod{q_i^2}, \qquad i = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, 2017.$$

The solution is unique modulo $Q = q_0^2 q_1^2 \cdots q_{2017}^2$. We choose $n$
 to be the **smallest positive** solution, so $n \geq 1$.

**Verification.** Fix any $i \in \{0, 1, \ldots, 2017\}$.

- From $n + i \equiv q_i \pmod{q_i^2}$, we write $n + i = q_i + k_i\, q_i^2$ for
 some integer $k_i$.
- Then $q_i \mid (n+i)$, but $(n+i)/q_i = 1 + k_i\, q_i \equiv 1 \pmod{q_i}$, so
 $q_i \nmid \frac{n+i}{q_i}$.
- Therefore $q_i$ divides $n+i$ to **exactly the first power** (an odd power).

Since $q_i \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$ is a prime appearing to an **odd** power in the fa
ctorization of $n+i$, the Fermat–Euler theorem tells us that $n+i$ **cannot** be
 written as the sum of two squares of integers.

This argument applies to **every** $i = 0, 1, \ldots, 2017$, so no element of $\
{n, n+1, \ldots, n+2017\}$ is a sum of two squares.

**Positivity check.** Since $n \geq 1$ and $i \geq 0$, every $n+i \geq 1$, so al
l numbers in the set are positive integers and the Fermat–Euler theorem applies.
 $\blacksquare$
