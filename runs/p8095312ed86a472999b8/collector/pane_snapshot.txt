# Answer: No

The orthogonal projection of a lattice onto a subspace is **not** always a latti
ce. Below is a complete proof by counterexample.

---

## Counterexample

Let $L = \mathbb{Z}^2 \subset \mathbb{R}^2$, which is a (full-rank) lattice in $
\mathbb{R}^2$. Let $E$ be the one-dimensional subspace (line through the origin)
 spanned by the vector $v = (1, \sqrt{2})$:

$$E = \operatorname{span}\bigl((1, \sqrt{2})\bigr).$$

Let $\pi : \mathbb{R}^2 \to E$ denote the orthogonal projection onto $E$.

---

## Computing the projection

For any $(m, n) \in \mathbb{Z}^2$,

$$
\pi(m, n) = \frac{\langle (m,n),\, (1,\sqrt{2})\rangle}{\langle (1,\sqrt{2}),\,
(1,\sqrt{2})\rangle}\,(1, \sqrt{2}) = \frac{m + \sqrt{2}\,n}{1 + 2}\,(1, \sqrt{2
}) = \frac{m + \sqrt{2}\,n}{3}\,(1, \sqrt{2}).
$$

Hence

$$
\pi(L) = \pi(\mathbb{Z}^2) = \left\{ \frac{m + \sqrt{2}\,n}{3}\,(1, \sqrt{2}) \;
:\; m, n \in \mathbb{Z} \right\}.
$$

Identifying $E \cong \mathbb{R}$ via the coordinate $t \mapsto t\,(1,\sqrt{2})$,
 the projected set corresponds to

$$
S := \left\{ \frac{m + \sqrt{2}\,n}{3} : m, n \in \mathbb{Z} \right\} = \frac{1}
{3}\bigl(\mathbb{Z} + \sqrt{2}\,\mathbb{Z}\bigr).
$$

---

## $S$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$

Consider the additive subgroup $G = \mathbb{Z} + \sqrt{2}\,\mathbb{Z}$ of $\math
bb{R}$. We show $G$ is dense.

**Claim.** Every additive subgroup of $\mathbb{R}$ is either dense or cyclic (of
 the form $a\mathbb{Z}$ for some $a \geq 0$).

*Proof of claim.* Let $G \leq \mathbb{R}$ be an additive subgroup. If $G = \{0\}
$, it is cyclic. Otherwise, set $a = \inf\{g \in G : g > 0\}$.

- If $a > 0$: then $a \in G$ (otherwise elements of $G \cap (0, 2a)$ would contr
adict the infimum), and $G = a\mathbb{Z}$.
- If $a = 0$: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $g \in G$ with $0 < g < \
varepsilon$, so the multiples of $g$ form an $\varepsilon$-net in $\mathbb{R}$,
giving density.

Now, $G = \mathbb{Z} + \sqrt{2}\,\mathbb{Z}$ is **not** cyclic: if $G = a\mathbb
{Z}$ for some $a > 0$, then $1 = ka$ and $\sqrt{2} = \ell a$ for some $k, \ell \
in \mathbb{Z}$, yielding $\sqrt{2} = \ell / k \in \mathbb{Q}$, contradicting the
 irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$.

Therefore $G$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$, and so $S = \frac{1}{3}G$ is dense in $\
mathbb{R}$.

---

## $\pi(L)$ is not a lattice

A **lattice** (in $E$) is a **discrete** subgroup of $E$. Since $\pi$ is linear,
 $\pi(L)$ is an additive subgroup of $E$. Under the identification $E \cong \mat
hbb{R}$, this subgroup is $S$, which we just showed is **dense** in $\mathbb{R}$
.

A dense subgroup of $\mathbb{R}$ (that is not $\{0\}$) is **not discrete**: ever
y neighborhood of $0$ contains infinitely many elements of $S$. Explicitly, sinc
e $S$ is dense, for any $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists a nonzero $s \in S \cap (
-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$, so $\{0\}$ is not open in the subspace topology of
$S$.

Therefore $\pi(L)$ is **not discrete**, hence **not a lattice**.

---

## Conclusion

The orthogonal projection of a lattice $L$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ onto a subspace $E$
 is **not always a lattice**. The projection $\pi(\mathbb{Z}^2)$ onto the line $
\operatorname{span}((1, \sqrt{2}))$ yields a dense (non-discrete) subgroup of $E
$, which fails the discreteness requirement of a lattice.

**Remark.** The obstruction is arithmetic: $\pi(L)$ is a lattice in $E$ if and o
nly if $E \cap L^{*}$ (or equivalently the projection restricted to the rational
/real span) satisfies a rationality condition — specifically, $\pi(L)$ is discre
te in $E$ if and only if $E$ is **rational** with respect to $L$, meaning $E$ is
 spanned by vectors in $L \otimes \mathbb{Q}$. Irrational directions, as in the
example above, produce dense (non-lattice) projections.
