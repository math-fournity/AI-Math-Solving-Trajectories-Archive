## Proof

The operator is
$$(Au)(x) = \sin x \int_0^{2\pi} u(y)\cos y\,dy.$$

This is a **rank-one** operator of the form $A = |\sin\rangle\langle\cos|$, wher
e $\langle\cos,u\rangle = \int_0^{2\pi} u(y)\cos y\,dy$. Its range is the one-di
mensional subspace $\operatorname{span}\{\sin x\}$.

**Step 1. Any nonzero eigenvalue has an eigenfunction in the range.**

Suppose $Au = \lambda u$ with $\lambda \neq 0$. Then $u = \lambda^{-1}Au \in \op
eratorname{Range}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{\sin x\}$, so $u = c\sin x$ for some
 constant $c \neq 0$.

**Step 2. Compute $A(\sin x)$.**

$$A(\sin x) = \sin x \int_0^{2\pi} \sin y\cos y\,dy = \sin x \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\
int_0^{2\pi}\sin 2y\,dy = \sin x \cdot 0 = 0.$$

So $\sin x \in \ker A$, hence it is **not** an eigenvector with a nonzero eigenv
alue. Combined with Step 1, there are **no nonzero eigenvalues**.

**Step 3. $\lambda = 0$ is an eigenvalue.**

$Au = 0$ iff $\int_0^{2\pi} u(y)\cos y\,dy = 0$, i.e. $u \perp \cos$. The orthog
onal complement of $\cos$ in $L^2(0,2\pi)$ is infinite-dimensional, so $0$ is an
 eigenvalue with infinite (geometric) multiplicity. (In particular $\sin x$ itse
lf, and every function orthogonal to $\cos$, is an eigenfunction for $\lambda=0$
.)

**Conclusion.** The only eigenvalue of $A$ is $\boxed{0}$.
