## Proof

**Claim:** There exists a non-continuous bijection between $(0,1]$ and $\mathbb{
R}$. In fact, *every* bijection $(0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ is non-continuous.

The argument proceeds in two parts: (A) no continuous bijection $(0,1]\to\mathbb
{R}$ exists, and (B) a bijection $(0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ does exist. Together these
 imply that any bijection is necessarily non-continuous, so a non-continuous bij
ection exists.

---

### Part A: No continuous bijection $(0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ exists

Suppose for contradiction that $f:(0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ is continuous and bijectiv
e.

**Step 1 — $f$ is strictly monotone.** The domain $(0,1]$ is an interval (connec
ted). A continuous injective function on an interval is strictly monotone. Since
 $f$ is bijective it is injective, so $f$ is either strictly increasing or stric
tly decreasing on $(0,1]$.

**Step 2 — The image is a half-open interval.** Consider the case $f$ strictly i
ncreasing (the decreasing case is symmetric). Then:
- $f(1)$ is the maximum value of $f$, since $1$ is the largest point in the doma
in.
- $\displaystyle L := \lim_{x\to 0^+} f(x)$ exists in $[-\infty, \infty)$ (as th
e infimum, by monotonicity), and is not attained (since $0\notin$ domain).

Hence $f\big((0,1]\big) = (L,\, f(1)]$, a half-open interval possessing a maximu
m element $f(1)$.

In the strictly decreasing case, similarly, $f\big((0,1]\big) = [f(1),\, L)$ wit
h minimum $f(1)$.

**Step 3 — Contradiction.** The set $\mathbb{R}$ has no maximum (and no minimum)
. But the image of $f$ is a half-open interval that *does* have a maximum (or mi
nimum) element, namely $f(1)$. Therefore $f\big((0,1]\big) \neq \mathbb{R}$, con
tradicting surjectivity.

Hence **no continuous bijection** $(0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ exists. $\square$

---

### Part B: A bijection $(0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ exists (explicit construction)

We construct one explicitly. The map
$$\varphi(x) = \tan\!\big(\pi(x-\tfrac12)\big), \qquad x\in(0,1),$$
is a bijection $(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}$.

To extend the domain to $(0,1] = (0,1)\cup\{1\}$, we first build a bijection $\s
igma:(0,1]\to(0,1)$ that "absorbs" the extra point $1$ by shifting a countable c
hain:
$$
\sigma(1) = \tfrac12,\quad \sigma(\tfrac12)=\tfrac13,\quad \sigma(\tfrac13)=\tfr
ac14,\quad \dots,\quad \sigma(\tfrac1n)=\tfrac1{n+1},\quad \dots
$$
and $\sigma(x)=x$ for all $x\in(0,1]\setminus\{1/n : n\in\mathbb{N}\}$.

This $\sigma$ is a bijection: it permutes the countable set $\{1, 1/2, 1/3,\dots
\}$ via the shift $1/n\mapsto 1/(n+1)$ (with $1\mapsto 1/2$), and fixes everythi
ng else. Its inverse shifts back: $\sigma^{-1}(1/(n+1))=1/n$ and $\sigma^{-1}(x)
=x$ off the chain.

Now define
$$f := \varphi \circ \sigma : (0,1] \longrightarrow \mathbb{R}.$$
As a composition of two bijections, $f$ is a **bijection**. $\square$

---

### Conclusion

From Part A, no bijection $(0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ can be continuous. From Part B, a
 bijection $(0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ exists. Therefore **every** bijection $(0,1]\to\
mathbb{R}$ is non-continuous, and in particular **there exists a non-continuous
bijection** between $(0,1]$ and $\mathbb{R}$.

The explicit map $f = \varphi\circ\sigma$ above is one such non-continuous bijec
tion (it is discontinuous at $x=1$, since $\lim_{x\to 1^-}f(x)=\varphi(1)=+\inft
y$ is not finite, while $f(1)=\varphi(1/2)=0$).
