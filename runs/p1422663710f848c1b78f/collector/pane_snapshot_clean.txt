The integrand is an odd function of $x$, so the integral vanishes (when it conve
rges). Here is the complete proof.

---

**Claim.** For real $t$ and positive, distinct $a_1,\dots,a_n$ (in fact any $a_k
\neq 0$) with $n\ge 2$,

$$
I(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{x^{4}\sin(tx)}{\prod_{k=1}^{n}(x^{2}+a_k^{2})}
\,dx = 0.
$$

**Proof.**

Set
$$
f(x)=\frac{x^{4}\sin(tx)}{\prod_{k=1}^{n}(x^{2}+a_k^{2})}.
$$

*Parity.* The factor $x^{4}$ is even in $x$. The factor $\sin(tx)$ is odd in $x$
 since $\sin(t(-x))=\sin(-tx)=-\sin(tx)$. Each factor $x^{2}+a_k^{2}$ is even, h
ence the denominator $D(x)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}(x^{2}+a_k^{2})$ is even. Therefore
$$
f(-x)=\frac{(-x)^{4}\sin(-tx)}{D(-x)}=\frac{x^{4}\bigl(-\sin(tx)\bigr)}{D(x)}=-f
(x),
$$
so $f$ is an **odd** function on $\mathbb{R}$.

*Convergence.* The integrand is continuous (the $a_k^{2}>0$ keep the denominator
 away from $0$ on $\mathbb{R}$). At infinity,
$$
|f(x)|\sim \frac{|x|^{4}\,|\sin(tx)|}{|x|^{2n}}\sim |x|^{4-2n}|\sin(tx)|.
$$
- For $n\ge 3$, $4-2n\le -2$, so $|f(x)|=O(|x|^{-2})$; the integral converges ab
solutely.
- For $n=2$, $f(x)\sim \sin(tx)$ up to a constant, and more precisely $f(x)=\fra
c{x^{4}\sin(tx)}{(x^{2}+a_1^{2})(x^{2}+a_2^{2})}$; after polynomial division $x^
{4}/\!\bigl[(x^{2}+a_1^{2})(x^{2}+a_2^{2})\bigr]=1-\frac{(a_1^{2}+a_2^{2})x^{2}+
a_1^{2}a_2^{2}}{(x^{2}+a_1^{2})(x^{2}+a_2^{2})}$, so $f(x)=\sin(tx)+O(|x|^{-2})$
. The $\sin(tx)$ term integrates to $0$ over $\mathbb{R}$ (Dirichlet), and the $
O(|x|^{-2})$ remainder is absolutely integrable. Hence the improper integral con
verges (conditionally).
- For $n=1$, $f(x)\sim x^{2}\sin(tx)$, which is not integrable at infinity; the
improper integral does not exist. (The problem is meaningful for $n\ge 2$.)

*Symmetry argument.* For $n\ge 2$ the improper integral exists. Being the integr
al of an odd function over the symmetric interval $(-R,R)$, the partial integral
 vanishes for every $R$:
$$
\int_{-R}^{R}f(x)\,dx=0.
$$
Taking the limit $R\to\infty$ (which exists by the convergence established above
),
$$
I(t)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{-R}^{R}f(x)\,dx=\lim_{R\to\infty}0=0.
$$

(Equivalently, one may close the contour in the upper half-plane for $\frac{x^{4
}e^{itx}}{\prod(x^{2}+a_k^{2})}$ and sum residues at $z=ia_k$; the imaginary par
t gives $I(t)$. The residue sum is real-symmetric and one checks it equals $0$ p
recisely because the numerator degree $4$ is even while the sine factor is odd —
 consistent with the parity argument above.)

Therefore, for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$ and $n\ge 2$,

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{x^{4}\sin(tx)}{\prod_{k=1}^{n}(
x^{2}+a_k^{2})}\,dx=0.}
$$
