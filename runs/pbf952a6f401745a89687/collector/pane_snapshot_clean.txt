**Answer: Yes, $BA = I$.**

**Proof.**

Recall that a Euclidean domain is, by definition, a *commutative* integral domai
n equipped with a Euclidean function. Since $R$ is commutative, the usual determ
inant theory for matrices over a commutative ring applies to $M_n(R)$.

**Step 1: Determinant is multiplicative.** For any $A, B \in M_n(R)$, since $R$
is commutative, the standard proof of multiplicativity of the determinant goes t
hrough unchanged:
$$\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B).$$

**Step 2: $\det(A)$ is a unit.** From $AB = I_n$, taking determinants:
$$\det(A)\det(B) = \det(I_n) = 1.$$
Thus $\det(A)$ has a multiplicative inverse $\det(B)$ in $R$; i.e., $\det(A) \in
 R^\times$ is a unit.

**Step 3: $A$ is invertible via the adjugate.** For any square matrix over a com
mutative ring, the classical adjugate identity holds:
$$A \cdot \operatorname{adj}(A) = \operatorname{adj}(A) \cdot A = \det(A) \cdot
I_n.$$
Since $\det(A)$ is a unit with inverse $\det(A)^{-1} = \det(B)$, set $C := \det(
A)^{-1}\operatorname{adj}(A)$. Then
$$A \cdot C = C \cdot A = I_n,$$
so $A$ is invertible (two-sided) with $A^{-1} = C$.

**Step 4: Conclude $BA = I_n$.** From $AB = I_n$, left-multiply by $A^{-1}$:
$$B = A^{-1}.$$
Therefore
$$BA = A^{-1}A = I_n. \qquad \blacksquare$$

**Remark.** The commutativity of $R$ is essential here — it is what makes the de
terminant multiplicative and the adjugate identity valid. Over a *non-commutativ
e* ring, a one-sided inverse of a matrix need not be a two-sided inverse. A Eucl
idean domain, however, is commutative by definition, so the obstruction vanishes
 and $AB = I_n$ forces $BA = I_n$.
