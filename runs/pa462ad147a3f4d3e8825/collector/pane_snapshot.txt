## Proof

We determine the values of $r$ for which $K(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(nx)
}{n^r}$ converges uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$.

### Sufficiency: $r>1$ gives uniform convergence

For every $x\in\mathbb{R}$ and every $n\ge 1$,
$$\left|\frac{\cos(nx)}{n^r}\right|\le \frac{1}{n^r}.$$
When $r>1$, the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^r}$ is a convergent $p$-se
ries ($p=r>1$). By the **Weierstrass $M$-test** (with $M_n = 1/n^r$), the series
 $K(x)$ converges uniformly (and absolutely) on $\mathbb{R}$.

### Necessity: $r\le 1$ fails (not even pointwise convergence on $\mathbb{R}$)

Consider $x=0$. Then $\cos(n\cdot 0)=\cos 0 = 1$ for every $n$, so
$$K(0)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^r}.$$
This is the harmonic/$p$-series, which **diverges** for $r\le 1$.

Therefore, for $r\le 1$, the series $K(x)$ does not even converge at $x=0$ (nor
at $x=2k\pi$ for any integer $k$), so it certainly cannot converge uniformly on
$\mathbb{R}$.

(For completeness: when $0<r\le 1$, the series does converge pointwise for every
 $x\not\equiv 0\pmod{2\pi}$ by Dirichlet's test, since the partial sums $\sum_{n
=1}^{N}\cos(nx)$ are bounded for such $x$ and $1/n^r\to 0$ monotonically. But th
e divergence at $x=0$ already rules out uniform convergence on all of $\mathbb{R
}$.)

### Conclusion

The series $K(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(nx)}{n^r}$ converges uniformly on
 $\mathbb{R}$ **if and only if** $r>1$.
