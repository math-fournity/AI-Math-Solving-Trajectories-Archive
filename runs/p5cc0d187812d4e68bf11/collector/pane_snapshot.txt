**Proof.** Yes, such a subfield exists.

**Step 1: A transcendence basis of R over Q is uncountable.**

By Zorn's lemma, the extension R/Q admits a transcendence basis T: a subset T ⊆
R, algebraically independent over Q, such that R is algebraic over Q(T).

Suppose, for contradiction, that T were countable. Then Q(T) — the field of rati
onal functions in the elements of T with rational coefficients — is countable, s
ince every element involves only finitely many elements of T, and a countable un
ion of countable sets is countable. Since R is algebraic over Q(T), every real n
umber is a root of a polynomial over Q(T); there are only countably many such po
lynomials, each with finitely many roots, so R would be countable. This contradi
cts the uncountability of R. Hence **T is uncountable**.

**Step 2: Construct the subfield.**

Since T is uncountable, we may pick an element t₀ ∈ T and set

$$S = T \setminus \{t_0\}.$$

Then S is still uncountable. Let

$$K = \mathbb{Q}(S) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$$

be the smallest subfield of R containing Q and S.

**Step 3: K is uncountable.**

Because S is algebraically independent over Q (being a subset of the algebraical
ly independent set T), the field Q(S) is isomorphic to the rational function fie
ld Q(x_s : s ∈ S). Its cardinality is

$$|\mathbb{Q}(S)| = \max(|\mathbb{Q}|, |S|) = |S|,$$

which is uncountable. Hence K is an uncountable subset of R.

**Step 4: K is a proper subfield of R.**

The element t₀ lies in T but not in S. Since T is algebraically independent over
 Q, t₀ is transcendental over Q(S) = K. In particular, t₀ ∉ K. But t₀ ∈ R, so

$$K \subsetneq \mathbb{R}.$$

**Conclusion.** K = Q(T \ {t₀}) is an uncountable subset of R, is a subfield of
R, and is a proper subfield of R. Therefore, an uncountable proper subfield of R
 exists. ∎
