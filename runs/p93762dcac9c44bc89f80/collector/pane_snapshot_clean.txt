# Solution

I need to determine which statement **must** hold for every infinite topological
 space $X$ with $X \cong X \times X$. I'll show each of (a), (b), (c) has a coun
terexample, so the answer is **(d)**.

## Counterexample to (a): $X = \mathbb{R}^\omega$ (product topology)

**$X \cong X \times X$:** We have $\mathbb{R}^\omega \times \mathbb{R}^\omega \c
ong \mathbb{R}^{\omega \sqcup \omega}$. Since $\omega \sqcup \omega \cong \omega
$ (a countable disjoint union of countable sets is countable), reindexing gives
$\mathbb{R}^{\omega \sqcup \omega} \cong \mathbb{R}^\omega$.

**$X$ is connected:** $\mathbb{R}^\omega$ is a product of connected spaces, henc
e connected. It is clearly infinite.

So (a) fails: $X$ is connected.

## Counterexample to (b): $X = $ Cantor set $C$

**$X \cong X \times X$:** The Cantor set $C$ is the unique (up to homeomorphism)
 nonempty, compact, perfect, totally disconnected, metrizable space. The product
 $C \times C$ is:
- **Nonempty, compact, metrizable:** product of compact metrizable spaces.
- **Perfect (no isolated points):** a basic open set in $C \times C$ is $U \time
s V$ with $U, V$ open in $C$; since $C$ has no isolated points, $U$ and $V$ each
 contain at least two points, so $U \times V$ contains at least four points—no i
solated points.
- **Totally disconnected:** the product of totally disconnected spaces is totall
y disconnected (the product of clopen rectangles forms a clopen basis separating
 points).

By uniqueness, $C \times C \cong C$. The Cantor set is compact and infinite.

So (b) fails: $X$ is compact.

## Counterexample to (c): $X = \mathbb{Q}$

**$X \cong X \times X$:** By Sierpiński's theorem, $\mathbb{Q}$ is the unique (u
p to homeomorphism) countable, metrizable space with no isolated points. The pro
duct $\mathbb{Q} \times \mathbb{Q}$ is:
- **Countable:** $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable, so $\mathbb{Q} \times \mathbb{Q}$ is
 countable.
- **Metrizable:** product of metrizable spaces.
- **No isolated points:** any basic open set $U \times V$ contains infinitely ma
ny rational pairs.

By uniqueness, $\mathbb{Q} \times \mathbb{Q} \cong \mathbb{Q}$. Since $\mathbb{Q
} \subset \mathbb{R}$, the space $\mathbb{Q}$ is homeomorphic to a subset of $\m
athbb{R}$ (namely itself), and it is infinite.

So (c) fails: $X$ is homeomorphic to a subset of $\mathbb{R}$.

## Conclusion

Each of (a), (b), (c) admits a counterexample among infinite spaces homeomorphic
 to their own square:

| Statement | Counterexample | Why it fails |
|-----------|---------------|--------------|
| (a) not connected | $\mathbb{R}^\omega$ | connected |
| (b) not compact | Cantor set $C$ | compact |
| (c) not embeddable in $\mathbb{R}$ | $\mathbb{Q}$ | $\mathbb{Q} \subset \mathb
b{R}$ |

Therefore none of (a), (b), (c) is necessarily true.

**The answer is (d) None of the above.**
