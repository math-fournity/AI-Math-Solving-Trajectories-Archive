## Proof

We seek all $u:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ for which there exists a strictly monoto
nic $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ satisfying
$$f(x+y)=f(x)\,u(y)+f(y),\qquad \forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}. \tag{$\ast$}$$

**Step 1. Determine $f(0)$ and $u(0)$.**

Set $x=0$ in $(\ast)$: $f(y)=f(0)\,u(y)+f(y)$, hence $f(0)\,u(y)=0$ for all $y$.
 If $f(0)\neq 0$, then $u\equiv 0$, and $(\ast)$ gives $f(x+y)=f(y)$ for all $x,
y$, making $f$ constant—contradicting strict monotonicity. Therefore
$$f(0)=0.$$

Set $y=0$ in $(\ast)$: $f(x)=f(x)\,u(0)+f(0)=f(x)\,u(0)$. Since $f$ is strictly
monotonic, $f$ is not identically zero, so there exists $x$ with $f(x)\neq 0$, g
iving
$$u(0)=1.$$

**Step 2. A key identity.**

Swapping the roles of $x$ and $y$ in $(\ast)$ (since $x+y=y+x$):
$$f(x)\,u(y)+f(y)=f(y)\,u(x)+f(x),$$
that is,
$$f(x)\bigl(u(y)-1\bigr)=f(y)\bigl(u(x)-1\bigr),\qquad \forall x,y. \tag{$\ast\a
st$}$$

**Step 3. Case A — $u\equiv 1$.**

If $u(x)=1$ for all $x$, then $(\ast)$ becomes Cauchy's equation $f(x+y)=f(x)+f(
y)$. A strictly monotonic additive function is continuous, hence $f(x)=kx$ for s
ome constant $k\neq 0$. This is indeed strictly monotonic, and $u\equiv 1 = e^{0
\cdot x}$ works.

**Step 4. Case B — $u$ is not identically $1$.**

Choose $a$ with $u(a)\neq 1$, so $f(a)\neq 0$ (by $(\ast\ast)$ with $y=a$: $f(x)
(u(a)-1)=f(a)(u(x)-1)$; if $f(a)=0$ then $u\equiv 1$, contradiction). From $(\as
t\ast)$ with $y=a$:
$$u(x)-1=\frac{u(a)-1}{f(a)}\,f(x)=c\,f(x),\qquad c:=\frac{u(a)-1}{f(a)}\neq 0.$
$
Thus $u(x)=1+c\,f(x)$, i.e. $f(x)=\dfrac{u(x)-1}{c}$.

Substitute $f(x)=\frac{u(x)-1}{c}$ back into $(\ast)$:
$$\frac{u(x+y)-1}{c}=\frac{u(x)-1}{c}\,u(y)+\frac{u(y)-1}{c}.$$
Multiplying by $c$:
$$u(x+y)-1=\bigl(u(x)-1\bigr)u(y)+u(y)-1=u(x)u(y)-1,$$
so
$$u(x+y)=u(x)\,u(y),\qquad \forall x,y. \tag{$\ast\ast\ast$}$$

Moreover, $u=1+cf$ with $f$ strictly monotonic and $c\neq 0$ is itself **strictl
y monotonic**.

**Step 5. Classify strictly monotonic multiplicative $u$.**

From $(\ast\ast\ast)$: $u(0)=u(0)^2$ so $u(0)=1$ (consistent). Also $u(x)=u(x/2)
^2\geq 0$, and $u(x)\neq 0$ since $1=u(0)=u(x)u(-x)$. Hence $u(x)>0$ for all $x$
. Define $h(x)=\ln u(x)$; then $h(x+y)=h(x)+h(y)$, and $h$ is strictly monotonic
 (since $u$ is and $\ln$ is increasing). A strictly monotonic additive function
is continuous, so $h(x)=\lambda x$ for some constant $\lambda\neq 0$ (nonzero be
cause $h$, hence $u$, is strictly monotonic). Therefore
$$u(x)=e^{\lambda x},\qquad \lambda\neq 0.$$

One verifies directly: with $f(x)=\frac{e^{\lambda x}-1}{c}$ (any $c\neq 0$),
$$f(x)u(y)+f(y)=\frac{e^{\lambda x}-1}{c}e^{\lambda y}+\frac{e^{\lambda y}-1}{c}
=\frac{e^{\lambda(x+y)}-1}{c}=f(x+y),$$
and $f$ is strictly monotonic since $\lambda c\neq 0$. ✓

**Step 6. Unification.**

Case A gives $u\equiv 1 = e^{0\cdot x}$ (with $\lambda=0$). Case B gives $u(x)=e
^{\lambda x}$ with $\lambda\neq 0$. Both are covered by $u(x)=e^{\lambda x}$, $\
lambda\in\mathbb{R}$, and each admits a strictly monotonic $f$:
- $\lambda=0$: $f(x)=kx$, $k\neq 0$;
- $\lambda\neq 0$: $f(x)=\frac{e^{\lambda x}-1}{c}$, $c\neq 0$.

Conversely, the analysis above shows no other $u$ is possible.

## Conclusion

The functions $u:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ for which there exists a strictly mono
tonic $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ satisfying $f(x+y)=f(x)u(y)+f(y)$ are exactly
$$\boxed{u(x)=e^{\lambda x}\text{ for some constant }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.}$$
