## Proof

We seek the largest integer $n$ such that $n$ equals the product of $(n-3)$ cons
ecutive positive integers.

**Setup.** Let $m = n - 3$ (the number of consecutive positive integers). We nee
d $m \geq 1$ (so $n \geq 4$), and we require
$$n = m + 3 = k(k+1)(k+2)\cdots(k+m-1)$$
for some positive integer $k \geq 1$.

**Key bound.** The smallest possible product of $m$ consecutive positive integer
s is $1 \cdot 2 \cdots m = m!$ (achieved at $k=1$). Any larger $k$ only increase
s the product. Therefore a necessary condition for a solution is
$$m! \leq m + 3.$$

**Checking all feasible $m$:**

- **$m = 1$ ($n = 4$):** Need $k = 4$. ✓ Since $4 = 4$ and $n - 3 = 1$. **Soluti
on: $n = 4$.**

- **$m = 2$ ($n = 5$):** Need $k(k+1) = 5$. Testing: $k=1 \Rightarrow 2$, $k=2 \
Rightarrow 6$. No integer $k$ gives 5. ✗

- **$m = 3$ ($n = 6$):** Need $k(k+1)(k+2) = 6$. Testing: $k=1 \Rightarrow 1\cdo
t2\cdot3 = 6$. ✓ **Solution: $n = 6$.**

- **$m = 4$ ($n = 7$):** $m! = 24 > 7 = m+3$. The bound fails, so no $k$ works.
✗

- **$m \geq 4$:** Since $4! = 24 > 7$ and $m!$ grows faster than $m + 3$ (each i
ncrement of $m$ multiplies the left side by $m+1$ while adding only $1$ to the r
ight side), we have $m! > m + 3$ for all $m \geq 4$. **No solutions exist for $n
 \geq 7$.**

**Conclusion.** The only valid keys are $n = 4$ and $n = 6$. The maximal $n$ is:

$$\boxed{6}$$

Verification: $6 = 1 \times 2 \times 3$ (three consecutive positive integers), a
nd $6 - 3 = 3$. ✓
