## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, $f(t) \equiv 0$ on $I$.

**Proof by contradiction.** Suppose $f$ is not identically zero. Then there exis
ts a point $c \in (t_0, t_1)$ with $f(c) \neq 0$. Without loss of generality, as
sume $f(c) > 0$ (otherwise replace $f$ by $-f$, which does not affect the hypoth
esis since it holds for all $g$).

Since $f$ is continuous (being $C^k$ with $k \ge 2$), there exists a closed subi
nterval $[a, b] \subset (t_0, t_1)$ with $a < b$ and $c \in [a,b]$ such that $f(
t) > 0$ for all $t \in [a, b]$.

**Constructing a test function.** Define
$$
\varphi(t) = \begin{cases}
(t-a)^{k+1}(b-t)^{k+1}, & t \in [a, b], \\
0, & t \notin [a, b].
\end{cases}
$$
At $t = a$, the factor $(t-a)^{k+1}$ ensures that $\varphi$ and its derivatives
up to order $k$ all vanish (since the $(k+1)$-fold zero of $(t-a)^{k+1}$ kills d
erivatives up to order $k$). The same holds at $t = b$ via $(b-t)^{k+1}$. Hence
$\varphi \in C^k(\mathbb{R})$, and in particular $\varphi \in C^k(I)$.

Moreover, $\varphi(t_0) = \varphi(t_1) = 0$ since $[a,b] \subset (t_0,t_1)$, so
$\varphi$ vanishes identically near both endpoints $t_0, t_1$.

Also $\varphi(t) > 0$ for all $t \in (a, b)$, and $\varphi(t) \ge 0$ on $[a,b]$.

**Deriving the contradiction.** By hypothesis (applied to $g = \varphi$),
$$
\int_{t_0}^{t_1} f(t)\,\varphi(t)\,dt = 0.
$$
But the integrand satisfies $f(t)\varphi(t) > 0$ on $(a,b)$ (both factors strict
ly positive there) and $f(t)\varphi(t) \ge 0$ everywhere on $I$. The integral of
 a nonnegative, continuous function that is strictly positive on a nondegenerate
 interval is strictly positive:
$$
\int_{t_0}^{t_1} f(t)\,\varphi(t)\,dt = \int_a^b f(t)\,\varphi(t)\,dt > 0.
$$
This contradicts the hypothesis that the integral equals zero.

Therefore our assumption was false, and $f(t) \equiv 0$ on $I$. $\blacksquare$
