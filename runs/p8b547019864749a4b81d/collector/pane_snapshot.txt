## Direct Limit of $\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}$

**Setup.** Index the direct system by the directed set $(\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}, \m
id)$, where $m \preceq n \iff m \mid n$. For $m \mid n$ we have $\frac{1}{m}\mat
hbb{Z} \subseteq \frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}$, so the bonding map $\varphi_{m,n} : \fr
ac{1}{m}\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}$ is the inclusion. Thes
e maps satisfy $\varphi_{n,n} = \mathrm{id}$ and $\varphi_{n,k} \circ \varphi_{m
,n} = \varphi_{m,k}$ for $m \mid n \mid k$, giving a valid direct system.

**Claim.** $\varinjlim \frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Q}$.

**Proof.** View each $\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}$ as a subgroup of $\mathbb{Q}$ via t
he usual embedding $\frac{k}{n} \mapsto \frac{k}{n} \in \mathbb{Q}$, so every bo
nding map is literally the inclusion of subgroups of $\mathbb{Q}$.

Define
$$\Phi : \varinjlim \frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z} \longrightarrow \mathbb{Q}, \qquad \Ph
i\bigl([x]_n\bigr) = x \in \mathbb{Q},$$
where $[x]_n$ denotes the colimit class of $x \in \frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}$.

*Well-defined.* If $[x]_m = [y]_n$ in the colimit, then by construction of $\var
injlim$ there exists $k$ with $m \mid k$ and $n \mid k$ such that the two inclus
ions send $x$ and $y$ to the same element of $\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{Z}$. Since the
inclusions are the identity on the underlying rationals, this says $x = y$ in $\
frac{1}{k}\mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q}$, hence $x = y$ in $\mathbb{Q}$. So $\Ph
i$ is independent of the representative.

*Homomorphism.* For $[x]_n, [y]_n$ (common index after refinement), $\Phi([x]_n
+ [y]_n) = \Phi([x+y]_n) = x + y = \Phi([x]_n) + \Phi([y]_n)$.

*Surjective.* Every rational $q = \frac{a}{b}$ with $a \in \mathbb{Z}$, $b \geq
1$ lies in $\frac{1}{b}\mathbb{Z}$, so $q = \Phi([q]_b)$.

*Injective.* If $\Phi([x]_n) = 0$, then $x = 0$ as a rational, hence $x = 0 \in
\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}$, so $[x]_n = [0]_n = 0$ in the colimit. Thus $\ker \Phi =
 \{0\}$.

Therefore $\Phi$ is a group isomorphism, and

$$\boxed{\varinjlim \frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z} \;\cong\; \mathbb{Q}.}$$

**Remark.** Intuitively, the direct limit identifies each $\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}
$ with its copy inside $\mathbb{Q}$ and "glues" them along their common inclusio
ns; the union $\bigcup_{n \geq 1} \frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}$ inside $\mathbb{Q}$ is
exactly $\mathbb{Q}$, since every rational has some denominator. The colimit uni
versal property guarantees this union, equipped with the induced group structure
, is the direct limit.
