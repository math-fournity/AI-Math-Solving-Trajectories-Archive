## Proof

**Claim.** A polynomial $f(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ satisfies $f(p) \equiv 0 \pmod{
p}$ for every prime $p$ if and only if $f(0)=0$ (equivalently, $x \mid f(x)$). I
n particular, such polynomials **do exist** — for example $f(x)=x$ — and they ar
e *exactly* the integer polynomials divisible by $x$.

---

**Necessity ($\Rightarrow$).** Suppose $f(p)\equiv 0\pmod p$ for all primes $p$.
 Write
$$f(x)=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2+\cdots+a_dx^d,\qquad a_i\in\mathbb Z.$$
Fix any prime $p$. Evaluating at $x=p$ and reducing modulo $p$, every term $a_i
p^i$ with $i\ge 1$ is divisible by $p$, hence vanishes mod $p$:
$$f(p)\equiv a_0 \pmod p.$$
The hypothesis $f(p)\equiv 0\pmod p$ therefore forces
$$a_0\equiv 0\pmod p$$
for **every** prime $p$. But $a_0$ is a single fixed integer; the only integer d
ivisible by all primes simultaneously is $0$. Hence $a_0=0$, i.e. $f(0)=0$.

**Sufficiency ($\Leftarrow$).** If $f(0)=0$, then $f(x)=x\,g(x)$ for some $g(x)\
in\mathbb Z[x]$. For any prime $p$,
$$f(p)=p\,g(p),$$
which is manifestly divisible by $p$, so $f(p)\equiv 0\pmod p$.

---

**Conclusion.** The condition $f(p)\equiv 0\pmod p$ for all primes $p$ is satisf
ied precisely by the polynomials in $\mathbb Z[x]$ that vanish at the origin. Su
ch polynomials certainly exist — $f(x)=x$ being the simplest nontrivial example
— and every solution is a multiple of $x$. $\blacksquare$
