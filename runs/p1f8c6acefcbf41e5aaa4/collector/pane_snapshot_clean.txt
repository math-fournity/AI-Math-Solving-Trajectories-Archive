## Quadratic Variation of $M_t = \int_0^t \mathbb{1}_{\{B_s=0\}}\, dB_s$

**Answer-leak self-check.** The problem is phrased as a standard "show that" exe
rcise (state the identity, then prove it). No proof sketch, solution, or Lean fo
rmalization is embedded. Proceeding to solve.

---

### Step 1. Well-definedness of the stochastic integral

The integrand $H_s := \mathbb{1}_{\{B_s = 0\}}$ is a progressive (hence predicta
ble) process, since it is a Borel function of the progressive process $B$. Moreo
ver

$$
\int_0^t H_s^2\, ds = \int_0^t \mathbb{1}_{\{B_s=0\}}\, ds \le t < \infty \quad
\text{a.s.},
$$

so $M_t = \int_0^t H_s\, dB_s$ is a well-defined continuous local martingale (in
 fact a square-integrable martingale on every finite interval, since the integra
nd is bounded).

---

### Step 2. Quadratic variation of a stochastic integral

By the fundamental theorem on the quadratic variation of Itô integrals (see e.g.
 Revuz–Yor, Ch. IV, Prop. 1.5): for any predictable process $H$ with $\int_0^t H
_s^2\, ds < \infty$ a.s., the continuous local martingale $M_t = \int_0^t H_s\,
dB_s$ has quadratic variation

$$
\langle M \rangle_t = \int_0^t H_s^2\, ds.
$$

Applying this with $H_s = \mathbb{1}_{\{B_s=0\}}$ and using $H_s^2 = H_s$ (indic
ator is idempotent):

$$
\boxed{\;\langle M \rangle_t = \int_0^t \mathbb{1}_{\{B_s=0\}}\, ds.\;}
$$

---

### Step 3. The occupation-time formula forces the integral to vanish

We now show $\int_0^t \mathbb{1}_{\{B_s=0\}}\, ds = 0$ a.s.

The **occupation-times formula** (Revuz–Yor, Ch. VI, §1) states that for Brownia
n motion, with $L_t^a$ denoting the local time at level $a$:

$$
\int_0^t f(B_s)\, ds = \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(a)\, L_t^a\, da
$$

for every non-negative Borel function $f$, a.s.

Take $f = \mathbb{1}_{\{0\}}$, the Dirac indicator at the origin. Then

$$
\int_0^t \mathbb{1}_{\{B_s=0\}}\, ds = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{1}_{\{0\}}(a)\,
 L_t^a\, da.
$$

The right-hand side is the Lebesgue integral of the function $a \mapsto \mathbb{
1}_{\{0\}}(a)\, L_t^a$ over $\mathbb{R}$. Since $\mathbb{1}_{\{0\}}(a) = 0$ for
Lebesgue-almost-every $a$ (the singleton $\{0\}$ has Lebesgue measure $0$), the
integrand vanishes a.e., and therefore

$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{1}_{\{0\}}(a)\, L_t^a\, da = 0 \quad \text{a.s.}
$$

Hence

$$
\int_0^t \mathbb{1}_{\{B_s=0\}}\, ds = 0 \quad \text{a.s.}
$$

(Equivalently: the zero set $\{s \in [0,t] : B_s = 0\}$ has Lebesgue measure $0$
 a.s., which is the classical fact that Brownian motion spends zero Lebesgue tim
e at any fixed point.)

---

### Step 4. Conclusion

Combining Steps 2 and 3:

$$
\langle M \rangle_t = \int_0^t \mathbb{1}_{\{B_s=0\}}\, ds = 0 \quad \text{a.s.
for every } t \ge 0.
$$

Since the quadratic variation of the continuous local martingale $M$ is identica
lly zero, $M$ is a.s. constant; as $M_0 = 0$, we obtain $M_t \equiv 0$ a.s. In p
articular, the stochastic integral $\int_0^t \mathbb{1}_{\{B_s=0\}}\, dB_s$ is i
ndistinguishable from the zero process — a manifestation of the fact that Browni
an motion "does not charge" the instant it spends at the origin in the Itô sense
.
