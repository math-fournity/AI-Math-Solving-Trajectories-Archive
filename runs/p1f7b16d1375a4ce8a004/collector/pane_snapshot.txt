## Proof

The statement is **true**. The key tool is the Frobenius endomorphism (Freshman'
s dream) in $\mathbb{F}_p[[q]]$.

**Setup.** Define
$$f(q) = \prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1-q^{4n+1}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a(n)\, q
^n \in \mathbb{Z}[[q]],$$
where $a(n)$ counts the number of partitions of $n$ into parts congruent to $1 \
pmod{4}$. Then the left-hand side of the given identity is
$$\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-q^{4n+1})^p} = f(q)^p = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \p
hi(n)\, q^n,$$
with $\phi(0)=1$.

**The Freshman's Dream in $\mathbb{F}_p[[q]]$.** We work modulo $p$, i.e., in $\
mathbb{F}_p[[q]]$. For any formal power series $g(q) = \sum_{n \geq 0} b_n q^n \
in \mathbb{F}_p[[q]]$, we claim:
$$g(q)^p = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} b_n^p\, q^{pn} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} b_n\, q^{pn}
.$$

*Justification.* The first equality follows by iterating the Frobenius identity
$(A+B)^p = A^p + B^p$ in characteristic $p$ (which holds because $\binom{p}{k} \
equiv 0 \pmod{p}$ for $0 < k < p$). Formally, for a partial sum $g_N(q) = \sum_{
n=0}^{N} b_n q^n$, we have $g_N(q)^p = \sum_{n=0}^{N} b_n^p q^{pn}$ in $\mathbb{
F}_p[[q]]$, and taking $N \to \infty$ preserves the equality since the coefficie
nt of any fixed $q^m$ stabilizes. The second equality is Fermat's Little Theorem
: $b_n^p = b_n$ for every $b_n \in \mathbb{F}_p$. $\square$

**Applying to $f(q)$.** Reducing $f(q)$ modulo $p$ and applying the Freshman's d
ream:
$$f(q)^p \equiv \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a(n)\, q^{pn} \pmod{p}.$$

The right-hand side is a power series in $q^p$: it contains **only** terms $q^{p
n}$, i.e., powers of $q$ that are multiples of $p$.

**Extracting coefficients.** Writing $f(q)^p = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \phi(m)\, q^m
$ and comparing coefficients modulo $p$:

- If $p \nmid m$: the coefficient of $q^m$ on the right side is $0$, so $\phi(m)
 \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$.
- If $m = pn$: the coefficient of $q^{pn}$ on the right side is $a(n)$, so $\phi
(pn) \equiv a(n) \pmod{p}$.

**Conclusion.** For every natural number $n$ with $p \nmid n$, we have $\phi(n)
\equiv 0 \pmod{p}$. This is precisely the claimed statement: the congruence $\ph
i(n) \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$ holds for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$ except at multiples of
$p$.

**Remark.** The congruence genuinely fails at some multiples of $p$: for instanc
e, $a(1) = 1$ (the unique partition of $1$ into parts $\equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ is $\{
1\}$), so $\phi(p) \equiv a(1) = 1 \not\equiv 0 \pmod{p}$. This confirms the "ex
cept at multiples of $p$" clause is necessary.

**Remark on generality.** The specific choice of parts $\equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ plays
 no role in the proof; the argument applies to $\prod_{n}(1-q^{a_n})^{-p}$ for a
ny sequence $a_n \to \infty$ of positive integers. The entire phenomenon is a di
rect consequence of the Frobenius endomorphism in characteristic $p$.
