## Solution

**Answer: No.** The non-invertibility of $Df(x)$ does **not** imply that $f$ fai
ls to be locally invertible at $x$. The Inverse Function Theorem provides a *suf
ficient* condition (invertible derivative $\Rightarrow$ local invertibility), bu
t its converse is false.

### Counterexample

Take $X = Y = \mathbb{R}$ (a Banach space), $U = \mathbb{R}$, and
$$f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}, \qquad f(x) = x^3.$$

**$f$ is continuously differentiable.** Indeed $f'(x) = 3x^2$, which is continuo
us, so $f \in C^1(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R})$.

**$Df(0)$ is not invertible.** We have $Df(0) = f'(0) = 0$, the zero linear map
$\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$. This is not invertible (it is not bijective).

**$f$ is locally invertible at $0$.** The map $f(x) = x^3$ is a bijection $\math
bb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, with inverse
$$g(y) = y^{1/3},$$
which is continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$. In particular, restricted to any nei
ghborhood of $0$, $f$ is a homeomorphism onto its image. Hence $f$ is locally in
vertible at $x = 0$ (in the topological sense: there exists an open neighborhood
 $V$ of $0$ such that $f|_V$ is a homeomorphism onto an open set).

Thus at $x = 0$ we have simultaneously:
- $Df(0)$ is **not** invertible, yet
- $f$ **is** locally invertible at $0$.

### Justification / Discussion

The Inverse Function Theorem for Banach spaces states:

> *If $Df(x)$ is a bounded invertible linear isomorphism $X \to Y$, then $f$ is
locally invertible at $x$, and the local inverse is $C^1$ with derivative $Df(x)
^{-1}$ at $f(x)$.*

This is a one-way implication. The converse would read: *"$f$ locally invertible
 at $x$ $\Rightarrow$ $Df(x)$ invertible."* The counterexample above refutes thi
s converse.

**Key distinction.** If one strengthens "locally invertible" to "locally inverti
ble with a *Fréchet differentiable* inverse," then the converse **does** hold: b
y the chain rule applied to $g \circ f = \mathrm{id}_U$ and $f \circ g = \mathrm
{id}_{f(V)}$, one obtains
$$Dg(f(x)) \circ Df(x) = I_X, \qquad Df(x) \circ Dg(f(x)) = I_Y,$$
so $Df(x)$ is invertible with inverse $Dg(f(x))$. The counterexample exploits pr
ecisely the gap between *continuous* invertibility and *differentiable* invertib
ility: $g(y) = y^{1/3}$ is continuous but not differentiable at $y = 0$ (its der
ivative blows up), so the chain-rule argument cannot be applied.

### Conclusion

The non-invertibility of $Df(x)$ does not, in general, imply that $f$ is not loc
ally invertible at $x$. The map $f(x) = x^3$ on $\mathbb{R}$ provides an explici
t counterexample: $Df(0) = 0$ is non-invertible, yet $f$ is a homeomorphism (hen
ce locally invertible) at $0$. The Inverse Function Theorem's hypothesis is suff
icient but not necessary for local invertibility; it becomes necessary only if o
ne additionally requires the local inverse to be differentiable.
