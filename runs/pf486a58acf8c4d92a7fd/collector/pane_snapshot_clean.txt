## Answer

**No**, the uniform convergence of continuous (but non-differentiable) functions
 $f_n$ to $f$ on $\mathbb{C}$ does **not** guarantee the existence of $f'$.

### Proof

We exhibit an explicit counterexample.

Let $W:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}$ be the **Weierstrass function** (extended to $\m
athbb{C}$ by, e.g., $W(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a^k\cos(b^k\pi\,\Re(z))$ for $z=x+i
y$, depending only on $x=\Re(z)$, with $0<a<1$, $b$ an odd integer, and $ab>1+\t
frac{3\pi}{2}$). It is classical that:

- $W$ is **continuous** on $\mathbb{R}$ (hence on $\mathbb{C}$, being constant i
n $y$ and continuous in $x$),
- $W$ is **nowhere differentiable**, so $W'$ does not exist at any point.

Now define the sequence
$$f_n := W \quad\text{for every } n\ge 1.$$

Then:

1. **Each $f_n$ is continuous.** ✓ (since $W$ is continuous)
2. **$f_n'$ does not exist** for every $n$. ✓ (since $W$ is nowhere differentiab
le)
3. **$f_n \to f$ uniformly**, with $f = W$. ✓ (the sequence is constant, so $\su
p_{z\in\mathbb{C}}|f_n(z)-f(z)|=0$ for every $n$; convergence is trivially unifo
rm)
4. **$f' = W'$ does not exist** at any point of $\mathbb{C}$. ✓

Thus we have a sequence of continuous, non-differentiable functions converging u
niformly to a limit $f$, yet **$f'$ does not exist** (in fact, $f$ is nowhere di
fferentiable).

### Justification of the underlying principle

Uniform convergence preserves **continuity** (the uniform limit of continuous fu
nctions is continuous), but it does **not** preserve or create **differentiabili
ty**. Differentiability is a "first-order" (local linear-approximation) property
 requiring control of difference quotients, which uniform convergence of functio
n values alone cannot guarantee. The hypotheses of the problem give only:

- continuity of each $f_n$, and
- uniform convergence $f_n\rightrightarrows f$,

which suffice to conclude $f$ is continuous, but say nothing about the existence
 of the limit
$$f'(z_0)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(z_0+h)-f(z_0)}{h}.$$

The counterexample above shows this limit can fail to exist at every point even
when all the stated hypotheses hold. Hence the answer is **no**.
