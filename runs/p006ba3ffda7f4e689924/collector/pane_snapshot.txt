## Proof of `isGood_example`

**Theorem.** The set $A = \{p^2 : p \text{ is prime},\; p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}\}$ i
s *good*, meaning:

1. $A$ is infinite, and
2. there are no distinct $a, b, c \in A$ with $a \mid (b+c)$, $b > a$, $c > a$.

---

### Part 1: $A$ is infinite

We use the classical result that infinitely many primes $p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$ ex
ist.

**Proof.** Suppose for contradiction that $p_1, \dots, p_k$ are all primes $\equ
iv 3 \pmod{4}$. Set
$$N = 4\,p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k - 1.$$
Then $N \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$ and $N$ is odd with $N > 1$. Let $q$ be any prime div
isor of $N$. Then $q \neq 2$ (since $N$ is odd) and $q \neq p_i$ for every $i$ (
since $N \equiv -1 \pmod{p_i}$, so $p_i \nmid N$).

Now $N \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$. Every prime $\equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ is $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$
, and a product of numbers each $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$ is itself $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$.
 Since $N$ is odd, all its prime factors are odd. If every prime factor of $N$ w
ere $\equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, then $N \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, contradicting $N \equiv 3 \
pmod{4}$. Therefore $N$ has at least one prime factor $q \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$, and
 $q \notin \{p_1, \dots, p_k\}$ — contradiction. $\square$

Since there are infinitely many primes $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$, the set $A = \{p^2 :
p \equiv 3\pmod{4},\; p \text{ prime}\}$ is infinite (the map $p \mapsto p^2$ is
 injective on primes).

### Part 2: No forbidden triple exists

**Key Lemma.** *If $p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$ is prime and $p \mid x^2 + y^2$, then $
p \mid x$ and $p \mid y$.*

**Proof of Lemma.** Suppose $p \nmid y$. Then $\gcd(y, p) = 1$, so $y$ has a mul
tiplicative inverse $y^{-1}$ modulo $p$. From $p \mid x^2 + y^2$ we get $x^2 \eq
uiv -y^2 \pmod{p}$, hence
$$(x\,y^{-1})^2 \equiv -1 \pmod{p}.$$
This means $-1$ is a quadratic residue modulo $p$. By **Euler's criterion**, $-1
$ is a quadratic residue mod $p$ if and only if $p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$. (Indeed,
$(-1)^{(p-1)/2} \equiv \left(\frac{-1}{p}\right) \pmod{p}$ by Euler's criterion,
 and $(-1)^{(p-1)/2} = 1$ iff $(p-1)/2$ is even, iff $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.) Sinc
e $p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$, this is a contradiction. Therefore $p \mid y$. By symme
try (swapping $x$ and $y$), $p \mid x$. $\square$

**Main argument.** Suppose for contradiction that there exist distinct $a, b, c
\in A$ with $a \mid (b+c)$, $b > a$, $c > a$.

Write $a = p^2$, $b = q^2$, $c = r^2$ where $p, q, r$ are primes with $p \equiv
q \equiv r \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$.

From $a \mid (b + c)$, i.e., $p^2 \mid q^2 + r^2$, we deduce in particular $p \m
id q^2 + r^2$.

Applying the Key Lemma with $x = q$ and $y = r$ (and the prime $p \equiv 3 \pmod
 4$): we conclude $p \mid q$ and $p \mid r$.

Since $q$ is prime, $p \mid q$ forces $p = q$. Since $r$ is prime, $p \mid r$ fo
rces $p = r$. Therefore $p = q = r$, which gives $a = b = c = p^2$.

But this contradicts the hypothesis that $a, b, c$ are **distinct**. Therefore n
o such triple exists. $\square$
