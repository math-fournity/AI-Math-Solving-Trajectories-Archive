## Solution

We evaluate
$$J = \int_0^{2\pi} e^{e^{-i\theta}}\,d\theta.$$

**Step 1: Power-series expansion.**
Using the Taylor series of the exponential, $e^z = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{z^n
}{n!}$, with $z = e^{-i\theta}$:
$$e^{e^{-i\theta}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(e^{-i\theta})^n}{n!} = \sum_{n=0
}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-in\theta}}{n!}.$$
This series converges absolutely and uniformly in $\theta$ (since $\sum 1/n!$ co
nverges), so we may integrate term by term.

**Step 2: Term-by-term integration.**
$$J = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n!}\int_0^{2\pi} e^{-in\theta}\,d\theta.$$

For $n = 0$:
$$\int_0^{2\pi} e^{0}\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} 1\,d\theta = 2\pi.$$

For $n \geq 1$:
$$\int_0^{2\pi} e^{-in\theta}\,d\theta = \left[\frac{e^{-in\theta}}{-in}\right]_
0^{2\pi} = \frac{e^{-2\pi i n}-1}{-in} = \frac{1-1}{-in} = 0,$$
since $e^{-2\pi i n} = 1$ for every integer $n$.

**Step 3: Conclusion.**
Every term with $n \geq 1$ vanishes; only the $n=0$ term survives:
$$J = \frac{1}{0!}\cdot 2\pi + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\cdot 0 = 2\pi.$$

**Sanity check (real-part interpretation).** Writing $e^{-i\theta}=\cos\theta -
i\sin\theta$,
$$e^{e^{-i\theta}} = e^{\cos\theta}\bigl(\cos(\sin\theta) - i\sin(\sin\theta)\bi
gr).$$
The imaginary part $-e^{\cos\theta}\sin(\sin\theta)$ is an odd function about $\
theta=\pi$, so its integral over $[0,2\pi]$ is $0$. The real part $e^{\cos\theta
}\cos(\sin\theta)$ is the real part of $e^{e^{-i\theta}}$, whose Fourier expansi
on has constant term $1/0!=1$; hence its integral is $2\pi\cdot 1 = 2\pi$. This
confirms $J = 2\pi$ (real).

$$\boxed{J = 2\pi}$$
