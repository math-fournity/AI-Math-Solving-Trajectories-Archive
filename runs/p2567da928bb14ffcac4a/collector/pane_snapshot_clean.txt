## Proof

**Answer: Yes, it is possible.**

**Construction.** Let $E = (0, 1)$, so $m(E) = 1 > 0$. Define the non-negative m
easurable function
$$f(x) = \frac{1}{x}, \quad x \in (0,1).$$
Define the sequence of measurable subsets
$$E_n = \left(e^{-n},\; 2e^{-n}\right), \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

**Verification of all required properties.**

**1. Each $E_n$ is a measurable subset of $E$.** For $n \geq 1$, we have $2e^{-n
} \leq 2e^{-1} < 1$, so $E_n \subset (0,1) = E$. Each $E_n$ is an open interval,
 hence Lebesgue measurable.

**2. $m(E_n) \to 0$.** We have $m(E_n) = 2e^{-n} - e^{-n} = e^{-n} \to 0$ as $n
\to \infty$.

**3. $\int_E f = +\infty$.** Since $f(x) = 1/x$ is non-negative and measurable o
n $(0,1)$,
$$\int_E f\,dm = \int_0^1 \frac{1}{x}\,dx = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0^+} \int_\var
epsilon^1 \frac{1}{x}\,dx = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0^+} \bigl(-\ln \varepsilon\bi
gr) = +\infty.$$

**4. $\lim_{n\to\infty} \int_{E_n} f \neq 0$.** For every $n \geq 1$,
$$\int_{E_n} f\,dm = \int_{e^{-n}}^{2e^{-n}} \frac{1}{x}\,dx = \ln\!\left(2e^{-n
}\right) - \ln\!\left(e^{-n}\right) = \ln 2.$$
Therefore,
$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \int_{E_n} f\,dm = \ln 2 \neq 0.$$

**Why this works — the role of non-integrability.** The key principle is the *ab
solute continuity of the Lebesgue integral*: if $g \geq 0$ is integrable (i.e.,
$\int_E g\,dm < \infty$), then for every sequence of measurable sets $A_n \subse
t E$ with $m(A_n) \to 0$, we necessarily have $\int_{A_n} g\,dm \to 0$. This is
a standard consequence of the fact that the measure $\nu(A) = \int_A g\,dm$ is a
bsolutely continuous with respect to $m$ and is a finite measure, hence satisfie
s the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ continuity property.

The construction above *escapes* this constraint precisely because $f$ is **not*
* integrable: $\int_E f\,dm = +\infty$. The function $1/x$ concentrates its mass
 near $x = 0$, and the sets $E_n = (e^{-n}, 2e^{-n})$ are chosen to "ride" this
singularity — each set sits in a region where $f$ is large enough (of order $e^n
$) that the integral over a set of measure $e^{-n}$ remains constant at $\ln 2$.
 The product "value of $f$ × measure of $E_n$" stays bounded away from zero even
 as $m(E_n) \to 0$, because $f$ grows exactly fast enough to compensate.

This demonstrates that the conclusion of absolute continuity **fails** for non-i
ntegrable non-negative functions: there exist sets of arbitrarily small measure
on which the integral remains bounded away from zero (or even equals $+\infty$).
