Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether there exists a model of Hil
bert's incidence axioms in which every line intersects at least one plane.

**Answer: Yes.** In fact, the axioms *force* every line to lie in a plane, so th
is holds in every model — not just some.

---

## Proof

We prove that in **any** model satisfying Hilbert's incidence axioms, every line
 is contained in (and hence intersects) at least one plane. Since a model exists
 (e.g., the four-point tetrahedron model, or ordinary Euclidean 3-space), this i
mmediately establishes that such a geometric figure can be constructed.

**Setup.** Let $\mathcal{M}$ be any model of Hilbert's incidence axioms, and let
 $\ell$ be an arbitrary line in $\mathcal{M}$.

**Step 1: $\ell$ contains at least two distinct points.**
By the incidence axiom stating that every line contains at least two points, the
re exist two distinct points $A, B$ on $\ell$.

**Step 2: There exists a point $C$ not on $\ell$.**
By the existence axiom, there exist at least three points not all lying on the s
ame line. Call them $P, Q, R$. These three points are non-collinear, so they can
not all lie on $\ell$ (otherwise they would be collinear). Hence at least one of
 them — call it $C$ — does not lie on $\ell$.

**Step 3: $A, B, C$ are non-collinear, so they determine a plane.**
Since $C \notin \ell$ and $\ell$ is the (unique) line through $A$ and $B$, the t
hree points $A, B, C$ are not all on the same line. By the plane-existence axiom
, there exists a plane $\pi$ containing $A$, $B$, and $C$.

**Step 4: The entire line $\ell$ lies in $\pi$.**
By the incidence axiom (often numbered I.5): *if two distinct points of a line l
ie in a plane, then every point of that line lies in the plane.* Since $A$ and $
B$ are two distinct points of $\ell$ and both lie in $\pi$, we conclude $\ell \s
ubseteq \pi$.

**Conclusion.** The line $\ell$ is contained in the plane $\pi$, so $\ell$ inter
sects $\pi$ (in fact $\ell \cap \pi = \ell$). Since $\ell$ was arbitrary, **ever
y line in $\mathcal{M}$ intersects at least one plane**. ∎

---

**Remark.** The proof uses only four of the incidence axioms:
1. Every line has at least two points.
2. There exist at least three non-collinear points.
3. Three non-collinear points determine a plane.
4. If two points of a line lie in a plane, the whole line lies in that plane.

Notably, the axiom requiring four non-coplanar points (I.7) is not needed. The c
onclusion is a theorem of the incidence axioms, not merely a possibility — it ho
lds in *every* model, including the minimal four-point model (tetrahedron) where
 each of the 6 lines (edges) lies in 2 of the 4 planes (faces).
