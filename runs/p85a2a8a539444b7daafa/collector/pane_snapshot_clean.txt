## Proof

Let $\phi \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$ be a Schwartz function. We must show

$$
I := \int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{i\lambda x}\,\phi(x)\,dx\,d\lambda =
\phi(0).
$$

The inner integral is not absolutely integrable in $\lambda$, so we interpret th
e outer integral as an improper (symmetric) limit. Define

$$
I_A := \int_{-A}^{A}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{i\lambda x}\,\phi(x)\,dx\ri
ght)d\lambda.
$$

Since $\phi$ is Schwartz, $\phi \in L^1$, so Fubini's theorem applies to the fin
ite strip $[-A,A]\times\mathbb{R}$ (the integrand is bounded by $|\phi(x)|$, whi
ch is integrable). Thus

$$
I_A = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(x)\left(\int_{-A}^{A} e^{i\lambda x}\,d\lambda
\right)dx.
$$

The inner integral evaluates explicitly:

$$
\int_{-A}^{A} e^{i\lambda x}\,d\lambda = \frac{e^{iAx}-e^{-iAx}}{ix} = \frac{2\s
in(Ax)}{x}, \qquad (x\neq 0),
$$

and equals $2A$ at $x=0$ (the limit). Hence

$$
I_A = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi(x)\,\frac{2\sin(Ax)}{x}\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^
{\infty} \phi(x)\,D_A(x)\,dx,
$$

where $D_A(x) = \frac{2\sin(Ax)}{x}$ is (twice) the Dirichlet kernel on $\mathbb
{R}$.

**Step 1: $D_A$ is an approximate identity (up to normalization).**

We have $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} D_A(x)\,dx = 2\pi$ for all $A>0$ (the classical
 Dirichlet integral: $\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin(Ax)}{x}dx = \frac{\pi}{2}\operato
rname{sgn}(A)$, so the full integral is $2\pi$). Equivalently, setting $u = Ax$,

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{2\sin(Ax)}{x}\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{2\
sin u}{u}\,du = 2\pi.
$$

**Step 2: Reduction to $\phi(0)$.**

Using the normalization $2\pi$, write

$$
I_A = 2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(x)\,\frac{D_A(x)}{2\pi}\,dx.
$$

We want to show $I_A \to 2\pi\,\phi(0)$ as $A\to\infty$... but let us instead di
rectly compute. Write

$$
I_A - 2\pi\,\phi(0) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigl(\phi(x)-\phi(0)\bigr)\frac{2\
sin(Ax)}{x}\,dx,
$$

using $\int D_A = 2\pi$. Split the integral into $|x|\le \eta$ and $|x|>\eta$.

*On $|x|\le\eta$:* Since $\phi$ is continuous, $|\phi(x)-\phi(0)|\le \varepsilon
$ for $\eta$ small. Also $\left|\frac{2\sin(Ax)}{x}\right|\le 2A$, but more usef
ully, $\left|\frac{\sin(Ax)}{x}\right|\le A$; however we use the bound $\left|\f
rac{2\sin(Ax)}{x}\right|\le 2A$ giving $2A\cdot 2\eta\cdot\varepsilon$ which is
not $A$-independent. Instead use the better fact: $\frac{\sin t}{t}$ is bounded,
 so $\left|\frac{2\sin(Ax)}{x}\right| = 2A\left|\frac{\sin(Ax)}{Ax}\right|\le 2A
$. This still grows. We need a different split.

Let us use the standard Riemann–Lebesgue argument instead. Set $g(x) = \frac{\ph
i(x)-\phi(0)}{x}$ for $x\neq 0$ and $g(0)=\phi'(0)$. Since $\phi$ is smooth and
Schwartz, $g$ is continuous at $0$ and $g\in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ (because $\phi$ is
 Schwartz, so $g$ decays faster than any polynomial). Then

$$
I_A - 2\pi\,\phi(0) = 2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x)\sin(Ax)\,dx.
$$

By the **Riemann–Lebesgue lemma** (applicable since $g\in L^1$), this integral t
ends to $0$ as $A\to\infty$. Therefore

$$
\lim_{A\to\infty} I_A = 2\pi\,\phi(0).
$$

**Step 3: Convention check / conclusion.**

With the Fourier convention $\widehat{f}(\lambda)=\int f(x)e^{i\lambda x}dx$ (no
 $2\pi$ normalization), the inverse carries a $\frac{1}{2\pi}$, and the distribu
tional identity is

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{i\lambda x}\,d\lambda = 2\pi\,\delta(x),
$$

so that $\int\int e^{i\lambda x}\phi(x)\,dx\,d\lambda = 2\pi\,\phi(0)$, i.e. $\w
idehat{1} = 2\pi\,\delta$.

The problem statement asks to show the double integral equals $\phi(0)$. This ho
lds under the **symmetric convention** where the Fourier transform is normalized
 by $\frac{1}{2\pi}$, i.e. $\widehat{f}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int f(x)e^{-i\la
mbda x}dx$, or equivalently when one reads the outer measure $d\lambda$ as $\fra
c{d\lambda}{2\pi}$. Under that normalization the computation above gives exactly

$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{i\lambda x}\phi(x)\,dx\,\frac{d\lambda}{2\
pi} = \phi(0).
$$

**Summary of the argument.** For $\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$:

1. Truncate the $\lambda$-integral to $[-A,A]$ and apply Fubini (justified by $\
phi\in L^1$).
2. Evaluate the inner $\lambda$-integral to obtain the Dirichlet kernel $D_A(x)=
\frac{2\sin(Ax)}{x}$.
3. Use $\int_{\mathbb{R}} D_A\,dx = 2\pi$ to subtract $2\pi\phi(0)$, leaving $2\
int g(x)\sin(Ax)\,dx$ with $g(x)=\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(0)}{x}\in L^1$.
4. Apply the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma to conclude the remainder $\to 0$.

Hence, with the $\frac{1}{2\pi}$ normalization on $d\lambda$ (the convention und
er which $\widehat{1}=\delta$),

$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{i\lambda x}\phi(x)\,dx\,\frac{d\lambda}{2\
pi} = \phi(0) = \langle \delta,\phi\rangle,
$$

which is precisely the distributional identity $\widehat{1}=\delta$. $\blacksqua
