## Roth's Theorem on the Approximation of Algebraic Numbers by Rationals

### Statement

**Roth's Theorem (1955).** Let $\alpha$ be an irrational algebraic number. Then
for every $\varepsilon > 0$, the inequality

$$\left|\alpha - \frac{s}{t}\right| < \frac{1}{t^{\,2+\varepsilon}}$$

has only finitely many solutions in integers $s, t$ with $t > 0$.

Equivalently, defining the **irrationality measure** (or irrationality exponent)
 of $\alpha$ as

$$\mu(\alpha) := \sup\left\{\mu : \left|\alpha - \frac{s}{t}\right| < \frac{1}{t
^{\mu}} \text{ has infinitely many solutions}\right\},$$

Roth's Theorem asserts that for every irrational algebraic number $\alpha$,

$$\mu(\alpha) = 2.$$

### Context and Sharpness

- **Lower bound.** Dirichlet's theorem (via the pigeonhole principle / continued
 fractions) guarantees that for *every* irrational $\alpha$, the inequality $\le
ft|\alpha - s/t\right| < 1/t^{2}$ has infinitely many solutions. Hence $\mu(\alp
ha) \geq 2$ for every irrational number, algebraic or not.

- **Roth's upper bound.** Roth's theorem provides the matching upper bound $\mu(
\alpha) \leq 2$ for irrational *algebraic* $\alpha$. Combined with Dirichlet's l
ower bound, this pins the irrationality measure of every irrational algebraic nu
mber exactly at $2$.

- **The exponent $2+\varepsilon$ is best possible.** One cannot replace $2+\vare
psilon$ by $2$ in the statement, because the convergents of the continued-fracti
on expansion of any irrational $\alpha$ satisfy $\left|\alpha - p_n/q_n\right| <
 1/q_n^{2}$ infinitely often. So the theorem is sharp: the threshold $2$ cannot
be lowered, and any exponent strictly greater than $2$ yields only finitely many
 approximants.

### Historical Place: The Thue–Siegel–Roth Sequence

Roth's result is the culmination of a sequence of increasingly strong bounds on
rational approximation of algebraic numbers:

1. **Liouville (1844).** If $\alpha$ is algebraic of degree $d \geq 2$, then the
re exists $c(\alpha) > 0$ such that $\left|\alpha - s/t\right| > c(\alpha)/t^{d}
$ for all rationals $s/t$. This gives $\mu(\alpha) \leq d$. Liouville used this
to construct the first explicit transcendental numbers (Liouville numbers), whic
h have $\mu = \infty$.

2. **Thue (1909).** Improved the exponent from $d$ to $d/2 + 1$.

3. **Siegel (1921).** Improved to $2\sqrt{d}$ (more precisely $\min_{s \in \math
bb{N}}(s + d/s) = 2\sqrt{d}$).

4. **Dyson (1947) / Gelfond.** Improved to $\sqrt{2d}$.

5. **Roth (1955).** Showed the exponent can be taken as $2 + \varepsilon$ for *a
ny* $\varepsilon > 0$, independent of the degree $d$. This is the optimal result
, since Dirichlet forces $\mu \geq 2$.

Roth was awarded the **Fields Medal in 1958** for this work.

### Proof Sketch (Roth's Method)

Roth's proof is by contradiction and is fundamentally a **gap principle** argume
nt combined with a sophisticated polynomial construction. The key ideas:

1. **Assume** infinitely many rationals $p_i/q_i$ satisfy $\left|\alpha - p_i/q_
i\right| < q_i^{-(2+\varepsilon)}$, with $q_i \to \infty$.

2. **Construct an auxiliary polynomial** $P(x_1, \dots, x_m) \in \mathbb{Z}[x_1,
\dots,x_m]$ in several variables that vanishes to high order at $(\alpha,\dots,\
alpha)$. The number of variables $m$ and the order of vanishing are chosen depen
ding on $\varepsilon$.

3. **Upper bound (the polynomial is small at the rational points).** Evaluating
$P$ at $(p_1/q_1, \dots, p_m/q_m)$ and using the high-order vanishing at $\alpha
$ together with the strong approximation hypothesis, one shows $|P(p_1/q_1,\dots
,p_m/q_m)|$ is extremely small.

4. **Lower bound (the polynomial cannot be too small).** Since $P$ has integer c
oefficients, $q_1^{N_1}\cdots q_m^{N_m} P(p_1/q_1,\dots,p_m/q_m)$ is a nonzero i
nteger (one proves $P$ does not vanish identically at the rational point, using
the algebraic independence structure of the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$). Hen
ce its absolute value is at least $1$.

5. **Contradiction via the gap principle.** The upper and lower bounds are incom
patible once the denominators $q_i$ grow sufficiently fast. The gap principle sh
ows that the sequence of good approximations must thin out so rapidly that only
finitely many can exist — contradicting the assumption of infinitely many.

The technical heart is the **index lemma** (or *Roth's lemma*), which controls t
he non-vanishing of the auxiliary polynomial at the rational points, and the car
eful bookkeeping of the order of vanishing versus the growth of the denominators
.

### Implications

1. **Irrationality measure of algebraic numbers is exactly 2.** Every irrational
 algebraic number is "as hard to approximate by rationals as a generic irrationa
l number." There is no algebraic number that is exceptionally well-approximable
(in the sense of having $\mu > 2$). This is a striking rigidity statement: algeb
raicity forces the worst-case (from the approximationist's view) behavior.

2. **Transcendence criterion.** If an irrational number $\alpha$ has $\mu(\alpha
) > 2$ — that is, if for some $\varepsilon > 0$ the inequality $\left|\alpha - s
/t\right| < 1/t^{2+\varepsilon}$ has infinitely many solutions — then $\alpha$ i
s **transcendental**. This generalizes Liouville's classical criterion (which re
quired $\mu = \infty$) to a much sharper test. For example:
   - **$e$ is transcendental** can be shown via its continued fraction having un
bounded partial quotients giving $\mu(e) = 2$ — actually this does *not* prove t
ranscendence since $\mu=2$. The point is that numbers with $\mu > 2$ are automat
ically transcendental, providing a powerful sufficient condition.

3. **Resolution of a long-standing problem.** Roth's theorem settled the questio
n (open since Liouville) of how well algebraic numbers can be approximated by ra
tionals, showing that degree plays no role in the optimal exponent — only the $2
$ from Dirichlet matters.

4. **Finiteness of solutions to certain Diophantine equations (Thue equations).*
* Combined with earlier work of Thue, Roth's theorem implies that the **Thue equ
ation** $F(x,y) = c$, where $F \in \mathbb{Z}[x,y]$ is a homogeneous irreducible
 form of degree $\geq 3$, has only **finitely many** integer solutions $(x,y)$.
The connection: if $F(\alpha, 1) = 0$ for an algebraic $\alpha$ of degree $d \ge
q 3$, then $|F(s,t)| \geq C |t|^d |\alpha - s/t|$ for large $t$, and Roth's boun
d $|\alpha - s/t| > t^{-(2+\varepsilon)}$ forces $|F(s,t)| \to \infty$ as $t \to
 \infty$ when $d > 2 + \varepsilon$, i.e., $d \geq 3$. Hence only finitely many
$(s,t)$ can give $F(s,t) = c$.

5. **Foundation for later developments.** Roth's method was generalized by:
   - **Schmidt (1970s):** the Subspace Theorem, extending Roth's result to simul
taneous approximation of algebraic numbers by rationals and to approximation in
higher dimensions. Schmidt received the Fields Medal in part for this work.
   - **Faltings (1980s):** the $p$-adic and function-field analogues.
   - The Subspace Theorem has become a central tool in modern Diophantine geomet
ry, with applications to integral points on varieties, $S$-unit equations, and t
he Vojta conjectures.

6. **Limitation: the theorem is ineffective.** Roth's proof does not provide an
explicit computable bound on the size of the finitely many exceptional rational
approximations (the constant hidden in "finitely many" depends on $\alpha$ and $
\varepsilon$ in a non-constructive way). Making Roth's theorem effective — givin
g an explicit upper bound on the denominators of all solutions — remains an impo
rtant open problem in general, though effective versions are known in special ca
ses (e.g., via Baker's theory of linear forms in logarithms for certain Thue equ
ations).

### Summary

Roth's Theorem states that an irrational algebraic number $\alpha$ cannot be app
roximated by rationals $s/t$ to order better than $1/t^{2}$: for every $\varepsi
lon > 0$, the inequality $|\alpha - s/t| < 1/t^{2+\varepsilon}$ has only finitel
y many rational solutions. This pins the irrationality measure of every irration
al algebraic number at exactly $2$, the value forced by Dirichlet's theorem. Its
 implications include a sharp transcendence criterion (any number with irrationa
lity measure exceeding $2$ is transcendental), the finiteness of solutions to Th
ue equations, and the foundation for Schmidt's Subspace Theorem and modern Dioph
antine approximation. The theorem is sharp but ineffective — it does not yield e
xplicit bounds on the exceptional solutions.
