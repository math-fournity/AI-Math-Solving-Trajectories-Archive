## Proof

**Claim.** Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional real inner product space with inner p
roduct $(\cdot,\cdot)$, and let $Q:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}$ be a symmetric biline
ar form. Then there exists a unique linear self-adjoint operator $K:V\to V$ such
 that
$$(K(x),y)=Q(x,y)\quad\text{for all }x,y\in V.$$

**Construction of $K$.** Fix $x\in V$. The map $y\mapsto Q(x,y)$ is a linear fun
ctional on $V$ (since $Q$ is bilinear). Since $V$ is finite-dimensional with inn
er product $(\cdot,\cdot)$, the Riesz representation theorem applies: there exis
ts a **unique** vector in $V$, which we denote $K(x)$, such that
$$Q(x,y)=(K(x),y)\quad\text{for all }y\in V.$$
This defines a map $K:V\to V$.

**$K$ is linear.** Let $x_1,x_2\in V$ and $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}$. For all $
y\in V$:
$$(K(\alpha x_1+\beta x_2),y)=Q(\alpha x_1+\beta x_2,y)=\alpha\,Q(x_1,y)+\beta\,
Q(x_2,y)$$
$$=\alpha(K(x_1),y)+\beta(K(x_2),y)=(\alpha K(x_1)+\beta K(x_2),y).$$
Since this holds for all $y$ and the inner product is non-degenerate, $K(\alpha
x_1+\beta x_2)=\alpha K(x_1)+\beta K(x_2)$. Hence $K$ is linear.

**$K$ is self-adjoint.** Using the symmetry of $Q$, for all $x,y\in V$:
$$(K(x),y)=Q(x,y)=Q(y,x)=(K(y),x)=(x,K(y)),$$
where the last step uses symmetry of the inner product. By definition of the adj
oint, $(K(x),y)=(x,K^*(y))$ for all $x,y$, so $(x,K(y))=(x,K^*(y))$ for all $x,y
$. Non-degeneracy gives $K(y)=K^*(y)$ for all $y$, i.e., $K=K^*$, so $K$ is self
-adjoint.

**Uniqueness.** Suppose $K_1,K_2$ both satisfy $(K_i(x),y)=Q(x,y)$ for all $x,y$
. Then $((K_1-K_2)(x),y)=0$ for all $x,y$. Setting $y=(K_1-K_2)(x)$ gives $\|(K_
1-K_2)(x)\|^2=0$ for all $x$, so $K_1=K_2$.

**Matrix viewpoint (consistency check).** In an orthonormal basis $\{e_i\}$, let
 $A$ be the matrix of $K$ and $B$ the matrix of $Q$ (i.e., $B_{ij}=Q(e_i,e_j)$).
 Then $(K(e_i),e_j)=A_{ji}$ (since the basis is orthonormal, the inner product p
icks out the $j$-th coordinate of $K(e_i)=\sum_k A_{ki}e_k$, giving $A_{ji}$). T
he condition $(K(e_i),e_j)=Q(e_i,e_j)=B_{ij}$ yields $A_{ji}=B_{ij}$, i.e., $A=B
^\top$. Since $Q$ is symmetric, $B=B^\top$, so $A=B$, which is symmetric — confi
rming $K$ is self-adjoint. $\square$

**Conclusion.** Yes, such a linear self-adjoint operator $K$ always exists (and
is unique). It is constructed via the Riesz representation theorem applied to ea
ch slice $y\mapsto Q(x,y)$; linearity follows from bilinearity of $Q$, and self-
adjointness follows from symmetry of $Q$.
