## Proof

**Setup.** Substitute $x = e^{-t}$ with $t \to 0^+$, so $x \to 1^-$. Then
$$f(e^{-t}) = \sum_{n \ge 1} e^{-t \cdot n!}, \qquad 1 - e^{-t} = t \cdot \frac{
1-e^{-t}}{t} \sim t.$$
Since $(1-e^{-t})/t \to 1$, it suffices to show
$$\lim_{t \to 0^+} t \sum_{n \ge 1} e^{-t \cdot n!} = 0.$$

**Key observation (lacunarity of $n!$).** The factorials $1!, 2!, 6!, 24!, \dots
$ grow super-exponentially. For small $t$, only those terms with $t \cdot n! = O
(1)$ contribute meaningfully; all others are either $\approx 1$ (when $t \cdot n
! \ll 1$) or $\approx 0$ (when $t \cdot n! \gg 1$). The number of "active" terms
 is controlled by the index $N$ where $N! \approx 1/t$, and the crucial point is
 that $N/N! \to 0$.

**Rigorous upper bound.** For each $t \in (0,1)$, define $N = N(t)$ as the uniqu
e integer satisfying
$$N! \le \frac{1}{t} < (N+1)!, \qquad \text{i.e., } t \cdot N! \le 1 < t \cdot (
N+1)!.$$
Note $N(t) \to \infty$ as $t \to 0^+$.

Split the sum:
$$S(t) := \sum_{n \ge 1} e^{-t \cdot n!} = \underbrace{\sum_{n=1}^{N} e^{-t \cdo
t n!}}_{S_1} + \underbrace{\sum_{n \ge N+1} e^{-t \cdot n!}}_{S_2}.$$

*Bound on $S_1$:* Each term satisfies $e^{-t \cdot n!} \le 1$, so $S_1 \le N$.

*Bound on $S_2$:* For $n \ge N+1$, we have $n! = (N+1)! \cdot \frac{n!}{(N+1)!}$
, and since $t > \frac{1}{(N+1)!}$ (from $1/t < (N+1)!$):
$$t \cdot n! > \frac{n!}{(N+1)!}.$$
- For $n = N+1$: $t \cdot (N+1)! > 1$, so $e^{-t(N+1)!} < e^{-1} < 1$.
- For $n \ge N+2$: $\frac{n!}{(N+1)!} \ge (N+2)^{n-N-1} \ge N+2$, so $e^{-t \cdo
t n!} < e^{-(N+2)^{n-N-1}}$.

Therefore
$$S_2 \le 1 + \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} e^{-(N+2)^j} \le 1 + \frac{e^{-(N+2)}}{1 - e^{
-(N+2)}} = 1 + o(1).$$

Combining: $S(t) \le N + 1 + o(1)$.

**Squeezing.** Since $t \le \frac{1}{N!}$ (from $N! \le 1/t$):
$$0 \le t \cdot S(t) \le t \cdot (N + 1 + o(1)) \le \frac{N + 1 + o(1)}{N!}.$$
As $N \to \infty$, $\frac{N+1}{N!} \to 0$ (since $N!$ grows faster than any poly
nomial in $N$). By the squeeze theorem:
$$\lim_{t \to 0^+} t \sum_{n \ge 1} e^{-t \cdot n!} = 0.$$

**Conclusion.** Returning to the original variable,
$$(1-x)\,f(x) = \frac{1 - e^{-t}}{t} \cdot t \sum_{n \ge 1} e^{-t \cdot n!} \;\l
ongrightarrow\; 1 \cdot 0 = 0 \qquad (t \to 0^+).$$

The limit **exists** and equals $\boxed{0}$.

**Remark.** The super-exponential growth of $n!$ is essential. For the geometric
 series $\sum z^n$, the analogous limit is $1$; for $\sum z^{n^2}$ it is $0$ as
well (by a theta-function/Gaussian integral estimate), but the mechanism here is
 even more elementary: the factorial gaps are so large that the number of contri
buting terms ($\sim N$ with $N! \sim 1/t$) is negligible compared to $1/t$, yiel
ding $t \cdot N \le N/N! \to 0$.
