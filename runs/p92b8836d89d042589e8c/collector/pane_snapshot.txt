## Proof

**The answer is $n = 14$.**

**Setup.** Numbers $1,\ldots,n$ are on the board. Moves: A erases 1 number, B er
ases 2 consecutive, A erases 3 consecutive, B erases 4 consecutive. B wants to g
uarantee both her moves succeed; A plays adversarially.

**B's first move is easy.** For $n\ge 4$, removing one number breaks at most 2 o
f the $n-1\ge 3$ consecutive pairs, so at least one pair of consecutive numbers
remains. B can always move.

**Reduction to intervals.** After A removes number $m$, the board splits into tw
o intervals of lengths $a=m-1$ and $b=n-m$, with $a+b=n-1$. B then removes 2 con
secutive from one interval (shortening or splitting it). A then removes 3 consec
utive from one interval of length $\ge 3$.

**Lemma.** When A removes 3 consecutive from an interval of length $L\ge 3$, the
 largest remaining piece has length exactly $\lceil(L-3)/2\rceil$ (A achieves th
is by splitting near the center; A cannot do better).

**B's winning condition after her first move.** Let the resulting interval lengt
hs be $\ell_1\ge\ell_2\ge\cdots$. B wins iff:
- **(W1)** $\ell_1\ge 10$: A attacks it, but the largest piece is $\ge\lceil 7/2
\rceil=4$.
- **(W2)** $\ell_1\ge 4$ and $\ell_2\ge 4$: A can attack only one; the other sur
vives with length $\ge 4$.

A wins iff some interval $j$ satisfies $3\le\ell_j\le 9$ with all others $\le 3$
 (A attacks $j$, reducing it to pieces $\le 3$).

**$n=14$ works.** For any $m$, we have $a+b=13$. By symmetry assume $a\le b$, so
 $b\ge 7$. B's strategy:

| $m$ | $a$ | $b$ | B removes 2 from end of | Resulting lengths | Condition |
|-----|-----|-----|--------------------------|-------------------|-----------|
| 1 | 0 | 13 | $b$ | $0,\ 11$ | W1 ($11\ge10$) |
| 2 | 1 | 12 | $b$ | $1,\ 10$ | W1 ($10\ge10$) |
| 3 | 2 | 11 | $a$ | $0,\ 11$ | W1 ($11\ge10$) |
| 4 | 3 | 10 | $a$ | $1,\ 10$ | W1 ($10\ge10$) |
| 5 | 4 | 9 | $b$ | $4,\ 7$ | W2 ($4,7\ge4$) |
| 6 | 5 | 8 | $b$ | $5,\ 6$ | W2 ($5,6\ge4$) |
| 7 | 6 | 7 | $a$ | $4,\ 7$ | W2 ($4,7\ge4$) |

Cases $m=8,\ldots,14$ are symmetric. In every case B achieves W1 or W2, so after
 A's best second move, an interval of length $\ge 4$ remains, and B can erase 4
consecutive numbers.

**$n=13$ fails.** A chooses $m=4$, giving intervals $[1,3]$ (length 3) and $[5,1
3]$ (length 9). Whatever B does:

- **Remove 2 from $[1,3]$:** leaves lengths $1, 9$. A splits the 9 into pieces $
\le 3$. All intervals $\le 3$.
- **Remove 2 from an end of $[5,13]$:** leaves $3, 7$. A splits the 7 into piece
s $\le 2$. All $\le 3$.
- **Split $[5,13]$ from the middle:** the best split (removing $\{8,9\}$) gives
$3, 3, 4$. A attacks the 4, leaving 1. All $\le 3$. Any other middle removal giv
es at most one interval $\ge 4$ with length $\le 9$, which A destroys.

In all cases, no 4 consecutive numbers remain, so B cannot make her second move.
 Thus $n=13$ is insufficient.

**Conclusion.** Since $n=14$ suffices and $n=13$ does not, the smallest $n$ is $
\boxed{14}$.
