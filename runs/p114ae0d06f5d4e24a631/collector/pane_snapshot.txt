**Proof.**

Let $c_m = \#\{k : a_k = m\}$ for each positive integer $m$. Then $b_n = \sum_{m
=1}^{n} c_m$ and

$$\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{a_k} = \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} \frac{c_m}{m}.$$

Since $c_m \ge 0$ and $\sum c_m/m$ converges, denote its sum by $S < \infty$.

**Step 1: Abel summation.** For each $N \ge 1$, applying summation by parts with
 $f(m)=1/m$:

$$\sum_{m=1}^{N} \frac{c_m}{m} = \frac{b_N}{N} + \sum_{m=1}^{N-1} b_m\!\left(\fr
ac{1}{m}-\frac{1}{m+1}\right) = \frac{b_N}{N} + \sum_{m=1}^{N-1} \frac{b_m}{m(m+
1)}.$$

Every term is non-negative, so taking $N\to\infty$:

$$S = \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{b_N}{N} + \sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{b_m}{m(m+1)},$$

where both terms on the right are non-negative and their sum equals $S$. In part
icular,

$$\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{b_m}{m(m+1)} \le S < \infty. \tag{$\star$}$$

**Step 2: Contradiction argument.** Suppose, for contradiction, that $b_n/n \not
\to 0$. Then there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ and a sequence $n_j \to \infty$ with

$$b_{n_j} \ge \varepsilon\, n_j \quad\text{for all } j.$$

Since $n_j\to\infty$, we may pass to a subsequence (still denoted $n_j$) with $2
n_j < n_{j+1}$ for every $j$, so the intervals $[n_j,\, 2n_j]$ are pairwise disj
oint.

**Step 3: Lower bound on a block.** Since $b_m$ is non-decreasing, for every $m
\in [n_j, 2n_j]$ we have $b_m \ge b_{n_j} \ge \varepsilon\, n_j \ge \frac{\varep
silon}{2}\,m$ (using $m \le 2n_j$, i.e.\ $n_j \ge m/2$). Hence

$$\frac{b_m}{m(m+1)} \ge \frac{(\varepsilon/2)\,m}{m(m+1)} = \frac{\varepsilon}{
2(m+1)} \ge \frac{\varepsilon}{2(2n_j+1)} \ge \frac{\varepsilon}{6\,n_j}$$

for all $m\in[n_j,2n_j]$ and $n_j$ sufficiently large. Summing over the block (w
hich contains $n_j+1$ integers):

$$\sum_{m=n_j}^{2n_j}\frac{b_m}{m(m+1)} \;\ge\; (n_j+1)\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{6
\,n_j} \;\ge\; \frac{\varepsilon}{6}.$$

**Step 4: Contradiction.** Since the blocks $[n_j,2n_j]$ are pairwise disjoint,

$$\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{b_m}{m(m+1)} \;\ge\; \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=n_j}^
{2n_j}\frac{b_m}{m(m+1)} \;\ge\; \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\varepsilon}{6} \;=\;
\infty,$$

contradicting $(\star)$. Therefore our assumption was false, and

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{b_n}{n}=0. \qquad\blacksquare$$
