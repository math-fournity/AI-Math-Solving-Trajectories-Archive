No. The property "every nonzero element is a unit or a zero divisor" does **not*
* force a ring to have only finitely many ideals.

## Counterexample

Let \(R = \prod_{n=1}^{\infty} F\), the direct product of countably many copies
of a field \(F\) (e.g. \(F = \mathbb{F}_2\)). This is a commutative ring with id
entity, and it is infinite.

### Claim 1. Every nonzero element of \(R\) is a unit or a zero divisor.

Let \(a = (a_1, a_2, \dots) \in R\), \(a \neq 0\).

- **Case 1: every \(a_n \neq 0\).** Then \(a\) is a unit, with inverse \(a^{-1}
= (a_1^{-1}, a_2^{-1}, \dots)\), since each coordinate is invertible in \(F\).
- **Case 2: some coordinate \(a_k = 0\).** Since \(a \neq 0\), there is some \(m
\) with \(a_m \neq 0\), so in particular \(a\) is not the zero element. Let \(e_
k \in R\) be the element that is \(1\) in the \(k\)-th coordinate and \(0\) else
where. Then \(e_k \neq 0\), and
  \[
  a \cdot e_k = (0, \dots, 0, a_k, 0, \dots)\cdot(\dots) = 0,
  \]
  because the only coordinate where \(e_k\) is nonzero is the \(k\)-th, where \(
a_k = 0\). Hence \(a\) is a zero divisor.

In either case \(a\) is a unit or a zero divisor. ∎

### Claim 2. \(R\) has infinitely many ideals.

For each subset \(S \subseteq \mathbb{N}\), define
\[
I_S = \{(a_n) \in R : a_n = 0 \text{ for all } n \notin S\}.
\]
Equivalently, \(I_S = \prod_{n \in S} F \times \prod_{n \notin S} \{0\}\). This
is clearly an additive subgroup, and it is closed under multiplication by arbitr
ary elements of \(R\) (coordinatewise), so \(I_S\) is an ideal of \(R\).

If \(S \neq T\), say \(k \in S \setminus T\), then the element with \(1\) in the
 \(k\)-th coordinate and \(0\) elsewhere belongs to \(I_S\) but not to \(I_T\).
Hence \(I_S \neq I_T\).

Since \(\mathbb{N}\) has infinitely many (indeed uncountably many) subsets, the
ideals \(\{I_S : S \subseteq \mathbb{N}\}\) form an infinite family of distinct
ideals of \(R\). ∎

## Conclusion

The ring \(R = \prod_{n=1}^{\infty} F\) satisfies the hypothesis — every nonzero
 element is either a unit or a zero divisor — yet it possesses infinitely many i
deals. Therefore the answer to the question is **no**: such a ring need not cont
ain only finitely many ideals.
