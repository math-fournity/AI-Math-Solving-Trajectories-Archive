## Proof

We interpret the conditions as: $f$ is a probability density on $[0,2\pi]$ (nonn
egative, total mass $1$) with $\mathbb{E}[\sin X]=r$, and we seek to maximize $\
mathbb{E}[\cos X]$, where $X$ has density $f$.

**Step 1: Reformulation as a convex-hull constraint.**

Set $c = \int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos x\,dx = \mathbb{E}[\cos X]$. The pair
$$
(\mathbb{E}[\cos X],\,\mathbb{E}[\sin X]) = (c, r)
$$
is the barycenter of the probability measure $\mu(dx)=f(x)\,dx$ on the curve
$$
\Gamma = \{(\cos x,\sin x) : x\in[0,2\pi]\},
$$
which is the unit circle. Since a barycenter of a measure supported on $\Gamma$
lies in the convex hull of $\Gamma$, and the convex hull of the unit circle is t
he closed unit disk $\{(u,v): u^2+v^2\le 1\}$, we must have
$$
c^2 + r^2 \le 1.
$$
Hence $c \le \sqrt{1-r^2}$ (taking the nonnegative root, since we are maximizing
).

**Step 2: The bound is sharp (supremum = $\sqrt{1-r^2}$).**

The point $(\sqrt{1-r^2},\, r)$ lies on the unit circle, so it equals $(\cos\the
ta,\sin\theta)$ for some $\theta$. Concretely, since $r\in[-1,1]$, pick
$$
\theta = \begin{cases}\arcsin r & r\ge 0,\\ 2\pi+\arcsin r & r<0,\end{cases}
$$
so that $\theta\in[0,2\pi]$, $\sin\theta=r$, and $\cos\theta=\sqrt{1-r^2}$.

A Dirac mass at $\theta$ would give $c=\sqrt{1-r^2}$ exactly, but it is not a po
sitive density. However, we can approximate it: let $\varphi_\varepsilon$ be a p
ositive, smooth bump of total mass $1$ supported in $(\theta-\varepsilon,\theta+
\varepsilon)\cap[0,2\pi]$ (shrinking toward $\theta$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$), and
 set $f_\varepsilon(x) = \varphi_\varepsilon(x) + \delta_\varepsilon$ with a tin
y positive constant $\delta_\varepsilon$ (renormalized) to ensure strict positiv
ity everywhere. By continuity of $\sin$ and $\cos$,
$$
\int f_\varepsilon\sin x\,dx \to \sin\theta = r, \qquad \int f_\varepsilon\cos x
\,dx \to \cos\theta = \sqrt{1-r^2}.
$$
A small correction (shifting the bump slightly) keeps the sine-moment exactly $r
$ while the cosine-moment tends to $\sqrt{1-r^2}$. Thus the supremum of $\int f\
cos x\,dx$ over admissible positive densities is exactly $\sqrt{1-r^2}$.

**Step 3: Attainment.**

Strictly, with $f$ required to be a *strictly positive* density (not a measure),
 the value $\sqrt{1-r^2}$ is the supremum and is approached arbitrarily closely
but not attained, since attaining a boundary point of the disk as a barycenter f
orces the measure to be a point mass. In the standard formulation where $f\ge 0$
 (a probability density, allowing concentration), the maximum is attained in the
 limit and equals $\sqrt{1-r^2}$. Either way, the maximum value (supremum) is

$$
\boxed{\sqrt{1-r^2}}.
$$
