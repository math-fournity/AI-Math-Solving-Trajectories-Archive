No, $\mathbb{Q}$ cannot be expressed as the direct product of two proper subgrou
ps.

## Proof

We work with $\mathbb{Q}$ as an additive abelian group. Recall that a group $G$
is **divisible** if for every $g \in G$ and every positive integer $n$, there ex
ists $h \in G$ with $n h = g$. The group $\mathbb{Q}$ is divisible: given $r \in
 \mathbb{Q}$ and $n \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$, the rational $r/n$ satisfies $n \cdot
(r/n) = r$.

**Step 1: Direct summands of a divisible group are divisible.**

Suppose $\mathbb{Q} = A \oplus B$ (internal direct sum). For any $a \in A$ and a
ny $n \geq 1$, divisibility of $\mathbb{Q}$ gives some $x \in \mathbb{Q}$ with $
n x = a$. Write $x = a' + b'$ with $a' \in A$, $b' \in B$. Then $n a' + n b' = a
 + 0$, and uniqueness of decomposition in a direct sum gives $n a' = a$ and $n b
' = 0$. Hence $a' \in A$ with $n a' = a$. So $A$ is divisible; likewise $B$ is d
ivisible.

**Step 2: Every nonzero divisible subgroup of $\mathbb{Q}$ equals $\mathbb{Q}$.*
*

Let $A \leq \mathbb{Q}$ be a nonzero divisible subgroup. Pick $0 \neq a \in A$,
written $a = p/q$ in lowest terms with $p \neq 0$. Since $A$ is divisible, there
 exists $x \in A$ with $p \cdot x = a = p/q$, giving $x = 1/q \in A$. Then $q \c
dot (1/q) = 1 \in A$. Applying divisibility again to $1 \in A$ with arbitrary $n
 \geq 1$, there is $y \in A$ with $n y = 1$, so $1/n \in A$. Finally, for any $m
/n \in \mathbb{Q}$, we have $m \cdot (1/n) = m/n \in A$ since $A$ is a subgroup.
 Thus $A = \mathbb{Q}$.

**Step 3: Conclusion.**

Suppose for contradiction that $\mathbb{Q} = A \oplus B$ with $A, B$ proper subg
roups. Since the direct sum is nontrivial, both $A$ and $B$ are nonzero (a zero
summand would make the other summand equal to $\mathbb{Q}$, contradicting proper
ness). By Step 1, both $A$ and $B$ are divisible. By Step 2, each nonzero divisi
ble subgroup of $\mathbb{Q}$ is all of $\mathbb{Q}$, so $A = \mathbb{Q}$ and $B
= \mathbb{Q}$. But then $A \cap B = \mathbb{Q} \neq \{0\}$, contradicting the de
finition of internal direct sum (which requires $A \cap B = \{0\}$). Equivalentl
y, neither $A$ nor $B$ is proper — contradicting the hypothesis.

Therefore $\mathbb{Q}$ is **indecomposable**: it cannot be written as the direct
 product (direct sum) of two proper subgroups.

**Answer: No.**
