## Proof

**Setup.** Place distinct values 1–6 at the 6 vertices of a regular hexagon. A v
ertex is a *local maximum* (canopy highlight) if its value exceeds both neighbor
s. We count arrangements with exactly 3 local maxima, modulo the dihedral group
$D_6$ (order 12).

**Step 1: Structure of 3 local maxima.**

In any circular arrangement of distinct values, local maxima and local minima al
ternate, and their counts are equal. With exactly 3 local maxima, there are exac
tly 3 local minima, alternating: max, min, max, min, max, min. Thus the maxima o
ccupy one of two alternating triples of positions: $\{1,3,5\}$ or $\{2,4,6\}$.

**Step 2: Count labeled arrangements for one alternating set.**

Fix maxima at positions $\{1,3,5\}$, minima at $\{2,4,6\}$. The cycle of adjacen
cies is:
$$\text{pos}_1 - \text{pos}_2 - \text{pos}_3 - \text{pos}_4 - \text{pos}_5 - \te
xt{pos}_6 - \text{pos}_1$$

Each maximum must exceed both adjacent minima. Choose a 3-element peak set $S \s
ubset \{1,\dots,6\}$; the valley set $T$ is its complement. We need a bijection
from $S$ to peak positions and $T$ to valley positions satisfying all dominance
constraints.

**Necessary conditions:** Let $a = \min(S)$, $f = \max(T)$.
- The smallest peak $a$ has two valley neighbors, both must be $< a$: need $\geq
 2$ elements of $T$ below $a$.
- The largest valley $f$ has two peak neighbors, both must be $> f$: need $\geq
2$ elements of $S$ above $f$.

Checking all $\binom{6}{3}=20$ peak sets, only two satisfy both conditions:

| Peak set $S$ | Valley set $T$ | $\#\{t \in T: t < \min S\}$ | $\#\{s \in S: s
> \max T\}$ | Valid? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\{3,5,6\}$ | $\{1,2,4\}$ | 2 ($\{1,2\}$) | 2 ($\{5,6\}$) | ✓ |
| $\{4,5,6\}$ | $\{1,2,3\}$ | 3 ($\{1,2,3\}$) | 3 ($\{4,5,6\}$) | ✓ |

All other peak sets fail at least one condition (any set containing 1 or 2 fails
 the first; $\{3,4,5\}$ and $\{3,4,6\}$ fail the second).

**Counting valid assignments:**

*Peak set $\{4,5,6\}$, valleys $\{1,2,3\}$:* Every peak ($\geq 4$) exceeds every
 valley ($\leq 3$), so all $3!\times 3! = 36$ assignments work.

*Peak set $\{3,5,6\}$, valleys $\{1,2,4\}$:* Peak 3 must have both neighbors fro
m $\{1,2\}$ (not 4, since $4>3$). Peak 3 can be placed at any of the 3 peak posi
tions (3 choices). Its two adjacent valley positions must receive $\{1,2\}$, for
cing the remaining valley position to get 4. The two adjacent valleys can be arr
anged in 2 ways, and the two remaining peaks $\{5,6\}$ in 2 ways. Total: $3 \tim
es 2 \times 2 = 12$.

**Total for one alternating set:** $36 + 12 = 48$.

By symmetry, the other alternating set $\{2,4,6\}$ also gives 48.

**Total labeled arrangements with exactly 3 local maxima:** $2 \times 48 = 96$.

**Step 3: Apply Burnside's lemma.**

The dihedral group $D_6$ has 12 elements: 6 rotations and 6 reflections. Since a
ll 6 values are distinct, any non-identity symmetry forces at least two position
s to share the same value (every non-identity element of $D_6$ maps at least one
 position to a different position while requiring equality). This is impossible
with distinct values, so:

$$|\text{Fix}(g)| = \begin{cases} 96 & g = \text{identity} \\ 0 & g \neq \text{i
dentity}\end{cases}$$

By Burnside's lemma:

$$\text{Distinct arrangements} = \frac{1}{|D_6|}\sum_{g \in D_6}|\text{Fix}(g)|
= \frac{96}{12} = \boxed{8}$$
