**Answer: No.** There need not always be a prime between $x$ and $y$ when $\oper
atorname{li}(y)-\operatorname{li}(x)=1$.

---

### Proof

Recall that $\operatorname{li}(t)=\int_0^t \dfrac{du}{\ln u}$ (with the usual Ca
uchy-principal-value interpretation at $u=1$), so for $t>1$,
$$
\operatorname{li}'(t)=\frac{1}{\ln t}>0,
$$
hence $\operatorname{li}$ is continuous and strictly increasing on $(1,\infty)$.

Let $p_n$ denote the $n$-th prime and $g_n=p_{n+1}-p_n$ the $n$-th prime gap.

**Key input (Westzynthius, 1931).**
$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{g_n}{\ln p_n}=+\infty .
$$
In particular, there are infinitely many $n$ with $g_n>\ln p_n$; indeed the rati
o is unbounded.

Pick such an $n$ with $\dfrac{g_n}{\ln p_n}>2$ and $p_n$ large enough that $\dfr
ac{\ln p_{n+1}}{\ln p_n}<\dfrac{3}{2}$ (possible since $p_{n+1}/p_n\to 1$, hence
 $\ln p_{n+1}/\ln p_n\to 1$). Then
$$
\operatorname{li}(p_{n+1})-\operatorname{li}(p_n)
=\int_{p_n}^{p_{n+1}}\frac{dt}{\ln t}
\;\ge\;\frac{p_{n+1}-p_n}{\ln p_{n+1}}
=\frac{g_n}{\ln p_{n+1}}
>\frac{2\ln p_n}{\ln p_{n+1}}
>\frac{2}{3/2}
=\frac{4}{3}>1 .
$$
(The first inequality uses that $1/\ln t$ is decreasing, so the integrand is at
least $1/\ln p_{n+1}$ on $[p_n,p_{n+1}]$.)

Thus for such an $n$,
$$
\operatorname{li}(p_{n+1})-\operatorname{li}(p_n)>1 .
$$

Now choose $x$ with $p_n<x<p_{n+1}$ and $x$ close enough to $p_n$ that, by conti
nuity of $\operatorname{li}$,
$$
\operatorname{li}(p_{n+1})-\operatorname{li}(x)>1 .
$$
Define $F(y)=\operatorname{li}(y)-\operatorname{li}(x)$ on $[x,p_{n+1}]$. Then $
F$ is continuous, $F(x)=0$, and $F(p_{n+1})>1$. By the Intermediate Value Theore
m there exists $y\in(x,\,p_{n+1})$ with
$$
F(y)=\operatorname{li}(y)-\operatorname{li}(x)=1 .
$$

Since $p_n<x<y<p_{n+1}$, the entire interval $(x,y)$ lies strictly between two c
onsecutive primes, so **$(x,y)$ contains no prime number**. Yet $\operatorname{l
i}(y)-\operatorname{li}(x)=1$ and $x<y$.

Therefore the condition $\operatorname{li}(y)-\operatorname{li}(x)=1$ with $x<y$
 does **not** guarantee a prime in $(x,y)$. $\blacksquare$
