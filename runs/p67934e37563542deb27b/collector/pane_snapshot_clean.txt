## Proof

**Definition.** A *Hamel basis* of a vector space $V$ over a field $F$ is a line
arly independent set $B \subseteq V$ such that every $v \in V$ is a **finite** l
inear combination of elements of $B$. The finiteness requirement is the defining
 feature distinguishing a Hamel (algebraic) basis from a Schauder (topological)
basis.

We give two complementary justifications. The first is a pure cardinality argume
nt; the second, based on the Baire Category Theorem, applies to the most importa
nt class of infinite-dimensional spaces (Banach spaces). Together they explain w
hy, in the settings one typically encounters, a Hamel basis is uncountable.

---

### 1. Cardinality argument (countable field)

**Claim.** Let $V$ be a vector space over a countable (or finite) field $F$. If
$V$ has a countable Hamel basis, then $V$ is countable.

*Proof of claim.* Let $B = \{b_1, b_2, b_3, \dots\}$ be a countable Hamel basis.
 Every $v \in V$ can be written uniquely as
$$v = \sum_{i \in I} c_i\, b_i,$$
where $I \subseteq \mathbb{N}$ is **finite** and $c_i \in F \setminus \{0\}$.

The set of all finite subsets of $\mathbb{N}$ is countable (it is $\bigcup_{n=0}
^{\infty} \mathbb{N}^{(n)}$, a countable union of countable sets). For a fixed f
inite subset $I$ of size $n$, the number of choices of coefficients $(c_i)_{i \i
n I} \in (F \setminus \{0\})^n$ is $|F|^n$. Since $F$ is countable, $|F|^n$ is c
ountable. Hence the total number of representable vectors is at most
$$\aleph_0 \cdot \sup_{n < \omega} \aleph_0^n = \aleph_0.$$
Therefore $V$ is countable. $\square$

**Corollary (the classical example).** Consider $\mathbb{R}$ as a vector space o
ver $\mathbb{Q}$. Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable and $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountabl
e, the claim forces the Hamel basis of $\mathbb{R}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ to be **un
countable**. (In fact its cardinality is $\mathfrak{c} = 2^{\aleph_0}$.)

More generally, whenever $V$ is uncountable and the ground field $F$ is countabl
e or finite, the Hamel basis of $V$ must be uncountable — a countable basis coul
d only span a countable set.

### 2. Baire Category argument (Banach spaces)

Many of the infinite-dimensional spaces arising in analysis ($\ell^p$, $C([0,1])
$, $L^p$, Hilbert spaces, …) are **Banach spaces**: complete normed vector space
s, typically over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$. For these, the ground field is *
uncountable*, so the cardinality argument above does not directly apply. A stron
ger, topological argument is needed.

**Theorem.** No infinite-dimensional Banach space admits a countable Hamel basis
.

*Proof.* Let $X$ be a Banach space and suppose, for contradiction, that $B = \{b
_1, b_2, \dots\}$ is a countable Hamel basis. Define
$$X_n := \operatorname{span}\{b_1, \dots, b_n\}, \qquad n = 1, 2, \dots$$
Each $X_n$ is a finite-dimensional subspace of $X$, hence **closed** (finite-dim
ensional subspaces of a normed space are complete, hence closed). Because $B$ is
 a Hamel basis,
$$X = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} X_n.$$

We now show each $X_n$ has **empty interior**. Since $X$ is infinite-dimensional
, $X_n$ is a *proper* subspace of $X$. A proper subspace of a normed space has e
mpty interior: if $X_n$ contained some open ball $B(x_0, r)$, then translating b
y $-x_0$ would give $B(0, r) \subseteq X_n$, and then every $x \in X$ with $\|x\
| < r$ would lie in $X_n$; by scaling, $X_n$ would contain all of $X$, contradic
ting properness.

Thus $X$ is a countable union of closed sets with empty interior — i.e., $X$ is
*meager in itself*. But by the **Baire Category Theorem**, a complete metric spa
ce is not meager in itself. This is a contradiction. $\square$

**Corollary.** Every infinite-dimensional Banach space has an **uncountable** Ha
mel basis.

### 3. Why "typically"

Not *every* infinite-dimensional vector space has an uncountable Hamel basis. Fo
r instance:

- The polynomial ring $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is infinite-dimensional
with the **countable** Hamel basis $\{1, x, x^2, \dots\}$.
- The space $c_{00}$ of finitely-supported sequences over any field has the coun
table Hamel basis $\{e_1, e_2, \dots\}$.

These are precisely the spaces that are *defined* as the collection of finite li
near combinations of a countable set — they are "small" by construction.

What makes the uncountable case "typical" is that the natural infinite-dimension
al spaces of analysis and geometry are:

1. **Uncountable as sets over a countable field** (e.g. $\mathbb{R}$ over $\math
bb{Q}$) — forcing uncountability of the basis by the cardinality argument; or
2. **Complete metric (Banach/Hilbert) spaces** — forcing uncountability of the b
asis by the Baire Category Theorem.

In both of these ubiquitous settings, a countable Hamel basis is impossible, so
the Hamel basis is uncountable. The "atypical" counterexamples are exactly the a
lgebraically-constructed spaces of finite support, which avoid both the cardinal
ity obstruction (they are countable) and the topological obstruction (they are n
ot complete). This is why a Hamel basis of an infinite-dimensional vector space
is, in the cases one normally meets, uncountable.
