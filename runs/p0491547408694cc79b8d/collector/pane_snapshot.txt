## Proof

**Setup.** Let $\bar{\mu} = \mathbb{E}_{\lambda \sim \Lambda}[\mu_\lambda]$ and
$\bar{\Sigma} = \mathbb{E}_{\lambda \sim \Lambda}[\Sigma_\lambda]$. Then $Y \sim
 \mathcal{N}(\bar{\mu}, \bar{\Sigma})$.

**Step 1: Compare covariances.**

By the law of total covariance:
$$\text{Cov}(X) = \mathbb{E}[\text{Cov}(X \mid \lambda)] + \text{Cov}(\mathbb{E}
[X \mid \lambda]) = \bar{\Sigma} + \text{Cov}(\mu_\lambda).$$

Since $\text{Cov}(Y) = \bar{\Sigma}$, if $X \stackrel{d}{=} Y$, then their covar
iances must be equal:
$$\bar{\Sigma} + \text{Cov}(\mu_\lambda) = \bar{\Sigma} \implies \text{Cov}(\mu_
\lambda) = 0.$$

Since $\text{Cov}(\mu_\lambda)$ is positive semidefinite and equals zero, $\mu_\
lambda$ is constant $\Lambda$-a.s. Denote this constant value by $\mu_0$.

**Step 2: With $\mu_\lambda$ constant, compare characteristic functions.**

Now $X$ is a mixture of Gaussians all with mean $\mu_0$ but (possibly) different
 covariances $\Sigma_\lambda$, and $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \bar{\Sigma})$. Th
eir characteristic functions are:

$$\varphi_X(t) = e^{it^T \mu_0} \cdot \mathbb{E}_\lambda\!\left[e^{-\frac{1}{2}
t^T \Sigma_\lambda t}\right], \qquad \varphi_Y(t) = e^{it^T \mu_0} \cdot e^{-\fr
ac{1}{2} t^T \bar{\Sigma} t}.$$

For $X \stackrel{d}{=} Y$, we need for all $t \in \mathbb{R}^k$:
$$\mathbb{E}_\lambda\!\left[e^{-\frac{1}{2} t^T \Sigma_\lambda t}\right] = e^{-\
frac{1}{2} t^T \bar{\Sigma} t} = e^{\,\mathbb{E}_\lambda[-\frac{1}{2} t^T \Sigma
_\lambda t]}.$$

**Step 3: Apply Jensen's inequality.**

Set $h(\lambda) = -\frac{1}{2} t^T \Sigma_\lambda t$, which is real-valued. The
equality above reads $\mathbb{E}[e^{h}] = e^{\mathbb{E}[h]}$. Since $\exp$ is **
strictly convex**, Jensen's inequality gives $\mathbb{E}[e^{h}] \geq e^{\mathbb{
E}[h]}$ with equality **if and only if** $h$ is constant a.s.

Therefore, for each $t$, $t^T \Sigma_\lambda t$ must be constant $\Lambda$-a.s.
Since this must hold for **all** $t \in \mathbb{R}^k$, the quadratic form $t \ma
psto t^T \Sigma_\lambda t$ is determined $\Lambda$-a.s. for all $t$, which means
 $\Sigma_\lambda$ itself is constant $\Lambda$-a.s. (a symmetric matrix is deter
mined by its quadratic form).

**Step 4: Conclusion.**

We have shown: if $X \stackrel{d}{=} Y$, then $\mu_\lambda$ is constant a.s. (St
ep 1) and $\Sigma_\lambda$ is constant a.s. (Step 3). Conversely, if both are co
nstant a.s., the mixture is a single Gaussian and trivially $X \stackrel{d}{=} Y
$.

**Answer.** $X$ and $Y$ are identically distributed **if and only if** the mixtu
re is trivial — that is, $(\mu_\lambda, \Sigma_\lambda)$ is constant $\Lambda$-a
lmost surely (all components are the same Gaussian, or equivalently $\Lambda$ is
 a point mass). In any **non-trivial** mixture (where there is genuine variation
 in either $\mu_\lambda$ or $\Sigma_\lambda$), $X$ and $Y$ are **not** identical
ly distributed.

The two mechanisms are:
1. **Variation in means** inflates $\text{Cov}(X)$ beyond $\bar{\Sigma}$ (law of
 total covariance), so $X$ cannot match $Y$.
2. **Variation in covariances** (even with fixed means) causes the mixture's cha
racteristic function to deviate from a Gaussian's, by strict convexity of $\exp$
 (Jensen's inequality).
