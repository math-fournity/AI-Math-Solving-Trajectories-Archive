Let G = ⟨R₁, R₂⟩⁻ (the closure of the group generated by R₁ and R₂ in SO(3)). We
 show G = SO(3).

**Step 1: G contains all rotations about the z-axis.**

clic group ⟨R₁⟩ = {R₁ⁿ : n ∈ ℤ} consists of rotations about z by angles nθπ (mod
 2π). Since θ is irrational, the set {nθ mod 2 : n ∈ ℤ} is dense in [0, 2) (by t
he equidistribution theorem / Kronecker's theorem). Therefore ⟨R₁⟩ is dense in S
O(2)_z, the group of all rotations about the z-axis. Since G is closed and conta
ins ⟨R₁⟩, we have SO(2)_z ⊆ G.

**Step 2: Classification of closed subgroups of SO(3).**

We use the classical theorem: every closed subgroup of SO(3) is conjugate to one
 of the following:
- Finite subgroups: {I}, Cₙ, Dₙ, T (tetrahedral), O (octahedral), I (icosahedral
);
- Infinite closed subgroups: SO(2) (rotations about a fixed axis), O(2) (rotatio
ns preserving a fixed axis as a set), or SO(3) itself.

Since G is closed and contains SO(2)_z (which is infinite), G must be infinite.
Therefore G is conjugate to SO(2), O(2), or SO(3).

**Step 3: Ruling out G = SO(2).**

If G were conjugate to SO(2), then since G contains SO(2)_z, we would need G = S
O(2)_z (the only subgroup conjugate to SO(2) that contains SO(2)_z is SO(2)_z it
self). But R₂ is a rotation about an axis at angle φ from the z-axis with 0 < φ,
 so R₂ ∉ SO(2)_z. This contradicts R₂ ∈ G. Hence G ≠ SO(2).

**Step 4: Ruling out G = O(2).**

The subgroup O(2)_z ⊂ SO(3) consists of all rotations that preserve the z-axis a
s a set:
- Rotations about the z-axis by any angle (i.e., SO(2)_z), and
- Rotations by π about axes lying in the xy-plane (these map z ↦ -z).

If G were conjugate to O(2), then since G ⊇ SO(2)_z, we would need G = O(2)_z (t
he only subgroup conjugate to O(2) containing SO(2)_z is O(2)_z itself).

Now, R₂ is a rotation about an axis **u** at angle φ from the z-axis with 0 < φ
< π/2. For R₂ to belong to O(2)_z, its axis **u** must be either:
- the z-axis (so that R₂ is a z-rotation), or
- perpendicular to the z-axis (so that R₂ is a π-rotation about a horizontal axi
s, or more generally preserves the z-axis).

But 0 < φ < π/2 means **u** is neither parallel to z nor perpendicular to z. A r
otation about such an axis does **not** preserve the z-axis (it tilts it), so R₂
 ∉ O(2)_z. This contradicts R₂ ∈ G. Hence G ≠ O(2).

**Step 5: Conclusion.**

Having ruled out all proper infinite closed subgroups, the only remaining possib
ility is:

$$G = SO(3).$$

Therefore ⟨R₁, R₂⟩ is dense in SO(3).

is not the z-axis), and φ < π/2 rules out G = O(2)_z (the axis of R₂ is not perp
