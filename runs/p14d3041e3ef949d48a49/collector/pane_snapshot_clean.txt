Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether a continuous bijection on a
 compact smooth manifold must be a diffeomorphism, or whether it can fail.

**Answer: Yes, a continuous bijection on a compact smooth manifold can fail to b
e a diffeomorphism.**

## Proof

**Step 1: What compactness gives us.**

Let $M$ be a compact smooth manifold and $f: M \to M$ a continuous bijection. Si
nce $M$ is compact and Hausdorff (manifolds are Hausdorff), the standard topolog
ical theorem applies: a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff
 space is a **homeomorphism**. So $f$ is automatically a homeomorphism—its inver
se $f^{-1}$ is continuous.

However, a diffeomorphism requires more: both $f$ and $f^{-1}$ must be **smooth*
*. Compactness bridges the gap between "continuous bijection" and "homeomorphism
," but it does **not** bridge the gap between "homeomorphism" and "diffeomorphis
m."

**Step 2: Explicit counterexample.**

I will exhibit a **smooth** bijection on the compact smooth manifold $S^1$ whose
 inverse is **not** smooth, showing the failure even under the stronger assumpti
on that the map itself is smooth.

Define $g: S^1 \to S^1$ by
$$g(e^{i\theta}) = e^{i(\theta - \sin\theta)}, \qquad \theta \in [0, 2\pi).$$

*$g$ is well-defined and smooth:* The function $\theta \mapsto \theta - \sin\the
ta$ satisfies $h(\theta + 2\pi) = h(\theta) + 2\pi$, so it descends to a well-de
fined map on $S^1 = \mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}$. In any local angle-coordinate ch
art, $g$ is represented by $\theta \mapsto \theta - \sin\theta$, which is a smoo
th function $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$. Hence $g$ is smooth as a map of manifol
ds.

*$g$ is a bijection:* Let $h(\theta) = \theta - \sin\theta$. Then $h'(\theta) =
1 - \cos\theta \geq 0$ for all $\theta$, with $h'(\theta) = 0$ only at $\theta =
 0 \pmod{2\pi}$. Thus $h$ is strictly increasing on $(0, 2\pi)$, with $h(0) = 0$
 and $\lim_{\theta \to 2\pi^-} h(\theta) = 2\pi$. Therefore $h$ maps $[0, 2\pi)$
 bijectively onto $[0, 2\pi)$, and $g$ is a bijection on $S^1$.

*The inverse $g^{-1}$ is not smooth:* The inverse function $h^{-1}$ satisfies $h
^{-1}(\theta - \sin\theta) = \theta$. Near $\theta = 0$, we have the Taylor expa
nsion:
$$h(\theta) = \theta - \sin\theta = \theta - \left(\theta - \frac{\theta^3}{6} +
 \cdots\right) = \frac{\theta^3}{6} - \frac{\theta^5}{120} + \cdots$$

So $h(\theta) \sim \theta^3/6$ near $\theta = 0$, and the inverse satisfies $h^{
-1}(\varphi) \sim (6\varphi)^{1/3}$ near $\varphi = 0$. The function $\varphi \m
apsto (6\varphi)^{1/3}$ is **not differentiable** at $\varphi = 0$ (its derivati
ve blows up). Therefore $g^{-1}$ is not smooth at the point $e^{i \cdot 0} = 1 \
in S^1$.

**Step 3: Conclusion.**

The map $g: S^1 \to S^1$ is a smooth bijection (hence a continuous bijection) on
 the compact smooth manifold $S^1$, but $g^{-1}$ is not smooth. Therefore $g$ is
 **not a diffeomorphism**.

This demonstrates that a continuous bijection on a compact smooth manifold can f
ail to be a diffeomorphism. The essential reason is that compactness guarantees
only that the inverse is *continuous* (homeomorphism), not that it is *smooth* (
diffeomorphism). The inverse function theorem ensures smoothness of the inverse
only at points where the derivative is invertible; at critical points—like $\the
ta = 0$ in our example, where $h'(0) = 0$—the inverse can fail to be smooth, and
 no compactness argument can prevent such critical points from existing.
