## Proof

The answer is **yes**: $g$ is necessarily a bivariate rational function on $\mat
hbb{F}^2$.

**Interpretation.** Since $g:\mathbb{F}^2\to\mathbb{F}$ is defined on all of $\m
athbb{F}^2$ and $g(x,\cdot)$ is a rational function (an element of $\mathbb{F}(y
)$ that is a function $\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}$), the coprime representation $g(
x,y)=p_x(y)/q_x(y)$ has $q_x$ with **no roots in $\mathbb{F}$**; otherwise $g(x,
\cdot)$ would have a genuine pole at some $y_0\in\mathbb{F}$, contradicting that
 $g$ is defined at $(x,y_0)$. The same holds in the $x$-direction. In particular
 $g(x,y)=p_x(y)/q_x(y)$ for *every* $y\in\mathbb{F}$.

---

**Step 1 (a degree stratum is infinite).** For each $x$, write $g(x,y)=p_x(y)/q_
x(y)$ with $\gcd(p_x,q_x)=1$, $q_x$ monic, and set $d(x):=\max(\deg p_x,\deg q_x
)$. Let $T'_n:=\{x\in\mathbb{F}:d(x)=n\}$. Since $\mathbb{F}=\bigcup_{n\ge 0}T'_
n$ is a countable union and $\mathbb{F}$ is uncountable, some $T'_N$ is **infini
te**.

**Step 2 (a linear system with a 1‑dimensional kernel).** Pick $2N+1$ distinct p
oints $y_1,\dots,y_{2N+1}\in\mathbb{F}$ (possible since $\mathbb{F}$ is infinite
). For each $j$, the function $R_j(x):=g(x,y_j)$ is rational in $x$ by hypothesi
s. Consider unknowns $a_0,\dots,a_N,b_0,\dots,b_N$ and the homogeneous linear sy
stem
$$\sum_{k=0}^N a_k\,y_j^k \;=\; R_j(x)\sum_{\ell=0}^N b_\ell\,y_j^\ell,\qquad j=
1,\dots,2N+1. \tag{$\ast$}$$
Its coefficient matrix $M(x)$ has size $(2N+1)\times(2N+2)$ and entries that are
 rational functions of $x$.

For $x\in T'_N$, a solution $(a,b)$ of $(\ast)$ produces polynomials $A(y)=\sum
a_k y^k$, $B(y)=\sum b_\ell y^\ell$ (both of degree $\le N$) with $A(y_j)/B(y_j)
=g(x,y_j)=p_x(y_j)/q_x(y_j)$, i.e.
$$(A\,q_x-p_x\,B)(y_j)=0,\quad j=1,\dots,2N+1.$$
The polynomial $Aq_x-p_xB$ has degree $\le 2N$ and vanishes at $2N+1$ distinct p
oints, hence $Aq_x=p_xB$. Since $\gcd(p_x,q_x)=1$, we get $A=p_xC$ and $B=q_xC$
for some $C\in\mathbb{F}[y]$. Now $\deg C\le N-\deg p_x$ and $\deg C\le N-\deg q
_x$, so $\deg C\le N-d(x)=N-N=0$; thus $C$ is a constant. Therefore every soluti
on of $(\ast)$ is a scalar multiple of $(p_x,q_x)$: **the kernel of $M(x)$ is ex
actly $1$-dimensional**, i.e. $\operatorname{rank}M(x)=2N+1$.

**Step 3 (the kernel varies rationally with $x$).** Since $M(x)$ has full row ra
nk $2N+1$ on $T'_N$, some $(2N+1)\times(2N+1)$ minor $\Delta(x)$ is nonzero at a
 point of $T'_N$. As a rational function of $x$, $\Delta\not\equiv 0$, so $\Delt
a(x)\neq 0$ on a cofinite (hence infinite) set. Set $S:=T'_N\cap\{x:\Delta(x)\ne
q 0\}$; this is infinite. On $S$, by Cramer's rule / the adjugate, the kernel of
 $M(x)$ is spanned by a vector $v(x)=(a_0(x),\dots,a_N(x),b_0(x),\dots,b_N(x))$
whose entries are **rational functions of $x$**.

Because $(p_x,q_x)$ is a scalar multiple of $v(x)$, the ratio is unaffected by t
hat scalar:
$$g(x,y)=\frac{p_x(y)}{q_x(y)}=\frac{\sum_{k}a_k(x)\,y^k}{\sum_{\ell}b_\ell(x)\,
y^\ell}=: \frac{A(x,y)}{B(x,y)},\qquad x\in S,\;y\in\mathbb{F},$$
with $A,B\in\mathbb{F}(x)[y]$. Clearing the (finitely many) rational denominator
s gives $A/B=P/Q$ with $P,Q\in\mathbb{F}[x,y]$. Thus
$$R(x,y):=\frac{P(x,y)}{Q(x,y)}\in\mathbb{F}(x,y)$$
is a bivariate rational function, and $g(x,y)=R(x,y)$ for all $x\in S$ and all $
y\in\mathbb{F}$.

**Step 4 (propagation to all of $\mathbb{F}^2$).** Fix any $y_0\in\mathbb{F}$. B
oth $g(\cdot,y_0)$ (by hypothesis) and $R(\cdot,y_0)=P(\cdot,y_0)/Q(\cdot,y_0)$
are rational functions in $x$, and they agree on the infinite set $S$ (away from
 finitely many poles). A nonzero rational function in $\mathbb{F}(x)$ has only f
initely many zeros (its numerator is a nonzero polynomial), so $R(\cdot,y_0)-g(\
cdot,y_0)\equiv 0$ in $\mathbb{F}(x)$. Hence $R(x,y_0)=g(x,y_0)$ for every $x\in
\mathbb{F}$ (where defined); since $g(\cdot,y_0)$ is defined on all of $\mathbb{
F}$, the rational function $R(\cdot,y_0)$ has no poles in $\mathbb{F}$ and the e
quality holds everywhere. As $y_0$ was arbitrary, $g=R$ on all of $\mathbb{F}^2$
.

---

**Conclusion.** $g$ coincides on $\mathbb{F}^2$ with the bivariate rational func
tion $R(x,y)=P(x,y)/Q(x,y)$. The uncountability of $\mathbb{F}$ is used exactly
once, to guarantee that some degree stratum $T'_N$ is infinite; this is also whe
re the argument would fail over a countable field (where a countable union of fi
nite sets can exhaust the field). Hence **$g$ is necessarily a bivariate rationa
l function**.
