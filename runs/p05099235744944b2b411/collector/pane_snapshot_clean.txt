## Proof of `erdos_10.variants.two_pows`

**Theorem.** The set $\{n \in \mathbb{N} : n \text{ is even}\} \setminus \mathrm
{sumPrimeAndTwoPows}(2)$ is infinite.

### Step 1: Classification of even representable integers

Let $n$ be a positive even integer with $n = p + 2^a + 2^b$, $p$ prime, $0 \leq
a \leq b$ (multiset of size ≤ 2).

**0 powers:** $n = p$. Even and prime forces $n = 2$.

**1 power ($a = b$ as a single-element multiset, or one exponent):** $n = p + 2^
a$.
- $a = 0$: $n = p + 1$, so $p = n-1$ (odd). Requires $n-1$ prime.
- $a \geq 1$: $2^a$ even, $n$ even, so $p = 2$, giving $n = 2 + 2^a$.

**2 powers ($a \leq b$, two-element multiset):**
- $a = b = 0$: $n = p + 2$. Even $n$ forces $p = 2$, $n = 4$.
- $a = b \geq 1$: $n = p + 2^{a+1}$. Even, so $p = 2$, $n = 2 + 2^{a+1}$ (subsum
ed by 1-power case).
- $a = 0, b \geq 1$: $n = p + 1 + 2^b$. Since $1 + 2^b$ is odd, $p$ is odd. Requ
ires $n - 1 - 2^b$ to be an odd prime.
- $1 \leq a < b$: $2^a + 2^b$ even, so $p = 2$, $n = 2 + 2^a + 2^b$.

**Summary:** An even $n \geq 4$ lies in $\mathrm{sumPrimeAndTwoPows}(2)$ iff one
 of:
- **(S)** $n = 2 + 2^a$ for some $a \geq 1$, or $n = 2 + 2^a + 2^b$ for $1 \leq
a < b$ *(sparse: $O((\log n)^2)$ values up to $N$)*;
- **(M1)** $n - 1$ is prime;
- **(M2)** $n - 1 - 2^b$ is prime for some $b \geq 1$ with $2^b < n-1$.

### Step 2: Covering system for exponents

We cover all positive integers $b \geq 1$ by seven residue classes:

| Class $b \equiv r_i \pmod{q_i}$ | Prime $p_i$ | $\mathrm{ord}_{p_i}(2)$ | $2^{
r_i} \bmod p_i$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1 \pmod{2}$ | $3$ | $2$ | $2$ |
| $2 \pmod{4}$ | $5$ | $4$ | $4$ |
| $4 \pmod{8}$ | $17$ | $8$ | $16$ |
| $8 \pmod{16}$ | $257$ | $16$ | $256$ |
| $16 \pmod{32}$ | $65537$ | $32$ | $65536 \equiv -1$ |
| $32 \pmod{64}$ | $641$ | $64$ | $640 \equiv -1$ |
| $0 \pmod{64}$ | $6700417$ | $64$ | $1$ |

**Covering verification:** Every positive integer $b$ is either odd ($\equiv 1 \
pmod 2$), or $b = 2^v \cdot u$ ($u$ odd, $v \geq 1$). Then $v = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ g
ive classes $2 \pmod 4$, $4 \pmod 8$, $8 \pmod{16}$, $16 \pmod{32}$, $32 \pmod{6
4}$ respectively, and $v \geq 6$ gives $0 \pmod{64}$. ✓

**Order verification:** The primes $3, 5, 17, 257, 65537$ are Fermat primes with
 $\mathrm{ord}_{F_k}(2) = 2^{k+1}$. The primes $641$ and $6700417$ are the two f
actors of $F_5 = 2^{32}+1$ (Euler's factorization), so $2^{32} \equiv -1$ modulo
 each, giving order $64$. In each case $\mathrm{ord}_{p_i}(2) \mid q_i$. ✓

### Step 3: Chinese Remainder Theorem

By CRT (all seven moduli are distinct primes), there exists $m_0$ such that:

$$m \equiv 2 \pmod{3},\quad m \equiv 4 \pmod{5},\quad m \equiv 16 \pmod{17},\qua
d m \equiv 256 \pmod{257},$$
$$m \equiv 65536 \pmod{65537},\quad m \equiv 640 \pmod{641},\quad m \equiv 1 \pm
od{6700417}.$$

The modulus $M = 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 17 \cdot 257 \cdot 65537 \cdot 641 \cdot 670041
7$ is **odd**, so among the solutions $m = m_0 + kM$ ($k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$), in
finitely many are **odd** (parity alternates since $M$ is odd).

### Step 4: Covering argument

Let $m$ be an odd solution with $m$ large, and set $n = m + 1$ (even). For any $
b \geq 1$ with $2^b < m$:

1. $b$ falls in class $i$, so $b \equiv r_i \pmod{q_i}$.
2. Since $\mathrm{ord}_{p_i}(2) \mid q_i$: $2^b \equiv 2^{r_i} \pmod{p_i}$.
3. Since $m \equiv 2^{r_i} \pmod{p_i}$: $p_i \mid (m - 2^b)$.

Now $m$ is odd and $2^b$ is even (as $b \geq 1$), so $m - 2^b$ is **odd and posi
tive**. Since $p_i \mid (m - 2^b)$ and $m - 2^b > 0$, we get $m - 2^b \geq p_i \
geq 3$.

- If $m - 2^b > p_i$: then $m - 2^b$ is a proper multiple of $p_i$, hence **comp
osite**. ✓
- If $m - 2^b = p_i$: then $m = 2^b + p_i$, and $m - 2^b = p_i$ is prime — this
is the only **bad case**.

### Step 5: Handling condition (M1) — $m$ is composite

Since $m \equiv 2 \pmod{3}$ and $m > 3$ (for large $m$), we have $3 \mid m$ and
$m > 3$, so **$m$ is composite**. Thus $n - 1 = m$ is not prime, and condition (
M1) fails automatically. ✓

### Step 6: Avoiding bad values

The bad values are $m = 2^b + p_i$ for some class $i$ and valid $b$. For each cl
ass, the number of valid $b$ with $2^b \leq N$ is $O(\log N)$, giving $O(\log N)
$ bad values total up to $N$.

The sparse set (S) contributes $m = 1 + 2^a$ or $m = 1 + 2^a + 2^b$, which is $O
((\log N)^2)$ values up to $N$.

Our arithmetic progression has $\Theta(N/M)$ elements up to $N$. Since $O(\log N
) + O((\log N)^2) = o(N/M)$, for sufficiently large $N$, **most** elements of th
e progression are good. In particular, **infinitely many** odd $m$ in the progre
ssion avoid all bad values.

### Step 7: Conclusion

For each such good odd $m$ (with $m$ large), set $n = m + 1$. Then:

- $n$ is even and $n \geq 4$. ✓
- **(M1) fails:** $n - 1 = m$ is composite (divisible by 3, $m > 3$). ✓
- **(M2) fails:** For every $b \geq 1$ with $2^b < m$, $m - 2^b$ is divisible by
 $p_i \geq 3$ and $m - 2^b > p_i$ (since $m$ is not bad), so $m - 2^b = n - 1 -
2^b$ is composite. ✓
- **(S) fails:** $m$ avoids $1 + 2^a$ and $1 + 2^a + 2^b$, so $n$ is not of the
form $2 + 2^a$ or $2 + 2^a + 2^b$. ✓

Therefore $n \notin \mathrm{sumPrimeAndTwoPows}(2)$, and since there are infinit
ely many such $n$, the set

$$\{n \in \mathbb{N} : n \text{ is even}\} \setminus \mathrm{sumPrimeAndTwoPows}
(2)$$

is infinite. $\blacksquare$
