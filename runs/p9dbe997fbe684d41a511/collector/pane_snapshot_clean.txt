**Proof.**

Let $S = \{a_1, a_2, \dots, a_{10}\}$ be a set of ten distinct two-digit numbers
, so each $a_i \in \{10, 11, \dots, 99\}$.

Consider all subsets of $S$. There are $2^{10} = 1024$ subsets in total. For eac
h subset $T \subseteq S$, define its sum $\sigma(T) = \sum_{a \in T} a$.

**Bounding the possible sums.** The empty subset has sum $0$. Every non-empty su
bset has sum at least $10$ (since the smallest element is at least $10$) and at
most $10 \cdot 99 = 990$ (since there are at most $10$ elements, each at most $9
9$). Thus every subset sum lies in the range
$$0 \le \sigma(T) \le 990,$$
which gives at most $991$ distinct possible values.

**Applying the pigeonhole principle.** We have $1024$ subsets but only $991$ pos
sible sum values. Since $1024 > 991$, by the pigeonhole principle there exist tw
o **distinct** subsets $A, B \subseteq S$ with
$$\sigma(A) = \sigma(B).$$

**Making them disjoint.** Set $C = A \setminus B$ and $D = B \setminus A$. Then
$C$ and $D$ are disjoint. Moreover,
$$\sigma(C) = \sigma(A) - \sigma(A \cap B) = \sigma(B) - \sigma(A \cap B) = \sig
ma(D),$$
so $C$ and $D$ have the same sum.

**Both are non-empty.** Suppose for contradiction that $C = A \setminus B = \emp
tyset$. Then $A \subseteq B$. Since $A \neq B$, we have $A \subsetneq B$, so $D
= B \setminus A$ is non-empty. But then
$$\sigma(D) = \sigma(B) - \sigma(A) = 0,$$
which is impossible because $D$ is a non-empty subset of two-digit (hence positi
ve) numbers, so $\sigma(D) \ge 10 > 0$. Contradiction. Hence $C \neq \emptyset$,
 and by the symmetric argument $D \neq \emptyset$.

Therefore $C = A \setminus B$ and $D = B \setminus A$ are two **non-empty, disjo
int** subsets of $S$ whose members have the same sum. $\blacksquare$
