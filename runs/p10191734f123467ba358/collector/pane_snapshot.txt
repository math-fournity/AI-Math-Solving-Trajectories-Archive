## Solution

**The answer is NO.** The capacity ordering does *not* guarantee a continuous in
jection into a finite-radius ball. The obstruction is topological: metric capaci
ty controls only cardinality-at-a-scale, not connectedness.

---

### Counterexample

Let
- $X = [0,1]$ with the usual metric $d_X(s,t)=|s-t|$ (compact, nonempty),
- $Y = \mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}$ with the discrete metric $d(m,n)=1$ for $m\ne
q n$ and $d(m,m)=0$ (nonempty).

Use the standard definition of metric capacity as the packing number
$$\kappa_Z(\varepsilon)=\sup\bigl\{|S|:S\subseteq Z,\ S\text{ is }\varepsilon\te
xt{-separated}\bigr\},\qquad \varepsilon\in(0,1].$$

**Capacity condition holds.** For $X=[0,1]$, any $\varepsilon$-separated set has
 at most $\lfloor 1/\varepsilon\rfloor+1$ points, so $\kappa_X(\varepsilon)\le 1
/\varepsilon+1<\infty$. For $Y=\mathbb{N}$ with the discrete metric, every two d
istinct points are at distance $1\ge\varepsilon$, so *every* subset of $Y$ is $\
varepsilon$-separated; hence $\kappa_Y(\varepsilon)=|\mathbb{N}|=\aleph_0$. Ther
efore
$$\kappa_X(\varepsilon)\le\aleph_0=\kappa_Y(\varepsilon)\quad\text{for all }\var
epsilon\in(0,1].$$

(The same conclusion holds if "metric capacity" is instead interpreted as the co
vering number: covering $[0,1]$ needs $\asymp 1/\varepsilon$ balls, finite, whil
e covering infinite discrete $\mathbb{N}$ with $\varepsilon$-balls ($\varepsilon
<1$) needs $\aleph_0$ balls; the inequality is preserved.)

**No continuous injection into any ball exists.** With the discrete metric, ever
y metric ball in $Y$ is either a singleton or the whole space:
$$B(m_0,r)=\{m:d(m,m_0)<r\}=\begin{cases}\{m_0\},& r\le 1,\\ \mathbb{N},& r>1.\e
nd{cases}$$
(Closed balls behave identically: singleton for $r<1$, all of $\mathbb{N}$ for $
r\ge 1$.) In either case the ball is totally disconnected — it is either a singl
e point or a subset of a discrete space.

Suppose $f:X\to B\subseteq Y$ is continuous, where $B$ is some finite-radius bal
l. Since $X=[0,1]$ is connected, its continuous image $f(X)$ is a connected subs
et of $B$. But the only connected subsets of a totally disconnected space are si
ngletons, so $f(X)=\{c\}$ for some $c$; i.e. $f$ is constant. A constant map can
not be injective because $|X|>1$.

Thus **no** continuous injection of $X$ into any finite-radius ball in $Y$ exist
s, despite the capacity ordering $\kappa_X(\varepsilon)\le\kappa_Y(\varepsilon)$
 holding for every $\varepsilon\in(0,1]$.

---

### Why the implication fails (structural reason)

Metric capacity is a purely *cardinal*, scale-by-scale quantity: it records how
many points can be mutually separated at distance $\ge\varepsilon$. It is blind
to the topology of $Y$ — in particular to whether $Y$ is connected, totally disc
onnected, or has nontrivial components. A continuous injection from a *connected
* compact space must land in a connected subset of the target ball; capacity say
s nothing about whether such connected subsets exist with enough room. The discr
ete target $Y$ has unlimited capacity at every scale yet contains no nontrivial
connected subset, so it admits no continuous injection from $[0,1]$ into any of
its balls.

Hence the proposed implication is **false**.
