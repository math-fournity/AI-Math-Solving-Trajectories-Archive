## Proof

We classify entire functions by the nature of their singularity at infinity.

**Case 1: Removable singularity at infinity.** Then $f$ is bounded, hence consta
nt by Liouville's theorem. A constant $f \equiv c$ attains the value $c$ at *eve
ry* point of $\mathbb{C}$, i.e. infinitely many times, violating the hypothesis
that every value is taken no more than three times. So this case is excluded.

**Case 2: Essential singularity at infinity.** Then $f$ is a transcendental enti
re function. By the **Great Picard Theorem**, in every neighborhood of $\infty$
(equivalently, outside every sufficiently large disk), $f$ takes every complex v
alue infinitely often, with at most one exception. In particular, there is at le
ast one value (indeed all but possibly one) that $f$ attains at infinitely many
distinct points. This contradicts the hypothesis that every value is taken no mo
re than three times. So this case is also excluded.

**Case 3: Pole at infinity.** Then $f$ is a polynomial. Let $\deg f = n \geq 1$
(nonconstant, since constants were excluded in Case 1). For any $w \in \mathbb{C
}$, the equation $f(z) = w$ is equivalent to $f(z) - w = 0$, a polynomial equati
on of degree $n$. By the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, it has exactly $n$ root
s counting multiplicity, hence at most $n$ distinct roots. The hypothesis "every
 value is taken no more than three times" therefore forces $n \leq 3$.

Conversely, every polynomial of degree $n \leq 3$ satisfies the condition: for e
ach $w$, the equation $f(z)=w$ has at most $n \leq 3$ solutions (counting multip
licity, and hence at most $n \leq 3$ distinct solutions).

**Conclusion.** The entire functions that take every value no more than three ti
mes are precisely the nonconstant polynomials of degree at most three:

$$\boxed{f(z) = az^3 + bz^2 + cz + d, \quad a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{C},\ a \neq 0 \t
ext{ (cubic)}, \text{ or degree } 1 \text{ or } 2.}$$

Equivalently, $f$ is a polynomial with $1 \leq \deg f \leq 3$.
