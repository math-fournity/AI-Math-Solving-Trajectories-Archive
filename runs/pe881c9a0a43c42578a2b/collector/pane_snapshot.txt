## Part 1: Evaluation of $\int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-itx}\,dx$

**Classical convergence fails.** Since $|e^{-itx}|=1$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$,
the integral does not converge absolutely. As an improper Riemann integral,

$$\int_{-R}^{R} e^{-itx}\,dx = \frac{e^{-itR}-e^{itR}}{-it}=\frac{2\sin(tR)}{t},
$$

which diverges at $t=0$ and oscillates without a limit for every $t\neq 0$. Henc
e the integral has no classical value.

**Distributional evaluation.** We interpret the integral as a tempered distribut
ion in $t$. Introduce the Gaussian regularizer

$$I_\varepsilon(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-\varepsilon x^{2}}e^{-itx}\,dx=\sqrt{\
tfrac{\pi}{\varepsilon}}\,e^{-t^{2}/(4\varepsilon)},\qquad \varepsilon>0.$$

For every Schwartz test function $\psi$,

$$\langle I_\varepsilon,\psi\rangle=\sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{\varepsilon}}\int_{\mathbb
{R}} e^{-t^{2}/(4\varepsilon)}\psi(t)\,dt \xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}} 2\p
i\,\psi(0)=\langle 2\pi\delta_0,\psi\rangle,$$

because $g_\varepsilon(t):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\varepsilon}}e^{-t^{2}/(4\varepsil
on)}$ is a standard approximate identity with $\int g_\varepsilon=1$, so $\sqrt{
\pi/\varepsilon}\,e^{-t^{2}/(4\varepsilon)}=2\pi\,g_\varepsilon(t)\to 2\pi\delta
_0$ in $\mathcal{S}'$. Therefore

$$\boxed{\int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-itx}\,dx = 2\pi\,\delta(t)\quad\text{in }\mathcal
{S}'(\mathbb{R}).}$$

## Part 2: Convergence Discussion

- **Absolute convergence:** impossible since the integrand has modulus $1$.
- **Improper (symmetric) convergence:** $\frac{2\sin(tR)}{t}$ has no limit as $R
\to\infty$ for $t\neq 0$, and blows up at $t=0$.
- **Distributional convergence:** the regularized family $I_\varepsilon$ converg
es in $\mathcal{S}'$ to $2\pi\delta$. This is the only meaningful sense in which
 the integral "exists."

Equivalently, the symmetric partial integral $\frac{2\sin(tR)}{t}$ is itself a d
istributional approximation: $\frac{2\sin(tR)}{t}\to 2\pi\delta$ in $\mathcal{S}
'$ as $R\to\infty$ (the Dirichlet kernel identity), giving the same result witho
ut regularization.

## Part 3: Relation to Density Recovery from $\phi(t)$

The characteristic function of a random variable $X$ with density $f$ is

$$\phi(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{itX}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{ity}f(y)\,dy,$$

i.e. $\phi$ is the Fourier transform of $f$. The **Fourier inversion formula**

$$f(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-itx}\phi(t)\,dt \tag{$\ast$}$$

is derived *exactly* by the distributional identity of Part 1:

$$\frac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{-itx}\phi(t)\,dt=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{-itx}\!\left(\int
 e^{ity}f(y)\,dy\!\right)dt=\int f(y)\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{it(
y-x)}\,dt\right)}_{=\,\delta(y-x)}dy=f(x).$$

Thus $\int e^{-itx}dx=2\pi\delta(t)$ is the **inversion kernel**: it is the iden
tity that turns "Fourier transform of the density" back into the density. Withou
t the distributional interpretation, the inversion formula ($\ast$) could not ev
en be stated for general $\phi$.

## Part 4: The Density for $\phi(t)=\tfrac14+\tfrac14 e^{-it}+\tfrac12\cdot\tfra
c{3}{4-e^{2it}}$

We invert $\phi$ using ($\ast$), or equivalently by recognizing each term as the
 characteristic function of a known distribution.

**Term 1:** $\frac14\cdot 1$ is $\frac14$ times the characteristic function of t
he point mass at $0$, i.e. $\frac14\,\delta_0$.

**Term 2:** $\frac14 e^{-it}=\frac14\,\mathbb{E}[e^{it(-1)}]$ is $\frac14$ times
 the point mass at $-1$, i.e. $\frac14\,\delta_{-1}$.

**Term 3:** Since $|e^{2it}/4|=\tfrac14<1$,

$$\frac{3}{4-e^{2it}}=\frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{1-e^{2it}/4}=\frac{3}{4}\sum_{n=0
}^{\infty}\frac{e^{2int}}{4^{n}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{3}{4^{n+1}}\,e^{2int}.
$$

This is the characteristic function of a discrete distribution placing mass $\fr
ac{3}{4^{n+1}}$ at $2n$, $n=0,1,2,\dots$ (a geometric distribution on the even n
on-negative integers). The masses sum to $\frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{1-1/4}=1$, co
nfirming validity. Multiplying by $\frac12$ gives the third component of the mix
ture.

**Combining (as a generalized density / probability mass function).** The point
$0$ receives mass from both Term 1 and Term 3 (at $n=0$):

$$P(X=0)=\tfrac14+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac34=\tfrac14+\tfrac38=\tfrac58.$$

The point $-1$ receives mass only from Term 2:

$$P(X=-1)=\tfrac14.$$

For $n\geq 1$, the point $2n$ receives mass only from Term 3:

$$P(X=2n)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{3}{4^{n+1}}=\tfrac{3}{2\cdot 4^{n+1}},\qquad n=1,2
,3,\dots$$

**Verification of total mass:**

$$\tfrac58+\tfrac14+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\tfrac{3}{2\cdot 4^{n+1}}=\tfrac78+\tfrac
{3}{2}\cdot\frac{1/16}{1-1/4}=\tfrac78+\tfrac{3}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{12}=\tfrac78+\
tfrac18=1.\;\checkmark$$

**Generalized density (distributional):**

$$f(x)=\tfrac58\,\delta(x)+\tfrac14\,\delta(x+1)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\tfrac{3}{2\
cdot 4^{n+1}}\,\delta(x-2n).$$

**Interpretation.** $X$ is a mixture of three sub-distributions:
- with probability $\frac14$, $X\equiv 0$;
- with probability $\frac14$, $X\equiv -1$;
- with probability $\frac12$, $X$ is geometric on $\{0,2,4,6,\dots\}$ with $P(X=
2n)=\frac{3}{4^{n+1}}$.

The overlap at $0$ merges the first sub-distribution's atom with the $n=0$ atom
of the third, yielding the combined mass $\frac58$.

1. $\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-itx}dx$ diverges classically but equals $2\pi\delta(t)$

2. This distributional identity is precisely the Fourier inversion kernel $\frac

and $P(X=2n)=\frac{3}{2\cdot 4^{n+1}}$ for $n\geq 1$.
