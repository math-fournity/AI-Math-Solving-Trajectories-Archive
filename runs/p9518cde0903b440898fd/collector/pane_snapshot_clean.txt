We prove that for every integer $n>1$ there exists an $n$-digit *pal* number (no
 zero digit, and the sum of the squares of its digits is a perfect square).

**Small cases.** We exhibit explicit examples:

- $n=2$: $34$, since $3^2+4^2=9+16=25=5^2$.
- $n=3$: $244$, since $2^2+4^2+4^2=4+16+16=36=6^2$.
- $n=4$: $1111$, since $1^2+1^2+1^2+1^2=4=2^2$.
- $n=5$: $11123$, since $1^2+1^2+1^2+2^2+3^2=1+1+1+4+9=16=4^2$.

All have no zero digit, so they are pal.

**The construction for $n\ge 6$.** We use only the digits $1,2,3,4$, whose squar
ed contributions are $1,4,9,16$. Suppose a candidate number has $a$ digits equal
 to $1$, $b$ equal to $2$, $c$ equal to $3$, $e$ equal to $4$, with $a+b+c+e=n$.
 Its digit-square-sum is
$$
a+4b+9c+16e=(n-b-c-e)+4b+9c+16e=n+3b+8c+15e.
$$
Thus we need nonnegative integers $b,c,e$ with $b+c+e\le n$ such that $n+3b+8c+1
5e$ is a perfect square. Equivalently, setting $T=3b+8c+15e$, we need $T\ge 0$,
$T\le 8n$ (so that $b+c+e\le n$ is achievable, as shown below), and $n+T$ a perf
ect square.

**A coin lemma.** Every integer $T\ge 14$ can be written as $T=8c+3b+15e$ with $
b,c,e\ge 0$ and $b+c+e\le \lfloor T/8\rfloor+3$.

*Proof of the coin lemma.* Write $T=8q+r$ with $q=\lfloor T/8\rfloor\ge 1$ and $
r\in\{0,\dots,7\}$. We give a representation for each remainder (all are checked
 by direct substitution):

| $r$ | representation | count $b+c+e$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | $8q$ | $q$ |
| 1 | $8(q-1)+3\cdot 3$ | $q+2$ |
| 2 | $15+8(q-2)+3\cdot 1$ | $q$ |
| 3 | $8q+3\cdot 1$ | $q+1$ |
| 4 | $8(q-1)+3\cdot 4$ | $q+3$ |
| 5 | $15+8(q-2)+3\cdot 2$ | $q+1$ |
| 6 | $8q+3\cdot 2$ | $q+2$ |
| 7 | $8(q-1)+15$ | $q$ |

For $r\in\{2,5\}$ we need $q\ge 2$, which holds because the smallest $T\ge 14$ i
n those classes is $18$ and $21$ respectively. The largest count is $q+3=\lfloor
 T/8\rfloor+3$. $\square$

**Choosing $T$.** We claim that for every $n\ge 6$ the interval $[n+14,\,9n-24]$
 contains a perfect square. Indeed, its length is $8n-38$, and the next perfect
square after $n+14$ is at most $(\lceil\sqrt{n+14}\rceil)^2 < n+15+2\sqrt{n+14}$
. The inequality $n+15+2\sqrt{n+14}\le 9n-24$, i.e. $2\sqrt{n+14}\le 8n-39$, hol
ds at $n=6$ (since $2\sqrt{20}\approx 8.944\le 9$) and its two sides diverge the
reafter (the right side grows linearly, the left as $\sqrt n$). Hence a perfect
square $S^2\in[n+14,\,9n-24]$ exists.

Set $T=S^2-n$. Then $14\le T\le 8n-24$. By the coin lemma, $T=8c+3b+15e$ with $b
+c+e\le \lfloor T/8\rfloor+3\le (8n-24)/8+3=n$. Put $a=n-b-c-e\ge 0$. Form the $
n$-digit number consisting of $a$ copies of $1$, $b$ copies of $2$, $c$ copies o
f $3$, and $e$ copies of $4$ (in any order, say all the $1$'s first). It has no
zero digit, and its digit-square-sum is
$$
a+4b+9c+16e=n+3b+8c+15e=n+T=S^2,
$$
a perfect square. Hence it is pal.

Combining the explicit examples for $n=2,3,4,5$ with the construction for $n\ge
6$, we conclude that a pal number with $n$ digits exists for every $n>1$.
