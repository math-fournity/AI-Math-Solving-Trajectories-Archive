Looking at this problem, I need to find all integers $k$ such that there are inf
initely many integer triples $(x,y,z)$ with $\frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{y}+\frac{1}{z}
=\frac{k}{xyz}$ and $\gcd(k,xyz)=1$.

## Proof

**Reformulation.** The equation $\frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{y}+\frac{1}{z}=\frac{k}{xy
z}$ (with $x,y,z\neq 0$) is equivalent to
$$xy+yz+xz=k.$$
We seek all integers $k$ for which infinitely many integer triples $(x,y,z)$ sat
isfy $xy+yz+xz=k$ and $\gcd(k,xyz)=1$.

---

**Step 1: Even $k$ is impossible.**

Suppose $2\mid k$ and $(x,y,z)$ is a solution with $\gcd(k,xyz)=1$. Since $2\mid
 k$, we need $\gcd(k,xyz)=1$, which forces $2\nmid xyz$, so $x,y,z$ are all odd.
 But then $xy+yz+xz\equiv 1+1+1\equiv 1\pmod{2}$, contradicting $2\mid k$. There
fore **no solutions exist when $k$ is even**.

---

**Step 2: Odd $k$ yields infinitely many solutions.**

Assume $k$ is odd. We use the substitution $(x+z)(y+z)=z^2+k$. For any integer $
z$, taking the factorization $1\cdot(z^2+k)$ gives:
$$x=1-z,\qquad y=z^2+k-z,\qquad z=z.$$
One verifies directly: $xy+yz+xz=(z^2+k-z)\bigl[(1-z)+z\bigr]+z(1-z)=(z^2+k-z)+z
-z^2=k.$

We need $x,y,z\neq 0$ and $\gcd(k,xyz)=1$. The non-vanishing conditions exclude
only finitely many $z$ (namely $z=0$, $z=1$, and the at most two integer roots o
f $z^2-z+k=0$).

For the coprimality condition, note:
$$xyz = (1-z)(z^2-z+k)\cdot z.$$
If a prime $p\mid k$ also divides $xyz$, then $p\mid z$ or $p\mid(z-1)$ or $p\mi
d(z^2-z+k)$. But $p\mid k$ and $p\mid(z^2-z+k)$ implies $p\mid z(z-1)$, i.e., $p
\mid z$ or $p\mid(z-1)$. So $\gcd(k,xyz)=1$ is equivalent to:
$$\gcd(k,z)=1 \quad\text{and}\quad \gcd(k,z-1)=1.$$

Let $p_1,\dots,p_m$ be the prime factors of $k$. Since $k$ is odd, every $p_i\ge
q 3$. For each $p_i$, the conditions $z\not\equiv 0\pmod{p_i}$ and $z\not\equiv
1\pmod{p_i}$ leave $p_i-2\geq 1$ residue classes. By the Chinese Remainder Theor
em, there are infinitely many integers $z$ satisfying all these congruence condi
tions simultaneously. After excluding the finitely many values where $x$, $y$, o
r $z$ vanishes, infinitely many valid $z$ remain.

Each such $z$ produces a distinct triple $(1-z,\; z^2+k-z,\; z)$ satisfying $xy+
yz+xz=k$ and $\gcd(k,xyz)=1$. Hence there are **infinitely many solutions**.

---

**Conclusion.** Even $k$ admits no solutions at all; odd $k$ admits infinitely m
any. Therefore the set of all possible values of $k$ is:

$$\boxed{k \text{ is any odd integer, i.e., } k\equiv 1\pmod{2}.}$$
