## Analysis

**Setup.** $E, F$ Banach; $f: [0,1]\times E \to F$ is $C^1$ (i.e., $Df$ exists a
nd is continuous, so $\partial_1 f$ and $\partial_2 f$ exist and are continuous)
. Define $g: E \to C^1([0,1],F)$, $g(y) = f(\cdot, y)$. Since $\partial_1 f$ is
continuous, $t \mapsto f(t,y)$ is indeed $C^1$, so $g$ is well-defined as a map
into $C^1$.

**The candidate.** The proposed derivative at $y$ in direction $h$ is $(L_y h)(t
) = \partial_2 f(t,y)\,h$.

**Obstruction.** For $L_y: E \to C^1([0,1],F)$ to even be a bounded linear map,
we need $L_y h \in C^1([0,1],F)$ for each $h$, i.e., $t \mapsto \partial_2 f(t,y
)\,h$ must be $C^1$ in $t$. Its $t$-derivative is $(\partial_1\partial_2 f)(t,y)
\,h$, which requires the mixed partial $\partial_1\partial_2 f$ to exist. But $f
 \in C^1$ guarantees only **first** partials; second partials need not exist.

## Part 1 — Positive result: $g: E \to C([0,1],F)$ is differentiable

Equip $C([0,1],F)$ with the sup norm $\|u\|_\infty = \sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|u(t)\|_F$
.

**Claim.** $g: E \to C([0,1],F)$ is Fréchet differentiable at every $y\in E$, wi
th $(Dg(y)\,h)(t) = \partial_2 f(t,y)\,h$.

**Proof.** Fix $y \in E$. Define $L_y h \in C([0,1],F)$ by $(L_y h)(t) = \partia
l_2 f(t,y)\,h$. This is bounded linear $E \to C([0,1],F)$: $\|L_y h\|_\infty \le
 \sup_t \|\partial_2 f(t,y)\|\,\|h\|_E < \infty$ since $\partial_2 f$ is continu
ous on the compact $\{y\}\times[0,1]$.

Let $r(h)(t) = f(t,y+h) - f(t,y) - \partial_2 f(t,y)\,h$. We must show $\|r(h)\|
_\infty / \|h\|_E \to 0$.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus applied to $s \mapsto f(t, y+sh)$ (valid
since $\partial_2 f$ is continuous):

$$r(h)(t) = \int_0^1 \bigl[\partial_2 f(t,\,y+sh) - \partial_2 f(t,\,y)\bigr]\,h
\;ds.$$

Hence
$$\|r(h)(t)\|_F \le \sup_{s\in[0,1]}\|\partial_2 f(t,\,y+sh) - \partial_2 f(t,\,
y)\|\;\|h\|_E.$$

Since $\partial_2 f$ is continuous on $[0,1]\times E$, it is **uniformly continu
ous** on the compact set $[0,1]\times \overline{B}(y, R)$ for any $R>0$. For $\|
h\|_E \le R$ and $s\in[0,1]$, the points $(t, y+sh)$ and $(t,y)$ both lie in thi
s compact set, so

$$\sup_{t\in[0,1],\, s\in[0,1]}\|\partial_2 f(t,\,y+sh) - \partial_2 f(t,\,y)\|
\;\xrightarrow{\|h\|\to 0}\; 0.$$

Therefore $\|r(h)\|_\infty \le \omega(\|h\|)\,\|h\|_E$ with $\omega(\|h\|)\to 0$
, giving $\|r(h)\|_\infty/\|h\|_E \to 0$. ∎

## Part 2 — Negative result: $g: E \to C^1([0,1],F)$ is not necessarily differen
tiable

**Counterexample.** Let $E = F = \mathbb{R}$. Define
$$f(t, y) = |t + y - 1|^{3/2}, \qquad (t,y)\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}.$$

**$f \in C^1$.** We have $\partial_1 f = \partial_2 f = \varphi'(t+y-1)$ where $
\varphi(u) = |u|^{3/2}$ and $\varphi'(u) = \tfrac{3}{2}|u|^{1/2}\operatorname{sg
n}(u)$, which is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ (including at $u=0$, where $\varphi'
(0)=0$). Thus $Df$ exists and is continuous.

**The candidate fails to land in $C^1$.** At $y_0 = \tfrac{1}{2}$:
$$(L_{y_0}\,h)(t) = \partial_2 f(t,\tfrac12)\,h = \tfrac{3}{2}|t-\tfrac12|^{1/2}
\operatorname{sgn}(t-\tfrac12)\,h.$$
This function of $t$ is **not** differentiable at $t = \tfrac12$: its difference
 quotient behaves like $|t-\tfrac12|^{-1/2}$, which diverges. Hence $L_{y_0}\,h
\notin C^1([0,1])$, so $L_{y_0}$ is not even a map $E \to C^1([0,1])$.

**$g$ is not differentiable at $y_0 = \tfrac12$ (with any derivative).** Suppose
 for contradiction that $g: \mathbb{R} \to C^1([0,1])$ were Fréchet differentiab
le at $y_0$ with derivative $D: \mathbb{R} \to C^1([0,1])$. Then for each $h$,
$$\left\|\,\bigl(f(\cdot,\,y_0+h) - f(\cdot,y_0) - Dh\bigr)\,\right\|_{C^1} = o(
|h|).$$
In particular $\|f(\cdot,y_0+h) - f(\cdot,y_0) - Dh\|_\infty = o(|h|)$. For each
 fixed $t \neq \tfrac12$, the map $y \mapsto f(t,y)$ is differentiable at $y_0$
with derivative $\partial_2 f(t,y_0)$, so pointwise $(Dh)(t) = \partial_2 f(t,y_
0)\,h = \tfrac{3}{2}|t-\tfrac12|^{1/2}\operatorname{sgn}(t-\tfrac12)\,h$. But $D
h \in C^1([0,1])$ by assumption, while the function $t \mapsto \tfrac{3}{2}|t-\t
frac12|^{1/2}\operatorname{sgn}(t-\tfrac12)\,h$ is **not** in $C^1([0,1])$ — con
tradiction. ∎

## Conclusion

The formula $(\partial g(y)\,h)(t) = \partial_2 f(t,y)\,h$ is **correct** when $
g$ is regarded as a map
$$g: E \longrightarrow C\bigl([0,1],\,F\bigr) \quad (\text{sup norm}),$$
and $g$ is Fréchet differentiable there (Part 1).

However, the statement is **false** as posed for $g: E \to C^1([0,1],F)$. Under
the hypothesis $f \in C^1$ alone, the mixed partial $\partial_1\partial_2 f$ nee
d not exist, so:

1. The candidate $(L_y h)(t) = \partial_2 f(t,y)\,h$ may not even belong to $C^1
([0,1],F)$ (it fails to be differentiable in $t$), and
2. $g: E \to C^1([0,1],F)$ need not be differentiable at all (Part 2 counterexam
ple).

To make the statement true for $g: E \to C^1([0,1],F)$, one must strengthen the
hypothesis to $f \in C^2$ (or at least require $\partial_1\partial_2 f$ to exist
 and be continuous), so that $L_y h \in C^1$ and the remainder can be controlled
 in the $C^1$ norm.
