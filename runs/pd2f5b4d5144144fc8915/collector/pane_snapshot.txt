## Proof

**Step 1 — Reduction to angles.** Four points are thrown uniformly into the disk
. By rotational symmetry, each point's polar angle is uniform on $[0,2\pi)$, and
 the four angles are independent. Since a sector is defined by angles from the c
enter, which sector contains a point depends only on its angle, not its radius.
Thus the problem reduces to four i.i.d. uniform angles on $[0,2\pi)$.

**Step 2 — Parametrizing the division.** A division into four identical (90°) se
ctors is determined by an angle $\theta$, giving sectors $[\theta + k\pi/2,\;\th
eta+(k+1)\pi/2)$ for $k=0,1,2,3$. Rotating $\theta$ by $\pi/2$ merely permutes t
he sectors, so we take $\theta\in[0,\pi/2)$.

For each point $i$ with angle $\alpha_i$, define:
- $\beta_i = \alpha_i \bmod (\pi/2)\in[0,\pi/2)$ — the **crossing time**,
- $q_i = \lfloor \alpha_i/(\pi/2)\rfloor + 1 \in\{1,2,3,4\}$ — the **initial qua
drant** at $\theta=0$.

Since $\alpha_i$ is uniform on $[0,2\pi)$, the pair $(\beta_i, q_i)$ has $\beta_
i$ uniform on $[0,\pi/2)$ and $q_i$ uniform on $\{1,2,3,4\}$, independently; the
 four pairs are i.i.d.

**Step 3 — Crossing process.** As $\theta$ increases from $0$ to $\pi/2$, point
$i$ crosses a boundary at $\theta=\beta_i$, moving from quadrant $q_i$ to $q_i-1
\pmod 4$ (with $1\to 4$). The crossing order is a uniformly random permutation (
the $\beta_i$ are i.i.d. continuous), **independent** of the $q_i$.

Relabel points by crossing order: point 1 crosses first, …, point 4 crosses last
. The $q_i$ remain i.i.d. uniform on $\{1,2,3,4\}$. Set $r_i = q_i - 1 \in \{0,1
,2,3\}$.

After $k$ crossings ($k=0,1,2,3,4$), point $i$ is at position
$$P_i(k) = r_i + 3\cdot\mathbf{1}[i\le k] \pmod 4,$$
i.e., points $1,\dots,k$ have shifted by $-1\pmod 4$, the rest are unchanged. We
 seek $\Pr\bigl[\exists\, k\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}:\;P_1(k),P_2(k),P_3(k),P_4(k)\text{
all distinct}\bigr]$.

**Step 4 — The events $E_k$ are disjoint.** Let $E_k$ = "the four positions afte
r $k$ crossings are all distinct" for $k=0,1,2,3$ (note $E_4=E_0$ since all poin
ts shift equally). If $E_k$ holds, the four positions form a permutation of $\{0
,1,2,3\}$, so their sum is $0+1+2+3=6\equiv 2\pmod 4$. But the sum equals $\sum
r_i + 3k\pmod 4$. Hence $E_k$ forces
$$\sum r_i + 3k \equiv 2 \pmod 4,$$
which uniquely determines $k\pmod 4$. Therefore $E_0,E_1,E_2,E_3$ are **mutually
 disjoint**.

**Step 5 — Probability of each $E_k$.** For fixed $k$, the four values $P_i(k)=
r_i + 3\cdot\mathbf{1}[i\le k]\pmod 4$ are i.i.d. uniform on $\{0,1,2,3\}$ (each
 $r_i$ is uniform and independent; adding a constant mod 4 preserves this). The
probability that four i.i.d. uniform draws from $\{0,1,2,3\}$ are all distinct i
s
$$\Pr(E_k) = \frac{4!}{4^4} = \frac{24}{256} = \frac{3}{32}.$$

**Step 6 — Conclusion.** By disjointness and symmetry,
$$\Pr(\text{success}) = \sum_{k=0}^{3}\Pr(E_k) = 4\cdot\frac{3}{32} = \frac{12}{
32} = \boxed{\dfrac{3}{8}}.$$
