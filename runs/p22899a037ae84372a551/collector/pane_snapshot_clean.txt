## Proof

**Yes**, such a set exists. We work in the Banach space $X = \mathbb{R}$ equippe
d with the usual norm $\|x\| = |x|$.

### Construction

Define an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{R}$ by
$$x \sim y \iff x - y \in \mathbb{Q}.$$
The equivalence class of $x$ is the coset $x + \mathbb{Q} = \{x + q : q \in \mat
hbb{Q}\}$, which is countable.

**The quotient $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}$ is uncountable.** Indeed, each coset is c
ountable, and $\mathbb{R}$ is the disjoint union of all cosets. If there were on
ly countably many cosets, then $\mathbb{R}$ would be a countable union of counta
ble sets, hence countable — a contradiction. (In fact $|\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}| =
 \mathfrak{c}$, since the quotient of a set of size $\mathfrak{c}$ by a countabl
e equivalence relation has size $\mathfrak{c}$.)

By the **Axiom of Choice**, there exists a set $S \subset \mathbb{R}$ that conta
ins exactly one representative from each equivalence class (a *transversal* of $
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}$). Since $S$ is in bijection with $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}$,
 it is uncountable (of cardinality $\mathfrak{c}$).

### Verification

Let $x, y \in S$ be distinct. Since $S$ contains at most one element from each c
oset, $x$ and $y$ lie in **different** equivalence classes, i.e. $x \not\sim y$.
 By definition of $\sim$, this means
$$x - y \notin \mathbb{Q}.$$

We claim $|x - y|$ is irrational. Observe the equivalence
$$|x - y| \in \mathbb{Q} \iff x - y \in \mathbb{Q}:$$
- ($\Leftarrow$) $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under absolute value.
- ($\Rightarrow$) If $|x-y| = q$ for some $q \in \mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}$, then $x -
 y = \pm q \in \mathbb{Q}$.

Since $x - y \notin \mathbb{Q}$, it follows that $|x - y| \notin \mathbb{Q}$, i.
e. $\|x - y\| = |x - y|$ is irrational.

### Conclusion

$S$ is an uncountable subset of the Banach space $\mathbb{R}$ such that for ever
y pair of distinct elements $x, y \in S$, the distance $\|x - y\| = |x - y|$ is
an irrational number. Hence such a set exists. $\blacksquare$
