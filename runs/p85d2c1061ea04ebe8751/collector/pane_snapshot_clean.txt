## Solution

Let
\[
I=\int_2^4 \frac{\sqrt{\ln(9-x)}}{\sqrt{\ln(9-x)}+\sqrt{\ln(x+3)}}\,dx.
\]

**Key observation.** The two arguments satisfy
\[
(9-x)+(x+3)=12,
\]
a constant. This suggests reflecting about the midpoint $x=3$ of the interval $[
2,4]$.

**Substitution.** Set $y=6-x$ (so $x=6-y$), giving $dx=-dy$. The limits map $x=2
\mapsto y=4$ and $x=4\mapsto y=2$. Under this substitution:
\[
9-x = 9-(6-y)=3+y=y+3,\qquad x+3=(6-y)+3=9-y.
\]
Hence
\[
I=\int_4^2 \frac{\sqrt{\ln(y+3)}}{\sqrt{\ln(y+3)}+\sqrt{\ln(9-y)}}\,(-dy)
   =\int_2^4 \frac{\sqrt{\ln(y+3)}}{\sqrt{\ln(y+3)}+\sqrt{\ln(9-y)}}\,dy.
\]
Renaming the dummy variable back to $x$:
\[
I=\int_2^4 \frac{\sqrt{\ln(x+3)}}{\sqrt{\ln(x+3)}+\sqrt{\ln(9-x)}}\,dx.
\]

**Adding the two forms.** Adding the original expression for $I$ and the reflect
ed one:
\[
2I=\int_2^4\frac{\sqrt{\ln(9-x)}+\sqrt{\ln(x+3)}}{\sqrt{\ln(9-x)}+\sqrt{\ln(x+3)
}}\,dx
   =\int_2^4 1\,dx = 2.
\]

**Well-definedness check.** On $[2,4]$: $9-x\in[5,7]$ and $x+3\in[5,7]$, so $\ln
(9-x)>0$ and $\ln(x+3)>0$. Both square roots are real and strictly positive, and
 the denominator never vanishes. The reflection and addition are therefore valid
.

Therefore
\[
\boxed{I=1}.
\]
