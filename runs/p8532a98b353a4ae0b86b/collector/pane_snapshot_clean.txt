## Solution

**Answer: Yes, it is necessarily true.**

Assume every coin has a positive integer value (the standard meaning of "worth a
n integer number of turgiks"). We prove the coins can always be split into three
 groups of total value $120$.

---

**Step 1. Most coins are 1‑turgik coins.**

Let $k$ be the number of coins of value $1$, and let the remaining $m=241-k$ coi
ns have value $\ge 2$. Their total value is at least $2m$, so

$$360 = k + (\text{sum of the } m \text{ larger coins}) \ge k + 2m = k + 2(241-k
) = 482 - k,$$

hence $k \ge 122$. So there are at least $122$ one‑turgik coins, and $m \le 119$
.

Let $W$ be the total value of the $m$ coins of value $\ge 2$. Then

$$W = 360 - k = 360 - (241-m) = 119 + m \le 119 + 119 = 238.$$

Each of these $m$ coins lies in $\{2,3,\dots,120\}$: the lower bound is by defin
ition, and the upper bound holds because the largest among them is at most $W-2(
m-1)=121-m\le 120$.

---

**Step 2. Reduction to a bin‑packing question.**

If we can partition the $m$ "large" coins (values $\ge 2$) into three groups who
se sums $s_1,s_2,s_3$ are each $\le 120$, then we fill group $i$ with exactly $d
_i = 120 - s_i \ge 0$ one‑turgik coins. The total number of one‑turgik coins nee
ded is

$$d_1+d_2+d_3 = 360 - (s_1+s_2+s_3) = 360 - W = k,$$

which is exactly the number of one‑turgik coins available. So the original probl
em reduces to:

> Can the $m$ coins of values in $\{2,\dots,120\}$, with total $W=119+m\le 238$,
 always be packed into three bins of capacity $120$?

---

**Step 3. At most two of the large coins are $\ge 61$.**

Suppose three of them were $\ge 61$. Their sum would be $\ge 183$, and the remai
ning $m-3$ coins contribute $\ge 2(m-3)$, so

$$W \ge 183 + 2(m-3) = 177 + 2m.$$

But $W = 119+m$, giving $119+m \ge 177+2m$, i.e. $m\le -58$, impossible. Hence *
*at most two** large coins have value $\ge 61$.

---

**Step 4. First‑Fit‑Decreasing never needs a fourth bin.**

Sort the $m$ large coins in decreasing order and place them greedily (First‑Fit‑
Decreasing) into three bins of capacity $120$. Suppose, for contradiction, that
some coin $a_j$ cannot be placed into any of the three bins.

Since $a_j$ does not fit in a bin, that bin's current load satisfies $\text{load
} \ge 121 - a_j$ (all values are integers). Thus the three bins together contain
 at least $3(121-a_j)=363-3a_j$ in total, and

$$W \ge (363 - 3a_j) + a_j = 363 - 2a_j.$$

Using $W\le 238$, we get $363 - 2a_j \le 238$, so $a_j \ge 63$.

All coins placed *before* $a_j$ have value $\ge a_j \ge 63$. A bin holding two o
f them would have load $\ge 126 > 120$, impossible; so each of the three bins ho
lds **exactly one** coin, and each of those three coins is $\ge 63$. Together wi
th $a_j$ itself, that makes **four** coins of value $\ge 63 \ge 61$.

This contradicts Step 3, which says at most two large coins are $\ge 61$. Theref
ore FFD always succeeds in packing the $m$ large coins into three bins of capaci
ty $120$.

---

**Step 5. Conclusion.**

By Step 4, the $m$ coins of value $\ge 2$ can be partitioned into three groups e
ach of sum $\le 120$. By Step 2, filling the remaining capacity of each group wi
th one‑turgik coins (of which there are exactly enough) produces three groups ea
ch summing to exactly $120$.

Therefore the coins can always be divided into three groups of equal total value
. $\blacksquare$
