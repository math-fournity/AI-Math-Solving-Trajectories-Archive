## Proof

Let $f \in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^d)$ (scalar-valued, so $\nabla f \in L^p(\mathbb{R
}^d;\mathbb{R}^d)$), and suppose the weak gradient $\nabla f$ admits a represent
ative $g:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}^d$ which is $C^\infty$. We claim $f$ has a $C
^\infty$ representative.

**Step 1: $g$ is curl-free.** Since $g = \nabla f$ in the distributional sense,
for every test function $\varphi\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R}^d)$ and every $i,j$,
$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} g_j\,\partial_i\varphi\,dx = -\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \partial
_i f\,\varphi\,dx \quad\text{and}\quad \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} g_i\,\partial_j\varph
i\,dx = -\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \partial_j f\,\varphi\,dx.$$
Using $\partial_i\partial_j f = \partial_j\partial_i f$ distributionally, we get
 $\partial_i g_j = \partial_j g_i$ as distributions. But $g$ is $C^\infty$, so t
hese distributional derivatives coincide with the classical ones, giving
$$\partial_i g_j = \partial_j g_i \quad\text{pointwise on } \mathbb{R}^d.$$

**Step 2: Construct a smooth potential.** Since $\mathbb{R}^d$ is simply connect
ed and $g$ is a smooth closed $1$-form, by the Poincaré lemma there exists $F\in
 C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^d)$ with $\nabla F = g$. Explicitly, one may take the radia
l primitive
$$F(x) = \int_0^1 g(tx)\cdot x\,dt, \qquad F(0)=0.$$
This is $C^\infty$ (differentiation under the integral, $g$ smooth), and a direc
t computation using $\partial_i g_j = \partial_j g_i$ verifies $\partial_i F = g
_i$.

**Step 3: $f - F$ is constant a.e.** Set $h = f - F$. Then $h \in W^{1,p}_{\text
{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^d)$ and $\nabla h = \nabla f - \nabla F = g - g = 0$ in the di
stributional sense. A distribution with zero gradient on a connected open set is
 constant (test against $\partial_i\varphi$ with $\varphi$ of mean zero, or conv
olve with a mollifier: the mollification $h*\rho_\varepsilon$ has zero gradient,
 hence is constant, and converges to $h$ in $L^1_{\text{loc}}$). Thus $h = c$ a.
e. for some constant $c\in\mathbb{R}$.

**Step 4: Conclusion.** We have $f = F + c$ a.e., where $F+c \in C^\infty(\mathb
b{R}^d)$. Therefore $f$ has a smooth representative, namely $\tilde f := F + c$.

**Remark on the hypothesis $p$.** The argument uses only that $\nabla f$ equals
a smooth $g$ a.e.; the integrability condition $f\in W^{1,p}$ ensures $f\in L^p$
 (so $F+c\in L^p$, which is automatic here since $g\in L^p$ and $F$ grows at mos
t linearly combined with $f\in L^p$ forcing $c$ appropriately, though the conclu
sion of smoothness of the representative is independent of $p$). The key point i
s that smoothness of the weak derivative plus the Poincaré lemma on $\mathbb{R}^
d$ forces $f$ to agree a.e. with a smooth function.
