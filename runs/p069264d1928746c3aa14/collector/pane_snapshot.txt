## Proof

**Step 1: Reduction via division by $e^z$.**

Since $e^z$ is entire and never vanishes, the quotient
$$h(z) = \frac{f(z)}{e^z}$$
is an entire function. The condition $|f(z) + e^z| > |e^z|$ for all $z \in \math
bb{C}$ becomes
$$|e^z|\,|h(z) + 1| > |e^z|,$$
and since $|e^z| > 0$, this is equivalent to
$$|h(z) + 1| > 1 \quad \text{for all } z \in \mathbb{C}.$$

**Step 2: $h$ omits an open disk.**

The inequality $|h(z) + 1| > 1$ means $h(z) \notin \overline{D(-1,\, 1)}$ for ev
ery $z$. In particular, $h$ omits the **open** disk $D(-1, 1) = \{w : |w+1| < 1\
}$, which contains uncountably many points.

**Step 3: Apply Picard's theorem.**

By the (Great) Picard theorem, a non-constant entire function omits **at most on
e** point of $\mathbb{C}$. Since $h$ omits the entire open disk $D(-1,1)$ (infin
itely many points), $h$ cannot be non-constant. Therefore $h$ is constant:
$$h(z) = c$$
for some $c \in \mathbb{C}$.

**Step 4: Determine the admissible constants.**

Substituting back, $f(z) = c\,e^z$, and the condition $|h(z)+1| > 1$ becomes
$$|c + 1| > 1.$$

**Step 5: Verification.**

For $f(z) = c\,e^z$ with $|c+1| > 1$:
$$|f(z) + e^z| = |c\,e^z + e^z| = |e^z|\,|c+1| > |e^z|,$$
so the condition is satisfied for all $z \in \mathbb{C}$. Conversely, every solu
tion must arise this way by Steps 1–3.

**Conclusion.**

The entire functions $f$ satisfying $|f(z) + e^z| > |e^z|$ for all $z \in \mathb
b{C}$ are exactly
$$\boxed{f(z) = c\,e^z}$$
where $c \in \mathbb{C}$ is a constant with $|c + 1| > 1$.
