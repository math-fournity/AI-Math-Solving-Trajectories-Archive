## Solution

**Answer: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^2 f(x)\,dx = 2$.**

### Proof

Adopt the standard Fourier transform convention

$$g(w) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,e^{-iwx}\,dx.$$

We assume $f$ is sufficiently well-behaved (e.g. $f$ and $x^2 f(x)$ integrable,
$g$ twice differentiable) so that the moment–derivative exchange below is justif
ied.

**Step 1: Relate moments of $f$ to derivatives of $g$ at the origin.**

Differentiating under the integral sign twice with respect to $w$:

$$g'(w) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,(-ix)\,e^{-iwx}\,dx,$$

$$g''(w) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,(-ix)^2\,e^{-iwx}\,dx = -\int_{-\infty}
^{\infty} x^2 f(x)\,e^{-iwx}\,dx.$$

Evaluating at $w = 0$ (where $e^{-iwx}=1$):

$$g''(0) = -\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^2 f(x)\,dx.$$

Hence

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^2 f(x)\,dx = -\,g''(0). \tag{1}$$

**Step 2: Extract $g''(0)$ from the small-$w$ expansion.**

We are given that for small $w$,

$$g(w) \approx 1 - w^2.$$

Interpreting this as the Taylor expansion of $g$ about $w=0$,

$$g(w) = g(0) + g'(0)\,w + \tfrac{1}{2}g''(0)\,w^2 + \cdots = 1 - w^2 + \cdots.$
$

Matching the constant term gives $g(0)=1$ (consistent, since $g(0)=\int f(x)\,dx
=1$). Matching the quadratic coefficient:

$$\tfrac{1}{2}\,g''(0) = -1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g''(0) = -2.$$

**Step 3: Conclude.**

Substituting into (1):

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^2 f(x)\,dx = -\,g''(0) = -(-2) = 2.$$

**Justification summary.** The identity $\int x^2 f(x)\,dx = -g''(0)$ is the sta
ndard "second moment = minus second derivative of the Fourier transform at zero"
 relation, valid whenever $x^2 f(x)$ is integrable (which is implicit in the exi
stence of the quadratic term in the expansion of $g$). The given approximation $
g(w)\approx 1-w^2$ is precisely the Taylor expansion of $g$ at $0$ up to second
order, so $g''(0) = -2$, yielding the value $2$.
