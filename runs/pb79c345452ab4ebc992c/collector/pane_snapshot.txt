## Proof

We determine the maximum number of lines through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^5$ su
ch that the angle between any two distinct lines is the same.

**Setup.** Represent each line by a unit vector $v_i$. Equiangularity means $|v_
i \cdot v_j| = \alpha$ for all $i \neq j$, for some fixed $\alpha \in (0,1)$. Th
e Gram matrix is $G = I + \alpha S$, where $S$ is a symmetric matrix with zero d
iagonal and $\pm 1$ off-diagonal entries. Since $G$ is a Gram matrix of vectors
in $\mathbb{R}^5$, it is positive semidefinite with $\operatorname{rank}(G) \leq
 5$.

### Upper Bound: $n \leq 10$

**Neumann's Theorem** (1973): If $n > 2d$ equiangular lines exist in $\mathbb{R}
^d$, then $1/\alpha^2$ is an odd integer, i.e., $\alpha^2 = \frac{1}{2k-1}$ for
some positive integer $k$.

For $d=5$: if $n > 10$, then $\alpha^2 = \frac{1}{2k-1}$ for some $k \geq 1$.

**Key Lemma (Conjugate-Pair Argument).** Let $S$ be an $n \times n$ symmetric in
teger matrix. If $-\sqrt{m}$ is an eigenvalue of $S$ with multiplicity $p$, wher
e $m$ is a positive non-square integer, then $+\sqrt{m}$ is also an eigenvalue w
ith multiplicity $p$. In particular, $2p \leq n$.

*Proof of Lemma.* The characteristic polynomial of $S$ has integer coefficients.
 The polynomial $x^2 - m$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ (since $m$ is not a p
erfect square). Since $-\sqrt{m}$ is a root of the characteristic polynomial and
 $x^2 - m$ is the minimal polynomial of $-\sqrt{m}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, the facto
r $(x^2 - m)^p$ divides the characteristic polynomial. Hence $+\sqrt{m}$ is also
 a root with multiplicity $p$, giving $2p \leq n$. $\square$

**Case 1: $2k-1$ is not a perfect square.** Then $-1/\alpha = -\sqrt{2k-1}$ is i
rrational. Since $G = I + \alpha S$ is PSD with rank $\leq 5$, the matrix $S$ ha
s eigenvalue $-1/\alpha = -\sqrt{2k-1}$ with multiplicity $p \geq n - 5$. By the
 Lemma, $\sqrt{2k-1}$ is also an eigenvalue with multiplicity $p$, so $2p \leq n
$. Combining:
$$2(n-5) \leq 2p \leq n \implies n \leq 10.$$

**Case 2: $2k-1 = s^2$ is a perfect square.** Then $-1/\alpha = -s$ is rational,
 and the conjugate-pair argument does not apply. We use the **relative bound**:
when $d\alpha^2 < 1$,
$$n \leq \frac{d(1-\alpha^2)}{1 - d\alpha^2} = \frac{5(s^2 - 1)}{s^2 - 5}.$$
- $s = 1$ ($k=1$, $\alpha=1$): only $n=1$ line (all vectors parallel). Not relev
ant for $n > 10$.
- $s = 3$ ($k=5$, $\alpha = 1/3$): $n \leq \frac{5 \cdot 8}{4} = 10$.
- $s = 5$ ($k=13$, $\alpha = 1/5$): $n \leq \frac{5 \cdot 24}{20} = 6$.
- $s \geq 5$: the bound $\frac{5(s^2-1)}{s^2-5}$ is decreasing in $s$, so $n \le
q 6 < 10$.

In every subcase, $n \leq 10$.

**Conclusion of upper bound.** For $n > 10$, Neumann's theorem forces $\alpha^2
= 1/(2k-1)$. Whether $2k-1$ is a perfect square or not, we derive $n \leq 10$—a
contradiction. Therefore $n \leq 10$.

### Lower Bound: Construction of 10 equiangular lines

We construct 10 equiangular lines in $\mathbb{R}^5$ with $\alpha = 1/3$ using th

The Petersen graph has 10 vertices and is 3-regular. Let $A$ be its adjacency ma

$$S = J - I - 2A.$$
The off-diagonal entries of $S$ are $\pm 1$ (entry $+1$ for non-edges, $-1$ for
edges), and the diagonal is $0$.

The eigenvalues of $A$ for the Petersen graph are $3$ (multiplicity 1), $1$ (mul

 - 2A$:

- On the all-ones vector ($A$-eigenvalue $3$, $J$-eigenvalue $10$): $S$-eigenval

bf{1}$, so $J$-eigenvalue $0$): $S$-eigenvalue $= 0 - 1 - 2 = -3$.
- On the $A$-eigenspace for eigenvalue $-2$ (multiplicity 4, orthogonal to $\mat
hbf{1}$, so $J$-eigenvalue $0$): $S$-eigenvalue $= 0 - 1 + 4 = 3$.

So $S$ has eigenvalues $3$ (multiplicity 5) and $-3$ (multiplicity 5).

Set $G = I + \frac{1}{3}S$. Then $G$ has eigenvalues:
- $1 + \frac{3}{3} = 2$ (multiplicity 5),

Thus $G$ is positive semidefinite with rank $5$, and has $1$'s on the diagonal a
