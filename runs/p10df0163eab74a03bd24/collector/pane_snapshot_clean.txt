## Problem

Determine whether there exist two rational numbers $p$ and $q$ such that $e^p =
q$.

## Solution

We treat two cases: the trivial case $p = 0$ and the nontrivial case $p \neq 0$.

### Case 1: $p = 0$.

$0$ is rational, and $e^0 = 1$, which is rational. So with $p = 0$, $q = 1$ we h
ave $e^p = q$. **A solution exists trivially.**

### Case 2: $p \neq 0$ (nontrivial solutions).

We show that **no** nonzero rational $p$ can yield a rational $e^p$.

We use the **Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem**, in the following standard form:

> If $\alpha$ is a nonzero algebraic number, then $e^{\alpha}$ is transcendental
.

(Equivalently: if $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n$ are distinct algebraic numbers, th
en $e^{\alpha_1}, \dots, e^{\alpha_n}$ are linearly independent over $\overline{
\mathbb{Q}}$. Taking $n=1$: a single nonzero algebraic $\alpha$ gives $e^{\alpha
}$ transcendental, since otherwise $e^{\alpha} \in \overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ would
give the nontrivial $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$-linear relation $e^{\alpha} - 0 \cdo
t e^{0} = 0$... more directly, the one-element form states $e^{\alpha} \notin \o
verline{\mathbb{Q}}$.)

Now suppose, for contradiction, that $p \in \mathbb{Q}$, $p \neq 0$, and $e^p =
q \in \mathbb{Q}$.

- Since $p$ is a nonzero rational number, $p$ is a nonzero **algebraic** number
(every rational is algebraic).
- By Lindemann–Weierstrass, $e^p$ is **transcendental**.
- But $q = e^p$ is rational, hence algebraic.

This is a contradiction: a number cannot be both transcendental and algebraic. T
herefore no nonzero rational $p$ satisfies $e^p \in \mathbb{Q}$.

### Conclusion

There exist rational $p, q$ with $e^p = q$ **only in the trivial case** $p = 0$,
 $q = 1$ (and $q = e^0 = 1$ is forced). No nontrivial solution with $p \neq 0$ e
xists.

Equivalently: $\{(p, q) \in \mathbb{Q}^2 : e^p = q\} = \{(0, 1)\}$.
