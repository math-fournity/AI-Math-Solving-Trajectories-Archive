## Evaluation of $I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\sin^2 x}{x^2}\, dx$

**Step 1: Integration by parts.**

Consider the integral over $[-R, R]$ (with $R \to \infty$) and integrate by part
s with
$$u = \sin^2 x, \quad dv = \frac{dx}{x^2}, \qquad du = \sin(2x)\,dx, \quad v = -
\frac{1}{x}.$$

Then
$$\int_{-R}^{R} \frac{\sin^2 x}{x^2}\,dx = \left[-\frac{\sin^2 x}{x}\right]_{-R}
^{R} + \int_{-R}^{R} \frac{\sin(2x)}{x}\,dx.$$

The boundary term satisfies $\left|-\sin^2 x / x\right| \le 1/|x| \to 0$ as $R \
to \infty$, so it vanishes in the limit. Hence
$$I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\sin(2x)}{x}\,dx.$$

**Step 2: Substitution.**

Let $t = 2x$, so $dx = dt/2$ and $x = t/2$:
$$I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\sin t}{t/2}\cdot\frac{dt}{2} = \int_{-\inft
y}^{\infty} \frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt = 2\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt.$$

**Step 3: The Dirichlet integral $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin t}{t}
\,dt = \frac{\pi}{2}$.**

Define, for $a \ge 0$,
$$F(a) = \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{\sin t}{t}\,e^{-at}\,dt.$$

For $a > 0$, differentiating under the integral sign (justified by dominated con
vergence, since $|\partial_a[(\sin t/t)e^{-at}]| = |{-e^{-at}\sin t}| \le e^{-at
}$, integrable on $[0,\infty)$):
$$F'(a) = -\int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-at}\sin t\,dt = -\frac{1}{1+a^2},$$
using the standard Laplace transform $\int_0^\infty e^{-at}\sin t\,dt = \frac{1}
{1+a^2}$.

Integrating,
$$F(a) = -\arctan a + C.$$

As $a \to \infty$: by dominated convergence ($|(\sin t/t)e^{-at}| \le e^{-at} \t
o 0$ pointwise, and is dominated by the integrable $e^{-t}$ on $[1,\infty)$ toge
ther with the bounded $1$ on $[0,1]$), $F(a) \to 0$. Since $\arctan a \to \pi/2$
, we get $C = \pi/2$. Therefore
$$F(a) = \frac{\pi}{2} - \arctan a,$$
and setting $a = 0$:
$$\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt = F(0) = \frac{\pi}{2}.$$

**Step 4: Conclusion.**

$$I = 2 \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} = \pi.$$

$$\boxed{I = \pi}$$
