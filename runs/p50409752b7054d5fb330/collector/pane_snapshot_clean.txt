# IMO 2003, Problem 6

**Claim.** Let $p$ be a prime. There exists a prime $q$ such that for every inte
ger $n$, $q \nmid (n^p - p)$.

**Proof.**

Define
$$N = \Phi_p(p) = 1 + p + p^2 + \cdots + p^{p-1} = \frac{p^p - 1}{p - 1}.$$
Since $p \ge 2$, we have $N \ge 1 + 2 = 3 > 1$, so $N$ possesses at least one pr
ime divisor.

---

**Step 1. Every prime divisor $q$ of $N$ satisfies $q \neq p$ and $\operatorname
{ord}_q(p) = p$ (hence $q \equiv 1 \pmod p$).**

Since $N \equiv 1 \pmod p$ (all terms $p, p^2, \ldots$ vanish mod $p$), we have
$\gcd(N, p) = 1$, so $q \neq p$.

Since $N \mid (p^p - 1)$, we get $p^p \equiv 1 \pmod q$, so $\operatorname{ord}_
q(p) \mid p$. As $p$ is prime, $\operatorname{ord}_q(p) \in \{1, p\}$. If $\oper
atorname{ord}_q(p) = 1$, then $p \equiv 1 \pmod q$, i.e. $q \mid (p-1)$. But the
n, modulo $q$,
$$N = 1 + p + p^2 + \cdots + p^{p-1} \equiv \underbrace{1 + 1 + \cdots + 1}_{p}
= p \pmod q,$$
so $q \mid p$, forcing $q = p$ — contradicting $q \neq p$. Therefore $\operatorn
ame{ord}_q(p) = p$, which gives $p \mid (q - 1)$, i.e. $q \equiv 1 \pmod p$. $\s
quare$

---

**Step 2. For a prime $q \mid N$, $p$ is a $p$-th power residue modulo $q$ if an
d only if $q \equiv 1 \pmod{p^2}$.**

Since $q \equiv 1 \pmod p$, the group $(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}$ is cyc
lic of order $q - 1$ with $p \mid (q-1)$. The $p$-th powers form the unique subg
roup of index $p$, i.e. an element $a$ is a $p$-th power residue iff $a^{(q-1)/p
} \equiv 1 \pmod q$.

Applying this to $a = p$: by Step 1, $\operatorname{ord}_q(p) = p$, so
$$p^{(q-1)/p} \equiv 1 \pmod q \iff p \;\Big|\; \frac{q-1}{p} \iff p^2 \mid (q-1
) \iff q \equiv 1 \pmod{p^2}. \quad\square$$

---

**Step 3. Not every prime divisor of $N$ is $\equiv 1 \pmod{p^2}$; hence there e
xists a prime $q \mid N$ with $q \not\equiv 1 \pmod{p^2}$.**

Compute $N$ modulo $p^2$:
$$N = 1 + p + p^2 + \cdots + p^{p-1} \equiv 1 + p \pmod{p^2}.$$
Since $1 + p \not\equiv 1 \pmod{p^2}$ (as $p \not\equiv 0 \pmod{p^2}$), we have
$N \not\equiv 1 \pmod{p^2}$.

If every prime divisor $q_i$ of $N$ satisfied $q_i \equiv 1 \pmod{p^2}$, then $N
 = \prod q_i^{e_i} \equiv 1 \pmod{p^2}$, a contradiction. So some prime divisor
$q$ of $N$ has $q \not\equiv 1 \pmod{p^2}$. $\square$

---

**Step 4. This $q$ works.**

Fix a prime $q \mid N$ with $q \not\equiv 1 \pmod{p^2}$ (which exists by Step 3)
. By Step 1, $q \equiv 1 \pmod p$ and $q \neq p$. By Step 2, $p$ is **not** a $p
$-th power residue modulo $q$.

Now take any integer $n$:

- **If $q \mid n$:** then $n^p \equiv 0 \pmod q$, while $p \not\equiv 0 \pmod q$
 (since $q \neq p$). So $n^p - p \equiv -p \not\equiv 0 \pmod q$.

- **If $q \nmid n$:** suppose for contradiction that $n^p \equiv p \pmod q$. The
n $p$ is a $p$-th power residue modulo $q$ (witnessed by $n$), contradicting Ste
p 2.

In both cases $q \nmid (n^p - p)$. Since $n$ was arbitrary, the prime $q$ has th
e required property. $\blacksquare$
