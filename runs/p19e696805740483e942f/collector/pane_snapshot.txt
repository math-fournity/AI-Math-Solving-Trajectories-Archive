Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the existence of a constant
 $c > 0$ such that $\|f\|_{L^q(\mathbb{R}^n)} \le c \|\nabla f\|_{L^p(\mathbb{R}
^n)}$ for all $f \in C_0^\infty(\mathbb{R}^n)$ forces $q = \frac{np}{n-p}$.

**Answer: Yes.** Here is the proof via a scaling argument.

---

**Proof.**

Suppose there exists $c > 0$ such that
$$\|f\|_{L^q(\mathbb{R}^n)} \le c\,\|\nabla f\|_{L^p(\mathbb{R}^n)} \quad \text{
for all } f \in C_0^\infty(\mathbb{R}^n). \tag{$\star$}$$

**Step 1: Scaling.** Fix any nonzero $f \in C_0^\infty(\mathbb{R}^n)$ and define
, for $\lambda > 0$, the dilation
$$f_\lambda(x) := f(\lambda x).$$
Clearly $f_\lambda \in C_0^\infty(\mathbb{R}^n)$, so $(\star)$ applies to $f_\la
mbda$.

**Step 2: Compute the two norms under scaling.** By the change of variables $y =
 \lambda x$ (so $dx = \lambda^{-n}\,dy$):

$$\|f_\lambda\|_{L^q}^q = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} |f(\lambda x)|^q\,dx = \lambda^{-n
}\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}|f(y)|^q\,dy = \lambda^{-n}\|f\|_{L^q}^q,$$
so
$$\|f_\lambda\|_{L^q} = \lambda^{-n/q}\|f\|_{L^q}.$$

For the gradient, $\nabla f_\lambda(x) = \lambda\,\nabla f(\lambda x)$, so
$$\|\nabla f_\lambda\|_{L^p}^p = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n}|\lambda\,\nabla f(\lambda x
)|^p\,dx = \lambda^{p}\lambda^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}|\nabla f(y)|^p\,dy = \lamb
da^{p-n}\|\nabla f\|_{L^p}^p,$$
so
$$\|\nabla f_\lambda\|_{L^p} = \lambda^{(p-n)/p}\|\nabla f\|_{L^p}.$$

**Step 3: Apply the inequality to $f_\lambda$.** Substituting into $(\star)$:
$$\lambda^{-n/q}\|f\|_{L^q} \le c\,\lambda^{(p-n)/p}\|\nabla f\|_{L^p} \quad \te
xt{for all } \lambda > 0. \tag{1}$$

Since $f$ is fixed and nonzero, $\|f\|_{L^q} > 0$ and $\|\nabla f\|_{L^p} > 0$.
Dividing both sides by $\lambda^{(p-n)/p}$:

$$\lambda^{-n/q - (p-n)/p}\|f\|_{L^q} \le c\,\|\nabla f\|_{L^p} \quad \text{for
all } \lambda > 0. \tag{2}$$

**Step 4: The exponent must vanish.** Set $\alpha := -n/q - (p-n)/p = -n/q + (n-
p)/p$. Equation (2) reads:

$$\lambda^{\alpha}\|f\|_{L^q} \le c\,\|\nabla f\|_{L^p} \quad \text{for all } \l
ambda > 0. \tag{3}$$

- If $\alpha > 0$: taking $\lambda \to \infty$ makes $\lambda^\alpha \to \infty$
, so the left-hand side of (3) diverges while the right-hand side is fixed — **c
ontradiction**.
- If $\alpha < 0$: taking $\lambda \to 0^+$ makes $\lambda^\alpha \to \infty$, a
gain the left-hand side diverges — **contradiction**.

Therefore $\alpha = 0$, i.e.,
$$-\frac{n}{q} + \frac{n-p}{p} = 0 \implies \frac{n}{q} = \frac{n-p}{p} \implies
 q = \frac{np}{n-p}.$$

**Step 5: Existence (the Sobolev inequality).** The value $q = \frac{np}{n-p}$ i
s not only necessary but sufficient: the **Gagliardo–Nirenberg–Sobolev inequalit
y** states that for $1 \le p < n$ there exists a constant $C = C(n,p) > 0$ such
that
$$\|f\|_{L^{p^*}(\mathbb{R}^n)} \le C\,\|\nabla f\|_{L^p(\mathbb{R}^n)}, \quad p
^* := \frac{np}{n-p},$$
for all $f \in C_0^\infty(\mathbb{R}^n)$. This confirms that $q = \frac{np}{n-p}
$ is the unique exponent for which an inequality of the form $(\star)$ can hold.

**Conclusion.** The inequality $\|f\|_{L^q(\mathbb{R}^n)} \le c\,\|\nabla f\|_{L
^p(\mathbb{R}^n)}$ holding for all $f \in C_0^\infty(\mathbb{R}^n)$ with some $c
 > 0$ implies that $q$ must equal the **critical Sobolev exponent** $\displaysty
le q = \frac{np}{n-p}$. $\blacksquare$
