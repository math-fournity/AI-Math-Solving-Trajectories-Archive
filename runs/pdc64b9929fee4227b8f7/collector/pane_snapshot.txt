Yes. The QR algorithm, generalized to the **QZ algorithm** (Moler & Stewart, 197
3), solves the generalized eigenvalue problem $Ax = \lambda Bx$ without ever for
ming $B^{-1}$. Here is the complete argument.

---

## Problem Setup

The generalized eigenvalue problem seeks scalars $\lambda$ and nonzero vectors $
x$ satisfying

$$Ax = \lambda Bx, \qquad A, B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}.$$

The **naive reduction** to a standard problem is $B^{-1}Ax = \lambda x$, which r
equires inverting $B$. This is undesirable because:

1. $B$ may be singular or ill-conditioned, making $B^{-1}$ numerically catastrop
hic.
2. Even when $B$ is invertible, forming $B^{-1}A$ squares the condition number,
destroying accuracy.

The question is whether the QR algorithm can be adapted to avoid this inversion
entirely.

---

## Key Idea: Work on the Matrix Pencil, Not on $B^{-1}A$

The generalized eigenvalues depend only on the **matrix pencil** $(A, B)$ up to
the equivalence

$$(A, B) \sim (Q^T A Z,\; Q^T B Z)$$

for any **orthogonal** (or unitary) matrices $Q, Z$. This is because if $Ax = \l
ambda Bx$ and we set $x = Z\tilde{x}$, then

$$A Z \tilde{x} = \lambda B Z \tilde{x} \implies Q^T A Z \tilde{x} = \lambda\, Q
^T B Z \tilde{x},$$

so the pencil $(Q^T A Z,\; Q^T B Z)$ has exactly the same generalized eigenvalue
s as $(A, B)$.

The QZ algorithm exploits this freedom to simultaneously reduce $A$ and $B$ to s
imple triangular forms using **only orthogonal transformations** — no inversion
of $B$ is ever performed.

---

## The QZ Algorithm: Two Stages, No Inversion

### Stage 1 — Simultaneous Hessenberg–Triangular Reduction

Find orthogonal $Q_1, Z_1$ such that

$$Q_1^T A Z_1 = H \quad (\text{upper Hessenberg}), \qquad Q_1^T B Z_1 = R \quad
(\text{upper triangular}).$$

**How it works (sketch):** First, reduce $B$ to upper triangular form via Househ
older reflections from the left: $Q_B^T B = R$. Apply the same $Q_B^T$ to $A$: $
\tilde{A} = Q_B^T A$. Now $\tilde{A}$ is a full matrix. Then use Householder ref
lections applied **simultaneously from the left and right** to drive $\tilde{A}$
 to Hessenberg form while keeping $R$ triangular: each left reflection that intr
oduces a zero in $\tilde{A}$ is compensated by a right reflection (applied to bo
th $\tilde{A}$ and $R$) that restores the triangular structure of $R$. This is p
ossible because right-multiplication by an orthogonal matrix preserves the zero
pattern below the diagonal of an upper-triangular matrix **if the reflection is
chosen to act within rows at or below the subdiagonal level being zeroed**.

**Crucially:** Throughout this stage, only matrix–matrix products with orthogona
l matrices are performed. $B$ is never inverted.

### Stage 2 — The QZ Iteration (Generalized QR Step)

Now we have the pencil $(H, R)$ with $H$ Hessenberg and $R$ upper triangular. We
 apply the **QZ iteration**, which is the direct generalization of the QR iterat
ion to pencils:

**Single-shift QZ step** (the double-shift version is used in practice for real
arithmetic, exactly paralleling the Francis double-shift QR):

1. **Choose a shift** $\mu$ from the pencil — e.g., the generalized eigenvalue o
f the trailing $2\times 2$ subpencil $(H_{n-1:n,\, n-1:n},\; R_{n-1:n,\, n-1:n})
$, computed from the $2\times 2$ problem without inverting any large matrix (onl
y a $2\times 2$ system is involved).

2. **Form the first column** of $H - \mu R$, which (because $H$ is Hessenberg an
d $R$ is triangular) has only **two nonzero entries** in positions 1 and 2.

3. **Determine a Householder reflection** $P_0$ (acting on the left, $2\times 2$
) that zeros the second entry. Apply $P_0$ to both $H$ and $R$ from the left.

4. **Determine a Householder reflection** $Z_0$ (acting on the right) that resto
res the upper-triangular structure of $R$ (which was perturbed by the left multi
plication). Apply $Z_0$ to both $H$ and $R$ from the right.

5. **"Chase the bulge"**: The right multiplication introduces a bulge in $H$ (a
nonzero element below the subdiagonal). Apply alternating left and right Househo
lder reflections to push this bulge down and off the bottom of the matrices, exa
ctly as in the standard QR algorithm — but now each left reflection on $H$ is pa
ired with a right reflection that repairs $R$'s triangularity, and each right re
flection on $H$ is paired with a left reflection that repairs $H$'s Hessenberg s
tructure.

6. After the bulge is chased off, both $H$ (Hessenberg) and $R$ (triangular) str
uctures are restored, and the pencil has been updated by orthogonal equivalence.

**Convergence:** Under the same conditions as the QR algorithm (and with the sam
e shift strategies — Wilkinson, Francis double-shift), the subdiagonal elements
$H_{i+1,i}$ converge to zero. When $H_{i+1,i} \to 0$, the pencil decouples, and
the generalized eigenvalue is

$$\lambda_i = \frac{H_{ii}}{R_{ii}}.$$

**Crucially:** Every operation in the iteration is a multiplication by an orthog
onal matrix (Householder reflection or, in the complex case, Givens rotation). T
he only "division" is the final $H_{ii}/R_{ii}$, which divides two **scalars** —
 this is not an inversion of the matrix $B$.

---

## Why No Inversion of $B$ Occurs

Let me make the absence of $B^{-1}$ explicit:

| Operation in QZ | Type | Involves $B^{-1}$? |
|---|---|---|
| Hessenberg-triangular reduction | Orthogonal matrix products | No |
| Shift selection ($2\times 2$ pencil) | Solve $2\times 2$ system | No (scalar-l
evel only) |
| QZ iteration step | Householder/Givens reflections | No |
| Deflation & eigenvalue extraction | Scalar ratio $H_{ii}/R_{ii}$ | No (scalar
division, not matrix inversion) |

The entire algorithm operates on the **pair** $(A, B)$ through orthogonal equiva
lence transformations. The matrix $B$ is transformed into an upper-triangular $R
$; its "inverse" is never formed. The generalized eigenvalues emerge as ratios o
f diagonal entries of the reduced $H$ and $R$.

---

## Correctness: The Generalized Eigenvalues Are Preserved

At every step, the pencil is updated as

$$(A, B) \mapsto (Q^T A Z,\; Q^T B Z), \quad Q, Z \text{ orthogonal}.$$

As shown above, this preserves the generalized eigenvalue spectrum exactly (in e
xact arithmetic). At convergence, $H$ is upper quasi-triangular (real Schur form
 for the pencil) and $R$ is upper triangular. The generalized eigenvalues are th
en read off from the $1\times 1$ and $2\times 2$ diagonal blocks:

- **$1\times 1$ block:** $\lambda = H_{ii}/R_{ii}$ (when $R_{ii} \neq 0$); if $R
_{ii} = 0$ and $H_{ii} \neq 0$, the eigenvalue is $\infty$; if both are zero, th
e pencil is singular.

- **$2\times 2$ block:** The generalized eigenvalues of the $2\times 2$ pencil $
(H_{ii:i+1,\, i:i+1},\; R_{ii:i+1,\, i:i+1})$ are found by solving the quadratic
 $\det(H_{2\times 2} - \lambda R_{2\times 2}) = 0$, which involves only $2\times
 2$ determinant computation — no inversion of the full $B$.

---

## Numerical Stability Advantage

The QZ algorithm is **backward stable**: the computed generalized eigenvalues ar
e the exact eigenvalues of a nearby pencil $(A + \delta A, B + \delta B)$ with

$$\|\delta A\| \leq \varepsilon \cdot O(\|A\|), \quad \|\delta B\| \leq \varepsi
lon \cdot O(\|B\|),$$

where $\varepsilon$ is machine epsilon. This is because only orthogonal (norm-pr
eserving) transformations are used throughout. In contrast, the $B^{-1}A$ approa
ch has backward error proportional to $\|B^{-1}\| \cdot \|A\|$, which can be arb
itrarily large when $B$ is ill-conditioned. This is precisely why the QZ algorit
hm was developed — to handle cases where $B$ is nearly singular, where inversion
-based methods fail catastrophically.

---

## Conclusion

**Yes.** The QR algorithm, in its generalized form known as the **QZ algorithm**
, finds all eigenvalues of the generalized eigenvalue problem $Ax = \lambda Bx$
**without inverting $B$**. The method:

1. Reduces $(A, B)$ simultaneously to (Hessenberg, triangular) form via orthogon
al transformations.
2. Applies QR-like iterations (with bulge-chasing) to the pencil, using paired l
eft/right orthogonal reflections that maintain both structures.
3. Extracts eigenvalues as scalar ratios $H_{ii}/R_{ii}$ from the converged tria
ngular forms.

At no point is the matrix $B$ (or any large submatrix of it) inverted. The only
divisions are of individual scalars at the very end. The algorithm is backward s
table and handles singular or ill-conditioned $B$ gracefully — exactly the scena
rios where the $B^{-1}A$ approach breaks down.
