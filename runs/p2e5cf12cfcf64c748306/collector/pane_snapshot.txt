## Solution

**Claim:** In general, a closed subset $A$ of a metric space $X$ need **not** sa
tisfy $A = \operatorname{int}(A) \cup E(A)$. The inclusion $\operatorname{int}(A
) \cup E(A) \subseteq A$ holds for closed $A$, but the reverse inclusion can fai
l.

### Preliminaries

Recall the standard definitions in a metric space $(X, d)$:

- $x \in \operatorname{int}(A)$ iff $\exists\, r > 0:\ B(x,r) \subseteq A$.
- $x \in E(A)$ (a limit/accumulation point of $A$) iff $\forall\, r > 0:\ B(x,r)
 \cap (A \setminus \{x\}) \neq \emptyset$.
- $A$ is **closed** iff $A = \overline{A} = A \cup E(A)$, i.e. iff $E(A) \subset
eq A$.

A point $x \in A$ that is **not** a limit point of $A$ is an **isolated point**
of $A$: there exists $r > 0$ with $B(x,r) \cap A = \{x\}$.

### The inclusion $\operatorname{int}(A) \cup E(A) \subseteq A$ (for closed $A$)

Since $A$ is closed, $E(A) \subseteq A$. Also $\operatorname{int}(A) \subseteq A
$ trivially. Hence
$$\operatorname{int}(A) \cup E(A) \subseteq A.$$

### The reverse inclusion can fail: a counterexample

Take $X = \mathbb{R}$ with the usual metric, and let
$$A = \{0\} \cup \left\{\tfrac{1}{n} : n \in \mathbb{N},\ n \ge 1\right\} = \{0,
 1, \tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac14, \dots\}.$$

**$A$ is closed.** The only accumulation point of $A$ is $0$: any neighborhood o
f $0$ contains $1/n$ for all sufficiently large $n$, and for any $x \neq 0$ one
can find a small ball avoiding the discrete set $\{1/n\}$. Since $0 \in A$, we h
ave $E(A) = \{0\} \subseteq A$, so $A = \overline{A}$; $A$ is closed.

**The point $1$ belongs to $A$ but to neither $\operatorname{int}(A)$ nor $E(A)$
.**

- *Not a limit point:* Take $r = \tfrac13$. Then $B(1, \tfrac13) = (\tfrac23, \t
frac43)$, and
  $$B(1, \tfrac13) \cap A = \{1\},$$
  since the next nearest point of $A$ is $\tfrac12$, and $\tfrac12 \notin (\tfra
c23, \tfrac43)$. Hence $B(1,\tfrac13) \cap (A \setminus \{1\}) = \emptyset$, so
$1 \notin E(A)$.

- *Not an interior point:* For any $s > 0$, the ball $B(1, s) = (1-s, 1+s)$ is a
n interval of positive length and therefore contains irrational numbers (and ind
eed all but finitely many reals near $1$), which are not in $A$. Thus $B(1, s) \
not\subseteq A$ for every $s > 0$, so $1 \notin \operatorname{int}(A)$.

Therefore
$$1 \in A \quad\text{but}\quad 1 \notin \operatorname{int}(A) \cup E(A),$$
which gives
$$A \neq \operatorname{int}(A) \cup E(A).$$

### Characterization of the gap

More generally, for a closed set $A$ the points of $A \setminus (\operatorname{i
nt}(A) \cup E(A))$ are exactly the **isolated points of $A$ that are not isolate
d points of $X$**:

- If $x \in A \setminus E(A)$, then $x$ is isolated in $A$: $\exists\, r > 0$ wi
th $B(x,r) \cap A = \{x\}$.
- Such $x$ is an interior point of $A$ iff $\exists\, s > 0$ with $B(x,s) \subse
teq A$. Combined with $B(x,r) \cap A = \{x\}$, this forces $B(x,s) = \{x\}$, i.e
. $x$ is an isolated point of $X$ itself.

So $A = \operatorname{int}(A) \cup E(A)$ holds for a closed $A$ **if and only if
** every isolated point of $A$ is also an isolated point of the ambient space $X
$. This is automatic when $X$ has no isolated points (e.g. $X = \mathbb{R}^n$ an
d $A$ is perfect-or-has-no-isolated-points), but it is **not** automatic in gene
ral, as the counterexample above shows.

### Conclusion

The statement is **false** in general. A closed subset $A$ of a metric space $X$
 need not decompose as $A = \operatorname{int}(A) \cup E(A)$; the isolated point
s of $A$ that are not isolated in $X$ provide counterexamples, e.g. $A = \{0\} \
cup \{1/n : n \ge 1\}$ in $\mathbb{R}$, where $1 \in A \setminus (\operatorname{
int}(A) \cup E(A))$.
