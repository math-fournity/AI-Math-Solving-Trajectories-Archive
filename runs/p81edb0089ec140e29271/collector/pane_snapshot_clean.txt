Looking at the problem, I need to determine whether $P(z+qi)+P(z-qi)$ has $m$ re
al roots, given that $P(z)=\sum_{i=0}^m a_i z^i$ has $m$ real roots.

**Answer leak check:** The problem text contains no solutions, answer values, or
 formalization notes. Clean.

**Answer: Yes, $P(z+qi)+P(z-qi)$ always has exactly $m$ real roots.**

---

## Proof

**Setup.** Since $P$ has degree $m$ and $m$ real roots (counting multiplicity),
write
$$P(z) = a_m \prod_{k=1}^{m}(z - r_k), \qquad r_k \in \mathbb{R}.$$
The coefficients $a_i$ are $a_m$ times elementary symmetric polynomials of the $
r_k$, hence all real multiples of $a_m$. Writing $P(z) = a_m R(z)$ where $R(z) =
 \prod(z-r_k)$ has **real** coefficients, we get
$$Q(z) := P(z+qi)+P(z-qi) = a_m\bigl[R(z+qi)+R(z-qi)\bigr].$$
Multiplication by the nonzero constant $a_m$ does not change roots, so it suffic
es to prove the claim for $R$, i.e., we may assume **$P$ has real coefficients a
nd leading coefficient $a_m > 0$** (absorbing any sign into the constant). By re
placing $q$ with $|q|$, assume **$q > 0$**. The case $q=0$ gives $Q=2P$, trivial
ly with $m$ real roots, so assume $q>0$.

**Key observation.** For real $z$, since $P$ has real coefficients, $P(\bar w) =
 \overline{P(w)}$, so
$$P(z-qi) = P\!\left(\overline{z+qi}\right) = \overline{P(z+qi)}.$$
Therefore
$$Q(z) = 2\,\operatorname{Re}\!\bigl[P(z+qi)\bigr], \qquad z \in \mathbb{R}.$$

**Argument (phase) analysis.** For real $z$,
$$P(z+qi) = a_m \prod_{k=1}^{m}\bigl((z-r_k)+qi\bigr).$$
Each factor $(z-r_k)+qi$ lies in the **upper half-plane** (since $q>0$), so its
argument $\theta_k(z) = \arg\bigl((z-r_k)+qi\bigr) \in (0,\pi)$ is well-defined
and continuous. Explicitly:
$$\theta_k(z) = \begin{cases} \arctan\!\bigl(\tfrac{q}{z-r_k}\bigr) \in (0,\tfra
c{\pi}{2}), & z > r_k, \\ \tfrac{\pi}{2}, & z = r_k, \\ \pi - \arctan\!\bigl(\tf
rac{q}{r_k-z}\bigr) \in (\tfrac{\pi}{2},\pi), & z < r_k. \end{cases}$$

Define the total phase:
$$\Theta(z) = \arg(a_m) + \sum_{k=1}^{m}\theta_k(z).$$
With $a_m > 0$ real, $\arg(a_m)=0$, so $\Theta(z) = \sum_{k=1}^m \theta_k(z)$.

**Properties of $\Theta$:**
- **Continuity:** Each $\theta_k$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ (the point $(z-r
_k,\, q)$ stays in the open upper half-plane, never crossing a branch cut). Henc
e $\Theta$ is continuous.
- **Strict monotonicity:** Each $\theta_k$ is strictly decreasing (as $z$ increa
ses, the point $(z-r_k, q)$ rotates clockwise). Hence $\Theta$ is **strictly dec
reasing**.
- **Limits:** As $z \to +\infty$, each $\theta_k \to 0^+$, so $\Theta(z) \to 0^+
$. As $z \to -\infty$, each $\theta_k \to \pi^-$, so $\Theta(z) \to m\pi^-$.

Thus $\Theta : \mathbb{R} \to (0,\, m\pi)$ is continuous and strictly decreasing
, with $\Theta(z)\to 0$ as $z\to+\infty$ and $\Theta(z)\to m\pi$ as $z\to-\infty
$.

**Zeros of $Q$.** Writing $P(z+qi) = a_m\prod|(z-r_k)+qi|\cdot e^{i\Theta(z)}$ (
with $a_m>0$),
$$Q(z) = 2\,a_m\prod_{k=1}^m\bigl|(z-r_k)+qi\bigr|\;\cos\Theta(z).$$
The product $\prod|(z-r_k)+qi|$ is strictly positive for all real $z$ (each fact
or has modulus $\sqrt{(z-r_k)^2+q^2} > 0$). Therefore
$$Q(z) = 0 \iff \cos\Theta(z) = 0 \iff \Theta(z) = \tfrac{(2n+1)\pi}{2},\quad n
\in \mathbb{Z}.$$

**Counting solutions.** The values $\frac{(2n+1)\pi}{2}$ lying in the open inter
val $(0,\, m\pi)$ are:
$$\frac{\pi}{2},\;\frac{3\pi}{2},\;\frac{5\pi}{2},\;\ldots,\;\frac{(2m-1)\pi}{2}
,$$
corresponding to $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, m-1$ — exactly **$m$ values**.

Since $\Theta$ is continuous and strictly decreasing from $m\pi$ to $0$, it is a
 bijection $\mathbb{R} \to (0, m\pi)$. Each of the $m$ target values is attained
 **exactly once**. Hence $Q(z)=0$ has exactly $m$ distinct real solutions.

**Degree check.** The leading term of $Q(z) = P(z+qi)+P(z-qi)$ is $a_m z^m + a_m
 z^m = 2a_m z^m \neq 0$, so $\deg Q = m$. A degree-$m$ polynomial has at most $m
$ roots; we have found $m$ distinct real roots, so these are **all** the roots.

**Conclusion.** For any polynomial $P$ of degree $m$ with $m$ real roots and any
 real $q$, the polynomial $P(z+qi)+P(z-qi)$ has exactly $m$ real roots (in fact,
 $m$ distinct real roots). $\blacksquare$
