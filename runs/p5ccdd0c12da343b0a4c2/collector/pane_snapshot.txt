## Proof

**Claim:** For $f \in H^1(\mathbb{R}_+;\mathbb{C})$, the limit $f(\infty) := \li
m_{x\to\infty} f(x)$ exists and equals $0$. Consequently the boundary term in th
e displayed integration-by-parts identity satisfies $|f(x)|^2\big|_0^{\infty} =
-|f(0)|^2$.

---

### Step 1. $f$ has a continuous representative

Since $f \in H^1(\mathbb{R}_+)$, both $f$ and its weak derivative $f'$ lie in $L
^2(\mathbb{R}_+)$. In one dimension, the Sobolev embedding $H^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\
mathbb{R}_+) \hookrightarrow C^{0,1/2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_+)$ furnishes a
 representative (still denoted $f$) that is locally Hölder-continuous of exponen
t $1/2$, hence continuous on $\mathbb{R}_+$. In particular the point values $f(0
)$ and $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)$ (if it exists) are meaningful.

### Step 2. $f$ is uniformly continuous on $\mathbb{R}_+$

For $0 \le a < b$,
$$|f(b)-f(a)| = \left|\int_a^b f'(t)\,dt\right| \le \left(\int_a^b |f'|^2\right)
^{1/2}(b-a)^{1/2} \le \|f'\|_{L^2}\,(b-a)^{1/2}.$$
So $f$ is Hölder-$1/2$ on all of $\mathbb{R}_+$, hence uniformly continuous.

### Step 3. $f(x)\to 0$ as $x\to\infty$

Suppose not. Then there exist $\varepsilon>0$ and a sequence $x_n\to\infty$ with
 $|f(x_n)|\ge\varepsilon$. By uniform continuity (Step 2), there is $\delta>0$ (
e.g. $\delta = (\varepsilon/2\|f'\|_{L^2})^2$, or any $\delta$ with $\|f'\|_{L^2
}\sqrt\delta < \varepsilon/2$) such that
$$|x-x_n|<\delta \implies |f(x)-f(x_n)|<\varepsilon/2 \implies |f(x)|\ge\varepsi
lon/2.$$
Passing to a subsequence we may assume the intervals $[x_n,x_n+\delta]$ are pair
wise disjoint. Then
$$\int_{x_n}^{x_n+\delta}|f(x)|^2\,dx \ge \frac{\varepsilon^2}{4}\,\delta \quad\
text{for every }n.$$
Summing over the disjoint intervals,
$$\|f\|_{L^2(\mathbb{R}_+)}^2 \ge \sum_n \frac{\varepsilon^2\delta}{4} = \infty,
$$
contradicting $f\in L^2(\mathbb{R}_+)$. Hence $f(x)\to 0$, i.e. $f(\infty)=0$.

### Step 4. Validation of the integration-by-parts manipulation

Integrate by parts on $[0,R]$ (legitimate since $f$ is absolutely continuous on
compact intervals):
$$\int_0^R f'(x)\overline{f(x)}\,dx = |f(R)|^2 - |f(0)|^2 - \int_0^R f(x)\overli
ne{f'(x)}\,dx.$$
Both integrals converge as $R\to\infty$ because $f,f'\in L^2$ and $f'\bar f,\, f
\bar f'\in L^1$ by Cauchy–Schwarz. Taking $R\to\infty$ and using Step 3 ($|f(R)|
^2\to 0$):
$$\int_0^\infty f'(x)\overline{f(x)}\,dx = \underbrace{|f(\infty)|^2}_{=\,0} - |
f(0)|^2 - \int_0^\infty f(x)\overline{f'(x)}\,dx.$$

### Conclusion

The upper endpoint of the boundary term vanishes **precisely because** $f(\infty
)=0$, which is forced by the membership $f\in H^1(\mathbb{R}_+;\mathbb{C})\subse
t L^2(\mathbb{R}_+)$ together with the uniform continuity inherited from $f'\in
L^2$. Thus

$$|f(x)|^2\Big|_0^{\infty} = -|f(0)|^2,\qquad f(\infty)=0.$$
