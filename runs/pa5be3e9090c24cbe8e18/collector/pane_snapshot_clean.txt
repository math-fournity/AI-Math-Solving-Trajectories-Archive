## Proof

**Claim.** The supremum of the total variation of a continuously differentiable
function on $[0,1]$ is $+\infty$.

**Recall.** For a continuously differentiable function $f\in C^1([0,1])$, the to
tal variation is

$$
V_0^1(f) \;=\; \sup_{0\le t_0<\cdots<t_n\le 1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}|f(t_k)-f(t_{k-1})|
\;=\; \int_0^1 |f'(x)|\,dx,
$$

the equality being the standard characterization of variation for $C^1$ function
s.

**Lower bound: the supremum is infinite.**

For each $n\ge 1$, define

$$
f_n(x)=n\,x, \qquad x\in[0,1].
$$

Then $f_n\in C^1([0,1])$, with $f_n'(x)=n$. Its total variation is

$$
V_0^1(f_n)=\int_0^1 |f_n'(x)|\,dx=\int_0^1 n\,dx=n.
$$

Since $n$ can be taken arbitrarily large,

$$
\sup_{f\in C^1([0,1])} V_0^1(f)\;\ge\;\sup_{n\ge 1} n \;=\;+\infty.
$$

**Upper bound.** Total variation is a non-negative extended real quantity, so th
e only possible upper bound consistent with the lower bound above is $+\infty$ i
tself. Concretely, for every $M>0$ we have exhibited $f\in C^1([0,1])$ with $V_0
^1(f)>M$ (take $f(x)=\lceil M\rceil\, x$), so no finite upper bound exists.

**Conclusion.** Combining both sides,

$$
\boxed{\sup_{f\in C^1([0,1])} V_0^1(f)\;=\;+\infty.}
$$

The family $\{f_n(x)=nx\}$ shows the total variation is unbounded above within $
C^1([0,1])$, hence the supremum is $+\infty$.
