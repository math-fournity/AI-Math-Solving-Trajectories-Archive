**Answer: Yes.** Every multiplicative subset $\Gamma \subseteq B$ is of the form

**Proof.**

Recall that a subset $S$ of a ring is *multiplicative* (multiplicatively closed)
 if $1 \in S$ and $S$ is closed under multiplication.

Let $f: A \to B$ be a surjective ring homomorphism and let $\Gamma \subseteq B$
be a multiplicative subset. Set

$$\Sigma := f^{-1}(\Gamma) = \{a \in A : f(a) \in \Gamma\}.$$

We claim $\Sigma$ is a multiplicative subset of $A$ and $f(\Sigma) = \Gamma$.

**$\Sigma$ is multiplicative.**

- *Contains $1_A$:* Since $f$ is a ring homomorphism, $f(1_A) = 1_B$. Because $\
Gamma$ is multiplicative, $1_B \in \Gamma$, so $1_A \in f^{-1}(\Gamma) = \Sigma$
.

- *Closed under multiplication:* Let $a, a' \in \Sigma$. Then $f(a), f(a') \in \
Gamma$. Since $\Gamma$ is closed under multiplication,
$$f(aa') = f(a)\,f(a') \in \Gamma,$$
so $aa' \in \Sigma$.

Hence $\Sigma$ is a multiplicative subset of $A$.

**$f(\Sigma) = \Gamma$.**

The inclusion $f(\Sigma) \subseteq \Gamma$ holds by definition of $\Sigma$: if $
a \in \Sigma$ then $f(a) \in \Gamma$.

For the reverse inclusion, let $\gamma \in \Gamma \subseteq B$. Since $f$ is **s
urjective**, there exists $a \in A$ with $f(a) = \gamma$. Then $a \in f^{-1}(\Ga
mma) = \Sigma$ and $f(a) = \gamma$, so $\gamma \in f(\Sigma)$. Thus $\Gamma \sub
seteq f(\Sigma)$.

Therefore $f(\Sigma) = \Gamma$. $\blacksquare$
