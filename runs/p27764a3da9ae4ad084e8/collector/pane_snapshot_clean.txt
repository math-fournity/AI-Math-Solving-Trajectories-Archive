Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the Sobolev embedding inequ
ality with constant 1, holding for all $k > r$, extends to $k = r$.

## Proof

**Setup.** Define the optimal embedding constant
$$C(s) := \sup\left\{\frac{\|f\|_{L^\infty(\mathbb{R})}}{\|f\|_{H^s(\mathbb{R})}
} : f \in H^s(\mathbb{R}),\; f \neq 0\right\}.$$
The inequality $\|f\|_{L^\infty} \le \|f\|_{H^k}$ for all $f \in H^k$ is equival
ent to $C(k) \le 1$.

**Step 1: Explicit computation of $C(s)$ for $s > 1/2$.**

Using the Fourier transform with convention $\hat{f}(\xi) = \int f(x)e^{-ix\xi}\
,dx$, $f(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int \hat{f}(\xi)e^{ix\xi}\,d\xi$, the Sobolev norm
is
$$\|f\|_{H^s}^2 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}} (1+\xi^2)^s |\hat{f}(\xi)|^2\,
d\xi.$$

For any $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}$, write
$$f(x_0) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int (1+\xi^2)^{-s/2}\cdot (1+\xi^2)^{s/2}\hat{f}(\xi)\
,e^{ix_0\xi}\,d\xi.$$

By Cauchy–Schwarz:
$$|f(x_0)| \le \frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+\xi^2)^{-s}\,d\xi\right)^
{1/2}\|f\|_{H^s}.$$

The integral $\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+\xi^2)^{-s}\,d\xi = \sqrt{\pi}\,\frac{\Gamma(s
-1/2)}{\Gamma(s)}$ converges if and only if $s > 1/2$.

**Equality is attained** when $(1+\xi^2)^{s/2}\hat{f}(\xi) = c\,(1+\xi^2)^{-s/2}
e^{-ix_0\xi}$, i.e., $\hat{f}(\xi) = c\,(1+\xi^2)^{-s}e^{-ix_0\xi}$. For $s > 1/
2$, this $\hat{f}$ satisfies $(1+\xi^2)^s|\hat{f}|^2 = c^2(1+\xi^2)^{-s} \in L^1
$ (so $f \in H^s$) and $\hat{f} \in L^1$ (so $f$ is continuous and bounded). The
refore the supremum is achieved, and
$$C(s) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\sqrt{\pi}\,\frac{\Gamma(s-1/2)}{\Gamma(s)}\right)^
{1/2}, \qquad s > \tfrac{1}{2}.$$

**Step 2: Continuity and monotonicity of $C(s)$.**

Since $\Gamma$ is smooth and strictly positive on $(0,\infty)$, the function $C(
s)$ is **continuous** (indeed smooth) on $(1/2, \infty)$.

Moreover, $C(s)$ is **strictly decreasing**: the integral $\int (1+\xi^2)^{-s}\,
d\xi$ is strictly decreasing in $s$ (the integrand decreases pointwise for $\xi
\neq 0$).

Asymptotically: $C(s) \to +\infty$ as $s \to (1/2)^+$ (since $\Gamma(s-1/2) \to
\infty$), and $C(s) \to 0$ as $s \to \infty$ (by Stirling's approximation, $\Gam
ma(s-1/2)/\Gamma(s) \sim s^{-1/2}$).

**Step 3: Analysis of the hypothesis.**

The hypothesis states $C(k) \le 1$ for all $k > r$.

- **Case $r \le 1/2$:** Since $C(s) \to \infty$ as $s \to (1/2)^+$, there exist
values $k > r$ (with $k$ near $1/2$) for which $C(k) > 1$. (For $k \le 1/2$, $C(
k) = \infty$ since $H^k \not\hookrightarrow L^\infty$.) Thus the hypothesis $C(k
) \le 1$ for all $k > r$ is **false**, and the implication is **vacuously true**
.

- **Case $r > 1/2$:** By the continuity of $C$ at $r$ (established in Step 2),
$$C(r) = \lim_{k \to r^+} C(k) \le 1,$$
where the inequality uses $C(k) \le 1$ for all $k > r$. Therefore $\|f\|_{L^\inf
ty} \le \|f\|_{H^r}$ for all $f \in H^r(\mathbb{R})$.

**Conclusion.** In every case where the hypothesis is satisfied, the conclusion
$\|f\|_{L^\infty(\mathbb{R})} \le \|f\|_{H^r(\mathbb{R})}$ holds. The answer is
**yes**.

The essential mechanism is that the optimal constant $C(s)$ in the Sobolev embed
ding $H^s(\mathbb{R}) \hookrightarrow L^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ is a **continuous**
function of $s$ on $(1/2, \infty)$. Continuity prevents any "jump" at $s = r$: t
he constant at $r$ equals the right-hand limit, which is controlled by the hypot
hesis. $\square$
