## Counterexample: Cofinite topology on an uncountable set

Let $X$ be an uncountable set equipped with the **cofinite topology** (open sets
 are $\emptyset$ and all cofinite subsets).

### Step 1: $X$ is $T_1$ but not normal

**$T_1$:** Every singleton $\{x\}$ is finite, hence closed. So $X$ is $T_1$.

**Not normal:** Take two distinct points $a, b \in X$. The sets $\{a\}$ and $\{b
\}$ are disjoint closed sets. Any open set $U \ni a$ is cofinite, and any open s
et $V \ni b$ is cofinite. Since $X$ is infinite, two cofinite sets always inters
ect: $|X \setminus U| < \infty$ and $|X \setminus V| < \infty$, so $X \setminus
(U \cap V) = (X \setminus U) \cup (X \setminus V)$ is finite, hence $U \cap V \n
eq \emptyset$. Thus $\{a\}$ and $\{b\}$ cannot be separated by disjoint open set
s, so $X$ is **not normal**.

### Step 2: The only continuous real-valued functions are constants

Suppose $f: X \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous and non-constant, taking values $\al
pha \neq \beta$. Since $\mathbb{R}$ is Hausdorff, there exist disjoint open $U,
V \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ with $\alpha \in U$, $\beta \in V$. Then $f^{-1}(U)$ and
 $f^{-1}(V)$ are disjoint nonempty open sets in $X$, both cofinite. But two cofi
nite subsets of an infinite set always intersect — contradiction. Hence every co
ntinuous $f: X \to \mathbb{R}$ is constant.

### Step 3: The only zero-sets are $\emptyset$ and $X$

Since every continuous $f$ is constant: $f \equiv c$ gives $f^{-1}(\{0\}) = X$ i
f $c = 0$, and $f^{-1}(\{0\}) = \emptyset$ if $c \neq 0$. So the zero-sets are e
xactly $\{\emptyset, X\}$.

### Step 4: The only closed $G_\delta$-sets are $\emptyset$ and $X$

**Closed sets** in the cofinite topology are finite sets and $X$ itself.

**$G_\delta$-sets:** A $G_\delta$-set is $\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} U_n$ with each
$U_n$ open (cofinite or $\emptyset$). If any $U_n = \emptyset$, the intersection
 is $\emptyset$. Otherwise, each $U_n$ is cofinite, so $\bigcap U_n = X \setminu
s \bigcup_{n}(X \setminus U_n)$, where each $X \setminus U_n$ is finite. The uni
on $\bigcup_n (X \setminus U_n)$ is **countable**, so $\bigcap U_n$ has a **coun
table** complement — in particular, $\bigcap U_n$ is co-countable (hence uncount
able, since $X$ is uncountable).

**Closed $\cap$ $G_\delta$:** A finite set $F$ is closed. For $F$ to be a $G_\de
lta$, we'd need $X \setminus F = \bigcup_n (X \setminus U_n)$ (a countable union
 of finite sets, hence countable). But $X$ is uncountable and $F$ is finite, so
$X \setminus F$ is **uncountable** — contradiction. Therefore no nonempty finite
 set is a $G_\delta$.

The only closed $G_\delta$-sets are $\emptyset$ and $X$.

### Step 5: Verification

Every closed $G_\delta$-set ($\emptyset$ or $X$) is a zero-set (Step 3). Yet $X$
 is not normal (Step 1).

## Conclusion

The cofinite topology on an uncountable set satisfies the condition that every c
losed $G_\delta$-set is a zero-set, but the space is $T_1$ and **not** normal. T
herefore, the answer is **no** — it does not follow that $X$ is normal.
