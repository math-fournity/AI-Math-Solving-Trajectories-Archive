Looking at this problem, I need to determine for which $n \geq 2$ there exist pa
irwise distinct positive integers $A_1, \ldots, A_n$ such that $\prod_{i=1}^n (A
_i + k)$ is a perfect power for every natural number $k$.

**Answer: There are no such $n \geq 2$.**

## Proof

I show that for **every** $n \geq 2$ and **every** choice of pairwise distinct p
ositive integers $A_1, \ldots, A_n$, there exists a natural number $k$ for which
 the product is **not** a perfect power.

**Setup.** Let $A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_n$ be pairwise distinct positive integers, w
ith $n \geq 2$. Define
$$D = \max_{i \neq j} |A_i - A_j|,$$
which is a fixed positive integer (since the $A_i$ are distinct, at least one di
fference is nonzero).

**Choosing $k$.** By the infinitude of primes, there exists a prime $p > \max(D,
 A_1)$. Set
$$k = p - A_1.$$
Since $p > A_1 \geq 1$, we have $k \geq 1$, so $k$ is a natural number.

**Analyzing the product.** Consider $P(k) = \prod_{i=1}^n (A_i + k)$.

- **For $i = 1$:** $A_1 + k = A_1 + (p - A_1) = p$, which is prime.

- **For $i \neq 1$:** $A_i + k = A_i + p - A_1 = p + (A_i - A_1)$. Since $A_i \n
eq A_1$, we have $A_i - A_1 \neq 0$, and since $|A_i - A_1| \leq D < p$, we get
$0 < |A_i - A_1| < p$. Therefore:
  - $A_i + k \equiv A_i - A_1 \pmod{p}$, and since $0 < |A_i - A_1| < p$, we con
clude $p \nmid (A_i + k)$.

**Valuation argument.** Since $p$ divides the factor $A_1 + k = p$ exactly once
(it is prime), and $p$ does not divide any other factor $A_i + k$ for $i \neq 1$
, the $p$-adic valuation of the product is:
$$v_p\!\left(\prod_{i=1}^n (A_i + k)\right) = 1.$$

**Conclusion.** Suppose for contradiction that $P(k) = m^e$ for some integers $m
 \geq 1$ and $e \geq 2$ (i.e., $P(k)$ is a perfect power). Then:
$$v_p(P(k)) = v_p(m^e) = e \cdot v_p(m),$$
which is a multiple of $e \geq 2$. But we showed $v_p(P(k)) = 1$, which is not d
ivisible by any integer $e \geq 2$. **Contradiction.**

Therefore $P(k)$ is not a perfect power for this choice of $k$.

**Remark.** The same argument works for $n = 1$ as well (choosing $k$ so that $A
_1 + k$ is a sufficiently large prime gives a product that is prime, hence not a
 perfect power). But the problem restricts to $n \geq 2$.

Since for every $n \geq 2$ and every choice of pairwise distinct positive intege
rs $A_1, \ldots, A_n$, we can find a natural number $k$ making the product not a
 perfect power, **there is no $n \geq 2$ for which such integers exist**. $\blac
ksquare$
