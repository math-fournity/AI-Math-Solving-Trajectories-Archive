## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. If $R$ is a commutative ring with unity, finitely generated as a
 $\mathbb{Z}$-algebra, then the Jacobson radical $J(R)$ is nilpotent.

The proof proceeds in four steps.

### Step 1: $R$ is Noetherian.

$\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian (it is a PID). By the Hilbert Basis Theorem, if $A$ i
s Noetherian then $A[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is Noetherian, and any quotient of a Noet
herian ring is Noetherian. Since $R$ is finitely generated over $\mathbb{Z}$, we
 have a surjection $\mathbb{Z}[x_1, \dots, x_n] \twoheadrightarrow R$ for some $
n$. Therefore $R$ is Noetherian.

### Step 2: $R$ is a Jacobson ring.

Recall that a ring $A$ is **Jacobson** (a Hilbert ring) if every prime ideal of
$A$ is an intersection of maximal ideals.

**$\mathbb{Z}$ is Jacobson.** The prime ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$ are $(0)$ and $(p
)$ for rational primes $p$. Each $(p)$ is maximal. And $(0) = \bigcap_{p \text{
prime}} (p)$, so $(0)$ is an intersection of maximal ideals. Hence $\mathbb{Z}$
is Jacobson.

**General Jacobson theorem:** If $A$ is a Jacobson ring and $B$ is a finitely ge
nerated $A$-algebra, then $B$ is Jacobson. (This is the generalized Nullstellens
atz / Jacobson transfer theorem.)

Since $R$ is finitely generated over $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}$ is Jacobson,
$R$ is Jacobson.

### Step 3: In a Jacobson ring, $J(R) = \operatorname{Nil}(R)$.

Let $\operatorname{Nil}(R) = \bigcap\{P : P \text{ prime}\}$ be the nilradical a
nd $J(R) = \bigcap\{M : M \text{ maximal}\}$ the Jacobson radical.

Since every maximal ideal is prime, $\operatorname{Nil}(R) \subseteq J(R)$ (inte
rsecting over a larger family yields a smaller ideal).

For the reverse inclusion, let $x \in J(R)$ and let $P$ be any prime ideal. Sinc
e $R$ is Jacobson, $P$ is an intersection of maximal ideals:
$$P = \bigcap\{M : M \text{ maximal}, \, M \supseteq P\}.$$
Since $x \in J(R) \subseteq M$ for **every** maximal ideal $M$, in particular $x
 \in M$ for every maximal $M \supseteq P$. Hence $x \in \bigcap\{M \supseteq P\}
 = P$.

Since $x \in P$ for every prime $P$, we have $x \in \operatorname{Nil}(R)$. Thus
 $J(R) \subseteq \operatorname{Nil}(R)$.

Combining both inclusions: $J(R) = \operatorname{Nil}(R)$.

### Step 4: In a Noetherian ring, the nilradical is nilpotent.

Since $R$ is Noetherian (Step 1), $\operatorname{Nil}(R)$ is finitely generated,
 say $\operatorname{Nil}(R) = (x_1, \dots, x_m)$. Each $x_i$ is nilpotent; let $
n_i$ be such that $x_i^{n_i} = 0$, and set $N = \max_i n_i$.

Any element of $\operatorname{Nil}(R)^{m(N-1)+1}$ is a sum of monomials of degre
e $m(N-1)+1$ in $x_1, \dots, x_m$. By the pigeonhole principle, each such monomi
al contains some generator $x_i$ raised to a power $\geq N \geq n_i$, hence is $
0$. Therefore
$$\operatorname{Nil}(R)^{m(N-1)+1} = 0,$$
so $\operatorname{Nil}(R)$ is nilpotent.

### Conclusion

By Step 3, $J(R) = \operatorname{Nil}(R)$. By Step 4, $\operatorname{Nil}(R)$ is
 nilpotent. Therefore $J(R)$ is nilpotent. $\blacksquare$
