## Proof

**Setup.** There are $\binom{10}{2}=45$ segments. We count unordered triples of
distinct segments where every pair shares a common point (endpoint or internal c
rossing). We classify by the number of distinct endpoints used by the three segm
ents: this can be 3, 4, 5, or 6.

---

### Case 1: Three distinct endpoints (Triangle)

The only three segments on three points are the three sides of the triangle. Eve
ry pair shares an endpoint, so all such triples are valid.

$$\binom{10}{3} = 120$$

### Case 2: Four distinct endpoints

Three edges on four vertices form either a **star** $K_{1,3}$ or a **path** $P_4
$ (the only two graph types with 4 vertices and 3 edges, excluding the triangle
which uses 3 vertices).

**Star** $(a,b),(a,c),(a,d)$: All pairs share the center $a$. Always valid. Choo
se center (10 ways) and 3 leaves from remaining 9:

$$10 \cdot \binom{9}{3} = 10 \cdot 84 = 840$$

**Path** $v_1\!-\!v_2\!-\!v_3\!-\!v_4$: The adjacent edge-pairs $(v_1v_2, v_2v_3
)$ and $(v_2v_3, v_3v_4)$ share endpoints. The end-edges $(v_1v_2)$ and $(v_3v_4
)$ share no endpoint, so they must **cross internally**.

For four points on a circle, the only crossing pair of disjoint chords is the pa
ir of diagonals. So the end-edges must be the two diagonals. The middle edge con
nects one endpoint of each diagonal — giving $2 \times 2 = 4$ distinct paths per
 4-point set (verified: each gives a unique edge set, and all 4 have crossing en
d-edges).

$$\binom{10}{4} \cdot 4 = 210 \cdot 4 = 840$$

**Case 2 total:** $840 + 840 = 1680$.

### Case 3: Five distinct endpoints

Exactly one point is shared by two segments; the third segment is endpoint-disjo
int from both and must **cross both**.

**Counting via 5-point sets.** Choose 5 points: $\binom{10}{5}=252$. For each se
t, we show exactly 5 valid configurations.

Place 5 points in circular order $p_1, p_2, p_3, p_4, p_5$. Let the shared point
 be $p_i$. The two segments from $p_i$ go to two other points, and the third seg
ment connects the remaining two. We need the third segment to cross both chords
from $p_i$.

**Claim:** For shared point $p_i$, the unique valid choice is: connect $p_i$ to
the two **non-adjacent** points (those not next to $p_i$ in the circular order),
 and the third segment connects the two **adjacent** points.

*Proof of claim.* Label the 5 points so $p_i = a$, its neighbors are $x, y$, and
 the remaining two (non-adjacent to $a$) are $b, c$, with circular order $a, x,
b, c, y$. Set the segments as $(a,b), (a,c), (x,y)$.

- $(x,y)$ crosses $(a,b)$: Chord $(a,b)$ separates $\{x\}$ from $\{c, y\}$ (sinc
e $x$ lies on the arc $a \to b$, while $y$ lies on the other arc). So $x$ and $y
$ are on opposite sides. ✓
- $(x,y)$ crosses $(a,c)$: Chord $(a,c)$ separates $\{x, b\}$ from $\{y\}$. So $
x$ and $y$ are on opposite sides. ✓

For uniqueness, if any segment from $a$ goes to an adjacent point (say $(a,x)$),
 then $(a,x)$ is a side of the pentagon and no chord with both endpoints among t
he remaining points can cross it (all remaining points lie on one side). So both
 chords from $a$ must go to non-adjacent points, forcing the configuration. □

Each of the 5 choices of shared point gives exactly 1 valid configuration:

$$\binom{10}{5} \cdot 5 = 252 \cdot 5 = 1260$$

### Case 4: Six distinct endpoints (all pairwise crossing)

No endpoints are shared, so every pair must cross internally. We need three chor
ds on six points, all pairwise crossing.

**Claim:** For six points in convex position, exactly **one** perfect matching h
as all chords pairwise crossing.

*Proof.* Label points $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$ in circular order. Point 1 is matched w
ith some point $k$:

- $k=2$ or $k=6$: $(1,k)$ is a side; all other points lie on one side, so no cho
rd crosses it. Invalid.
- $k=3$ or $k=5$: $(1,k)$ leaves one point alone on one arc and three on the oth
er. One remaining chord must use the lone point (crossing $(1,k)$), but the othe
r chord uses two points from the three-point side and doesn't cross $(1,k)$. Inv
alid.
- $k=4$: $(1,4)$ splits $\{2,3\}$ and $\{5,6\}$. Each remaining chord needs one
from each side: either $\{(2,5),(3,6)\}$ or $\{(2,6),(3,5)\}$. Only $\{(2,5),(3,
6)\}$ has the two chords crossing each other (verified by checking interleaving)
. So exactly one matching: $\{(1,4),(2,5),(3,6)\}$. □

$$\binom{10}{6} \cdot 1 = 210$$
