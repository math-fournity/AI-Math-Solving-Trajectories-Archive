## Proof

**Step 1. $L_\omega$ is countable (in ZF).**

By definition $L_\omega = \bigcup_{n<\omega} L_n$, where $L_0=\varnothing$ and $
L_{n+1}=\mathrm{Def}(L_n)$. A straightforward induction (provable in ZF) shows e
ach $L_n$ is finite: $L_0$ is empty, and if $L_n$ is finite then $\mathrm{Def}(L
_n)$ — the collection of subsets of $L_n$ first-order definable over $(L_n,\in)$
 with parameters from $L_n$ — is finite (finitely many formulas, finitely many p
arameter choices). Hence $L_\omega$ is a countable union of finite sets, so $L_\
omega$ is countable. In fact $L_\omega = \mathrm{HF}$, the hereditarily finite s
ets.

**Step 2. $L_{\omega+1}$ is countable (in ZF).**

$L_{\omega+1} = \mathrm{Def}(L_\omega) = \mathrm{Def}(\mathrm{HF})$. An element
of $L_{\omega+1}$ is a subset of $L_\omega$ definable over $(L_\omega,\in)$ by a
 first-order formula with finitely many parameters from $L_\omega$. There are co
untably many formulas (the language is finite/countable), and for each formula t
he parameters range over the countable set $L_\omega$. Thus the number of defina
ble subsets is at most
$$\aleph_0 \cdot \aleph_0^{<\omega} = \aleph_0.$$
So ZF proves $|L_{\omega+1}| \le \aleph_0$; in particular $L_{\omega+1}$ is coun
table.

**Step 3. $P(\mathbb{N})$ is uncountable (in ZF).**

This is Cantor's theorem applied to $\mathbb{N}$, which is provable in ZF withou
t any use of Choice: for any function $f:\mathbb{N}\to P(\mathbb{N})$, the diago
nal set $D=\{n\in\mathbb{N}: n\notin f(n)\}$ is a subset of $\mathbb{N}$ not in
the range of $f$. Hence $|P(\mathbb{N})|>|\mathbb{N}|=\aleph_0$.

**Step 4. ZF proves $P(\mathbb{N})\not\subseteq L_{\omega+1}$.**

From Step 2, $L_{\omega+1}$ is countable, so $L_{\omega+1}\cap P(\mathbb{N})$ is
 countable. From Step 3, $P(\mathbb{N})$ is uncountable. Therefore
$$|L_{\omega+1}\cap P(\mathbb{N})| \le \aleph_0 < |P(\mathbb{N})|,$$
so there exists a subset of $\mathbb{N}$ not belonging to $L_{\omega+1}$. This i
s a theorem of ZF.

**Step 5. Connection with arithmetical sets (clarifying the parenthetical).**

The subsets of $\mathbb{N}$ lying in $L_{\omega+1}=\mathrm{Def}(\mathrm{HF})$ ar
e exactly the **arithmetical** sets: a set $A\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ is arithmetica
l iff it is first-order definable in $(\mathbb{N},+,\times,<,0,1)$ (with paramet
ers), and coding arithmetic inside $\mathrm{HF}$ via the von Neumann numerals sh
ows this is the same as being definable over $(\mathrm{HF},\in)$ with parameters
 from $\mathrm{HF}$. Since there are only countably many arithmetical sets while
 $P(\mathbb{N})$ is uncountable, ZF proves the existence of non-arithmetical sub
sets of $\mathbb{N}$ — confirming the conclusion of Step 4 concretely.

**Conclusion.**

The statement "$P(\mathbb{N})\subseteq L_{\omega+1}$" is not merely independent
of ZF; it is **refutable** in ZF. ZF proves that $L_{\omega+1}$ is countable whi
le $P(\mathbb{N})$ is uncountable (Cantor), so $P(\mathbb{N})\not\subseteq L_{\o
mega+1}$. Equivalently, ZF proves that not every subset of $\mathbb{N}$ is arith
metical. Hence it is **not consistent** with ZF that $P(\mathbb{N})\subseteq L_{
\omega+1}$.
