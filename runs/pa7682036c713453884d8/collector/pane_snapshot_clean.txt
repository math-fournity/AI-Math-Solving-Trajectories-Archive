The statement is **true**. Here is the proof.

**Setup.** Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{C}$ denote the set of singularities of $f$.
We are given that $S$ is uncountable. A singularity $s \in S$ is *isolated* if t
here exists an open neighborhood $U$ of $s$ with $U \cap S = \{s\}$; otherwise $
s$ is a *non-isolated* singularity (every neighborhood of $s$ contains another s
ingularity). We must show $S$ contains a non-isolated point.

**Key topological fact.** *In a second-countable topological space, the set of i
solated points of any subset is at most countable.*

*Proof of fact.* $\mathbb{C}$ is second-countable; a countable base is
$$\mathcal{B} = \bigl\{B(q,r) : q \in \mathbb{Q}+i\mathbb{Q},\; r \in \mathbb{Q}
_{>0}\bigr\}.$$
For each isolated point $s$ of $S$, pick an open $U_s \ni s$ with $U_s \cap S =
\{s\}$. By the definition of a base, there exists $B_s \in \mathcal{B}$ with $s
\in B_s \subseteq U_s$, so $B_s \cap S = \{s\}$. The assignment $s \mapsto B_s$
is injective: if $B_s = B_t$ for distinct isolated points $s \neq t$, then $B_s
\cap S$ would contain both $s$ and $t$, contradicting $B_s \cap S = \{s\}$. Sinc
e $\mathcal{B}$ is countable and the map is injective, the set of isolated point
s of $S$ is at most countable. $\square$

**Conclusion.** Let $I \subseteq S$ be the (at most countable) set of isolated s
ingularities. Then
$$S \setminus I$$
is **uncountable**, since removing a countable set from an uncountable set leave
s an uncountable set. Every point of $S \setminus I$ is, by definition, a singul
arity that is *not* isolated — i.e., a non-isolated singularity. In particular $
S \setminus I \neq \varnothing$.

Therefore, if $f$ has an uncountable number of singularities, then $f$ must have
 a non-isolated singularity. $\blacksquare$
