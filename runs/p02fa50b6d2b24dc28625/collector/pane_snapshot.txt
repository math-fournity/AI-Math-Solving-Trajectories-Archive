## Proof

Let \(X\) be a compact (Hausdorff) space, and let \(\{f_1,\dots,f_n\}\) be a fin
ite family of continuous real-valued functions on \(X\) that **separates points*
*, i.e., for every \(x\neq y\) in \(X\) there is some \(i\) with \(f_i(x)\neq f_
i(y)\).

Define the evaluation map
\[
F:X\to\mathbb{R}^n,\qquad F(x)=(f_1(x),\dots,f_n(x)).
\]

**Step 1: \(F\) is continuous.** Each coordinate \(f_i\) is continuous, so \(F\)
 is continuous (a map into a product is continuous iff every coordinate is).

**Step 2: \(F\) is injective.** If \(x\neq y\), the separating property gives so
me \(i\) with \(f_i(x)\neq f_i(y)\), hence \(F(x)\neq F(y)\).

**Step 3: \(F\) is a homeomorphism onto its image.** \(X\) is compact and \(\mat
hbb{R}^n\) is Hausdorff. A continuous injection from a compact space into a Haus
dorff space is a closed map, hence a homeomorphism onto its image \(F(X)\subset\
mathbb{R}^n\).

**Step 4: \(F(X)\) is metrizable and separable.** \(\mathbb{R}^n\) with its usua
l metric is metrizable and separable (equivalently, second countable). Both metr
izability and second countability are hereditary to subspaces, so \(F(X)\) is me
trizable and separable.

**Step 5: Conclusion.** Since \(X\cong F(X)\), the space \(X\) is itself metriza
ble and separable. Therefore \(X\) **cannot** be separable but not metrizable —
the existence of a finite point-separating family of continuous real-valued func
tions on a compact space forces metrizability (via the embedding into \(\mathbb{
R}^n\)).

(Remark: the phrase "compact metric space" already entails metrizability tautolo
gically; the substantive content above is that the finite separating family alon
e — even without any a priori metric — already forces the space to be metrizable
, and separability then follows for free from the embedding into separable \(\ma
thbb{R}^n\).)
