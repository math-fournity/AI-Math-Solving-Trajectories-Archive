## Solution

Let $P_k = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k = 2\cdot 3\cdot 5 \cdots p_k$ denote the product o
f the first $k$ primes (the $k$-th primorial). Call $k$ *interesting* if $k \mid
 P_k$.

### Key characterization

**Claim.** A positive integer $k$ is interesting if and only if $k$ is squarefre
e.

**Proof of Claim.**

Since $P_k$ is a product of *distinct* primes, $P_k$ is squarefree. Every diviso
r of a squarefree number is itself squarefree, so if $k \mid P_k$ then $k$ must
be squarefree. This gives the "only if" direction.

Conversely, suppose $k$ is squarefree, and write $k = q_1 q_2 \cdots q_m$ where
$q_1 < q_2 < \cdots < q_m$ are distinct primes. Each prime factor satisfies $q_i
 \le k$. Since the $k$-th prime satisfies $p_k \ge k$ (indeed $p_k > k$ for all
$k\ge 1$), we have $q_i \le k \le p_k$. Being a prime not exceeding $p_k$, each
$q_i$ must be one of $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_k$. Hence $q_i \mid P_k$ for every $i$
. Since the $q_i$ are distinct primes, their product $k = q_1\cdots q_m$ divides
 $P_k$. This gives the "if" direction. $\square$

### Reducing to consecutive squarefree integers

By the claim, the problem asks for the maximal length of a run of consecutive sq
uarefree positive integers.

**Upper bound: at most 3.** Among any four consecutive positive integers $n, n+1
, n+2, n+3$, exactly one is divisible by $4$. Any multiple of $4$ has $2^2$ as a
 factor, so it is not squarefree. Therefore no four consecutive positive integer
s can all be squarefree, giving an upper bound of $3$.

**Lower bound: 3 is achievable.** The integers $5, 6, 7$ are consecutive and all
 squarefree:
- $5$ is prime,
- $6 = 2\cdot 3$ is squarefree,
- $7$ is prime.

(Indeed, $5\mid P_5 = 2310$, $6\mid P_6 = 30030$, $7\mid P_7 = 510510$.)

### Conclusion

The maximal possible number of consecutive interesting integers is $\boxed{3}$.
