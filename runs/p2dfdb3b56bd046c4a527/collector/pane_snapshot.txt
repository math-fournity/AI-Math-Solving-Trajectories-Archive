## Answer

**No**, we cannot conclude that $\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x)=0$. The following counte
rexample satisfies all the hypotheses yet fails to have limit $0$ at infinity.

---

### Construction

Work on $[0,\infty)$. For each integer $n\ge 1$, let $w_n = 2^{-n-2}$ (so $w_n<1
/4$, guaranteeing the supports below are disjoint) and define the triangular spi
ke

$$
T_n(x)=\max\!\Bigl(0,\;1-\frac{|x-n|}{w_n}\Bigr).
$$

$T_n$ is a triangle of height $1$ centered at $x=n$ with base $[n-w_n,n+w_n]$. S
et

$$
f(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} T_n(x).
$$

Since the bases are disjoint (each has length $2w_n<1/2$ and centers are spaced
$1$ apart), the sum is pointwise well defined and there is no interaction betwee
n spikes.

### Verification of the hypotheses

**$f$ is integrable.** Each spike has area $\int T_n = \tfrac12\cdot 1\cdot 2w_n
 = w_n$, so by disjointness

$$
\int_0^\infty |f(x)|\,dx = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \int T_n = \sum_{n=1}^\infty w_n =
\sum_{n=1}^\infty 2^{-n-2} = \frac12 < \infty.
$$

Hence $f\in L^1([0,\infty))$.

**$f$ is differentiable almost everywhere.** Each $T_n$ is piecewise linear, hen
ce differentiable except at the three points $n-w_n,\;n,\;n+w_n$. The union of a
ll these exceptional points is countable, hence of measure zero. On the compleme
nt $f$ equals locally a single linear function, so $f$ is differentiable a.e.

**$f'$ is not integrable.** On each spike, $|T_n'|=1/w_n$ on $(n-w_n,n)\cup(n,n+
w_n)$ and $0$ elsewhere, so

$$
\int_0^\infty |f'(x)|\,dx = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \int |T_n'| = \sum_{n=1}^\infty 2
= \infty.
$$

Thus $f'\notin L^1$.

### Failure of the conclusion

At every integer $n\ge 1$ we have $f(n)=T_n(n)=1$. Therefore

$$
\limsup_{x\to\infty} f(x)\ge 1,
$$

and in particular $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)\neq 0$ (the limit does not even exist).

### Conclusion

The hypotheses "$f$ integrable, differentiable a.e., $f'$ not integrable" do **n
ot** force $f(x)\to 0$ as $x\to\infty$. The spike construction above is an expli
cit counterexample: it is integrable, differentiable a.e., has a non‑integrable
derivative, yet satisfies $f(n)=1$ for all $n$, so the limit at infinity is not
$0$.
