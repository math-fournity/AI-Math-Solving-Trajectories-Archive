## Derivation of Poisson's Formula for the 2D Wave Equation

### 1. Setup and Strategy (Method of Descent)

We consider the Cauchy problem

$$u_{x_1 x_1} + u_{x_2 x_2} - u_{tt} = 0, \qquad u(x_1,x_2,0)=\phi(x_1,x_2),\qua
d u_t(x_1,x_2,0)=\psi(x_1,x_2). \tag{1}$$

The strategy is the **method of descent** (Hadamard): we embed the 2D problem in
to 3D by introducing a fictitious third variable $x_3$, extend the initial data
to be independent of $x_3$, solve the resulting 3D problem via Kirchhoff's formu
la, and then restrict back to two dimensions. The key observation is that if the
 data do not depend on $x_3$, neither does the solution, so the 3D solution rest
ricted to any plane $x_3=\text{const}$ solves the original 2D problem.

### 2. The 3D Problem and Kirchhoff's Formula

Define $\widetilde\phi(x_1,x_2,x_3) = \phi(x_1,x_2)$ and $\widetilde\psi(x_1,x_2
,x_3) = \psi(x_1,x_2)$, both independent of $x_3$. Consider the 3D Cauchy proble
m

$$\widetilde u_{x_1x_1}+\widetilde u_{x_2x_2}+\widetilde u_{x_3x_3}-\widetilde u
_{tt}=0,\qquad \widetilde u|_{t=0}=\widetilde\phi,\quad \widetilde u_t|_{t=0}=\w
idetilde\psi. \tag{2}$$

By uniqueness, $\widetilde u$ is independent of $x_3$, so $\widetilde u_{x_3x_3}
=0$ and $\widetilde u$ solves (1). Thus $u(x_1,x_2,t) = \widetilde u(x_1,x_2,0,t
)$.

Kirchhoff's formula (with wave speed $c=1$) gives the solution of (2) at the poi
nt $x=(x_1,x_2,0)$ and time $t>0$:

$$\widetilde u(x,t) = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[\frac{1}{4\pi t}\iint_{S_
t} \widetilde\phi(y)\,dS_y\right] + \frac{1}{4\pi t}\iint_{S_t} \widetilde\psi(y
)\,dS_y, \tag{3}$$

where $S_t = \{y\in\mathbb R^3 : |y-x|=t\}$ is the sphere of radius $t$ centered
 at $x=(x_1,x_2,0)$.

### 3. Reducing the Surface Integral to a Disk Integral

Write $y=(y_1,y_2,y_3)$ and $x=(x_1,x_2,0)$. The sphere $S_t$ is

$$(y_1-x_1)^2+(y_2-x_2)^2+y_3^2 = t^2.$$

Project $S_t$ onto the $(y_1,y_2)$-plane. The projection is the closed disk

$$D_t = \{(y_1,y_2) : (y_1-x_1)^2+(y_2-x_2)^2 \le t^2\}.$$

For a point $(y_1,y_2)\in D_t$ in the interior, there are two preimages on $S_t$
:

$$y_3 = \pm\sqrt{t^2 - (y_1-x_1)^2 - (y_2-x_2)^2}.$$

The surface element on the sphere relates to the area element $dy_1\,dy_2$ in th
e plane by

$$dS = \frac{t}{\sqrt{t^2-(y_1-x_1)^2-(y_2-x_2)^2}}\,dy_1\,dy_2.$$

(Indeed, the unit normal to the sphere has $x_3$-component $y_3/t$, so $dS = \fr
ac{t}{|y_3|}\,dy_1\,dy_2$.)

Since $\widetilde\phi$ and $\widetilde\psi$ are independent of $y_3$, the two he
mispherical contributions are equal. Summing the upper and lower hemispheres:

$$\iint_{S_t} \widetilde\phi(y)\,dS_y = 2\iint_{D_t} \phi(y_1,y_2)\,\frac{t}{\sq
rt{t^2-(y_1-x_1)^2-(y_2-x_2)^2}}\,dy_1\,dy_2. \tag{4}$$

The same identity holds with $\widetilde\psi$ and $\psi$.

### 4. Substituting into Kirchhoff's Formula

Insert (4) into (3). The factor of $2$ cancels the $4\pi$ to give $2\pi$:

$$\frac{1}{4\pi t}\iint_{S_t}\widetilde\phi\,dS = \frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{D_t}\frac
{\phi(y_1,y_2)}{\sqrt{t^2-(y_1-x_1)^2-(y_2-x_2)^2}}\,dy_1\,dy_2. \tag{5}$$

Define

$$I_\phi(t) := \iint_{D_t}\frac{\phi(y_1,y_2)}{\sqrt{t^2-(y_1-x_1)^2-(y_2-x_2)^2
}}\,dy_1\,dy_2, \tag{6}$$

and similarly $I_\psi(t)$. Then (3) becomes

$$u(x_1,x_2,t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}I_\phi(t) + \frac{1}{2
\pi}I_\psi(t). \tag{7}$$

### 5. Computing the Time Derivative

To differentiate $I_\phi(t)$, we pass to polar coordinates centered at $(x_1,x_2
)$: let $y_1 = x_1 + r\cos\theta$, $y_2 = x_2 + r\sin\theta$, with $0\le r\le t$
, $0\le\theta<2\pi$. Then

$$I_\phi(t) = \int_0^t\int_0^{2\pi} \frac{\phi(x_1+r\cos\theta,\,x_2+r\sin\theta
)}{\sqrt{t^2-r^2}}\,r\,d\theta\,dr.$$

Split the $r$-integral at $r=t$ and differentiate under the integral sign. Using
 the Leibniz rule, the boundary contribution at $r=t$ vanishes (the integrand ha
s an integrable singularity and the boundary term is zero), and we differentiate
 the kernel $\frac{1}{\sqrt{t^2-r^2}}$:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t^2-r^2}} = -\frac{t}{(t^2-r^2)^{3/2}}.$$

A cleaner way to evaluate $\frac{\partial}{\partial t}I_\phi(t)$ is to introduce
 the substitution $r = t\rho$ ($0\le\rho\le 1$):

$$I_\phi(t) = t\int_0^1\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\phi(x_1+t\rho\cos\theta,\,x_2+t\rho\s
in\theta)}{\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}\,\rho\,d\theta\,d\rho.$$

Then

$$\frac{\partial I_\phi}{\partial t} = \int_0^1\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\phi(\cdots)}{
\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}\,\rho\,d\theta\,d\rho + t\int_0^1\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\nabla\phi(
\cdots)\cdot(\rho\cos\theta,\,\rho\sin\theta)}{\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}\,\rho\,d\theta\,
d\rho.$$

The first term is exactly $\frac{1}{t}I_\phi(t)$, and reverting to the original
variables ($r=t\rho$):

$$\frac{\partial I_\phi}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{t}I_\phi(t) + \iint_{D_t}\frac{\
nabla\phi(y_1,y_2)\cdot(y_1-x_1,\,y_2-x_2)}{\bigl[t^2-(y_1-x_1)^2-(y_2-x_2)^2\bi
gr]^{3/2}}\,dy_1\,dy_2.$$

Equivalently, returning to the form (7), the final answer can be written compact
ly as:

### 6. Poisson's Formula (2D)

$$\boxed{\;u(x_1,x_2,t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\iint_{(y_1-x
_1)^2+(y_2-x_2)^2\le t^2}\frac{\phi(y_1,y_2)}{\sqrt{t^2-(y_1-x_1)^2-(y_2-x_2)^2}
}\,dy_1\,dy_2 + \frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{(y_1-x_1)^2+(y_2-x_2)^2\le t^2}\frac{\psi(y
_1,y_2)}{\sqrt{t^2-(y_1-x_1)^2-(y_2-x_2)^2}}\,dy_1\,dy_2\;} \tag{8}$$

or, equivalently, after carrying out the time derivative explicitly:

$$u(x_1,x_2,t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{D_t}\frac{\psi(y_1,y_2)}{\sqrt{t^2-r^2}}\,
dy_1\,dy_2 + \frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\iint_{D_t}\frac{\phi(y_1,
y_2)}{\sqrt{t^2-r^2}}\,dy_1\,dy_2,$$

where $r^2 = (y_1-x_1)^2+(y_2-x_2)^2$ and $D_t = \{(y_1,y_2): r\le t\}$.

### 7. Verification of Initial Conditions

**At $t=0$:** The disk $D_t$ shrinks to the single point $(x_1,x_2)$, so $I_\psi
(0)=0$ and $I_\phi(0)=0$, giving $u(x_1,x_2,0)=\phi(x_1,x_2)$ from the first ter
m (the derivative $\frac{\partial}{\partial t}I_\phi$ picks up the value $\phi$
at the center, as can be checked from the $\rho$-substitution form where the fir
st term gives $\phi(x_1,x_2)\cdot 2\pi$ at $t\to 0^+$, yielding $\phi$ after div
ision by $2\pi$).

**For $u_t$ at $t=0$:** Differentiating (8) and evaluating at $t=0$, the $\psi$-
term gives $\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot 2\pi\,\psi(x_1,x_2) = \psi(x_1,x_2)$ and the $\p
hi$-term's second derivative contributes zero, confirming $u_t(x_1,x_2,0)=\psi(x
_1,x_2)$.

### 8. Physical Interpretation (Huygens' Principle vs. Its Failure)

Unlike the 3D case (Kirchhoff), where the solution depends only on the sphere $|
y-x|=t$ (sharp signals, Huygens' principle holds), the 2D formula integrates ove
r the **entire disk** $|y-x|\le t$. This means a disturbance at a point produces
 a "tail" that persists for all $t>0$: Huygens' principle **fails** in even dime
nsions. This is precisely why the method of descent works — the 3D spherical mea
n, when the data are $x_3$-independent, accumulates contributions from the whole
 disk.
