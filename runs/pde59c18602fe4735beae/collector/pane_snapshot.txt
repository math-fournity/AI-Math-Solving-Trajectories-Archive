**Answer: 1**

**Proof.**

**Step 1: Any topological group structure on $S^1$ is a Lie group.**

Let $\mu$ be a group operation on the set $S^1$ such that $(S^1, \mu)$ is a topo
logical group with the subspace topology inherited from $\mathbb{R}^2$. The spac
e $S^1$ is a 1-dimensional topological manifold (it is locally homeomorphic to $
\mathbb{R}$). By the Gleason–Montgomery–Zippin theorem — the solution to Hilbert
's fifth problem — every locally Euclidean topological group is a Lie group. The
refore $(S^1, \mu)$ is a (real) Lie group.

**Step 2: The Lie group is 1-dimensional, compact, and connected.**

Since the underlying manifold is $S^1$, the Lie group has dimension 1. It is com
pact because $S^1$ is compact, and connected because $S^1$ is connected.

**Step 3: The Lie group is abelian.**

A 1-dimensional Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is necessarily abelian: the Lie brack
et $[\cdot,\cdot]: \mathfrak{g} \wedge \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$ vanishes s
ince $\dim(\mathfrak{g} \wedge \mathfrak{g}) = 0$ when $\dim \mathfrak{g} = 1$.
A connected Lie group whose Lie algebra is abelian is itself abelian (the commut
ator subgroup corresponds to the derived subalgebra $[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}
] = 0$, hence is trivial).

**Step 4: Classification forces the group to be the circle group.**

The connected 1-dimensional abelian Lie groups are classified: they are $\mathbb
{R}$ (the additive group of reals, non-compact) and $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z} \cong
 S^1$ (the circle group, compact). Since our group is compact, it must be isomor
phic to $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z} \cong S^1$, the standard circle group.

**Step 5: Existence and uniqueness.**

The standard circle group — unit complex numbers under multiplication — is a top
ological group structure on $S^1$, establishing existence. By Steps 1–4, every t
opological group structure on $S^1$ is isomorphic (as a topological group) to th

e on $S^1$ up to isomorphism.
