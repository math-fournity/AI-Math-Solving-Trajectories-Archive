## Proof

We are asked to find the differential of

$$I(t) = \int_0^t B_s \, ds$$

with respect to time $t$, where $(B_s)_{s \ge 0}$ is a standard Brownian motion.

**Key observation.** For each fixed sample path, $s \mapsto B_s(\omega)$ is a co
ntinuous function (Brownian motion has continuous sample paths almost surely). T
herefore the integral $\int_0^t B_s\,ds$ is an ordinary Riemann integral of a co
ntinuous function, and the map

$$t \mapsto I(t) = \int_0^t B_s\,ds$$

is a continuously differentiable function of $t$ (pathwise). By the **Fundamenta
l Theorem of Calculus**, applied pathwise to the continuous integrand $B_s$,

$$\frac{dI}{dt}(t) = B_t.$$

Hence the differential of $I(t)$ with respect to $t$ is

$$dI(t) = d\!\left(\int_0^t B_s\,ds\right) = B_t\,dt.$$

**Remark on the Itô framework.** Although $B_t$ is a stochastic process, the int
egral $\int_0^t B_s\,ds$ is a *Lebesgue/Riemann* integral in time (not an Itô in
tegral), so no quadratic-variation correction term appears. The differential is
purely the $dt$ term $B_t\,dt$, with no $dB_t$ component. This is consistent wit
h the fact that $I(t)$ is a finite-variation process (it is absolutely continuou
s in $t$), hence its Itô differential contains no martingale part:

$$dI(t) = B_t\,dt + 0\,dB_t.$$

### Conclusion

$$\boxed{d\!\left(\int_0^t B_s\,ds\right) = B_t\,dt.}$$
