The statement is **false**.

## Counterexample

Work in the affine algebraic set $\mathbb{A}^1_k$ (with $k$ algebraically closed
, as is standard for the Nullstellensatz). Let

$$\mathfrak a = (x^2) \subset k[x].$$

**The zero set $V(\mathfrak a)$ is irreducible.** We have
$$V(\mathfrak a) = V(x^2) = \{p \in \mathbb{A}^1 : p^2 = 0\} = \{0\},$$
a single point. A single point is irreducible (it cannot be written as a proper
union of two nonempty closed subsets).

**The ideal $\mathfrak a = (x^2)$ is not prime.** A prime ideal $\mathfrak p$ re
quires: whenever $fg \in \mathfrak p$, then $f \in \mathfrak p$ or $g \in \mathf
rak p$. Take $f = g = x$. Then
$$x \cdot x = x^2 \in (x^2),$$
yet $x \notin (x^2)$ (since $x$ is not a multiple of $x^2$ in $k[x]$). Hence $(x
^2)$ is not prime.

## Why the implication fails in general

The correct theorem (Hartshorne / standard AG) is:
$$V(\mathfrak a) \text{ irreducible} \iff \sqrt{\mathfrak a} \text{ is prime} \i
ff I(V(\mathfrak a)) \text{ is prime},$$
where the last equality uses the Nullstellensatz ($I(V(\mathfrak a)) = \sqrt{\ma
thfrak a}$).

Irreducibility of $V(\mathfrak a)$ controls only the **radical** $\sqrt{\mathfra
k a}$, not $\mathfrak a$ itself. An ideal can have a prime radical without being
 prime — any non-radical ideal whose radical is prime provides a counterexample,
 e.g. $(x^2), (x^n)$ for $n \geq 2$, or more generally any power $(\mathfrak p^n
)$ of a prime $\mathfrak p$ with $n \geq 2$ (when $\mathfrak p^n \neq \mathfrak
p$).

## Conclusion

We exhibited an ideal $\mathfrak a = (x^2) \subset k[x]$ such that $V(\mathfrak
a) = \{0\}$ is irreducible, yet $\mathfrak a$ is not prime. Therefore the statem
ent

> "If $V(\mathfrak a)$ is irreducible, then $\mathfrak a$ is a prime ideal"

is **false**. The correct statement replaces "$\mathfrak a$ is prime" with "$\sq
rt{\mathfrak a}$ is prime".
