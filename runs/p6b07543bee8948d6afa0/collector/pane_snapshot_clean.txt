## Proof

We prove that $f$ must be identically zero.

**Key fact.** If $f$ is a holomorphic function on a domain $D \subseteq \mathbb{
C}$ and $f$ is not identically zero, then every zero of $f$ is *isolated*: for e
ach zero $z_0$, there exists an open disk around $z_0$ containing no other zero
of $f$.

*Proof of the key fact.* Let $z_0 \in D$ with $f(z_0) = 0$ and $f \not\equiv 0$.
 Since $f$ is holomorphic, it has a power series expansion about $z_0$:
$$f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n (z - z_0)^n.$$
Since $f \not\equiv 0$, not all $a_n$ are zero. Let $m \geq 1$ be the smallest i
ndex with $a_m \neq 0$ (note $a_0 = f(z_0) = 0$). Then
$$f(z) = (z - z_0)^m g(z), \quad \text{where } g(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_{n+m
}(z-z_0)^n,$$
and $g$ is holomorphic with $g(z_0) = a_m \neq 0$. By continuity of $g$, there i
s a neighborhood $U$ of $z_0$ on which $g(z) \neq 0$. Hence within $U$, the only
 zero of $f$ is $z_0$ itself. ∎

**Countability of isolated zeros.** The set of isolated points in $\mathbb{C}$ i
s at most countable. Indeed, $\mathbb{C}$ has a countable base for its topology
(e.g., all open disks with rational radius centered at points with rational real
 and imaginary parts). If $S$ is a set of isolated points, then for each $s \in
S$ there is an open set $U_s$ with $U_s \cap S = \{s\}$. Choosing a basic open s
et $B_s$ with $s \in B_s \subseteq U_s$, the map $s \mapsto B_s$ is injective (s
ince $B_s \cap S = \{s\}$ determines $s$). As the base is countable, $S$ is coun
table.

**Conclusion.** Suppose $f$ is entire with uncountably many zeros. If $f \not\eq
uiv 0$, then by the key fact every zero of $f$ is isolated, and by the countabil
ity argument the set of zeros is at most countable — contradicting the assumptio
n that $f$ has uncountably many zeros. Therefore $f \equiv 0$.

Equivalently, by the *identity theorem*: if the zeros of $f$ have a limit point
in $\mathbb{C}$, then $f \equiv 0$. Any uncountable subset of $\mathbb{C}$ has a
 limit point (since $\mathbb{C} = \bigcup_{n} \overline{D(0,n)}$ is a countable
union of compact sets, and an uncountable set must be uncountable — hence infini
te with a limit point — in some $\overline{D(0,n)}$). Thus uncountably many zero
s force a limit point of zeros, and the identity theorem gives $f \equiv 0$.
