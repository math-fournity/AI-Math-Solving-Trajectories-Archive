## Proof

**Yes.** If $X$ is measurable with respect to the completion of $\sigma(Y)$, the
n $X$ is almost surely equal to a $\sigma(Y)$-measurable random variable.

---

**Setup.** Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a complete probability spa
ce, $\mathcal{G} := \sigma(Y)$, and $\overline{\mathcal{G}}$ the completion of $
\mathcal{G}$ with respect to $\mathbb{P}\big|_{\mathcal{G}}$. Concretely,
$$\overline{\mathcal{G}} = \{A \in \mathcal{F} : \exists\, B, C \in \mathcal{G},
\; B \subseteq A \subseteq C,\; \mathbb{P}(C \setminus B) = 0\}.$$
Equivalently, $A \in \overline{\mathcal{G}}$ iff $A = B \cup M$ where $B \in \ma
thcal{G}$ and $M \subseteq N$ for some $N \in \mathcal{G}$ with $\mathbb{P}(N)=0
$ (and $M \in \mathcal{F}$, which is automatic since $\mathcal{F}$ is complete).

We prove: every $\overline{\mathcal{G}}$-measurable random variable equals a $\m
athcal{G}$-measurable one a.s.

---

**Step 1 (Indicators).** Let $A \in \overline{\mathcal{G}}$. Write $A = B \cup M
$ with $B \in \mathcal{G}$, $M \subseteq N$, $N \in \mathcal{G}$, $\mathbb{P}(N)
=0$. Then $B \subseteq A \subseteq B \cup N$, so $\mathbf{1}_A$ and $\mathbf{1}_
B$ differ only on $A \setminus B \subseteq N$, a null set. Hence $\mathbf{1}_A =
 \mathbf{1}_B$ a.s., and $\mathbf{1}_B$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable.

**Step 2 (Simple functions).** Let $X = \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i \mathbf{1}_{A_i}$ wit
h $A_i \in \overline{\mathcal{G}}$. By Step 1, pick $B_i \in \mathcal{G}$ with $
\mathbf{1}_{A_i} = \mathbf{1}_{B_i}$ a.s. On $E^c := \bigcap_i \{\mathbf{1}_{A_i
} = \mathbf{1}_{B_i}\}$, which has $\mathbb{P}(E^c)=1$, we have $X = \sum a_i \m
athbf{1}_{B_i} =: Z$. So $X = Z$ a.s. with $Z$ $\mathcal{G}$-measurable and simp
le.

**Step 3 (Non-negative $X$).** Let $X \geq 0$ be $\overline{\mathcal{G}}$-measur
able. There exist $\overline{\mathcal{G}}$-measurable simple functions $0 \leq X
_n \uparrow X$. By Step 2, for each $n$ there is a $\mathcal{G}$-measurable simp
le $Z_n$ with $X_n = Z_n$ a.s. Set $E_n = \{X_n \neq Z_n\}$ and $E = \bigcup_n E
_n$; then $\mathbb{P}(E) = 0$. On $E^c$, $X_n = Z_n$ for all $n$, so $Z_n \uparr
ow X$. Define $Z := \limsup_{n\to\infty} Z_n$, which is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable
 (as a limsup of $\mathcal{G}$-measurable functions). On $E^c$, $\limsup Z_n = \
lim Z_n = X$. Thus $Z = X$ a.s.

**Step 4 (General $X$).** Write $X = X^+ - X^-$ with $X^\pm \geq 0$ and $\overli
ne{\mathcal{G}}$-measurable. By Step 3, there exist $\mathcal{G}$-measurable $Z^
+, Z^-$ with $Z^+ = X^+$ a.s. and $Z^- = X^-$ a.s. Then $Z := Z^+ - Z^-$ is $\ma
thcal{G}$-measurable and $Z = X$ a.s.

---

**Conclusion.** We have shown that $X$ being $\overline{\sigma(Y)}$-measurable i
mplies the existence of a $\sigma(Y)$-measurable random variable $Z$ with $X = Z
$ almost surely. Therefore $X$ **is** almost surely measurable with respect to $
\sigma(Y)$.
