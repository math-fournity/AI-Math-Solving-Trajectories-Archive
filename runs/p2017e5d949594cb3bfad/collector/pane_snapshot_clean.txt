## Proof

Let $Q(x,y) = x^2 + xy + y^2$. The associated symmetric matrix is
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 1 \end{pmatrix},$$
with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = \tfrac{3}{2}$ and $\lambda_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}$, both
 positive. Hence $Q$ is **positive definite**, and by the spectral theorem (with
 $r^2 = x^2+y^2$),
$$\tfrac{1}{2}\,r^2 \;\le\; Q(x,y) \;\le\; \tfrac{3}{2}\,r^2.$$

Consequently the denominator satisfies the two-sided bound
$$1 + \tfrac{1}{2}r^2 \;\le\; 1 + Q(x,y) \;\le\; 1 + \tfrac{3}{2}r^2,$$
so the integrand is comparable to $(1+r^2)^{-\alpha}$:
$$\bigl(1+\tfrac{3}{2}r^2\bigr)^{-\alpha} \;\le\; \frac{1}{(1+Q)^\alpha} \;\le\;
 \bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{2}r^2\bigr)^{-\alpha}.$$

Since all quantities are positive, the improper integral converges if and only i
f
$$\iint_{\mathbb R^2}\frac{dx\,dy}{(1+r^2)^\alpha}$$
converges (the constant factors $\tfrac12,\tfrac32$ do not affect convergence, a
s $(1+cr^2)^\alpha \sim c^\alpha r^{2\alpha}$ at infinity).

Passing to polar coordinates $(x,y)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)$:
$$\iint_{\mathbb R^2}\frac{dx\,dy}{(1+r^2)^\alpha}
= 2\pi\int_0^\infty \frac{r\,dr}{(1+r^2)^\alpha}.$$

**Near $r=0$:** the integrand $\frac{r}{(1+r^2)^\alpha}\sim r$, which is integra
ble on $[0,1]$ for every $\alpha$.

**Near $r=\infty$:** $\frac{r}{(1+r^2)^\alpha}\sim r^{1-2\alpha}$, and $\int_1^\
infty r^{1-2\alpha}\,dr$ converges if and only if $1-2\alpha<-1$, i.e.
$$\alpha > 1.$$

By comparability, the original integral $\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb R^2}\frac{d
x\,dy}{(1+x^2+xy+y^2)^\alpha}$ converges if and only if $\alpha>1$.

---

**Conclusion.** The integral converges precisely for
$$\boxed{\alpha > 1}.$$
