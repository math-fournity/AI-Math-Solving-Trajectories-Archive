## Proof

**Answer: Yes, such a $v$ exists.**

**Setup.** Since $\frac{1}{\sigma_\alpha} + \frac{\alpha}{p^*} = 1$, we have $\s
igma_\alpha = \frac{p^*}{p^*-\alpha}$, so $h \in L^{\sigma_\alpha}(\Omega)$ and
for any $v \in L^{p^*}(\Omega)$, Hölder's inequality gives $h\, v^\alpha \in L^1
(\Omega)$, making $\int_\Omega h\, v^\alpha$ well-defined. Since $\Omega$ is bou
nded and $\sigma_\alpha \ge 1$, we have $L^{\sigma_\alpha}(\Omega) \subset L^1_{
\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)$, so $h$ is locally integrable.

**Key idea.** Decompose $h = h^+ - h^-$ with $h^+ = \max(h,0)$, $h^- = \max(-h,0
)$. The hypothesis $h^+ \neq 0$ means $\{h > 0\}$ has positive measure. We const
ruct $v$ concentrated near a point where $h > 0$ (and hence $h^- = 0$), so the p
ositive contribution dominates.

**Choosing the base point.** Since $\Omega$ is open and $\{h^+ > 0\}$ has positi
ve measure, by the Lebesgue differentiation theorem we can pick a point $x_0 \in
 \Omega$ that is simultaneously:
- a Lebesgue point of $h^+$ with $h^+(x_0) > 0$,
- a Lebesgue point of $h^-$ (hence $h^-(x_0) = 0$, since $h^+(x_0)>0$ implies $h
(x_0)>0$),
- an interior point of $\Omega$ (so $B_\varepsilon(x_0) \subset \Omega$ for smal
l $\varepsilon$).

**Constructing the test function.** Let $\varphi \in C_0^\infty(B_1)$ with $\var
phi > 0$ on $B_1$ and $\varphi(0) = 1$. For $\varepsilon > 0$ small enough that
$\overline{B_\varepsilon(x_0)} \subset \Omega$, set
$$v_\varepsilon(x) = \varphi\!\left(\frac{x - x_0}{\varepsilon}\right).$$
Then $v_\varepsilon \in C_0^\infty(\Omega)$ and $v_\varepsilon > 0$ on $B_\varep
silon(x_0)$.

**Estimating the integral.** By the change of variables $y = (x-x_0)/\varepsilon
$:
$$\int_\Omega h\, v_\varepsilon^\alpha\, dx = \int_{B_\varepsilon(x_0)} h(x)\, \
varphi\!\left(\tfrac{x-x_0}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\!\alpha} dx = \varepsilon^n \i
nt_{B_1} h(x_0 + \varepsilon y)\, \varphi(y)^\alpha\, dy.$$

Split $h = h^+ - h^-$.

**Positive part.** Write $h^+(x_0+\varepsilon y) = h^+(x_0) + \bigl[h^+(x_0+\var
epsilon y) - h^+(x_0)\bigr]$. Then:
$$\varepsilon^n \int_{B_1} h^+(x_0+\varepsilon y)\,\varphi(y)^\alpha\, dy = \var
epsilon^n\, h^+(x_0)\!\int_{B_1}\varphi^\alpha\, dy + R_\varepsilon^+,$$
where
$$|R_\varepsilon^+| \le \|\varphi\|_\infty^\alpha \int_{B_\varepsilon(x_0)} |h^+
(x) - h^+(x_0)|\, dx = \|\varphi\|_\infty^\alpha \cdot o(\varepsilon^n),$$
since $x_0$ is a Lebesgue point of $h^+$ (i.e., $\frac{1}{|B_\varepsilon|}\int_{
B_\varepsilon(x_0)}|h^+-h^+(x_0)| \to 0$). So $R_\varepsilon^+ = o(\varepsilon^n
)$.

**Negative part.** Since $h^-(x_0) = 0$ and $x_0$ is a Lebesgue point of $h^-$:
$$\varepsilon^n \int_{B_1} h^-(x_0+\varepsilon y)\,\varphi(y)^\alpha\, dy = o(\v
arepsilon^n),$$
by the same argument (the leading term $\varepsilon^n h^-(x_0)\int \varphi^\alph
a = 0$ vanishes, and the remainder is $o(\varepsilon^n)$).

**Combining.** Therefore:
$$\int_\Omega h\, v_\varepsilon^\alpha\, dx = \varepsilon^n\, h^+(x_0)\!\int_{B_
1}\varphi^\alpha\, dy + o(\varepsilon^n).$$

Since $h^+(x_0) > 0$ and $\int_{B_1}\varphi^\alpha\, dy > 0$ (as $\varphi > 0$ o
n $B_1$), the leading coefficient is strictly positive. Hence for all sufficient

$$\int_\Omega h\, v_\varepsilon^\alpha\, dx > 0.$$

**Conclusion.** Taking $v = v_\varepsilon$ for such a small $\varepsilon$ gives
$v \in C_0^\infty(\Omega)$, $v > 0$, and $\int_\Omega h\, v^\alpha > 0$. $\black
