## Proof

**Claim:** There does **not** exist a meromorphic function $g$ such that $\dfrac
{g'}{g} = \dfrac{z}{(z-1)^2}$.

**Key fact.** The logarithmic derivative of a meromorphic function has only **si
mple** poles, and each residue is an integer.

*Proof of the fact.* Let $g$ be meromorphic on $\mathbb{C}$, and let $a\in\mathb
b{C}$. Near $a$, write
$$g(z) = (z-a)^{n}\, h(z),$$
where $n\in\mathbb{Z}$ is the order of $g$ at $a$ (positive for a zero, negative
 for a pole, zero otherwise) and $h$ is holomorphic with $h(a)\neq 0$. Then
$$g'(z) = n(z-a)^{n-1}h(z) + (z-a)^{n}h'(z),$$
so
$$\frac{g'(z)}{g(z)} = \frac{n}{z-a} + \frac{h'(z)}{h(z)}.$$
Since $h$ is holomorphic and nonzero at $a$, the term $h'/h$ is holomorphic near
 $a$. Hence $g'/g$ has **at most a simple pole** at $a$, with residue $n\in\math
bb{Z}$. At every other point $g'/g$ is holomorphic. ∎

**Applying the fact.** Now consider
$$f(z) = \frac{z}{(z-1)^{2}}.$$
Decompose it via $z = (z-1)+1$:
$$\frac{z}{(z-1)^{2}} = \frac{1}{z-1} + \frac{1}{(z-1)^{2}}.$$
The principal part at $z=1$ contains the nonzero term $\dfrac{1}{(z-1)^{2}}$, so
 $f$ has a **pole of order $2$** (a double pole) at $z=1$.

**Contradiction.** If a meromorphic $g$ satisfied $g'/g = f$, then by the key fa
ct $g'/g$ could only have simple poles. But $f$ has a double pole at $z=1$. This
 is impossible.

(Equivalently, integrating gives $\log g = \log(z-1) - \dfrac{1}{z-1} + C$, henc
e $g = A\,(z-1)\,e^{-1/(z-1)}$, which has an **essential singularity** at $z=1$
— not a pole — confirming that no *meromorphic* $g$ exists.)

**Conclusion.** No meromorphic function $g$ satisfies $\dfrac{g'}{g} = \dfrac{z}
{(z-1)^2}$.
