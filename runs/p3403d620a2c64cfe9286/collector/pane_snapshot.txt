## Problem

Determine whether $x^n + y^n = z^n$ has any solutions for $n > 1$, where $x, y,
z$ are positive real numbers.

## Proof

**Claim.** For every $n > 1$, the equation $x^n + y^n = z^n$ has solutions in po
sitive real numbers. In fact, it has infinitely many.

**Construction.** Let $n > 1$ be fixed. Choose any two positive real numbers $x,
 y > 0$ (for instance $x = y = 1$). Define

$$z := (x^n + y^n)^{1/n}.$$

Since $x, y > 0$, we have $x^n + y^n > 0$, so $z$ is a well-defined positive rea
l number (the $n$-th root of a positive real is positive). Raising both sides to
 the $n$-th power gives

$$z^n = x^n + y^n,$$

which is exactly the desired equation. Hence $(x, y, z)$ is a solution in positi
ve reals.

**Infinitely many solutions.** The choice of $(x, y)$ was arbitrary among positi
ve reals, and different pairs $(x, y)$ yield different values of $z = (x^n+y^n)^
{1/n}$. For example, taking $x = y = t$ for any $t > 0$ gives $z = (2t^n)^{1/n}
= 2^{1/n}\,t$, producing a distinct solution for every $t > 0$. Thus there are u
ncountably many solutions.

**Remark (contrast with Fermat's Last Theorem).** Fermat's Last Theorem — proved
 by Wiles — asserts that $x^n + y^n = z^n$ has no solutions in *positive integer
s* for $n > 2$. That is a statement about the discrete set $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$. Th
e present problem concerns *positive real numbers*, where the continuous structu
re of $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ guarantees that $z = (x^n + y^n)^{1/n}$ always exists fo
r any chosen positive $x, y$. For $n = 2$ even integer solutions exist (e.g., $3
^2 + 4^2 = 5^2$); for $n > 2$ the obstruction is purely arithmetic and disappear
s over the reals.

**Conclusion.** For every $n > 1$, the equation $x^n + y^n = z^n$ admits (infini
tely many) solutions in positive real numbers, given explicitly by $z = (x^n + y
^n)^{1/n}$ for any $x, y > 0$.
