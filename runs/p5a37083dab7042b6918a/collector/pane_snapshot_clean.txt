## Proof

**Yes**, $f(x,y)$ is $\mu_1 \times \mu_2$-almost everywhere zero.

**Setup.** Let $E = \{(x,y) \in M_1 \times M_2 : f(x,y) \neq 0\}$. Since $f$ is
measurable, $E$ is a measurable subset of the product space. We must show $(\mu_
1 \times \mu_2)(E) = 0$.

For each $y \in M_2$, define the **$y$-section** (slice)
$$E_y = \{x \in M_1 : (x,y) \in E\} = \{x \in M_1 : f(x,y) \neq 0\}.$$

**Applying Tonelli's theorem.** Since $\mathbf{1}_E$ is a non-negative measurabl
e function on the product space, Tonelli's theorem gives
$$(\mu_1 \times \mu_2)(E) = \int_{M_2} \mu_1(E_y)\, d\mu_2(y),$$
where $y \mapsto \mu_1(E_y)$ is a well-defined $\mu_2$-measurable function of $y
$.

**Evaluating the slices.** Split the integral over $N$ and its complement:

1. **For $y \in M_2 \setminus N$:** By hypothesis, $g(x) = f(x,y)$ is $\mu_1$-a.
e. zero, which means precisely $\mu_1(E_y) = 0$.

2. **For $y \in N$:** We have $\mu_2(N) = 0$. The integral of any non-negative m
easurable function over a set of measure zero is zero — even if the function tak
es the value $+\infty$ on that set. (Indeed, every non-negative simple function
$s \leq \mu_1(E_y)\cdot\mathbf{1}_N$ vanishes $\mu_2$-a.e., so its integral is $
0$; taking the supremum yields $0$.)

Therefore:
$$(\mu_1 \times \mu_2)(E) = \int_N \mu_1(E_y)\,d\mu_2(y) + \int_{M_2\setminus N}
\mu_1(E_y)\,d\mu_2(y) = 0 + 0 = 0.$$

**Conclusion.** The set where $f \neq 0$ has $\mu_1 \times \mu_2$-measure zero,
so $f$ is $\mu_1 \times \mu_2$-almost everywhere zero. $\blacksquare$
