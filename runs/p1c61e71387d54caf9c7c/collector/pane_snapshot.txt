**Yes.** If a symmetric matrix $A$ commutes with every symmetric matrix, then $A
 = \lambda I$ for some $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Proof.**

Let $A \in M_n(\mathbb{R})$ be symmetric and satisfy $AS = SA$ for every symmetr
ic matrix $S \in M_n(\mathbb{R})$.

**Step 1: $A$ is diagonal.**

Every diagonal matrix is symmetric, so $A$ commutes with each $E_{ii}$ (the matr
ix with $1$ in position $(i,i)$ and $0$ elsewhere). Compute the $(j,k)$-entry of
 both sides of $A E_{ii} = E_{ii} A$:

$$(AE_{ii})_{jk} = \sum_{l} A_{jl}(E_{ii})_{lk} = A_{ji}\,\delta_{ki}, \qquad (E
_{ii}A)_{jk} = \sum_{l}(E_{ii})_{jl}A_{lk} = \delta_{ji}\,A_{ik}.$$

Setting $k = i$ and $j \neq i$: the left side gives $A_{ji}$, the right side giv
es $0$. Hence $A_{ji} = 0$ for all $j \neq i$. Since this holds for every $i$, a
ll off-diagonal entries of $A$ vanish. Thus

$$A = \operatorname{diag}(a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n).$$

**Step 2: All diagonal entries are equal.**

For any pair $i \neq j$, consider the symmetric matrix $S = E_{ij} + E_{ji}$ (wi
th $1$ in positions $(i,j)$ and $(j,i)$, $0$ elsewhere). Since $A$ is diagonal,

$$(AS)_{ij} = a_i\,S_{ij} = a_i, \qquad (SA)_{ij} = S_{ij}\,a_j = a_j.$$

The commutation condition $AS = SA$ forces $a_i = a_j$. As $i,j$ were arbitrary,
 all $a_1 = \dots = a_n =: \lambda$.

**Conclusion.**

$A = \lambda I_n$ for some $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$. Conversely, every scalar mu
ltiple of the identity is symmetric and commutes with every matrix (hence with e
very symmetric matrix), so the condition is both necessary and sufficient.
