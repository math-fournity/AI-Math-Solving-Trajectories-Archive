## Solution

**Step 1: Identify the singularity.**

The integrand is

$$f(z) = \frac{e^{z^2}}{z^3}.$$

The only singularity is at $z = 0$, where there is a pole of order $3$ (since $e
^{z^2}$ is entire and $e^{0}=1 \neq 0$).

**Step 2: Compute the Laurent expansion about $z=0$.**

Using $e^{w} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{w^n}{n!}$ with $w = z^2$:

$$e^{z^2} = 1 + z^2 + \frac{z^4}{2!} + \frac{z^6}{3!} + \cdots$$

Dividing term-by-term by $z^3$:

$$\frac{e^{z^2}}{z^3} = \frac{1}{z^3} + \frac{1}{z} + \frac{z}{2!} + \frac{z^3}{
3!} + \cdots$$

**Step 3: Extract the residue.**

The residue at $z=0$ is the coefficient of $z^{-1}$ in the Laurent series:

$$\operatorname{Res}\!\left(\frac{e^{z^2}}{z^3},\, 0\right) = 1.$$

(Equivalently, using the pole-of-order-3 formula:
$$\operatorname{Res} = \frac{1}{2!}\lim_{z\to 0}\frac{d^2}{dz^2}\!\left[z^3 \cdo
t \frac{e^{z^2}}{z^3}\right] = \frac{1}{2}\cdot \frac{d^2}{dz^2}e^{z^2}\Big|_{z=
0} = \frac{1}{2}\cdot (2 + 4z^2\cdot(\cdots))\big|_{z=0}\cdot\ldots$$
More cleanly: $\frac{d}{dz}e^{z^2}=2ze^{z^2}$, $\frac{d^2}{dz^2}e^{z^2}=(2+4z^2)
e^{z^2}$, so at $z=0$ this equals $2$, and $\frac{1}{2!}\cdot 2 = 1$. ✓)

**Step 4: Apply the residue theorem.**

For $\sigma_r$ a positively oriented circle $|z|=r$ enclosing the origin (with $
r>0$), the residue theorem gives

$$\int_{\sigma_r} \frac{e^{z^2}}{z^3}\,dz = 2\pi i \cdot \operatorname{Res}\!\le
ft(\frac{e^{z^2}}{z^3},\, 0\right) = 2\pi i \cdot 1 = 2\pi i,$$

**independently of $r$** (as long as $r>0$, so the pole lies inside $\sigma_r$).
 Therefore the limit as $r \to 0$ is the same constant value:

$$\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\sigma_r}\frac{e^{z^2}}{z^3}\,dz = 2\pi i.$$

- Upper semicircle traversed counterclockwise (from $-r$ to $r$): $\;\pi i$.
