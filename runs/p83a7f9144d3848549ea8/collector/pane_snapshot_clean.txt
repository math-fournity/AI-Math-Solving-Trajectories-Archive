## Proof

**Claim.** Yes: $f$ is bounded, and $f$ is the derivative of some differentiable
 function on $\mathbb{R}$.

### Part 1. $f$ is bounded.

Since $f_n' \to f$ uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$, there exists $N$ such that for all
 $n \ge N$,
$$
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}} |f_n'(x) - f(x)| < 1.
$$
By hypothesis $f_N'$ is bounded, so there is $M$ with $|f_N'(x)| \le M$ for all
$x\in\mathbb{R}$. Hence for every $x$,
$$
|f(x)| \le |f(x) - f_N'(x)| + |f_N'(x)| < 1 + M.
$$
Thus $f$ is bounded. $\square$

### Part 2. $f$ is the derivative of some function.

Define
$$
F_n(x) := f_n(x) - f_n(0), \qquad x \in \mathbb{R}.
$$
Then each $F_n$ is differentiable, $F_n(0)=0$, and $F_n' = f_n' \to f$ uniformly
 on $\mathbb{R}$.

**Step (a): $\{F_n\}$ converges pointwise on $\mathbb{R}$.**

Fix $x\in\mathbb{R}$. For $n,m\in\mathbb{N}$, the function $F_n - F_m$ is differ
entiable with $(F_n-F_m)(0)=0$. By the Mean Value Theorem applied to $F_n - F_m$
 on the interval between $0$ and $x$, there exists $c$ between $0$ and $x$ such
that
$$
(F_n - F_m)(x) = (F_n - F_m)'(c)\cdot x = \bigl(f_n'(c) - f_m'(c)\bigr)\,x.
$$
Therefore
$$
|(F_n - F_m)(x)| \le \sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_n'(t)-f_m'(t)|\cdot |x|.
$$
Since $\{f_n'\}$ is uniformly Cauchy on $\mathbb{R}$ (it converges uniformly), $
\sup_{t}|f_n'(t)-f_m'(t)|\to 0$ as $n,m\to\infty$. Hence $|F_n(x)-F_m(x)|\to 0$;
 $\{F_n(x)\}$ is Cauchy and converges. Define
$$
F(x) := \lim_{n\to\infty} F_n(x).
$$
Note $F(0)=\lim F_n(0)=0$. Moreover the estimate above shows the convergence is
uniform on every bounded interval $[-R,R]$.

**Step (b): $F' = f$ on $\mathbb{R}$.**

Let $[a,b]$ be any closed bounded interval. On $[a,b]$ we have:
- $F_n \to F$ pointwise (in particular at every point of $[a,b]$),
- $F_n' = f_n' \to f$ uniformly on $[a,b]$ (since the convergence is uniform on
all of $\mathbb{R}$).

By the standard theorem on term-by-term differentiation (Rudin, *Principles of M
athematical Analysis*, Theorem 7.17): if $\{F_n\}$ converges at one point of $[a
,b]$ and $\{F_n'\}$ converges uniformly on $[a,b]$, then $F_n\to F$ uniformly on
 $[a,b]$, $F$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$, and
$$
F'(x) = \lim_{n\to\infty} F_n'(x) = f(x), \qquad x\in[a,b].
$$
Since $[a,b]$ was arbitrary, $F' = f$ on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

### Conclusion

$f$ is bounded (Part 1), and $f$ is the derivative of the function $F(x)=\lim_{n
\to\infty}\bigl(f_n(x)-f_n(0)\bigr)$ (Part 2). Both assertions hold.
