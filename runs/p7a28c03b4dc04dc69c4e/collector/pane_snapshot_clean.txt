## Proof

We determine the cardinality of the set $S = \{f : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N} \mi
d f \text{ is strictly decreasing}\}$, where "strictly decreasing" means $f(n+1)
 < f(n)$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

**Claim:** $S = \varnothing$, so $|S| = 0$.

**Proof.** Suppose for contradiction that there exists a strictly decreasing fun
ction $f : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$. Then for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$,
$$f(0) > f(1) > f(2) > \cdots > f(n),$$
so by induction on $n$,
$$f(n) \le f(0) - n.$$
(This holds since each step decreases by at least $1$: $f(n) \le f(n-1) - 1 \le
f(0) - n$.)

Now take $n = f(0) + 1$ (or any $n > f(0)$). Then
$$f(n) \le f(0) - n = f(0) - (f(0)+1) = -1.$$
But $f(n) \in \mathbb{N}$, and every element of $\mathbb{N}$ is $\ge 0$ (whether
 $\mathbb{N} = \{0,1,2,\dots\}$ or $\mathbb{N} = \{1,2,3,\dots\}$). This is a co
ntradiction: $f(n) \ge 0$ yet $f(n) \le -1$.

The contradiction arises from the assumption that such an $f$ exists. Equivalent
ly, this is the well-ordering principle: $\mathbb{N}$ admits no infinite strictl
y descending chain, so no function $\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$ can be strictly d
ecreasing on all of $\mathbb{N}$.

Therefore $S = \varnothing$, and

$$|S| = 0.$$
