## Condition for a Riemannian Manifold to be Terms of Sectional Curvatures

**Theorem.** A Riemannian manifold $(M, g)$ is flat — that is, locally isometric
 to Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$ — if and only if every sectional curvature va
nishes identically:

$$K(\sigma) = 0 \quad \text{for every 2-plane } \sigma \subset T_pM, \text{ at e
very point } p \in M.$$

---

### Proof

**Definitions and setup.**

Let $(M^n, g)$ be a Rannian manifold with Levi-Civita connection $\nabla$ and Ri
emann curvature tensor $R$. Recall that $R$ is a $(1,3)$-tensor:

$$R(X,Y)Z = \nabla_X \nabla_Y Z - \nabla_Y \nabla_X Z - \nabla_{[X,Y]} Z.$$

The **sectional curvature** of a non-degenerate 2-plane $\sigma = \operatorname{
span}(X, Y) \subset T_p M$ (with $X, Y$ linearly independent) is

$$K(\sigma) = K(X, Y) = \frac{\langle R(X,Y)Y, X \rangle}{\langle X,X\rangle\lan
gle Y,Y\rangle - \langle X,Y\rangle^2}.$$

The denominator is $|X \wedge Y|^2 > 0$, so $K$ is well-defined.

A manifold is called **flat** if it is locally isometric to Euclidean space, equ
ivalently (by the Killing–Hopf / Cartan theorem) if around every point there exi
st local coordinates in which $g_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$.

---

### $(\Rightarrow)$ Flat implies all sectional curvatures vanish.

If $(M, g)$ is flat, then locally $g_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$ in suitable coordinates
. In these coordinates the Christoffel symbols vanish identically, hence $\nabla
 = \partial$ (the flat connection) and

$$R(X,Y)Z = 0 \quad \text{for all } X, Y, Z.$$

Therefore $\langle R(X,Y)Y, X\rangle = 0$ for all $X, Y$, giving $K(\sigma) = 0$
 for every 2-plane $\sigma$.

---

### $(\Leftarrow)$ All sectional curvatures vanish implies flat.

This is the substantive direction. We prove that $K \equiv 0$ forces $R \equiv 0
$, and then invoke the classical theorem that $R \equiv 0$ is equivalent to flat
ness.

**Step 1: $K \equiv 0 \Rightarrow R \equiv 0$.**

The key algebraic fact is that the Riemann curvature tensor is **entirely determ
ined** by the sectional curvature function. Concretely, the curvature tensor sat
isfies the symmetries

$$\langle R(X,Y)Z, W\rangle = -\langle R(Y,X)Z,W\rangle = -\langle R(X,Y)W,Z\ran
gle = \langle R(Z,W)X,Y\rangle,$$

together with the first Bianchi identity $R(X,Y)Z + R(Y,Z)X + R(Z,X)Y = 0$.

Define the **curvature operator** (a $(0,4)$-tensor)

$$\mathcal{R}(X, Y, Z, W) := \langle R(X, Y)Z, W\rangle.$$

The symmetries above make $\mathcal{R}$ a symmetric bilinear form on $\Lambda^2
T_p M$:

$$\mathcal{R}(X \wedge Y,\; Z \wedge W) := \mathcal{R}(X,Y,Z,W),$$

and the sectional curvature is exactly the evaluation of this quadratic form on
decomposable 2-vectors:

$$K(X,Y) = \frac{\mathcal{R}(X \wedge Y,\; X \wedge Y)}{|X \wedge Y|^2}.$$

Now we use the following **algebraic lemma**:

> **Lemma.** A symmetric bilinear form $B$ on $\Lambda^2 V$ that vanishes on all
 decomposable elements ($B(\xi, \xi) = 0$ for every decomposable $\xi = X \wedge
 Y$) is identically zero.

*Proof of lemma.* By polarization, for any decomposable $\xi, \eta$:

$$B(\xi, \eta) = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[B(\xi+\eta, \xi+\eta) - B(\xi,\xi) - B(\eta,\
eta)\bigr].$$

If $\xi + \eta$ is decomposable, then $B(\xi+\eta,\xi+\eta) = 0$ and we get $B(\
xi,\eta) = 0$. For general decomposable $\xi = X\wedge Y$ and $\eta = Z \wedge W
$, one checks (by a dimension/counting argument or by choosing a basis) that $\x
i + \eta$ can always be written as a sum of decomposables each of which, togethe
r with $\xi$ and $\eta$, fits into a polarization chain. A clean way: fix a basi
s $\{e_i\}$ of $V$; the decomposable elements $e_i \wedge e_j$ ($i < j$) span $\
Lambda^2 V$. For distinct index pairs, $(e_i \wedge e_j) + (e_k \wedge e_l)$ is
decomposable when the pairs share an index (e.g., $e_i \wedge e_j + e_i \wedge e
_l = e_i \wedge (e_j + e_l)$), and polarization gives $B(e_i\wedge e_j,\; e_i\we
dge e_l) = 0$. For disjoint pairs, use the chain

$$B(e_i\wedge e_j,\; e_k\wedge e_l) = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[B(\alpha,\alpha) - B(\al
pha - e_k\wedge e_l,\, \alpha - e_k\wedge e_l) - B(e_k\wedge e_l, e_k\wedge e_l)
\bigr]$$

with $\alpha = e_i\wedge e_j + e_k\wedge e_l$, noting $\alpha$ and $\alpha - e_k
\wedge e_l = e_i \wedge e_j$ are decomposable. Since all the quadratic terms van
ish by hypothesis, $B(e_i\wedge e_j, e_k\wedge e_l) = 0$. Thus $B$ vanishes on a
 basis of $\Lambda^2 V$, hence $B \equiv 0$. $\square$

Applying the lemma with $B = \mathcal{R}_p$ (which vanishes on all decomposables
 because $K \equiv 0$), we conclude $\mathcal{R}_p \equiv 0$ for every $p$, henc
e $R \equiv 0$ on $M$.

**Step 2: $R \equiv 0 \Rightarrow$ flat.**

This is the classical **Cartan–Ambrose–Hicks theorem** (in its simplest, simply-
connected-local form). We give the standard argument:

At any point $p \in M$, choose a normal coordinate ball $U$ (geodesically convex
, simply connected, no conjugate points needed since $R = 0$). Fix an orthonorma
l frame $\{E_1, \dots, E_n\}$ at $p$ and extend it over $U$ by **parallel transp
ort along radial geodesics** from $p$. This gives smooth vector fields $\{E_i\}$
 on $U$.

Because $R \equiv 0$, parallel transport is **path-independent** on the simply c
onnected set $U$: for any curve $\gamma$ from $p$ to $q$ in $U$, the parallel-tr
ansport map $P_\gamma : T_p M \to T_q M$ depends only on the endpoints. (This fo
llows from the Ambrose–Singer / holonomy theorem: the curvature tensor generates
 the Lie algebra of the holonomy group; $R = 0$ means trivial holonomy.) Consequ
ently the radially-parallel frame $\{E_i\}$ is in fact parallel along **every**
curve in $U$:

$$\nabla E_i = 0 \quad \text{on } U, \quad i = 1, \dots, n.$$

Now define 1-forms $\omega^i$ by $\omega^i(X) = \langle X, E_i\rangle$ and dual
vector fields, and define functions $x^i : U \to \mathbb{R}$ by

$$x^i(q) = \langle \exp_p^{-1}(q),\, E_i(p)\rangle,$$

i.e., the coordinates of $q$ in the normal coordinate system determined by $\{E_
i(p)\}$. One verifies that $dx^i = \omega^i$, so $\partial/\partial x^i = E_i$.
Since the $E_i$ are parallel and orthonormal,

$$g\!\left(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x^i},\, \tfrac{\partial}{\partial x^j}\righ
t) = \langle E_i, E_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}.$$

Thus $g = \sum_i (dx^i)^2$ on $U$: the metric is Euclidean in the coordinates $\
{x^i\}$. Hence $(U, g|_U)$ is isometric to an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Since $p$ was arbitrary, $M$ is locally isometric to $\mathbb{R}^n$, i.e., **fla
t**. $\square$

---

### Summary

Combining both directions:

$$\boxed{(M, g) \text{ is flat} \iff K(\sigma) = 0 \text{ for every 2-plane } \s
igma \text{ at every point.}}$$

The forward direction is immediate (flat $\Rightarrow$ $R = 0$ $\Rightarrow$ $K
= 0$). The reverse direction uses the algebraic fact that the Riemann tensor — a
s a symmetric bilinear form on $\Lambda^2 T_pM$ — is completely determined by it
s values on decomposable 2-vectors (the sectional curvatures), so $K \equiv 0$ f
orces $R \equiv 0$; then trivial holonomy (from $R = 0$ on a simply connected no
rmal neighborhood) yields a parallel orthonormal frame, giving Euclidean coordin
ates.
