## Proof

**Answer-leak check:** The problem statement contains no solution, answer value,
 proof sketch, or formalization hint. Proceeding.

---

Let $f(z) = \left(1 - \dfrac{1}{z^{2}}\right)e^{\,z+1/z}$. We integrate over $S_
{1}^{+}(0)$, the positively oriented unit circle $|z|=1$.

**Step 1 — Recognize an antiderivative.**

Set $g(z) = z + \dfrac{1}{z}$, which is holomorphic on $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
$. Then
$$
g'(z) = 1 - \frac{1}{z^{2}}.
$$
Since the exponential is entire, the chain rule gives
$$
\frac{d}{dz}\,e^{\,g(z)} = g'(z)\,e^{\,g(z)} = \left(1-\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right)e^{
\,z+1/z} = f(z).
$$
Thus $F(z) := e^{\,z+1/z}$ is an antiderivative of $f$.

**Step 2 — The antiderivative is single-valued on the relevant domain.**

The map $z \mapsto z + 1/z$ is single-valued and holomorphic on $\mathbb{C}\setm
inus\{0\}$, and $w \mapsto e^{w}$ is entire and single-valued. Hence $F(z)=e^{\,
z+1/z}$ is a **single-valued holomorphic** function on $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
$, and
$$
F'(z) = f(z) \quad\text{for all } z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}.
$$

**Step 3 — Apply the fundamental theorem of contour integration.**

The unit circle $S_{1}^{+}(0)$ is a closed piecewise-$C^{1}$ curve lying entirel
y in $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$ (it avoids the origin). Since $f$ admits a singl
e-valued antiderivative $F$ on this domain,
$$
\oint_{S_{1}^{+}(0)} f(z)\,dz = F(\text{end}) - F(\text{start}) = 0,
$$
because the curve is closed (start = end).

**Step 4 — Role of the singularity at the origin.**

The origin is an essential singularity of $e^{\,1/z}$ (and hence of $F$ and of $
f$). However, the vanishing of the integral does **not** depend on the nature of
 this singularity. What matters is that $f$ possesses a single-valued primitive
on the domain $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$ in which the contour lies. The existenc
e of such a primitive forces the integral over *every* closed curve in that doma
in to vanish — the residue at $0$ is necessarily $0$.

**Verification via Laurent series (independent check).** Write
$$
e^{\,z+1/z} = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_{n}\,z^{n}, \qquad c_{n}=\sum_{k=\max(
0,n)}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!\,(k-n)!}.
$$
Then
$$
f(z) = \frac{d}{dz}e^{\,z+1/z} = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} n\,c_{n}\,z^{\,n-1}.
$$
The residue of $f$ at $0$ is the coefficient of $z^{-1}$, i.e. the term with $n-
1=-1$, so $n=0$:
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=0} f = 0\cdot c_{0} = 0.
$$
By the residue theorem,
$$
\oint_{S_{1}^{+}(0)} f(z)\,dz = 2\pi i\,\operatorname{Res}_{z=0}f = 0.
$$

Both arguments agree.

---

**Conclusion.** The integrand $\left(1-\dfrac{1}{z^{2}}\right)e^{\,z+1/z}$ is th
e derivative of the single-valued holomorphic function $e^{\,z+1/z}$ on $\mathbb
{C}\setminus\{0\}$. Therefore its integral over the closed unit circle (which li
es in this domain) is zero. The essential singularity at the origin lies outside
 the domain of the primitive and does not contribute a residue.

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle \oint_{S_{1}^{+}(0)}\left(1-\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right)e^{\,z+1/
z}\,dz \;=\; 0.}
$$
