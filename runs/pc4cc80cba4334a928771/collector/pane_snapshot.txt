## Proof

**Yes**, $Y$ is necessarily the completion of $X$ (up to isometry fixing $X$).

Recall the definition: a **completion** of a metric space $X$ is a pair $(\hat{X
}, i)$ where $\hat{X}$ is a complete metric space and $i: X \to \hat{X}$ is an i
sometric embedding with $i(X)$ dense in $\hat{X}$. The completion is **unique up
 to isometry**: if $(\hat{X}, i)$ and $(\hat{X}', i')$ are two completions of $X
$, there exists a bijective isometry $\varphi: \hat{X} \to \hat{X}'$ such that $
\varphi \circ i = i'$.

Since $X$ is a **subspace** of $Y$, the metric $d_X$ on $X$ is the restriction o
f $d_Y$ to $X \times X$. Consequently the inclusion map
$$j: X \hookrightarrow Y, \quad j(x) = x$$
is an **isometric embedding**: for all $x, x' \in X$,
$$d_Y(j(x), j(x')) = d_Y(x, x') = d_X(x, x').$$

By hypothesis:
- $Y$ is a complete metric space,
- $j(X) = X$ is dense in $Y$,
- $j: X \to Y$ is an isometric embedding.

Thus $(Y, j)$ satisfies exactly the three defining properties of a completion of
 $X$. By the uniqueness of completions up to isometry, $Y$ is isometric to the (
canonical) completion of $X$ via an isometry that fixes $X$ pointwise.

Therefore $Y$ **is** the completion of $X$. $\blacksquare$
