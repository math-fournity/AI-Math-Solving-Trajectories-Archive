The statement is **false**. Here is a counterexample.

**Counterexample.** Take $p = 2$ and the probability space $([0,1], \mathcal{B},
 \lambda)$ where $\lambda$ is Lebesgue measure. Define
$$
X_n = Y_n = n^{1/4} \mathbf{1}_{[0,\,1/n]}.
$$
Set $X = Y = 0$.

**Step 1: $X_n \to 0$ in $L^2$.**
$$
\|X_n - 0\|_2^2 = \int_0^1 |X_n|^2\,d\lambda = n^{1/2} \cdot \lambda([0,1/n]) =
n^{1/2} \cdot \frac{1}{n} = n^{-1/2} \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 0.
$$
So $\|X_n\|_2 = n^{-1/4} \to 0$, hence $X_n \to 0$ in $L^2$. Likewise $Y_n \to 0
$ in $L^2$.

**Step 2: $X_n Y_n \not\to 0$ in $L^2$.**
Since $X_n = Y_n$, we have
$$
X_n Y_n = X_n^2 = n^{1/2}\mathbf{1}_{[0,\,1/n]}.
$$
Its $L^2$-norm is
$$
\|X_n Y_n\|_2^2 = \int_0^1 |X_n Y_n|^2\,d\lambda = n \cdot \lambda([0,1/n]) = n
\cdot \frac{1}{n} = 1.
$$
Thus $\|X_n Y_n - 0\|_2 = 1$ for every $n$, which does **not** converge to $0$.
Therefore $X_n Y_n \not\to XY = 0$ in $L^2$.

**Conclusion.** We have exhibited $L^2$-convergent sequences $X_n \to 0$ and $Y_
n \to 0$ whose product does not converge to $0$ in $L^2$. Hence the statement is
 **false** in general.

(Remark: the statement *does* hold under additional hypotheses — for instance, i
f one factor is bounded, or more generally if $\{X_n\}$ and $\{Y_n\}$ are unifor
mly $L^\infty$-bounded, by the inequality $\|X_nY_n - XY\|_p \le \|X_n\|_{2p}\|Y
_n-Y\|_{2p} + \|Y\|_{2p}\|X_n-X\|_{2p}$ together with an $L^{2p}$-boundedness as
sumption. But without such assumptions, the claim fails as shown.)
