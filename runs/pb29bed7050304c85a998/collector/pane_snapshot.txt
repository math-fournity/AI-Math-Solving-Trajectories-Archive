**Answer: Yes, $T^{-1}$ is bounded.**

**Proof.**

We use the **Open Mapping Theorem**: *If $X$ and $Y$ are Banach spaces and $S:X\
to Y$ is a bounded, surjective linear operator, then $S$ is an open map (i.e., $
S$ maps open sets to open sets).*

Apply this with $Y=X$ and $S=T$. Since $X$ is a Banach space and $T:X\to X$ is b
ounded and bijective (hence surjective), the Open Mapping Theorem applies, and w
e conclude that $T$ is an **open map**.

Now we use the following elementary fact:

> **Claim.** A bijective map between topological spaces is open if and only if i
ts inverse is continuous.

*Proof of claim.* Let $f:A\to B$ be bijective. For any open $U\subseteq A$, $f$
being open means $f(U)$ is open in $B$. Since $f$ is bijective, $f(U) = (f^{-1})
^{-1}(U)$. Thus "$f$ is open" is equivalent to "$(f^{-1})^{-1}(U)$ is open for e
very open $U\subseteq A$", which is exactly the statement that $f^{-1}:B\to A$ i
s continuous. $\square$

Applying the claim to $T$ (a bijection that is open), we obtain that $T^{-1}:X\t
o X$ is **continuous**.

It remains to note that $T^{-1}$ is linear. Indeed, since $T$ is linear and bije
ctive, for any $x,y\in X$ and scalars $\alpha,\beta$:
$$T\bigl(\alpha\,T^{-1}x + \beta\,T^{-1}y\bigr) = \alpha\,T(T^{-1}x)+\beta\,T(T^
{-1}y) = \alpha x + \beta y = T\bigl(T^{-1}(\alpha x+\beta y)\bigr),$$
and injectivity of $T$ gives $T^{-1}(\alpha x+\beta y)=\alpha\,T^{-1}x+\beta\,T^
{-1}y$.

A continuous linear operator from a Banach space to a normed space is bounded (t
his is the definition: for linear operators between normed spaces, continuity at
 the origin is equivalent to boundedness). Therefore $T^{-1}$ is a bounded linea
r operator.

**Conclusion.** For a bounded bijective linear operator $T$ on a Banach space $X
$, the inverse $T^{-1}$ is automatically bounded. This is precisely the content
of the **Bounded Inverse Theorem**, a direct corollary of the Open Mapping Theor
em.
