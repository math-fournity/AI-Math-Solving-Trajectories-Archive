## Solution

**Reformulation.** "Every triangle has exactly two colors" means every triangle
is *neither monochromatic nor rainbow* (rainbow = three distinct colors). So the
 coloring has:
- no monochromatic triangle, and
- no rainbow triangle.

A coloring with no rainbow triangle is called a **Gallai coloring**. We use Gall
ai's structure theorem:

> **Gallai's Theorem.** In any Gallai coloring of a complete graph, the vertex s
et has a nontrivial partition $V_1,\dots,V_k$ ($k\ge 2$) such that
> (i) all edges between $V_i$ and $V_j$ have a single color $c_{ij}$;
> (ii) at most two colors appear among the inter-part edges;
> (iii) the reduced graph on $\{V_1,\dots,V_k\}$ (edge $V_iV_j$ colored $c_{ij}$
) is again a Gallai coloring.

### Lower bound: $n=10$ is attainable

Take two disjoint sets $A=\{a_1,\dots,a_5\}$ and $B=\{b_1,\dots,b_5\}$, ten poin
ts in general position. Color:
- every edge between $A$ and $B$ **red**;
- within $A$ (and identically within $B$): the $5$-cycle edges $a_1a_2,a_2a_3,a_
3a_4,a_4a_5,a_5a_1$ **yellow**, and the five diagonals **green**.

Check every triangle:
- **3 vertices in $A$** (or in $B$): the induced $K_3$ uses only yellow/green, a
nd the $5$-cycle-plus-diagonals $2$-coloring has no monochromatic triangle, so e
xactly two colors. ✓
- **2 in $A$, 1 in $B$**: the two cross edges are red, the intra-$A$ edge is yel
low or green → colors $\{\text{red},\text{yellow/green}\}$, exactly two. ✓
- **1 in $A$, 2 in $B$**: symmetric. ✓

So $n=10$ is achievable.

### Upper bound: $n\le 10$

Apply Gallai's theorem to obtain the partition $V_1,\dots,V_k$ with inter-part c
olors drawn from $\{\text{red},\text{yellow}\}$ (WLOG). The reduced graph is a $
2$-coloring of $K_k$ with no monochromatic triangle (a monochromatic triangle in
 the reduced graph lifts to one in the original). By $R(3,3)=6$, we have $k\le 5
$.

**Key constraint on each part.** Take $x,y\in V_i$ and $z\in V_j$. Then $xz=yz=c
_{ij}$, so the triangle $xyz$ has two edges of color $c_{ij}$; to have exactly t
wo colors, the edge $xy$ must satisfy $xy\ne c_{ij}$. Since this holds for every
 $j\ne i$:

$$\text{colors used inside }V_i\ \subseteq\ \{\text{red},\text{yellow},\text{gre
en}\}\setminus\{c_{ij}:j\ne i\}.$$

Two cases for part $V_i$:

- **Monochromatic-degree part:** all incident inter-part edges have the *same* c
olor (say red). Then inside $V_i$ only yellow/green are available — a $2$-colori
ng with no monochromatic triangle, so $|V_i|\le 5$ (again by $R(3,3)=6$).
- **Mixed part:** incident inter-part edges use *both* red and yellow. Then insi
de $V_i$ only green is available, forcing all intra-part edges green; to avoid a
 monochromatic triangle, $|V_i|\le 2$.

Now maximize $\sum|V_i|$ over $2$-colorings of $K_k$ ($k\le 5$) with no monochro
matic triangle:

- $k=2$: each part has a single incident edge → both monochromatic-degree → $5+5
=10$.
- $k=3$: a no-mono $2$-coloring of $K_3$ has two edges of one color, one of the
other; exactly one vertex is monochromatic-degree → $5+2+2=9$.
- $k=4$: no vertex can have all three incident edges the same color (that would
force the other three vertices to form a monochromatic $K_3$), so all parts are
mixed → $4\cdot 2=8$.
- $k=5$: likewise no vertex has all four incident edges the same color (the othe
r four vertices would form a monochromatic $K_4$), so all parts are mixed → $5\c
dot 2=10$.

The maximum over all cases is $\boxed{10}$.

Combining both parts, the maximum possible value of $n$ is $\boxed{10}$.
