## Proof

Let $\operatorname{inj}(p)$ denote the injectivity radius at $p$. The expression
 $\exp_p^{-1}$ is well-defined and single-valued on $\mathbb{B}(p,r)$ precisely
when $r < \operatorname{inj}(p)$, so the infimum is effectively taken over $r \i
n (0, \operatorname{inj}(p))$.

**Step 1: The supremum is $\geq 1$ for every $r \in (0, \operatorname{inj}(p))$.
**

Fix any $r \in (0, \operatorname{inj}(p))$ and any unit vector $w \in T_p\M$. Fo
r any $r' \in (0, r)$, set $u = p$ and $v = \exp_p(r' w)$. Then $u, v \in \mathb
b{B}(p, r)$, and:

- $\exp_p^{-1}(u) = 0$ and $\exp_p^{-1}(v) = r'w$, so $\|\exp_p^{-1}u - \exp_p^{
-1}v\|_p = r'$.
- Since $r' < \operatorname{inj}(p)$, the radial geodesic $t \mapsto \exp_p(t r'
 w)$ is the unique minimizing geodesic from $p$ to $v$, so $d(u, v) = d(p, v) =
r'$.

Thus the ratio equals $\frac{r'}{r'} = 1$ for this pair. Since the supremum is o
ver all pairs in the ball, we conclude:
$$\sup_{u,v \in \mathbb{B}(p,r)} \frac{\|\exp_p^{-1}u - \exp_p^{-1}v\|_p}{d(u,v)
} \geq 1 \quad \text{for all } r \in (0, \operatorname{inj}(p)).$$

**Step 2: The supremum tends to $1$ as $r \to 0^+$.**

Work in normal coordinates at $p$, identifying $\mathbb{B}(p,r)$ with the Euclid
ean ball $B(0,r) \subset T_p\M$ via $\exp_p$. In these coordinates, the metric t
ensor satisfies the well-known expansion:
$$g_{ij}(x) = \delta_{ij} - \tfrac{1}{3}R_{ikjl}(p)\, x^k x^l + O(|x|^3),$$
so $g_{ij}(x) = \delta_{ij} + O(r^2)$ uniformly on $B(0,r)$.

For any piecewise smooth curve $\gamma: [0,1] \to B(0,r)$, its Riemannian length
 satisfies:
$$L_g(\gamma) = \int_0^1 \sqrt{g_{ij}(\gamma(t))\,\dot\gamma^i\dot\gamma^j}\, dt
 = \int_0^1 |\dot\gamma(t)|\,(1 + O(r^2))\, dt = (1 + O(r^2))\, L_{\text{Euc}}(\
gamma),$$
where the $O(r^2)$ bound is uniform over all curves in $B(0,r)$ and all $r$ suff
iciently small.

For $r$ small enough, $B(0,r)$ is geodesically convex (normal balls are strongly
 convex), so the minimizing geodesic between any two points $x, y \in B(0,r)$ st
ays inside $B(0,r)$. Therefore:

- **Lower bound:** $d(\exp_p x, \exp_p y) = \inf_\gamma L_g(\gamma) \geq (1 - Cr
^2)\inf_\gamma L_{\text{Euc}}(\gamma) = (1 - Cr^2)\|x - y\|$.
- **Upper bound:** The Euclidean segment from $x$ to $y$ lies in $B(0,r)$, givin
g $d(\exp_p x, \exp_p y) \leq L_g(\text{segment}) \leq (1 + Cr^2)\|x - y\|$.

Here $C > 0$ is a constant depending on the curvature at $p$. Combining:
$$d(\exp_p x, \exp_p y) = \|x - y\|\,(1 + O(r^2)) \quad \text{uniformly for } \|
x\|, \|y\| < r.$$

Setting $u = \exp_p x$, $v = \exp_p y$, the ratio becomes:
$$\frac{\|\exp_p^{-1}u - \exp_p^{-1}v\|_p}{d(u,v)} = \frac{\|x - y\|}{\|x - y\|(
1 + O(r^2))} = 1 + O(r^2),$$
uniformly over all $u, v \in \mathbb{B}(p, r)$ with $u \neq v$. Taking the supre
mum:
$$\sup_{u,v \in \mathbb{B}(p,r)} \frac{\|\exp_p^{-1}u - \exp_p^{-1}v\|_p}{d(u,v)
} = 1 + O(r^2) \xrightarrow{r \to 0^+} 1.$$

**Step 3: Conclusion.**

From Step 1, the supremum is $\geq 1$ for every admissible $r > 0$, so the infim
um is $\geq 1$. From Step 2, the supremum can be made arbitrarily close to $1$ b
y taking $r$ sufficiently small, so the infimum is $\leq 1$. Therefore:

$$\inf_{r \geq 0} \sup_{u,v \in \mathbb{B}(p,r)} \frac{\|\exp_p^{-1}u - \exp_p^{
-1}v\|_p}{d(u,v)} = 1.$$

The claim is **true**. $\blacksquare$
