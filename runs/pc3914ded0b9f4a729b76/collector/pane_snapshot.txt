# Proof: $\pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(n)) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ for $n \geq 4$

## Step 1: The Fiber Bundle $\mathrm{SO}(n-1) \to \mathrm{SO}(n) \to S^{n-1}$

Consider the projection map $p: \mathrm{SO}(n) \to S^{n-1}$ that sends an orthog
onal matrix $A$ to its first column $Ae_1$. This map is surjective (any unit vec
tor can be extended to an orthonormal basis), and the fiber over $e_1$ consists
of matrices fixing $e_1$, which is isomorphic to $\mathrm{SO}(n-1)$ acting on th
e orthogonal complement $\langle e_1 \rangle^\perp \cong \mathbb{R}^{n-1}$.

This gives a fiber bundle:
$$\mathrm{SO}(n-1) \hookrightarrow \mathrm{SO}(n) \xrightarrow{p} S^{n-1}.$$

## Step 2: The Long Exact Sequence and the Key Isomorphism

The long exact sequence of homotopy groups for this fibration reads:
$$\cdots \to \pi_2(S^{n-1}) \to \pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(n-1)) \to \pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(n)
) \to \pi_1(S^{n-1}) \to \cdots$$

For $n \geq 4$, we have $n - 1 \geq 3$, so:
- $\pi_1(S^{n-1}) = 0$ (since $S^{n-1}$ is simply connected for $n-1 \geq 2$),
- $\pi_2(S^{n-1}) = 0$ (since $\pi_2(S^k) = 0$ for $k \geq 3$).

The exact sequence therefore reduces to:
$$0 \to \pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(n-1)) \to \pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(n)) \to 0,$$

which gives an isomorphism:
$$\pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(n-1)) \cong \pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(n)) \quad \text{for all } n \g
eq 4.$$

## Step 3: Reduction to $\mathrm{SO}(3)$

By iterating the isomorphism from Step 2 for $n, n-1, n-2, \ldots, 4$, we obtain
:
$$\pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(n)) \cong \pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(n-1)) \cong \cdots \cong \pi_1(\
mathrm{SO}(3)).$$

Thus it suffices to compute $\pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(3))$.

## Step 4: $\mathrm{SO}(3) \cong \mathbb{RP}^3$ via the Quaternions

Consider the group $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, which is isomorphic to $S^3$ (the group of
unit quaternions $\{q \in \mathbb{H} : |q| = 1\}$).

The imaginary quaternions $\mathrm{Im}(\mathbb{H}) \cong \mathbb{R}^3$ carry an
inner product. For a unit quaternion $q$, the map $\rho_q: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mat
hbb{R}^3$ defined by $\rho_q(v) = qvq^{-1}$ (conjugation) is an orientation-pres
erving orthogonal transformation of $\mathbb{R}^3$, i.e., an element of $\mathrm
{SO}(3)$.

This defines a surjective homomorphism:
$$\rho: \mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{SO}(3), \quad q \mapsto \rho_q.$$

The kernel is $\{\pm 1\}$: if $qvq^{-1} = v$ for all imaginary $v$, then $q$ com
mutes with all of $\mathbb{H}$, so $q \in \mathbb{R}$, and $|q|=1$ gives $q = \p
m 1$.

Therefore $\mathrm{SO}(3) \cong \mathrm{SU}(2)/\{\pm 1\} \cong S^3/\{\pm 1\} \co
ng \mathbb{RP}^3$.

## Step 5: $\pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(3)) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$

The quotient map $S^3 \to \mathbb{RP}^3$ is a 2-sheeted covering map. Since $S^3
$ is simply connected ($\pi_1(S^3) = 0$), this is the universal cover of $\mathb
b{RP}^3 = \mathrm{SO}(3)$.

By the theory of covering spaces, the fundamental group of the base is isomorphi
c to the group of deck transformations, which for the covering $S^3 \to \mathbb{
RP}^3$ is $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (generated by the antipodal map $q \mapsto -q
$).

Equivalently, from the exact sequence of the covering:
$$0 = \pi_1(S^3) \to \pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^3) \to \pi_0(\{\pm 1\}) \to \pi_0(S^3) =
0,$$
we get $\pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(3)) \cong \{\pm 1\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

## Conclusion

Combining Steps 2–5:
$$\boxed{\pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(n)) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \quad \text{for all
} n \geq 4.}$$

The generator of this group is represented by a rotation through angle $\theta$
about a fixed axis, as $\theta$ goes from $0$ to $2\pi$; this loop is non-contra
ctible, but traversing it twice (angle $0$ to $4\pi$) is contractible—this is th
e topological content of the "belt trick" (Dirac's string trick).
