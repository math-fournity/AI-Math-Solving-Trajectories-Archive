## Answer

**Yes, it is necessarily true that $f(p) = 0$**, provided $f$ is continuous (whi
ch is the standard assumption when one speaks of a vector field and its integral
 curves). The key ingredient is a classical lemma about limits of derivatives.

---

### Key Lemma

**Lemma.** Let $g:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^n$ be a $C^1$ function. If $\lim_{t\to
\infty} g(t) = p$ exists (and is finite) and $\lim_{t\to\infty} g'(t) = L$ exist
s, then $L = 0$.

**Proof of Lemma.** Suppose for contradiction that $L \neq 0$. Without loss of g
enerality, assume $L > 0$ (otherwise replace $g$ by $-g$). Then there exists $T
\geq 0$ such that for all $t > T$,
$$g'(t) > \frac{L}{2}.$$
Integrating from $T$ to $t$:
$$g(t) = g(T) + \int_T^t g'(s)\,ds > g(T) + \frac{L}{2}(t - T).$$
As $t \to \infty$, the right-hand side tends to $+\infty$, contradicting the ass
umption that $g(t) \to p$ (finite). Hence $L = 0$. $\square$

---

### Main Proof

Since $g$ is an integral curve of $f$, we have
$$g'(t) = f(g(t)) \quad \text{for all } t \in [0,\infty).$$

Since $g(t) \to p$ as $t \to \infty$ and $f$ is continuous, we have
$$\lim_{t\to\infty} g'(t) = \lim_{t\to\infty} f(g(t)) = f(p).$$

So $g'(t) \to f(p)$ as $t \to \infty$. Applying the Lemma (with $L = f(p)$), we
conclude
$$f(p) = 0. \qquad \square$$

---

### Remark: Continuity of $f$ is Essential

The continuity assumption cannot be dropped. Without it, the conclusion can fail
. Here is a one-dimensional counterexample:

Define $g(t) = \frac{1}{t+1}$ for $t \geq 0$, so that $g(t) \to 0 =: p$ as $t \t
o \infty$, and $g'(t) = -\frac{1}{(t+1)^2} = -g(t)^2$. Define the vector field $
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ by
$$f(x) = \begin{cases} -x^2 & \text{if } x > 0, \\ 1 & \text{if } x = 0. \end{ca
ses}$$
(One may define $f$ arbitrarily for $x < 0$.) Then $g'(t) = f(g(t))$ holds for a
ll $t \geq 0$, since $g(t) > 0$ for every finite $t$, so $f$ is never evaluated
at $0$ along the curve. Thus $g$ is a genuine integral curve of $f$ on $[0,\inft
y)$ with $g(t) \to 0$, yet
$$f(p) = f(0) = 1 \neq 0.$$

The discontinuity of $f$ at $p = 0$ is precisely what allows the limit of $f(g(t
))$ to differ from $f(p)$.

---

**Conclusion.** Under the standard assumption that the vector field $f$ is conti
nuous, the answer is **yes**: $f(p) = 0$. The proof rests on the classical fact
that a convergent $C^1$ function whose derivative has a limit must have that lim
it equal to zero.
