## Proof

Let $f: K \rightarrow L$ be a homeomorphism between finite simplicial complexes
$K$ and $L$. We show that the induced homomorphism $f_{*}: H_{n}(K; \mathbb{Z}_{
2}) \rightarrow H_{n}(L; \mathbb{Z}_{2})$ is an isomorphism for every $n \geq 0$
.

### Step 1. The induced map on chain complexes

A simplicial map $f: K \rightarrow L$ induces, for each $n$, a homomorphism on t
he chain groups
$$f_{\#}: C_{n}(K; \mathbb{Z}_{2}) \longrightarrow C_{n}(L; \mathbb{Z}_{2}),$$
defined on an oriented $n$-simplex $\sigma = [v_0, \dots, v_n]$ by
$$f_{\#}(\sigma) = [f(v_0), \dots, f(v_n)]$$
(when the images are distinct, so that the result is an $n$-simplex of $L$; othe
rwise the image is degenerate and the term vanishes). Working over $\mathbb{Z}_{
2}$ has the convenient feature that orientation signs are irrelevant, since $-1
= 1$ in $\mathbb{Z}_{2}$.

The key algebraic fact is that $f_{\#}$ **commutes with the boundary operators**
:
$$\partial_{n}^{L} \circ f_{\#} = f_{\#} \circ \partial_{n}^{K}.$$
This is verified by direct computation:
$$\partial f_{\#}([v_0,\dots,v_n]) = \partial [f(v_0),\dots,f(v_n)] = \sum_{i=0}
^{n} [f(v_0),\dots,\widehat{f(v_i)},\dots,f(v_n)] = f_{\#}\!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}
[v_0,\dots,\widehat{v_i},\dots,v_n]\right) = f_{\#}(\partial [v_0,\dots,v_n]).$$

Because $f_{\#}$ commutes with $\partial$, it is a **chain map**. A chain map se
nds cycles to cycles and boundaries to boundaries:
- If $z \in Z_n(K) = \ker \partial_n^K$, then $\partial_n^L f_{\#}(z) = f_{\#} \
partial_n^K(z) = f_{\#}(0) = 0$, so $f_{\#}(z) \in Z_n(L)$.
- If $b = \partial_{n+1}^K(c) \in B_n(K)$, then $f_{\#}(b) = f_{\#}\partial_{n+1
}^K(c) = \partial_{n+1}^L f_{\#}(c) \in B_n(L)$.

Therefore $f_{\#}$ descends to a well-defined homomorphism on homology:
$$f_{*}: H_n(K;\mathbb{Z}_2) \to H_n(L;\mathbb{Z}_2), \qquad f_{*}([z]) = [f_{\#
}(z)].$$

### Step 2. Functoriality

Simplicial homology with $\mathbb{Z}_2$ coefficients is a **functor** from the c
ategory of simplicial complexes and simplicial maps to the category of $\mathbb{
Z}_2$-vector spaces (or abelian groups). Concretely:

- $(\mathrm{id}_K)_* = \mathrm{id}_{H_n(K)}$, and
- $(g \circ f)_* = g_* \circ f_*$ for composable simplicial maps $f: K \to L$, $
g: L \to M$.

These follow immediately from the corresponding properties of $f_{\#}$ on chains
, which are themselves immediate from the definition.

### Step 3. Using that $f$ is a homeomorphism

Now we use the hypothesis that $f$ is a **homeomorphism**, i.e., $f$ is bijectiv
e, continuous, and has a continuous inverse $f^{-1}: L \to K$.

**Important subtlety.** A homeomorphism between the underlying topological space
s $|K|$ and $|L|$ need not be a simplicial map $K \to L$ in the given triangulat
ions. However, by the **Simplicial Approximation Theorem**, any continuous map $
|K| \to |L|$ between finite simplicial complexes is homotopic to a simplicial ma
p $K^{(m)} \to L$ after passing to a suitable barycentric subdivision $K^{(m)}$.
 Barycentric subdivision does not change the homology (the subdivision chain map
 is a chain homotopy equivalence), so the induced map on homology is well-define
d up to the homotopy class of the original continuous map.

Since $f$ is a homeomorphism, $f$ and $f^{-1}$ are continuous maps with
$$f^{-1} \circ f = \mathrm{id}_K, \qquad f \circ f^{-1} = \mathrm{id}_L.$$

Applying the homology functor and using functoriality:
$$(f^{-1})_* \circ f_* = (f^{-1} \circ f)_* = (\mathrm{id}_K)_* = \mathrm{id}_{H
_n(K;\mathbb{Z}_2)},$$
$$f_* \circ (f^{-1})_* = (f \circ f^{-1})_* = (\mathrm{id}_L)_* = \mathrm{id}_{H
_n(L;\mathbb{Z}_2)}.$$

These two identities say precisely that $f_*$ and $(f^{-1})_*$ are **mutual inve
rses**. Hence
$$f_*: H_n(K;\mathbb{Z}_2) \longrightarrow H_n(L;\mathbb{Z}_2)$$
is an isomorphism of $\mathbb{Z}_2$-vector spaces (equivalently, of abelian grou
ps), for every $n \geq 0$.

### Step 4. Significance: homology as a topological invariant

The result we have just proved has the following consequences, which together es
tablish simplicial homology as a **topological invariant**:

1. **Homeomorphic spaces have isomorphic homology.** If $K$ and $L$ are homeomor
phic, then $H_n(K;\mathbb{Z}_2) \cong H_n(L;\mathbb{Z}_2)$ for all $n$. The homo
logy groups depend only on the **topological type** of the space, not on the par
ticular choice of triangulation. This is the precise sense in which homology is
a *topological* invariant: it is preserved by homeomorphism.

2. **Triangulation independence.** A single topological space may admit many dif
ferent triangulations (e.g., a sphere can be triangulated as the boundary of a t
etrahedron, or as a more refined simplicial complex). The theorem guarantees tha
t all these triangulations yield the same homology groups, since they are all ho
meomorphic. Thus homology is well-defined on the underlying topological space, n
ot merely on the combinatorial simplicial complex.

3. **Distinguishing topological spaces.** The contrapositive is the practical to
ol: if $H_n(K;\mathbb{Z}_2) \not\cong H_n(L;\mathbb{Z}_2)$ for some $n$, then $K
$ and $L$ are **not** homeomorphic. This converts the difficult problem of provi
ng two spaces are topologically distinct into the tractable problem of computing
 homology groups (which are finitely generated, computable objects for finite co
mplexes). For example, the $n$-sphere and the $n$-disk have different homology (
the sphere has a nontrivial class in degree $n$, the disk is contractible and ha
s trivial reduced homology), so they cannot be homeomorphic.

4. **Functoriality and the categorical viewpoint.** The proof reveals *why* the
result holds: homology is a **functor** from topological spaces to algebraic obj
ects, and functors preserve isomorphisms. A homeomorphism is an isomorphism in t
he category of topological spaces, so its image under any functor is an isomorph
ism in the target category. This is the general principle that makes homology—an
d indeed any functorial invariant—automatically a topological invariant.

5. **Coefficients in $\mathbb{Z}_2$.** The choice of $\mathbb{Z}_2$ coefficients
 simplifies the theory by eliminating orientation issues (every simplex is its o
wn negative), making the chain groups vector spaces over the field $\mathbb{Z}_2
$. The same argument works for any coefficient group $G$ (in particular $\mathbb
{Z}$), yielding $f_*: H_n(K;G) \to H_n(L;G)$ an isomorphism. The $\mathbb{Z}_2$
version is often used in applications (e.g., computational topology, the classif
ication of surfaces) because of its computational simplicity.

In summary, the fact that $f_*$ is an isomorphism is a direct consequence of the
 **functoriality** of simplicial homology together with the fact that a homeomor
phism is an isomorphism in the topological category. This makes homology groups
computable algebraic invariants that classify spaces up to homeomorphism (in the
 sense that they are *necessary* conditions for homeomorphism), providing a powe
rful bridge between topology and algebra.
