## Proof

First, I analyze the hypothesis. Since $A \in GL_n(\mathbb{Z})$, both $A$ and $A
^{-1}$ have integer entries, which forces $\det(A) = \pm 1$. The condition $\det
(A^2) = \det(A)$ becomes $\det(A)^2 = \det(A)$, i.e. $\det(A)\bigl(\det(A)-1\big
r)=0$. With $\det(A)\in\{-1,1\}$, the only solution is $\det(A)=1$. Hence the hy
pothesis is simply $A \in SL_n(\mathbb{Z})$.

The conclusion claims that for **every** $A\in SL_n(\mathbb{Z})$, the matrix $A^
2$ must be one of $A,\, A^{-1},\, -A,\, -A^{-1}$. I show this is false with a si
ngle counterexample.

Take
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \in SL_2(\mathbb{Z}).
$$
Then $\det(A)=1$, so the hypothesis $\det(A^2)=\det(A)$ is satisfied. We compute
$$
A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$
Now compare $A^2$ against the four candidates:

- $A = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$: the $(1,2)$-entry $1\neq 2$, so $A
^2\neq A$.
- $A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$: the $(1,2)$-entry $-1\neq 2$
, so $A^2\neq A^{-1}$.
- $-A = \begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$: the diagonal entries $-1\neq 1
$, so $A^2\neq -A$.
- $-A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$: the diagonal entries $-1\n
eq 1$, so $A^2\neq -A^{-1}$.

Thus $A^2\notin\{A,\,A^{-1},\,-A,\,-A^{-1}\}$, even though $A\in GL_2(\mathbb{Z}
)$ and $\det(A^2)=\det(A)$.

Therefore the statement is **not** always true.

### PROOF COMPLETE
