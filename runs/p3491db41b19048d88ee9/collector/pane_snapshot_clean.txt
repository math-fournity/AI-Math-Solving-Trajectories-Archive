The answer is **yes**, and the justification is essentially immediate from the d
efinition of nilpotence.

## Proof

Recall that a bounded operator $T$ on a Banach space is said to be **nilpotent**
 if there exists a positive integer $k$ such that $T^k = 0$.

By hypothesis, there exists a positive integer $n$ with
$$T^{2n-1} = 0.$$
Since $n \geq 1$, the exponent $k := 2n-1$ is a positive integer (indeed $k \geq
 1$). Setting $k = 2n-1$, we have exhibited a positive integer $k$ for which $T^
k = 0$.

Therefore, by the very definition of nilpotence, $T$ is nilpotent (with nilpoten
cy index at most $2n-1$).

### Remarks on the hypotheses

- The assumption that the Banach space is **infinite-dimensional** is a red herr
ing. The implication "$T^k = 0$ for some $k \geq 1 \Rightarrow T$ is nilpotent"
is purely algebraic and holds in any dimension — finite or infinite.
- The assumption that $T$ is **bounded** is likewise irrelevant to the conclusio
n; it only ensures we are working within the Banach algebra of bounded operators
, where powers of $T$ are well-defined bounded operators. The argument uses noth
ing beyond the definition.
- The specific odd form $2n-1$ of the exponent plays no special role; any positi
ve exponent would do. One might speculate that the problem is designed to test w
hether the solver recognizes that nilpotence is an algebraic condition requiring
 no spectral, finite-dimensional, or structural hypotheses — and is not tempted
into unnecessary machinery (spectral theory, Jordan forms, etc.) that does not a
pply or is not needed in infinite dimensions.
