## Problem

Determine all positive integers $n$ for which there exist $n \times n$ real inve
rtible matrices $A$ and $B$ satisfying $AB - BA = B^{2}A$.

## Solution

**Claim.** Such matrices exist if and only if $n$ is even.

---

### Necessity (odd $n$ is impossible)

Rewrite the equation as
$$AB - BA = B^{2}A \implies AB = BA + B^{2}A = B(I+B)A.$$
Multiplying on the right by $A^{-1}$ (which exists by hypothesis):
$$ABA^{-1} = B + B^{2} = B(I+B). \tag{1}$$
Thus $B+B^{2}$ is **similar** to $B$; in particular they have the same character
istic polynomial, so their eigenvalue multisets (over $\mathbb{C}$) coincide:
$$\{\lambda + \lambda^{2} : \lambda \in \operatorname{spec}(B)\} = \{\lambda : \
lambda \in \operatorname{spec}(B)\}. \tag{2}$$

Let $f(x) = x + x^{2}$. Equation (2) says $f$ **permutes** the finite multiset $
\operatorname{spec}(B)$. Hence every eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $B$ is **periodic**
 under $f$: $f^{k}(\lambda) = \lambda$ for some $k \geq 1$.

**Real periodic points of $f$.** For real $x$:
- $x > 0$: $f(x) = x + x^{2} > x$, so the orbit strictly increases — never perio
dic.
- $x = 0$: $f(0) = 0$ — fixed (periodic).
- $-1 < x < 0$: $f(x) = x(1+x)$ with $0 < 1+x < 1$, so $x < f(x) < 0$ and $|f(x)
| < |x|$; the orbit converges to $0$ and never returns — not periodic.
- $x = -1$: $f(-1) = 0 \neq -1$ — not periodic.
- $x < -1$: $f(x) = x(1+x) > 0$, then the orbit increases — not periodic.

So the **only** real periodic point of $f$ is $0$.

If $n$ is odd, the real matrix $B$ has an odd number of eigenvalues (with multip
licity) over $\mathbb{C}$, and since non-real eigenvalues occur in conjugate pai
rs, $B$ has at least one **real** eigenvalue $\lambda$. By the periodicity argum
ent, $\lambda$ must be a real periodic point of $f$, so $\lambda = 0$. But $B$ i
s invertible, so $0 \notin \operatorname{spec}(B)$ — **contradiction**.

Therefore no solution exists for odd $n$. $\square$

---

### Sufficiency (every even $n$ works)

**Step 1: A $2 \times 2$ solution.** Take
$$B = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix
} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Both are invertible ($\det B = 2$, $\det A = -1$). Direct computation:
$$AB = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad BA = \begin{pmatri
x} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad AB - BA = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 \\ -2 &
 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
$$B^{2} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ -2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad B^{2}A = \begin{p
matrix} 0 & -2 \\ -2 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = AB - BA. \checkmark$$

**Why this works (eigenvalue viewpoint).** $B$ has eigenvalues $-1 \pm i$ (roots
 of $\lambda^{2}+2\lambda+2=0$), which form a **2-cycle** of $f$:
$$f(-1+i) = (-1+i)+(-1+i)^{2} = -1+i-2i = -1-i, \quad f(-1-i) = -1+i.$$
Since $B$ has distinct eigenvalues it is diagonalizable over $\mathbb{C}$, and $
B+B^{2}$ has the same eigenvalues $\{-1+i,-1-i\}$ (swapped), also diagonalizable
 — hence $B+B^{2}$ is similar to $B$, giving (1). The matrix $A$ above realizes
this conjugation (in fact $B+B^{2} = B^{\mathsf{T}}$, and $A B A^{-1} = B^{\math
sf{T}}$).

**Step 2: Block-diagonal extension.** For any even $n = 2m$, set
$$A = \operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{A_{0},\dots,A_{0}}_{m}), \qquad B = \opera
torname{diag}(\underbrace{B_{0},\dots,B_{0}}_{m}),$$
where $A_{0}, B_{0}$ are the $2\times 2$ matrices above. Then $A, B$ are $n \tim
es n$ real invertible matrices, and the equation $AB - BA = B^{2}A$ holds blockw
ise:
$$AB - BA = \operatorname{diag}(A_{0}B_{0}-B_{0}A_{0},\dots) = \operatorname{dia
g}(B_{0}^{2}A_{0},\dots) = B^{2}A. \checkmark$$

### Conclusion

Solutions exist precisely for **all even positive integers** $n$, i.e. $n \in \{
2, 4, 6, 8, \dots\}$.
