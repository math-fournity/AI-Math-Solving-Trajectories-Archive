## Proof

Let $f = (f_1, \dots, f_N)$ and write $\|f\|_{l^p} = \bigl(\sum_{i=1}^N |f_i|^p\
bigr)^{1/p}$, $\|f\|_{l^q} = \bigl(\sum_{i=1}^N |f_i|^q\bigr)^{1/q}$, with $1 \l
e p \le q < \infty$.

---

### First inequality: $\|f\|_{l^q} \le \|f\|_{l^p}$

If $f = 0$ both sides vanish. Assume $f \ne 0$ and set $b_i = |f_i|/\|f\|_{l^p}$
, so that $\sum_i b_i^p = 1$. In particular $b_i^p \le 1$, hence $b_i \le 1$ for
 every $i$. Since $q \ge p$,

$$b_i^q = b_i^p \cdot b_i^{q-p} \le b_i^p \qquad (\text{because } b_i \le 1).$$

Summing,

$$\sum_{i=1}^N b_i^q \le \sum_{i=1}^N b_i^p = 1,$$

so $\|f\|_{l^q} = \|f\|_{l^p}\bigl(\sum b_i^q\bigr)^{1/q} \le \|f\|_{l^p}$.

**Equality condition.** When $q > p$, the pointwise inequality $b_i^q \le b_i^p$
 is strict unless $b_i \in \{0,1\}$. Equality in the sum therefore requires ever
y $b_i \in \{0,1\}$; together with $\sum b_i^p = 1$ this forces exactly one $b_i
 = 1$ and all others $0$, i.e. **$f$ has exactly one nonzero component** (the ca
se $f=0$ gives equality trivially). When $q = p$ the inequality is an identity,
so equality holds for **all** $f$.

> **Equality in $\|f\|_{l^q}\le\|f\|_{l^p}$ (for $q>p$) $\iff$ $f$ is supported
on at most one point.**

---

### Second inequality: $\|f\|_{l^p} \le N^{1/p-1/q}\,\|f\|_{l^q}$

Set $a_i = |f_i|^p \ge 0$ and $r = q/p \ge 1$. The desired inequality is equival
ent (after dividing both sides by $N^{1/p}$ and raising to appropriate powers) t
o

$$\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N a_i\right)^{\!1/p} \;\le\; \left(\frac{1}{N}\sum
_{i=1}^N a_i^{\,r}\right)^{\!1/(rp)} = \left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N a_i^{\,r}\r
ight)^{\!1/q},$$

i.e. $\bigl(\frac1N\sum a_i\bigr)^{1/p} \le \bigl(\frac1N\sum a_i^r\bigr)^{1/q}$
, which is the **power-mean (Jensen) inequality**: since $x \mapsto x^r$ is conv
ex for $r \ge 1$,

$$\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N a_i^{\,r}\right)^{\!1/r} \;\ge\; \frac{1}{N}\sum
_{i=1}^N a_i,$$

and raising both sides to the power $1/p$ gives exactly the claim.

**Equality condition.** For $r > 1$ (i.e. $q > p$) the map $x \mapsto x^r$ is **
strictly** convex, so Jensen's inequality is an equality iff all the $a_i$ are e
qual, i.e. $|f_1|^p = \cdots = |f_N|^p$, i.e. **all $|f_i|$ are equal**. When $q
 = p$ the inequality is again an identity, so equality holds for all $f$.

> **Equality in $\|f\|_{l^p}\le N^{1/p-1/q}\|f\|_{l^q}$ (for $q>p$) $\iff$ $|f_1
|=|f_2|=\cdots=|f_N|$ (all components have equal modulus).**

---

### Summary

For $q > p$:

| Inequality | Equality condition |
|---|---|
| $\|f\|_{l^q} \le \|f\|_{l^p}$ | $f$ is supported on **at most one** of the $N$
 points (i.e. at most one component is nonzero). |
| $\|f\|_{l^p} \le N^{1/p-1/q}\|f\|_{l^q}$ | **All** components have equal modul
us: $|f_1| = |f_2| = \cdots = |f_N|$. |

For $q = p$ both inequalities are identities and equality holds for every $f$.

These two extremal cases are intuitively complementary: the first inequality $\|
f\|_{l^q}\le\|f\|_{l^p}$ is "most slack" when mass is spread out, and becomes ti
ght precisely when the mass is **maximally concentrated** (a single spike); the
second inequality $\|f\|_{l^p}\le N^{1/p-1/q}\|f\|_{l^q}$ is "most slack" when m
ass is concentrated, and becomes tight precisely when the mass is **maximally sp
read** (uniform across all $N$ points).
