## Proof

**Claim.** Yes, $UU^* = I_Y$ follows.

**Setup.** We have Hilbert spaces $X, Y$ and $U \in \mathcal{B}(X, Y)$ surjectiv
e, with $U^*U = I_X$.

**Step 1: $U$ is injective.**

Suppose $Ux = 0$ for some $x \in X$. Then
$$x = I_X x = U^*U x = U^*(0) = 0.$$
Hence $\ker U = \{0\}$, so $U$ is injective.

**Step 2: $U$ is bijective, hence boundedly invertible.**

By hypothesis $U$ is surjective; by Step 1 it is injective. Thus $U: X \to Y$ is
 a continuous (bounded) bijection between Banach spaces (Hilbert spaces are Bana
ch). By the **Bounded Inverse Theorem** (a consequence of the Open Mapping Theor
em), the inverse $U^{-1}: Y \to X$ exists and is a bounded linear operator.

**Step 3: $U^* = U^{-1}$.**

Starting from $U^*U = I_X$, right-compose with $U^{-1}$ (which exists as a bound
ed operator $Y \to X$ by Step 2):
$$U^* \, U \, U^{-1} = I_X \, U^{-1},$$
$$U^* \, I_Y = U^{-1},$$
$$U^* = U^{-1}.$$

**Step 4: $UU^* = I_Y$.**

Using $U^* = U^{-1}$ from Step 3:
$$UU^* = U \, U^{-1} = I_Y. \qquad \blacksquare$$

**Remark.** The two hypotheses are both essential. $U^*U = I_X$ alone (isometry)
 gives injectivity but not surjectivity, so $UU^*$ is only a projection $P_Y$ on
to $\operatorname{ran} U \subseteq Y$, not necessarily $I_Y$. Surjectivity alone
 does not force $U^*U = I_X$. The combination makes $U$ a surjective isometry, i
.e. a unitary, for which both $U^*U = I_X$ and $UU^* = I_Y$ hold.
