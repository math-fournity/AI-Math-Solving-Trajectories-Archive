## Proof

Since the characteristic polynomial of $A$ has no repeated roots, $A$ has $n$ di
stinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n \in \mathbb{C}$. A matrix with d
istinct eigenvalues is diagonalizable, so $A$ is similar to
$$D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n).$$

Similarity and commutation are both invariant under conjugation: $B$ is similar
to $A$ and commutes with $A$ if and only if $P^{-1}BP$ is similar to $D$ and com
mutes with $D$ (where $A = PDP^{-1}$). Hence we may assume $A = D$.

**Step 1: Matrices commuting with $D$.** Let $B = (b_{ij})$ commute with $D$. Th
e $(i,j)$-entry of $BD$ is $b_{ij}\lambda_j$ and of $DB$ is $\lambda_i b_{ij}$.
Equality gives
$$(\lambda_i - \lambda_j) b_{ij} = 0.$$
Since the eigenvalues are distinct, $\lambda_i \neq \lambda_j$ for $i \neq j$, f
orcing $b_{ij} = 0$ whenever $i \neq j$. Thus every matrix commuting with $D$ is
 diagonal: $B = \operatorname{diag}(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n)$.

**Step 2: Imposing similarity to $A$.** Two diagonalizable matrices (over $\math
bb{C}$) are similar if and only if they have the same eigenvalues with the same
multiplicities. Since $A$ has the distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambd
a_n$, a diagonal matrix $B = \operatorname{diag}(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n)$ is simila
r to $A$ if and only if
$$\{\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n\} = \{\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n\}$$
as multisets. Because the $\lambda_i$ are all distinct, this means $\mu_i = \lam
bda_{\sigma(i)}$ for some permutation $\sigma \in S_n$.

**Step 3: Counting.** Each permutation $\sigma \in S_n$ yields the diagonal matr
ix
$$B_\sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, \lambda_{\sigma(n)}
).$$
Since the $\lambda_i$ are distinct, different permutations give different diagon
al matrices. Conversely, every matrix that is both similar to and commutes with
$D$ arises this way. Therefore the number of such matrices is

$$\boxed{n!}.$$
