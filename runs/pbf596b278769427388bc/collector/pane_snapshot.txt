## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, there exists a countable subset $C$ of polynomials with rational
 coefficients such that every continuous function on $[a,b]$ can be uniformly ap
proximated by polynomials from $C$. In fact, we may take $C = \mathbb{Q}[x]$, th
e set of **all** polynomials with rational coefficients, which is itself countab
le.

---

### Step 1: $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ is countable.

A polynomial with rational coefficients is determined by a finite tuple of ratio
nals $(q_0, q_1, \dots, q_n)$. The set of all such finite tuples is a countable
union (over degree $n$) of the countable sets $\mathbb{Q}^{n+1}$. Since a counta
ble union of countable sets is countable, $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ is countable.

---

### Step 2: Every continuous function on $[a,b]$ is uniformly approximable by el
ements of $\mathbb{Q}[x]$.

Let $f \colon [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous and let $\varepsilon > 0$.

**Approximation by a real polynomial.** By the Weierstrass approximation theorem
, there exists a polynomial $p(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_n x^n$ with real co
efficients such that
$$|f(x) - p(x)| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \quad \text{for all } x \in [a,b].$$

**Approximation of $p$ by a rational-coefficient polynomial.** Set $M = \max(|a|
, |b|)$, so $|x| \le M$ for all $x \in [a,b]$. For each coefficient $a_i$, choos
e a rational number $q_i \in \mathbb{Q}$ with
$$|a_i - q_i| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2(n+1)(M+1)^i}.$$
This is possible because $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$. (We use $(M+1)^i
$ rather than $M^i$ to handle the case $M = 0$ cleanly.)

Define $q(x) = q_0 + q_1 x + \cdots + q_n x^n \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$. Then for every
 $x \in [a,b]$:
$$|p(x) - q(x)| \le \sum_{i=0}^{n} |a_i - q_i|\,|x|^i \le \sum_{i=0}^{n} |a_i -
q_i|\, M^i \le \sum_{i=0}^{n} \frac{\varepsilon}{2(n+1)(M+1)^i} M^i \le \sum_{i=
0}^{n} \frac{\varepsilon}{2(n+1)} = \frac{\varepsilon}{2}.$$

**Combining the two approximations.** By the triangle inequality,
$$|f(x) - q(x)| \le |f(x) - p(x)| + |p(x) - q(x)| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \fra
c{\varepsilon}{2} = \varepsilon$$
for all $x \in [a,b]$.

---

### Conclusion

The set $C = \mathbb{Q}[x]$ is countable (Step 1), and every continuous function
 on $[a,b]$ can be uniformly approximated to arbitrary precision by elements of
$C$ (Step 2). Therefore, such a countable subset exists. $\blacksquare$
