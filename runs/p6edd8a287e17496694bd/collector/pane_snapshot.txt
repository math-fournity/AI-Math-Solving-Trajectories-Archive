**The statement is FALSE.** In fact, under uniform equivalence the two metrics m
ust have the *same* completeness status, so the conclusion "one complete, the ot
her not" can never occur. Below is the proof and an explicit counterexample.

---

### Definitions

Let $d_1, d_2$ be two metrics on a set $X$.

- **Strongly (Lipschitz) equivalent**: $\exists\, c, C > 0$ with $c\,d_1(x,y)\le
 d_2(x,y)\le C\,d_1(x,y)$ for all $x,y\in X$.
- **Uniformly equivalent**: the identity $\mathrm{id}:(X,d_1)\to(X,d_2)$ and its
 inverse are uniformly continuous; equivalently, for every $\varepsilon>0$ there
 is $\delta>0$ with $d_1(x,y)<\delta\Rightarrow d_2(x,y)<\varepsilon$, and symme
trically with $1\leftrightarrow 2$.

### Key fact: uniform equivalence preserves Cauchy sequences and limits

Suppose $d_1,d_2$ are uniformly equivalent.

**Cauchy sequences coincide.** Let $(x_n)$ be $d_1$-Cauchy. Given $\varepsilon>0
$, by uniform continuity of $\mathrm{id}:(X,d_1)\to(X,d_2)$ pick $\delta>0$ with
 $d_1(p,q)<\delta\Rightarrow d_2(p,q)<\varepsilon$. Since $(x_n)$ is $d_1$-Cauch
y, $\exists N$ with $d_1(x_m,x_n)<\delta$ for $m,n\ge N$; then $d_2(x_m,x_n)<\va
repsilon$. So $(x_n)$ is $d_2$-Cauchy. The converse is symmetric. Hence

$$ (x_n)\text{ is }d_1\text{-Cauchy} \iff (x_n)\text{ is }d_2\text{-Cauchy}. $$

**Convergence (with limits) coincides.** Uniform equivalence implies topological
 equivalence (same open sets), so $d_1$-convergence to $x$ is the same as $d_2$-
convergence to $x$, with the same limit.

### Completeness is invariant

$(X,d_1)$ is complete iff every $d_1$-Cauchy sequence $d_1$-converges. By the tw
o coincidences above, this holds iff every $d_2$-Cauchy sequence $d_2$-converges
, i.e. iff $(X,d_2)$ is complete. Therefore

$$ (X,d_1)\text{ complete} \iff (X,d_2)\text{ complete}. $$

So whenever two metrics are uniformly equivalent, they are **either both complet
e or both incomplete**. The situation "one complete, the other not" is impossibl
e under the hypothesis of uniform equivalence. The implication in the problem th
erefore has a conclusion that can never be true when its hypothesis is true — th
e statement is **false**.

### Explicit counterexample (uniformly equivalent, not strongly equivalent, both
 complete)

Take $X=\mathbb{R}$, $d_1(x,y)=|x-y|$, and $d_2(x,y)=\min(|x-y|,1)$.

- **Uniformly equivalent.** Clearly $d_2\le d_1$. For $\varepsilon>0$: if $\vare
psilon<1$, take $\delta=\varepsilon$; then $d_1<\delta\Rightarrow d_2<\varepsilo
n$. Conversely, for $\delta<1$, $d_2<\delta\Rightarrow |x-y|<\delta\Rightarrow d
_1<\delta$. So both directions of uniform continuity hold.
- **Not strongly equivalent.** $d_2\le 1$ everywhere, while $d_1(x,y)=|x-y|$ is
unbounded, so there is no $C>0$ with $d_1\le C\,d_2$. Hence no Lipschitz equival
ence.
- **Both complete.** $(\mathbb{R},d_1)$ is complete. By the invariance proved ab
ove (or directly: $d_2$-Cauchy $\Leftrightarrow$ $d_1$-Cauchy and limits agree),
 $(\mathbb{R},d_2)$ is also complete.

Thus here we have two metrics that are uniformly equivalent, not strongly equiva
lent, yet **both** complete — directly contradicting the claimed conclusion that
 one must be complete and the other not.

---

**Conclusion:** The statement is false. Uniform equivalence alone forces identic
al completeness status; the additional condition "not strongly equivalent" is ir
relevant to completeness.
