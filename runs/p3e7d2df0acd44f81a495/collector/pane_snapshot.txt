The answer is **No**. Here is a counterexample.

## Counterexample

Let $\mathcal{D} = N(0,1)$, so that $\mathcal{G} = N(0,1)$ (since for i.i.d. $Y_
i \sim N(0,1)$, the normalized sum $(Y_1+\cdots+Y_n)/\sqrt{n} \sim N(0,1)$ for e
very $n$).

**Construction of $X_i$.** Let $Z_1, Z_2, \ldots$ be i.i.d. $N(0,1)$, and let $W
_1, W_2, \ldots$ be independent random variables (also independent of all $Z_i$)
 with

$$P(W_i = 0) = 1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{i}}, \qquad P(W_i = 1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{i}}.$$

Set $X_i = Z_i + W_i$. The $X_i$ are independent.

**Verification that $X_i \Rightarrow \mathcal{D}$.** Since $P(W_i \neq 0) = 1/\s
qrt{i} \to 0$, we have $W_i \to 0$ in probability. Since $Z_i \sim N(0,1)$ for a
ll $i$, by Slutsky's theorem $X_i = Z_i + W_i \Rightarrow N(0,1) = \mathcal{D}$.

**Verification that the $Y_i$-CLT gives $\mathcal{G}$.** With $Y_i \sim_{\text{i
.i.d.}} N(0,1)$, the sum $\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i / \sqrt{n} \sim N(0,1) = \mathcal{G}$
 exactly for every $n$.

**The normalized sums of $X_i$ converge to a different limit.** Write

$$\frac{S_n}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i \;+\; \frac{1}{\sqrt
{n}}\sum_{i=1}^n W_i.$$

The first term satisfies $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum Z_i \sim N(0,1)$ for every $n$.

For the second term, compute:
- **Mean:** $E[W_i] = 1/\sqrt{i}$, so
$$E\!\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^n W_i\right] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i
=1}^n \frac{1}{\sqrt{i}} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot 2\sqrt{n} =
 2.$$
- **Variance:** $\operatorname{Var}(W_i) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{i}} - \frac{1}{i}$, so
$$\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^n W_i\right) = \frac{1}
{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{i}}-\frac{1}{i}\right) \;\leq\; \frac{1}{n
}\cdot 2\sqrt{n} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{n}} \;\longrightarrow\; 0.$$

By Chebyshev's inequality, $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum W_i \to 2$ in probability. Ap
plying Slutsky's theorem:

$$\frac{S_n}{\sqrt{n}} \;\Rightarrow\; N(0,1) + 2 \;=\; N(2,1).$$

Since $N(2,1) \neq N(0,1) = \mathcal{G}$, the normalized partial sums of the $X_
i$ do **not** converge weakly to $\mathcal{G}$.

## Explanation

The failure is due to the fact that **weak convergence $X_i \Rightarrow \mathcal
{D}$ does not imply convergence of moments**. The perturbations $W_i$ vanish in
the weak limit (each $X_i$ is $N(0,1)$ with probability approaching 1), yet thei
r cumulative contribution to the partial sums is of order $\sqrt{n}$ because $E[
W_i] = 1/\sqrt{i}$ and $\sum_{i=1}^n 1/\sqrt{i} \sim 2\sqrt{n}$. This shifts the
 limiting distribution by a nonzero constant, producing $N(2,1)$ instead of $N(0
,1)$.
