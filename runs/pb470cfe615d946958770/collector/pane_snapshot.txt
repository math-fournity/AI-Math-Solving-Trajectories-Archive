Yes, it is possible for $X$ to be singular. Here is a complete proof by explicit
 example.

## Proof

We construct a non-constant morphism $f: X \to Y$ between integral curves with $
Y$ non-singular and $X$ singular.

**The source curve.** Let $k$ be an algebraically closed field (e.g. $k = \mathb
b{C}$). Consider the affine plane curve
$$X = V(y^2 - x^2(x+1)) \subset \mathbb{A}^2_k.$$
The polynomial $g(x,y) = y^2 - x^2(x+1) = y^2 - x^3 - x^2$ is irreducible over $
k[x,y]$ (it is quadratic in $y$ with discriminant $x^2(x+1)$, which is not a squ
are in $k(x)$, so $g$ is irreducible in $k(x)[y]$ and hence in $k[x,y]$ by Gauss
's lemma). Therefore $X$ is an integral affine curve.

**Singularity of $X$.** Compute the partial derivatives:
$$\frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = -3x^2 - 2x, \qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial
y} = 2y.$$
A singular point satisfies $g = 0$, $\partial g/\partial x = 0$, $\partial g/\pa
rtial y = 0$. From $\partial g/\partial y = 0$ we get $y = 0$; then $g = 0$ give
s $x^2(x+1) = 0$, so $x = 0$ or $x = -1$. Check $\partial g/\partial x = 0$: at
$x = 0$, $-3\cdot 0 - 0 = 0$ ✓; at $x = -1$, $-3 + 2 = -1 \neq 0$ ✗. Hence the u
nique singular point is the origin $(0,0)$, a node (the tangent cone $y^2 - x^2
= (y-x)(y+x)$ splits into two distinct lines). So $X$ is singular.

**The morphism.** Let $Y = \mathbb{A}^1_k$, which is a non-singular integral cur
ve. Define
$$f: X \longrightarrow Y, \qquad (x, y) \longmapsto x.$$
Equivalently, $f$ corresponds to the $k$-algebra homomorphism
$$f^*: k[t] \longrightarrow k[X] = k[x,y]/(y^2 - x^3 - x^2), \qquad t \longmapst
o x.$$
This is well-defined (it factors through the quotient since the target is $k[X]$
) and non-constant: the image of $f^*$ contains $x$, which is not a scalar, so $
f^*$ is not the map to $k$. Equivalently, $f$ is non-constant because $X$ contai
ns points with distinct $x$-coordinates (e.g. $(0,0)$ and $(-1,0)$, or any point
 with $x = a$ for $a \neq 0,-1$ and $y = \pm x\sqrt{a+1}$).

**Conclusion.** We have produced a non-constant morphism $f: X \to Y$ between in
tegral curves, with $Y = \mathbb{A}^1$ non-singular, and $X$ the nodal cubic, wh
ich is singular at the origin. Therefore it **is** possible for $X$ to be singul
ar.

*Remark.* The normalization $\nu: \widetilde{X} \to X$ (with $\widetilde{X} \con
g \mathbb{A}^1$) shows that every integral curve admits a non-constant morphism
*from* a non-singular curve, but the direction asked here is the opposite: a mor
phism *from* a possibly-singular $X$ *to* a non-singular $Y$. The projection-to-
a-coordinate construction above gives such examples abundantly—any singular plan
e curve projects non-constantly onto $\mathbb{A}^1$.
