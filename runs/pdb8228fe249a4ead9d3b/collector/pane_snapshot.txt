# Maximum Number of Attracting and Neutral Orbits for a Complex Polynomial of De
gree *d*

## Answer

The maximum number is **d − 1**.

## Proof

Let $f$ be a complex polynomial of degree $d \geq 2$. We count periodic orbits (
cycles) that are **non-repelling**, i.e., attracting or neutral (parabolic, Sieg
el, or Cremer). We show this number is at most $d-1$, and that the bound is atta
ined.

### Step 1. Critical points

A polynomial $f$ of degree $d$ has exactly $d-1$ critical points in the finite p
lane, counted with multiplicity (the zeros of $f'$, which has degree $d-1$). On
the Riemann sphere, $\infty$ is a superattracting fixed point and accounts for t
he remaining $d-1$ critical points (of the total $2d-2$ for a rational map of de
gree $d$), so the *finite* critical points number exactly $d-1$.

### Step 2. Fatou's theorem (attracting and parabolic cycles)

**Fatou's theorem.** Every attracting cycle, and every parabolic cycle, attracts
 at least one critical point into its (immediate) basin of attraction.

*Sketch.* The multiplier $\lambda$ of such a cycle satisfies $|\lambda|<1$ (attr
acting) or $\lambda$ is a root of unity (parabolic). In either case the local li
nearization/Koenigs–Leau–Fatou coordinate conjugates a neighborhood of the cycle
 to $w\mapsto \lambda w$ (or $w\mapsto w+1$). A branch of the inverse near the c
ycle is a strict contraction, so by the Schwarz lemma it has a unique attracting
 fixed point inside any disk; this fixed point must be a critical point (an inve
rse branch fixing a regular point would be the identity). Hence each such cycle
consumes at least one critical point. $\square$

Consequently, the number of attracting plus parabolic cycles is at most $d-1$.

### Step 3. Fatou–Shishikura inequality (all non-repelling cycles)

To include **irrationally indifferent** (Siegel and Cremer) cycles, we invoke:

**Fatou–Shishikura inequality (Shishikura, 1987).** For a polynomial of degree $
d\geq 2$, the total number of non-repelling periodic cycles is at most the numbe
r of finite critical points, i.e., at most $d-1$.

*Key idea of the proof.* One constructs a **puzzle/annulus argument** (or equiva
lently uses the **Fatou–Shishikura decomposition** of the Fatou set). For each n
on-repelling cycle $\mathcal{O}$, one builds a system of annuli (fundamental ann
uli around the cycle, or Siegel-disk annuli) with positive moduli. The Grötzsch
inequality forces the sum of these moduli to be bounded by the total modulus ava
ilable, which is controlled by the critical points that are "consumed" by the cy
cle. Concretely:

- For an attracting/parabolic cycle, Step 2 supplies a critical point in its bas
in.
- For a Siegel cycle, the boundary of the Siegel disk contains a recurrent criti
cal point (a theorem of Douady–Sullivan type: a Siegel disk boundary of a polyno
mial contains a critical point).
- For a Cremer cycle, a more delicate transverse-structure argument (Shishikura'
s original) shows it also forces a critical point to be associated.

Each non-repelling cycle is paired with a **distinct** critical point (distinct
because the immediate basins/Siegel boundaries/Cremer neighborhoods of different
 cycles are disjoint open sets, and a critical point lies in at most one). Since
 there are only $d-1$ critical points, the number of non-repelling cycles is at
most $d-1$. $\square$

### Step 4. The bound is sharp

The bound $d-1$ is attained. Consider a polynomial whose $d-1$ critical points a
ll lie in attracting basins, with each critical point belonging to a **distinct*
* attracting cycle. Concretely, one can perturb $f(z)=z^{d}$ (which has a single
 superattracting fixed point at $0$ and critical point $0$ of multiplicity $d-1$
) so as to split the $d-1$ critical multiplicities into $d-1$ simple critical po
ints, each landing in its own attracting fixed point. For example, a small pertu
rbation of the form

$$f(z) = z^{d} - \varepsilon\, z$$

for suitable small $\varepsilon$ produces $d-1$ simple critical points near $0$,
 and each critical point is attracted to a distinct attracting fixed point (the
$d-1$ small attracting fixed points bifurcating from the superattracting fixed p
oint at $0$). This yields exactly $d-1$ attracting cycles, attaining the upper b
ound.

(For $d=2$, the quadratic family $z\mapsto z^{2}+c$ has at most $1$ non-repellin
g cycle, attained e.g. by $c=0$, the superattracting fixed point — consistent wi
th $d-1=1$.)

### Conclusion

The maximum number of attracting and neutral (non-repelling) periodic orbits of
a complex polynomial of degree $d\geq 2$ is

$$\boxed{d-1}.$$
