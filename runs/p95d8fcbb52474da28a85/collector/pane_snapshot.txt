## Answer

**No.** The complements need not be homeomorphic. Here is a counterexample.

---

### Counterexample

Let $X = [0,1]$ with the usual (subspace) topology. Define

$$U = (0,\tfrac{1}{3}) \cup (\tfrac{2}{3},1), \qquad V = (0,\tfrac{1}{2}) \cup (
\tfrac{1}{2},1).$$

**Claim 1: $U \cong V$.**

Both $U$ and $V$ are disjoint unions of two open intervals. Every nonempty open
interval is homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}$, so

$$U \cong (0,\tfrac{1}{3}) \sqcup (\tfrac{2}{3},1) \cong \mathbb{R} \sqcup \math
bb{R}, \qquad V \cong (0,\tfrac{1}{2}) \sqcup (\tfrac{1}{2},1) \cong \mathbb{R}
\sqcup \mathbb{R}.$$

Hence $U$ and $V$ are homeomorphic (both are the disjoint union of two copies of
 $\mathbb{R}$, and a homeomorphism is obtained by mapping each component of $U$
homeomorphically onto a component of $V$).

**Claim 2: $X \setminus U \not\cong X \setminus V$.**

Compute the complements:

$$X \setminus U = [0,1] \setminus \bigl((0,\tfrac{1}{3})\cup(\tfrac{2}{3},1)\big
r) = \{0\} \cup [\tfrac{1}{3},\tfrac{2}{3}] \cup \{1\},$$

$$X \setminus V = [0,1] \setminus \bigl((0,\tfrac{1}{2})\cup(\tfrac{1}{2},1)\big
r) = \{0\} \cup \{\tfrac{1}{2}\} \cup \{1\} = \{0,\tfrac{1}{2},1\}.$$

Now examine the connected components:

- $X \setminus U$ has three connected components: the singleton $\{0\}$, the **c
losed interval** $[\tfrac{1}{3},\tfrac{2}{3}]$ (which is connected and contains
uncountably many points), and the singleton $\{1\}$.
- $X \setminus V$ has three connected components, all of which are singletons: $
\{0\}$, $\{\tfrac{1}{2}\}$, $\{1\}$.

A homeomorphism preserves the structure of connected components: in particular,
it maps connected components bijectively onto connected components, and a compon
ent containing more than one point must be sent to a component containing more t
han one point (equivalently, singletons are preserved as singletons, and non-deg
enerate connected components are preserved as non-degenerate connected component
s).

But $X \setminus U$ has a connected component $[\tfrac{1}{3},\tfrac{2}{3}]$ that
 is a non-degenerate continuum (a closed interval, uncountable and connected), w
hereas every connected component of $X \setminus V$ is a single point. Therefore
 no homeomorphism $X \setminus U \to X \setminus V$ can exist.

---

### Conclusion

We have exhibited a topological space $X = [0,1]$ and two homeomorphic subspaces
 $U, V \subseteq X$ such that $X \setminus U$ and $X \setminus V$ are **not** ho
meomorphic. Thus the statement is **false** in general: homeomorphism of subspac
es does not imply homeomorphism of their complements, because a homeomorphism $U
 \cong V$ only records the internal topology of the subspaces and carries no inf
ormation about how they are embedded in $X$.
