# Solution

We consider the function
$$f(x) = \begin{cases} e^{-1/x^2}, & x \neq 0, \\ 0, & x = 0. \end{cases}$$

This is the standard smooth extension of $e^{-1/x^2}$ to the origin (the only co
ntinuous extension, since $\lim_{x\to 0} e^{-1/x^2} = 0$).

## Step 1. All derivatives at the origin vanish

**Claim.** $f^{(n)}(0) = 0$ for every $n \geq 0$.

We prove by induction that for every $n \geq 0$ there exist polynomials $P_n$ su
ch that
$$f^{(n)}(x) = P_n(1/x)\, e^{-1/x^2}, \qquad x \neq 0,$$
and $f^{(n)}(0) = 0$.

*Base case.* $f^{(0)}(x) = e^{-1/x^2} = P_0(1/x)\,e^{-1/x^2}$ with $P_0(t)=1$, a
nd $f(0)=0$. ✓

*Inductive step.* Suppose $f^{(n)}(x) = P_n(1/x)\,e^{-1/x^2}$ for $x\neq 0$ with
 $P_n$ a polynomial. Differentiating (chain rule, writing $u=1/x$):
$$f^{(n+1)}(x) = \left[P_n'(u)\cdot(-u^2) + P_n(u)\cdot 2u^3\right] e^{-1/x^2} =
 Q(u)\,e^{-1/x^2},$$
where $Q(u) = -u^2 P_n'(u) + 2u^3 P_n(u)$ is again a polynomial in $u=1/x$. So t
he form is preserved.

To show $f^{(n+1)}(0)=0$: by the inductive definition,
$$f^{(n+1)}(0) = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{f^{(n)}(h) - f^{(n)}(0)}{h} = \lim_{h\to 0}
 \frac{P_n(1/h)\,e^{-1/h^2}}{h}.$$

Setting $t = 1/|h| \to \infty$, the magnitude is bounded by $C\, t^{d+1} e^{-t^2
}$ for some constants (where $d = \deg P_n$), and since $t^k e^{-t^2}\to 0$ for
every $k$, we get $f^{(n+1)}(0)=0$. ✓

## Step 2. The Taylor series is identically zero

Since $f^{(n)}(0)=0$ for all $n\geq 0$, the Taylor series of $f$ about $0$ is
$$T(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}\,x^n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} 0
= 0.$$

This series converges for **every** $x\in\mathbb{R}$ (trivially, to $0$).

## Step 3. Where does the Taylor series equal the function?

We need $T(x) = f(x)$, i.e.,
$$0 = f(x).$$

- For $x = 0$: $f(0) = 0 = T(0)$. ✓
- For $x \neq 0$: $f(x) = e^{-1/x^2} > 0$, while $T(x) = 0$. ✗

Therefore $T(x) = f(x)$ **only at** $x = 0$.

## Conclusion

The Taylor series of $e^{-1/x^2}$ about $0$ converges for all real $x$, but it c
onverges **to the function** $e^{-1/x^2}$ only at the single point

$$\boxed{x = 0}.$$

This is the canonical example of a $C^\infty$ function that is **not analytic**
at the origin: its Taylor series exists and converges everywhere, yet fails to r
eproduce the function at every point other than the center of expansion.
