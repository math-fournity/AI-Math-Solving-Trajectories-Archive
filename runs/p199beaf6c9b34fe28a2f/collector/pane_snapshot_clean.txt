**Answer: Yes.**

**Key observation:** $L^{10^{100}}$ is finite.

The sublanguage $L^{10^{100}}$ consists of all formulas of $L$ with at most $10^
{100}$ symbols. Since the alphabet of $L$ is finite and $10^{100}$ is a fixed fi
nite constant, the set of all strings of length $\leq 10^{100}$ over this alphab
et is finite. Therefore $L^{10^{100}}$ is a **finite** set of formulas. In parti
cular, the set of *sentences* (closed formulas) belonging to $L^{10^{100}}$ is f
inite.

**Definition of the target set.** Let
$$S = \{\,\varphi \in L^{10^{100}} : \varphi \text{ is a sentence and } \varphi
\text{ is true in the standard model } \mathbb{N}\,\}.$$
Since $S \subseteq L^{10^{100}}$ and $L^{10^{100}}$ is finite, $S$ is a **finite
** set.

**Every finite set is recursively enumerable.** For any finite set of strings $S
 = \{s_1, s_2, \dots, s_k\}$, there exists a Turing machine $M$ that, on empty i
nput, outputs $s_1, s_2, \dots, s_k$ in sequence (and then either halts or idles
 forever without further output). Such a machine is constructed by hard-coding t
he finite list into its transition table.

**Construction of $M$.** Take $M$ to be the Turing machine that hard-codes the (
finite) list of all elements of $S$ and prints them one after another on its out
put tape.

**Verification.**
- *Every true sentence is output:* If $\varphi$ is a true sentence of $L^{10^{10
0}}$, then $\varphi \in S$ by definition, and $M$ prints every element of $S$.
- *No false sentence is output:* $M$ prints only elements of $S$, and every elem
ent of $S$ is a true sentence by definition. Hence $M$ never outputs a false sen
tence of $L^{10^{100}}$.

**Remark on why the bound matters.** Tarski's undefinability theorem tells us th
at truth for the *full* language $L$ of arithmetic is not arithmetically definab
le, and there is no single computable procedure that decides or enumerates truth
 for all formulas of $L$ (the set of true sentences of $L$ is not r.e.). However
, the question restricts attention to the **bounded** fragment $L^{10^{100}}$, w
hich is finite. Finiteness collapses the computability-theoretic obstruction: an
y finite set is trivially recursively enumerable, regardless of whether its defi
ning property is computable in general. The non-computability of arithmetic trut
h is a statement about *uniform* decision across an *infinite* domain; it poses
no barrier to the existence of an enumerator for a single fixed finite fragment.

Therefore, such a Turing machine $M$ exists.
