## Solution

**The answer to the Bounded Burnside problem is NO: $G$ is not necessarily finit
e.**

The statement on the right-hand side of the biconditional,

$$\forall\, G\ \text{f.g.},\ \exists\, n>0,\ (\forall g\in G,\ g^n=1)\ \Rightarr
ow\ G\ \text{finite},$$

is **false**. Hence the correct answer is `answer(false)`, and the biconditional
 `answer(false) ↔ (false statement)` holds (both sides are false, so the equival
ence is true).

### Counterexample: the free Burnside groups $B(m,n)$

**Definition.** For integers $m\ge 1$ and $n\ge 1$, the *free Burnside group* $B
(m,n)$ is the quotient of the free group $F_m$ on $m$ generators by its normal s
ubgroup generated by all $n$-th powers:

$$B(m,n) = F_m \,/\, \langle\!\langle g^n : g\in F_m \rangle\!\rangle.$$

By construction $B(m,n)$ is generated by $m$ elements and has exponent dividing
$n$ (every element $g$ satisfies $g^n = 1$). The Bounded Burnside problem is the
refore equivalent to:

> **Is $B(m,n)$ finite for all $m\ge 2$ and $n\ge 1$?**

### The negative resolution

**Theorem (Novikov–Adian, 1968).** *For every odd $n \ge 665$ and every $m\ge 2$
, the free Burnside group $B(m,n)$ is infinite.*

This was the landmark result that resolved the Bounded Burnside problem in the n
egative. Novikov and Adian developed the theory of *periodic products* and an in
tricate combinatorial/geometric analysis of van Kampen diagrams over the Burnsid
e presentation to prove that the group is infinite. Adian later improved the low
er bound from $n\ge 4381$ (the original published bound) down to odd $n\ge 665$.

**Theorem (Olshanskii, 1982).** *For every sufficiently large prime $p$, there e
xists an infinite finitely generated group in which every proper subgroup is cyc
lic of order $p$. In particular this group has exponent $p$.*

These are the celebrated *Tarski monsters*: infinite, 2-generated, and every non
trivial element has order $p$. They provide especially striking counterexamples.

**Theorem (Ivanov, 1994; Lysenok, 1996).** *For all sufficiently large $n$ (incl
uding even $n$), and all $m\ge 2$, the free Burnside group $B(m,n)$ is infinite.
*

This extended the Novikov–Adian result to even exponents, settling the problem f
or all large $n$.

### Explicit counterexample

Take $m=2$ and any odd $n\ge 665$ (e.g., $n=665$). Then:

1. **Finitely generated:** $B(2, n)$ is generated by 2 elements. ✓
2. **Bounded exponent:** Every $g\in B(2,n)$ satisfies $g^n = 1$ by construction
. ✓
3. **Infinite:** By the Novov–Adian theorem, $B(2,665)$ is infinite. ✓

Therefore $G = B(2, 665)$ is a finitely generated group of bounded exponent that
 is **infinite**, contradicting the universal statement. The statement is false.

### Small cases (context, not needed for the counterexample)

For completeness: $B(m,2)$ is finite (it is a finite direct product of copies of
 $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, hence an elementary abelian 2-group, of order $2^m$).
 $B(m,3)$ is finite (Burnside himself, order $3^{m+\binom{m}{2}+\binom{m}{3}}$).
 $B(m,4)$ and $B(m,6)$ are finite (Sanov, Hall). But for large $n$, infinitude h
olds, which is all that is needed.

### Conclusion

The Bounded Burnside problem has a **negative** answer. There exist infinite fin
itely generated groups of finite exponent (e.g., $B(2, 665)$, Tarski monsters).
The universal claim

$$\forall\, G\ \text{f.g.},\ \exists\, n>0,\ (\forall g,\ g^n=1)\Rightarrow G\ \
text{finite}$$

is **false**, so the correct value is `answer(false)`, and the biconditional `an
swer(false) ↔ (the above false statement)` is a true proposition (false ↔ false)
.
