# Proof

**Claim:** Yes, there exists an uncountable chain of subsets of $\mathbb{N}$, to
tally ordered by inclusion.

**Construction.**

Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable, fix a bijection $f \colon \mathbb{N} \to \mathb
b{Q}$. For each real number $r \in \mathbb{R}$, define

$$A_r \;=\; \{\, n \in \mathbb{N} : f(n) < r \,\} \;\subseteq\; \mathbb{N}.$$

We claim the family $\mathcal{C} = \{A_r : r \in \mathbb{R}\}$ is an uncountable
 chain under inclusion.

**Totally ordered.** Let $r, s \in \mathbb{R}$ with $r \le s$. Then for every $n
 \in \mathbb{N}$,

$$n \in A_r \;\Longrightarrow\; f(n) < r \le s \;\Longrightarrow\; n \in A_s,$$

so $A_r \subseteq A_s$. Hence any two members of $\mathcal{C}$ are comparable by
 inclusion.

**Uncountable.** The map $r \mapsto A_r$ is injective. Indeed, if $r < s$, then
by density of $\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{R}$ there exists $q \in \mathbb{Q}$ with
$r < q < s$. Let $n = f^{-1}(q)$. Then $n \in A_s$ (since $q < s$) but $n \notin
 A_r$ (since $q > r$). Thus $A_r \subsetneq A_s$, so $A_r \ne A_s$. Therefore $|
\mathcal{C}| = |\mathbb{R}|$, which is uncountable.

**Conclusion.** $\mathcal{C}$ is an uncountable family of subsets of $\mathbb{N}
$ that is totally ordered by inclusion. $\blacksquare$
