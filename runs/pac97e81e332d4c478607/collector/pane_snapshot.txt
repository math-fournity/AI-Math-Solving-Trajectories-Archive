## Solution

**Problem.** Evaluate $\displaystyle \int_{|z|=1} \frac{\sin(z^2)}{(\sin z)^2}\,
dz$.

**Step 1: Locate the singularities.** The integrand $f(z)=\dfrac{\sin(z^2)}{(\si
n z)^2}$ is singular only where $\sin z = 0$, i.e. at $z=n\pi$, $n\in\mathbb{Z}$
. Within the disk $|z|\le 1$, the only such point is $z=0$ (since $\pi>1$).

**Step 2: Classify the singularity at $z=0$.** Using the Taylor expansions
$$\sin z = z - \tfrac{z^3}{6} + \tfrac{z^5}{120}-\cdots,\qquad \sin(z^2)=z^2-\tf
rac{z^6}{6}+\cdots,$$
we get
$$(\sin z)^2 = z^2\Big(1-\tfrac{z^2}{3}+\tfrac{2z^4}{45}-\cdots\Big).$$
Hence
$$f(z)=\frac{z^2\big(1-\tfrac{z^4}{6}+\cdots\big)}{z^2\big(1-\tfrac{z^2}{3}+\tfr
ac{2z^4}{45}-\cdots\big)}=\frac{1-\tfrac{z^4}{6}+\cdots}{1-\tfrac{z^2}{3}+\tfrac
{2z^4}{45}-\cdots}.$$
Expanding the reciprocal,
$$\frac{1}{1-\tfrac{z^2}{3}+\cdots}=1+\tfrac{z^2}{3}+\cdots,$$
so
$$f(z)=1+\tfrac{z^2}{3}-\tfrac{z^4}{10}+\cdots,$$
a convergent Taylor series in a neighborhood of $0$. Thus $z=0$ is a **removable
** singularity; after removal, $f$ is analytic at $0$ (with $f(0)=1$).

**Step 3: Apply Cauchy's theorem.** Since the only candidate singularity inside
$|z|=1$ is removable, $f(z)$ is analytic on and inside the contour $|z|=1$. By C
auchy's integral theorem,

$$\int_{|z|=1}\frac{\sin(z^2)}{(\sin z)^2}\,dz = 0.$$
