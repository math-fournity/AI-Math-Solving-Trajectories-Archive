## Solution

Let $f_n(x) = \dfrac{\sqrt{nx}}{1+n^{2}x}$ and $S(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_n(x)$.

### Pointwise convergence

- **At $x=0$:** every term is $0$, so $S(0)=0$.
- **At $x>0$:** $f_n(x)=\dfrac{\sqrt{x}\,\sqrt{n}}{1+n^{2}x}\sim\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{
x}}\cdot\dfrac{1}{n^{3/2}}$, and $\sum n^{-3/2}$ converges. Hence $S(x)$ converg
es for every $x>0$.

So the series converges pointwise on all of $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$.

### Uniform convergence on $[\delta,+\infty)$ for every $\delta>0$

For $x\geq\delta>0$,
$$
f_n(x)=\frac{\sqrt{n}\sqrt{x}}{1+n^{2}x}\leq\frac{\sqrt{n}\sqrt{x}}{n^{2}x}=\fra
c{1}{\sqrt{x}\,n^{3/2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}.
$$
Since $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-3/2}<\infty$, the **Weierstrass $M$-test** gives u
niform (and absolute) convergence on $[\delta,+\infty)$ for every $\delta>0$. In
 particular also on every $[\delta,M]\subset(0,\infty)$.

### Non-uniform convergence near $0$

**Step 1. The sum blows up as $x\to0^{+}$.** For $x>0$, substitute $n=t/\sqrt{x}
$ (so $f_n(x)=\dfrac{x^{1/4}\,t^{1/2}}{1+t^{2}}$) and approximate the sum by an
integral:
$$
S(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sqrt{nx}}{1+n^{2}x}\;\approx\;\xint{0}{\infty}\fr
ac{x^{1/4}\,t^{1/2}}{1+t^{2}}\,\frac{dt}{\sqrt{x}}
\;=\;x^{-1/4}\xint{0}{\infty}\frac{t^{1/2}}{1+t^{2}}\,dt.
$$
The integral is finite and positive: with $t=u^{2}$,
$$
\xint{0}{\infty}\frac{t^{1/2}}{1+t^{2}}\,dt=2\xint{0}{\infty}\frac{u^{2}}{1+u^{4
}}\,du=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}>0.
$$
A standard Riemann-sum argument (the function $g(t)=\frac{t^{1/2}}{1+t^{2}}$ is
integrable on $[0,\infty)$) makes this rigorous, giving
$$
S(x)\sim\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\,x^{-1/4}\xrightarrow[x\to0^{+}]{}+\infty.
\tag{1}
$$

**Step 2. Not uniform on $[0,b]$ (any $b>0$).** Each $f_n$ is continuous on $[0,
b]$. If the series converged uniformly on $[0,b]$, the sum $S$ would be continuo
us on $[0,b]$, implying $\lim_{x\to0^{+}}S(x)=S(0)=0$. This contradicts (1). Hen
ce **no uniform convergence on $[0,b]$** (nor on $[0,+\infty)$).

**Step 3. Not uniform on $(0,b]$ or $(0,+\infty)$.** It suffices to show $\sup_{
x\in(0,\infty)}|R_N(x)|\not\to0$, where $R_N(x)=\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}f_n(x)$. Ta
ke $x_N=1/N^{2}$. Then
$$
R_N(x_N)=\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{N\sqrt{n}}{N^{2}+n^{2}}.
$$
Approximating by an integral with $n=Nt$:
$$
R_N(x_N)\approx\xint{1}{\infty}\frac{N\sqrt{Nt}}{N^{2}(1+t^{2})}\,N\,dt
=\sqrt{N}\xint{1}{\infty}\frac{t^{1/2}}{1+t^{2}}\,dt\;\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}
\;+\infty.
$$
(The integral $\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{1/2}}{1+t^{2}}dt$ is a positive constan
t.) Thus $\sup_{x>0}|R_N(x)|\geq R_N(x_N)\to+\infty$, so the series does **not**
 converge uniformly on $(0,b]$ or on $(0,+\infty)$.

### Conclusion

The series $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sqrt{nx}}{1+n^{2}x}$ converge
s uniformly exactly on the intervals **bounded away from $0$**:

$$
\boxed{\text{Uniform convergence on }[\delta,+\infty)\text{ for every }\delta>0;
\quad\text{no uniform convergence on any interval with }0\text{ as an accumulati
on point}.}
$$

Equivalently: the intervals of uniform convergence are precisely those closed (o
r open) subintervals of $(0,+\infty)$ whose closure stays a positive distance aw
ay from $0$—most standardly, $[\delta,+\infty)$ for arbitrary $\delta>0$.
