## Proof

We seek the stationary point of

$$J[y]=\int_{0}^{1}\left(x^{2}y'^{2}+2y^{2}\right)dx,\qquad y(0)=0,\; y(1)=2.$$

### Step 1. Euler–Lagrange equation

With $F(x,y,y')=x^{2}y'^{2}+2y^{2}$,

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}=4y,\qquad \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}=2x^{2}y
',\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}\right)=2x^{2}y''+4xy
'.$$

The Euler–Lagrange equation $\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}-\frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\
frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}\right)=0$ gives

$$4y-2x^{2}y''-4xy'=0\;\Longrightarrow\; x^{2}y''+2xy'-2y=0.$$

### Step 2. Solve the Cauchy–Euler equation

This is a Cauchy–Euler equation. Substituting $y=x^{m}$:

$$m(m-1)+2m-2=0\;\Longrightarrow\; m^{2}+m-2=0\;\Longrightarrow\;(m+2)(m-1)=0,$$

so $m=1$ or $m=-2$. The general solution is

$$y(x)=C_{1}x+C_{2}x^{-2}.$$

### Step 3. Apply boundary conditions

- $y(0)=0$: as $x\to 0^{+}$, the term $C_{2}x^{-2}\to\infty$ unless $C_{2}=0$. H
ence $C_{2}=0$.
- $y(1)=2$: $C_{1}\cdot 1=2$, so $C_{1}=2$.

Therefore the extremal is

$$\boxed{y(x)=2x}.$$

### Step 4. Verify it is a minimum (stationary point is a minimizer)

The integrand $F=x^{2}y'^{2}+2y^{2}$ is nonnegative, and $F=0$ only when $y'=0$
and $y=0$ simultaneously, which is incompatible with $y(1)=2$. Hence $J[y]>0$ fo
r every admissible $y$. Moreover, $F$ is convex (in fact jointly convex) in $(y,
y')$ since the Hessian $\begin{pmatrix}4 & 0\\ 0 & 2x^{2}\end{pmatrix}$ is posit
ive semidefinite on $[0,1]$. Thus the functional $J$ is convex, and the unique s
tationary point $y=2x$ is the global minimizer.

For completeness, the value at the extremal:

$$J[2x]=\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(x^{2}\cdot 4+2\cdot 4x^{2}\bigr)dx=\int_{0}^{1}12x^{2}
\,dx=4.$$

### Conclusion

The stationary point of the functional, subject to $y(0)=0$ and $y(1)=2$, is

$$y(x)=2x.$$

### PROOF COMPLETE
