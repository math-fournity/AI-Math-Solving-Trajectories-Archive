## Proof

We evaluate
$$I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\sin x}{x - i}\,dx.$$

**Step 1: Decompose $\sin x$.** Using $\sin x = \dfrac{e^{ix} - e^{-ix}}{2i}$,

$$I = \frac{1}{2i}\underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{e^{ix}}{x-i}\,dx}_{I
_1} \;-\; \frac{1}{2i}\underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-ix}}{x-i}\,d
x}_{I_2}.$$

**Step 2: Evaluate $I_1$ by closing in the upper half-plane.**

For $z = x + iy$ with $y > 0$, $|e^{iz}| = e^{-y} \to 0$ as $y \to \infty$, so w
e close the contour in the upper half-plane. The integrand $\dfrac{e^{iz}}{z - i
}$ has a simple pole at $z = i$ (which lies in the upper half-plane) with residu
e
$$\operatorname{Res}_{z=i} \frac{e^{iz}}{z-i} = e^{i\cdot i} = e^{-1} = \frac{1}
{e}.$$

By the residue theorem (Jordan's lemma ensures the arc contribution vanishes),
$$I_1 = 2\pi i \cdot \frac{1}{e} = \frac{2\pi i}{e}.$$

**Step 3: Evaluate $I_2$ by closing in the lower half-plane.**

For $z = x + iy$ with $y < 0$, $|e^{-iz}| = e^{y} \to 0$ as $y \to -\infty$, so
we close in the lower half-plane. The only pole $z = i$ lies in the **upper** ha
lf-plane, so it is **not** enclosed by the lower-half-plane contour. Hence
$$I_2 = 0.$$

**Step 4: Combine.**

$$I = \frac{1}{2i}\cdot\frac{2\pi i}{e} - \frac{1}{2i}\cdot 0 = \frac{\pi}{e}.$$

Therefore,

$$\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\sin x}{x-i}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{e}}.$$
