## Step 1. Integral representation of $n!$

By the standard Gamma-function identity (valid for $\Re(s)>0$),

$$
n! = \Gamma(n+1) = \int_0^\infty t^n e^{-t}\,dt.
$$

We will extract the large-$n$ asymptotics of this integral by the saddle point (
steepest-descent) method.

---

## Step 2. Rewrite in exponential form and locate the saddle point

Write the integrand as $\exp\!\big(\phi_n(t)\big)$ with

$$
\phi_n(t) = n\log t - t.
$$

The saddle point $t_0$ is where $\phi_n'(t_0)=0$:

$$
\phi_n'(t) = \frac{n}{t}-1 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_0 = n.
$$

The second derivative is

$$
\phi_n''(t) = -\frac{n}{t^2}, \qquad \phi_n''(t_0) = -\frac{1}{n}.
$$

So $t_0=n$ is a **non-degenerate maximum** of $\phi_n$ along the real axis (a sa
ddle in the complex plane), and the curvature scale is $\sqrt{n}$.

---

## Step 3. Choice of contour

The original contour is the positive real axis $\{t>0\}$, which already passes t
hrough the saddle $t_0=n$. We keep this contour (it is the steepest-descent dire
ction for the real part of $\phi_n$ near $t_0$, since $\phi_n''(t_0)<0$ means th
e real axis is the direction of *decrease* of $\Re\phi_n$, i.e. the correct stee
pest-descent path through the saddle). No deformation of the contour is needed;
the real-axis contour is itself a steepest-descent contour.

---

## Step 4. Rescale around the saddle

Set $t = n + u\sqrt{n}$, so $dt = \sqrt{n}\,du$. Expanding $\phi_n$ in powers of
 $u$:

$$
\phi_n(n+u\sqrt{n}) = n\log(n+u\sqrt{n}) - (n+u\sqrt{n}).
$$

Using $\log(n+u\sqrt{n}) = \log n + \log\!\big(1+\tfrac{u}{\sqrt{n}}\big)$ and t
he Taylor series $\log(1+x)=x-\tfrac{x^2}{2}+\tfrac{x^3}{3}-\cdots$ with $x=u/\s
qrt{n}$:

$$
\phi_n(n+u\sqrt{n})
= n\log n + n\!\left(\frac{u}{\sqrt{n}}-\frac{u^2}{2n}+\frac{u^3}{3n^{3/2}}-\cdo
ts\right) - n - u\sqrt{n}.
$$

The linear terms $n\cdot\tfrac{u}{\sqrt{n}} = u\sqrt{n}$ cancel exactly with $-u
\sqrt{n}$. Collecting:

$$
\phi_n(n+u\sqrt{n}) = n\log n - n \;-\; \frac{u^2}{2} \;+\; \frac{u^3}{3\sqrt{n}
} \;-\; \frac{u^4}{4n} \;+\; \cdots
$$

The leading part is the Gaussian $-\tfrac{u^2}{2}$; the remainder is $O(n^{-1/2}
)$ for fixed $u$.

---

## Step 5. Gaussian (saddle-point) approximation

Substituting into the integral, with $t:0\to\infty$ corresponding to $u:-\sqrt{n
}\to\infty$:

$$
n! = \sqrt{n}\,e^{n\log n - n}\int_{-\sqrt{n}}^{\infty} \exp\!\left(-\frac{u^2}{
2} + \frac{u^3}{3\sqrt{n}} - \frac{u^4}{4n}+\cdots\right) du.
$$

For the **leading-order** asymptotics, extend the lower limit to $-\infty$ (the
error is exponentially small, $\sim e^{-n/2}$, since the Gaussian suppresses $|u
|\gg 1$) and keep only the quadratic term:

$$
n! \sim \sqrt{n}\,e^{n\log n - n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-u^2/2}\,du
= \sqrt{n}\,\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^n \sqrt{2\pi}.
$$

The Gaussian integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-u^2/2}\,du=\sqrt{2\pi}$ is wha
t produces the factor $\sqrt{2\pi}$.

---

## Step 6. Justifying the approximation (error control)

We must confirm that (a) extending the limit to $-\infty$ and (b) dropping the h
igher-order terms $u^3/(3\sqrt{n}),\,u^4/(4n),\ldots$ contribute only $o(1)$.

**(a) Extending the lower limit.** The omitted piece is $\int_{-\infty}^{-\sqrt{
n}} e^{-u^2/2}\,du \le e^{-n/2}$, exponentially small — negligible compared to a
ny power of $n$.

**(b) Higher-order terms.** The dominant correction comes from $\frac{u^3}{3\sqr
t{n}}$. Split the integral into $|u|\le n^{1/6}$ (central region) and $|u|>n^{1/
6}$ (tails).

- *Tails:* On $|u|>n^{1/6}$, the Gaussian $e^{-u^2/2}$ gives a factor $\le e^{-n
^{1/3}/2}$, exponentially small. So the tails contribute negligibly.
- *Central region $|u|\le n^{1/6}$:* Here $|u^3/\sqrt{n}|\le n^{1/2}/\sqrt{n}=1$
 is not yet small, so we use a tighter split. Actually take $|u|\le n^{1/4}$ won
't help either; the correct bound uses $|u|\le \delta\sqrt{n}$ with $\delta\to 0
$. On $|u|\le n^{2/5}$, $|u^3/\sqrt{n}|\le n^{6/5}/\sqrt{n}=n^{7/10}$ — that's t
oo big. The right approach: use $e^{u^3/(3\sqrt n)} = 1 + \frac{u^3}{3\sqrt n} +
 O(\frac{u^6}{n})$. The odd term $\int u^3 e^{-u^2/2}\,du=0$ by symmetry, and $\
int u^6 e^{-u^2/2}\,du = O(1)$, so the $u^3$ correction is $O(1/n)$. Similarly t
he $u^4$ term gives $\int u^4 e^{-u^2/2}\,du\cdot O(1/n)=O(1/n)$. Each higher te
rm is suppressed by an additional factor $n^{-1/2}$.

Thus the corrections are $O(1/n)$, and

$$
n! = \sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^n\left(1 + O\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\right
)\right).
$$

In particular,

$$
n! \sim \sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^n \quad (n\to\infty),
$$

which is Stirling's formula. $\blacksquare$
