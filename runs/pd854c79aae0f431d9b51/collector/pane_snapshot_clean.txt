## Solution

**Yes**, we can conclude that $a_1 = \cdots = a_n = 0$.

**Proof.** Write $c_i = e^{2\pi i \alpha_i}$ for some $\alpha_i \in \mathbb{R}$
(since $|c_i|=1$). The $c_i$ are distinct by hypothesis. Define

$$S_k = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i\, c_i^k, \qquad k = 0,1,2,\ldots$$

and assume $S_k \to 0$ as $k\to\infty$.

Fix any index $j \in \{1,\ldots,n\}$. Consider the Cesàro average

$$T_N^{(j)} := \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} S_k\,\overline{c_j}^{\,k}.$$

**Step 1: $T_N^{(j)} \to 0$.** Since $S_k \to 0$ and $|\overline{c_j}^{\,k}| = 1
$, we have $|S_k\,\overline{c_j}^{\,k}| = |S_k| \to 0$, so $S_k\,\overline{c_j}^
{\,k} \to 0$. By the Cesàro theorem (the Cesàro mean of a sequence converging to
 $0$ also converges to $0$), we get $T_N^{(j)} \to 0$.

**Step 2: $T_N^{(j)} \to a_j$.** Expanding the definition,

$$T_N^{(j)} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i\, c_i^k\,\overline{c
_j}^{\,k} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} (c_i\overline{c
_j})^k\right).$$

Set $z_i := c_i\overline{c_j} = e^{2\pi i(\alpha_i - \alpha_j)}$, which lies on
the unit circle.

- If $i = j$: $z_i = 1$, so $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} z_i^k = 1$.
- If $i \neq j$: $z_i \neq 1$ (since $c_i \neq c_j$), and

$$\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} z_i^k\right| = \left|\frac{1-z_i^N}{N(1-z_i)
}\right| \le \frac{2}{N\,|1-z_i|} \xrightarrow{N\to\infty} 0,$$

using $|z_i^N|=1$ so $|1-z_i^N|\le 2$.

Therefore,

$$T_N^{(j)} = a_j \cdot 1 + \sum_{i\neq j} a_i \cdot O(1/N) \xrightarrow{N\to\in
fty} a_j.$$

**Conclusion.** Combining Steps 1 and 2: $a_j = \lim_{N\to\infty} T_N^{(j)} = 0$
. Since $j$ was arbitrary, $a_1 = \cdots = a_n = 0$. $\blacksquare$
