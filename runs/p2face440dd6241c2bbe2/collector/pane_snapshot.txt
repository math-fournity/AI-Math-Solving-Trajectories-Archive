No. Such a function cannot exist: for a continuous self-map of $[0,1]$, having a
 dense orbit already forces surjectivity (and so does transitivity, independentl
y). Here is the proof.

---

**Theorem.** Let $T:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ be continuous. If $T$ has a dense orbit, then
 $T$ is onto. In particular, $T$ cannot be transitive (or have a dense orbit) wh
ile failing to be onto.

**Proof.**

Suppose $T$ has a dense orbit, i.e., there exists $x\in[0,1]$ such that
$$O(x):=\{T^{n}(x):n\ge 0\}$$
is dense in $[0,1]$.

**Step 1. The tail of the orbit is still dense.**

Consider the image of the orbit under $T$:
$$T\bigl(O(x)\bigr)=\{T(T^{n}(x)):n\ge 0\}=\{T^{n+1}(x):n\ge 0\}=\{T^{m}(x):m\ge
 1\}=O(x)\setminus\{x\}.$$

We claim $O(x)\setminus\{x\}$ is dense in $[0,1]$. Indeed, let $W\subseteq[0,1]$
 be any nonempty open set. Since $O(x)$ is dense, $W\cap O(x)\neq\varnothing$. I
f $W\cap O(x)$ contains a point other than $x$, we are done. If $W\cap O(x)=\{x\
}$, then $x\in W$ and $W\setminus\{x\}$ is open (difference of an open set and a
 closed singleton) and nonempty (every nonempty open subset of $[0,1]$ contains
an interval, hence infinitely many points). By density of $O(x)$, $(W\setminus\{
x\})\cap O(x)\neq\varnothing$, contradicting $W\cap O(x)=\{x\}$. Hence $W\cap\bi
gl(O(x)\setminus\{x\}\bigr)\neq\varnothing$, proving $O(x)\setminus\{x\}$ is den
se.

**Step 2. The image $T([0,1])$ contains a dense set, hence is dense.**

Since $O(x)\subseteq[0,1]$, we have
$$T\bigl(O(x)\bigr)\subseteq T([0,1]).$$
By Step 1, $T(O(x))=O(x)\setminus\{x\}$ is dense in $[0,1]$. Therefore $T([0,1])
$ contains a dense subset, so $T([0,1])$ is itself dense in $[0,1]$.

**Step 3. Density + compactness $\Rightarrow$ surjectivity.**

Since $[0,1]$ is compact and $T$ is continuous, $T([0,1])$ is compact, hence clo
sed in $[0,1]$. A closed set that is dense in $[0,1]$ must equal $[0,1]$. Theref
ore
$$T([0,1])=[0,1],$$
i.e., $T$ is onto. $\blacksquare$

---

**Remark (transitivity alone also suffices).** Even without assuming a dense orb
it, transitivity alone forces surjectivity for continuous self-maps of $[0,1]$.
If $T$ were not onto, then $T([0,1])=[a,b]\subsetneq[0,1]$, so either $a>0$ or $
b<1$. If $b<1$, take $U=(0,b/2)$ and $V=(b,1)$: both are nonempty and open, $U\c
ap V=\varnothing$, and for every $n\ge 1$, $T^{n}(U)\subseteq T^{n}([0,1])\subse
teq[0,b]$, which is disjoint from $V=(b,1)$. Thus $T^{n}(U)\cap V=\varnothing$ f
or all $n\ge 0$, contradicting transitivity. The case $a>0$ is symmetric. Hence
a transitive continuous map $[0,1]\to[0,1]$ is necessarily onto.

---

**Conclusion.** It is **not possible** for a continuous function $T:[0,1]\to[0,1
]$ to be transitive (or to have a dense orbit) without being onto. Both properti
es independently imply surjectivity.
