# Solution

**Claim:** No. Every conformal map defined on an open subset of the complex plan
e is analytic. Such maps do not exist.

## Proof

Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{C}$ be an open set and let $f: U \to \mathbb{C}$ be a c
onformal map. We use the standard definition: $f$ is conformal at $z_0 \in U$ if
 $f$ is real-differentiable at $z_0$ and its total derivative $Df(z_0): \mathbb{
R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ is a **non-singular orientation-preserving angle-preservi
ng** linear map—that is, a nonzero complex-linear map (a rotation composed with
a dilation).

**Step 1: Conformal $\Rightarrow$ complex-differentiable.**

At each $z_0 \in U$, since $f$ is conformal, $Df(z_0)$ is a nonzero complex-line
ar map. Concretely, there exist $r > 0$ and $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$ such that
$$Df(z_0)(h) = r\,e^{i\theta}\, h \quad \text{for all } h \in \mathbb{C} \cong \
mathbb{R}^2.$$
This means the real Jacobian of $f$ at $z_0$ has the form $\begin{pmatrix} a & -
b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}$ with $a^2+b^2 > 0$, which is precisely the Cauchy–Riem
ann equations holding at $z_0$ with nonvanishing derivative. Therefore $f$ is **
complex-differentiable** at $z_0$, with
$$f'(z_0) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(z_0+h)-f(z_0)}{h} = a+ib = r\,e^{i\theta} \neq
0.$$

Since $f$ is conformal at every point of $U$, $f$ is complex-differentiable at e
very point of $U$.

**Step 2: Complex-differentiable everywhere $\Rightarrow$ holomorphic $\Rightarr
ow$ analytic (Goursat's theorem).**

By the **Cauchy–Goursat theorem**, if a function is complex-differentiable at ev
ery point of an open set $U$, then it is holomorphic on $U$—that is, it is analy
tic (it admits a convergent power series expansion in a neighborhood of every po
int of $U$). The key point of Goursat's theorem is that **no assumption of conti
nuity of $f'$ is needed**; complex differentiability alone suffices. The proof p
roceeds by showing that $\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz = 0$ for every triangular path in
$U$ (via a subdivision argument), from which Morera's theorem yields holomorphic
ity, and holomorphic functions are analytic.

**Step 3: Conclusion.**

Combining Steps 1 and 2: every conformal map $f: U \to \mathbb{C}$ is complex-di
fferentiable everywhere on $U$, hence holomorphic by Goursat's theorem, hence an
alytic. Therefore $f$ is necessarily analytic.

Consequently, **there do not exist** conformal maps defined on any open subset o
f the complex plane that fail to be analytic. $\blacksquare$
