## Proof

**Setup and the Mellin transform identity.**

Since $f(z)=\sum_{n\ge 1}a_n z^n$ converges for $|z|<1$, we have $\limsup |a_n|^
{1/n}\le 1$, so $|a_n|$ grows subexponentially. Hence $D(s)=\sum a_n n^{-s}$ con
verges absolutely for $\operatorname{Re}(s)>\sigma_0$ for some $\sigma_0$.

For $\operatorname{Re}(s)$ sufficiently large, using $\Gamma(s)=\int_0^\infty x^
{s-1}e^{-x}\,dx$ and the substitution $x\mapsto nx$:

$$n^{-s}\Gamma(s)=\int_0^\infty x^{s-1}e^{-nx}\,dx.$$

Summing and interchanging (justified by absolute convergence in this region):

$$D(s)\Gamma(s)=\int_0^\infty x^{s-1}\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n e^{-nx}\,dx=\int_0^\i
nfty x^{s-1}f(e^{-x})\,dx,\tag{$\star$}$$

since $e^{-x}\in(0,1)$ for $x>0$ and $f(e^{-x})=\sum a_n e^{-nx}$.

**The tail integral is entire.** For any $\varrho>0$, the integral

$$T(s)=\int_\varrho^\infty x^{s-1}f(e^{-x})\,dx$$

is an entire function of $s$. Indeed, as $x\to\infty$, $e^{-x}\to 0$ and $f(e^{-
x})\sim a_1 e^{-x}$, so $|f(e^{-x})|\le C e^{-x/2}$ for large $x$. The exponenti
al decay dominates any polynomial growth of $|x^{s-1}|$, giving uniform converge
nce on compact subsets of the $s$-plane. Hence $T(s)$ is entire.

**Splitting the integral.** We write

$$D(s)\Gamma(s)=\int_0^\varrho x^{s-1}f(e^{-x})\,dx+T(s).$$

The analysis reduces to the near-origin integral $I(s)=\int_0^\varrho x^{s-1}f(e
^{-x})\,dx$, i.e., the behavior of $g(x):=f(e^{-x})$ near $x=0$ (equivalently, $
f(z)$ near $z=1$).

---

### Case 1: $z=1$ is a regular point of $f$.

Then $f$ is analytic at $z=1$, so $g(x)=f(e^{-x})$ is analytic at $x=0$. Choose
$\varrho$ small enough that $g(x)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty b_k x^k$ converges on $[0,\v
arrho]$. For $\operatorname{Re}(s)>0$:

$$I(s)=\int_0^\varrho x^{s-1}\sum_{k=0}^\infty b_k x^k\,dx=\sum_{k=0}^\infty b_k
\frac{\varrho^{s+k}}{s+k}.\tag{1}$$

The right-hand side of (1) defines a meromorphic function of $s$ with **simple p
oles** at $s=0,-1,-2,\dots$ (residue at $s=-k$ is $b_k$). Thus $D(s)\Gamma(s)$ i
s meromorphic with simple poles at $s=0,-1,-2,\dots$.

But $\Gamma(s)$ has **simple poles** at exactly $s=0,-1,-2,\dots$ with residues
$(-1)^k/k!$ at $s=-k$ (all nonzero). Therefore

$$D(s)=\frac{D(s)\Gamma(s)}{\Gamma(s)}$$

has all its potential poles at $s=0,-1,-2,\dots$ canceled by the corresponding p
oles of $\Gamma(s)$. Hence **$D(s)$ is entire**. $\checkmark$

---

### Case 2: $z=1$ is a pole of order $h$ of $f$.

Near $z=1$:

$$f(z)=\frac{c_h}{(z-1)^h}+\frac{c_{h-1}}{(z-1)^{h-1}}+\cdots+\frac{c_1}{z-1}+\p
hi(z),\qquad c_h\ne 0,$$

where $\phi$ is analytic at $z=1$. Substituting $z=e^{-x}$ and using $e^{-x}-1=-
x+\frac{x^2}{2}-\cdots=-x(1-\frac{x}{2}+\cdots)$:

$$\frac{1}{(e^{-x}-1)^j}=\frac{(-1)^j}{x^j}\left(1-\frac{x}{2}+\cdots\right)^{-j
}=\frac{(-1)^j}{x^j}+\frac{(-1)^j\cdot j/2}{x^{j-1}}+\cdots$$

Collecting all terms, $g(x)=f(e^{-x})$ has a **pole of order $h$ at $x=0$**:

$$g(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{h}\frac{d_j}{x^j}+\psi(x),\qquad d_h=(-1)^h c_h\ne 0,$$

where $\psi(x)$ is analytic at $x=0$ (combining the analytic parts of the Lauren
t expansion of the principal part with $\phi(e^{-x})$).

For $\operatorname{Re}(s)>h$:

$$I(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{h}d_j\int_0^\varrho x^{s-j-1}\,dx+\int_0^\varrho x^{s-1}\psi(
x)\,dx=\sum_{j=1}^{h}\frac{d_j\,\varrho^{s-j}}{s-j}+\sum_{k=0}^\infty e_k\frac{\
varrho^{s+k}}{s+k},\tag{2}$$

where $\psi(x)=\sum e_k x^k$. The right-hand side of (2) is meromorphic with:
- **Simple poles at $s=1,2,\dots,h$** from the first sum (residue at $s=j$ is $d
_j$),
- **Simple poles at $s=0,-1,-2,\dots$** from the second sum.

Together with the entire tail $T(s)$, we conclude $D(s)\Gamma(s)$ is meromorphic
 with simple poles at $s=1,2,\dots,h,0,-1,-2,\dots$.

Now divide by $\Gamma(s)$, which has simple poles at $s=0,-1,-2,\dots$ (all with
 nonzero residues):
- **At $s=0,-1,-2,\dots$:** Both $D(s)\Gamma(s)$ and $\Gamma(s)$ have simple pol
es, so they cancel. $D(s)$ is analytic at these points.
- **At $s=1,2,\dots,h$:** $\Gamma(s)$ is analytic and nonzero (at positive integ
ers, $\Gamma(j)=(j-1)!\ne 0$). So $D(s)$ inherits **simple poles** at those $s=j
$ where $d_j\ne 0$.

Therefore **$D(s)$ is meromorphic with possible simple poles only at $s=1,2,\dot
s,h$**. $\checkmark$

**The pole at $s=h$ must occur.** The residue of $D(s)\Gamma(s)$ at $s=h$ is $d_
h=(-1)^h c_h\ne 0$. Since $\Gamma(h)=(h-1)!\ne 0$, the residue of $D(s)$ at $s=h
$ is

$$\operatorname{Res}_{s=h}D(s)=\frac{d_h}{\Gamma(h)}=\frac{(-1)^h c_h}{(h-1)!}\n
e 0.$$

Hence $s=h$ is genuinely a pole of $D(s)$. $\checkmark$

For $1\le j<h$, the coefficient $d_j$ may vanish depending on the specific funct
ion, so the poles at $s=1,\dots,h-1$ are possible but not guaranteed.

---

**Summary.** The Mellin transform identity $(\star)$ expresses $D(s)\Gamma(s)$ a
s an integral whose singularity structure is determined by the behavior of $f(e^
{-x})$ near $x=0$, i.e., by the nature of $z=1$ as a singularity of $f$. If $z=1
$ is regular, $f(e^{-x})$ is analytic at $x=0$, and the poles of $D(s)\Gamma(s)$
 at nonpositive integers are exactly canceled by those of $\Gamma(s)$, making $D
(s)$ entire. If $z=1$ is a pole of order $h$, $f(e^{-x})$ has a pole of order $h
$ at $x=0$, producing additional simple poles of $D(s)\Gamma(s)$ at $s=1,\dots,h
$; after division by $\Gamma(s)$ (analytic and nonzero at positive integers), th
ese survive as the only possible poles of $D(s)$, with $s=h$ necessarily a pole.
