## Proof

**Setup and key definitions.** A positive integer $n$ is *perfect* if $\sigma(n)
 = 2n$, where $\sigma$ is the sum-of-divisors function. Recall that $\sigma$ is
multiplicative: if $\gcd(m,n)=1$, then $\sigma(mn)=\sigma(m)\sigma(n)$.

By the Euclid–Euler theorem, every even perfect number has the form
$$b = 2^{p-1}(2^p - 1)$$
where $M_p := 2^p - 1$ is a Mersenne prime. The only prime factors of $b$ are $2
$ and $M_p$.

---

**Step 1: If $\gcd(a,b)=1$, then $ab$ is perfect.**

If $a$ is an odd perfect number and $b$ is an even perfect number with $\gcd(a,b
)=1$, then by multiplicativity:
$$\sigma(ab) = \sigma(a)\,\sigma(b) = (2a)(2b) = 2ab.$$
So $ab$ is perfect. ∎

Since $a$ is odd, $\gcd(a,b)=1$ is equivalent to $M_p \nmid a$ (because the only
 odd prime factor of $b$ is $M_p$).

---

**Step 2: If $M_p \mid a$, then $ab$ is NOT perfect.**

Suppose $a = M^e \cdot c$ with $M = M_p$, $\gcd(M,c)=1$, and $e \geq 1$. Then
$$ab = 2^{p-1} M^{e+1} c.$$

Using multiplicativity (the three factors $2^{p-1}, M^{e+1}, c$ are pairwise cop
rime):
$$\sigma(ab) = \sigma(2^{p-1})\,\sigma(M^{e+1})\,\sigma(c) = (2^p-1)\cdot\frac{M
^{e+2}-1}{M-1}\cdot\sigma(c) = M\cdot\frac{M^{e+2}-1}{M-1}\cdot\sigma(c).$$

On the other hand:
$$2ab = 2^p\, M^{e+1}\, c = (M+1)\,M^{e+1}\,c.$$

Setting $\sigma(ab) = 2ab$ and dividing both sides by $M$:
$$\frac{M^{e+2}-1}{M-1}\,\sigma(c) = (M+1)\,M^e\,c. \tag{$\star$}$$

Now use the fact that $a$ is perfect: $\sigma(M^e)\,\sigma(c) = 2M^e c$, i.e.,
$$\frac{M^{e+1}-1}{M-1}\,\sigma(c) = 2M^e c. \tag{$\star\star$}$$

Observe that $\frac{M^{e+2}-1}{M-1} = \frac{M^{e+1}-1}{M-1} + M^{e+1}$. Substitu
ting $(\star\star)$ into $(\star)$:

$$2M^e c + M^{e+1}\sigma(c) = (M+1)M^e c.$$

Dividing by $M^e$:

$$2c + M\,\sigma(c) = (M+1)c = Mc + c,$$

$$M\,\sigma(c) = c(M-1), \qquad \sigma(c) = c\cdot\frac{M-1}{M}.$$

Since $M \geq 3$, we have $\frac{M-1}{M} < 1$, so $\sigma(c) < c$. But for any p
ositive integer $c$:
- If $c = 1$: $\sigma(1) = 1 \neq \frac{M-1}{M}$ (since $M \geq 3$).
- If $c > 1$: $\sigma(c) \geq c + 1 > c > c\cdot\frac{M-1}{M}$.

**Contradiction.** Therefore $ab$ cannot be perfect when $M_p \mid a$. ∎

---

**Step 3: Combining Steps 1 and 2.**

For an odd perfect number $a$ and an even perfect number $b = 2^{p-1}M_p$:

$$ab \text{ is perfect} \iff \gcd(a,b)=1 \iff M_p \nmid a.$$

So the original question reduces to:

> **Does there exist a Mersenne prime $M_p$ that does not divide $a$?**

---

**Step 4: Analysis of the reduced question.**

The odd perfect number $a$ has **finitely many** prime factors. Therefore:

- **If there are infinitely many Mersenne primes** (equivalently, infinitely man
y even perfect numbers): Since $a$ has finitely many prime factors, only finitel
y many Mersenne primes can divide $a$. With infinitely many Mersenne primes avai
lable, there must exist one not dividing $a$. Choosing $b = 2^{p-1}M_p$ for such
 a prime, $\gcd(a,b)=1$, and by Step 1, $ab$ is perfect. **Answer: Yes.**

- **If there are only finitely many Mersenne primes** $M_{p_1}, \ldots, M_{p_k}$
: If all of them divide $a$, then by Step 2, no even perfect number $b$ yields a
 perfect product $ab$. Whether an odd perfect number can be divisible by *all* M
ersenne primes simultaneously is not ruled out by any known constraint (Euler's
form $a = q^{4j+1}n^2$ with $q \equiv 1\pmod{4}$ only forces Mersenne primes, wh
ich are $\equiv 3\pmod{4}$, to appear to even powers — it does not forbid their
inclusion). **Answer: Not guaranteed.**

---

**Conclusion.**

$$\boxed{\text{Yes, if and only if there are infinitely many Mersenne primes (eq
uivalently, infinitely many even perfect numbers).}}$$

The forward direction (infinitely many Mersenne primes $\Rightarrow$ yes) is pro
ved unconditionally via Steps 1–4: the multiplicativity of $\sigma$ for coprime
arguments guarantees that any even perfect number $b$ coprime to $a$ yields a pe
rfect product $ab$, and such $b$ always exists when Mersenne primes are infinite
.

The obstruction in the converse direction (Step 2) is sharp: if the Mersenne pri
me $M_p$ dividing $b$ also divides $a$, the perfection of $ab$ leads to the impo
ssible equation $\sigma(c) = c\cdot\frac{M_p - 1}{M_p} < c$.

Since the infinitude of Mersenne primes is a famous open problem, the answer to
the original question is **conditional on this open conjecture**.
