**Answer: Yes, $f$ is necessarily an isomorphism.**

**Proof.**

Since $G$ is a finitely generated torsion-free abelian group, by the structure t
heorem for finitely generated abelian groups we have
$$G \cong \mathbb{Z}^{r}$$
for some integer $r \ge 0$ (the torsion-free part is free abelian of finite rank
, and the torsion part is trivial by hypothesis).

Fix an identification $G \cong \mathbb{Z}^{r}$. Any homomorphism $f: \mathbb{Z}^
{r} \to \mathbb{Z}^{r}$ is represented by a unique $r \times r$ integer matrix $
A \in M_{r}(\mathbb{Z})$, acting by left multiplication on column vectors:
$$f(\mathbf{v}) = A\mathbf{v}.$$

**Step 1: $A$ has full rank.** Suppose, for contradiction, that $\operatorname{r
ank}(A) < r$. Then the image $A(\mathbb{Z}^{r}) \subseteq \mathbb{Z}^{r}$ is con
tained in a proper subspace of $\mathbb{Q}^{r}$, hence has rank $< r$ as an abel
ian group. But $\mathbb{Z}^{r}$ has rank $r$, so $A(\mathbb{Z}^{r}) \neq \mathbb
{Z}^{r}$, contradicting surjectivity of $f$. Thus $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r$,
i.e. $\det A \neq 0$.

**Step 2: Surjectivity forces $|\det A| = 1$.** For an integer matrix $A$ of ful
l rank, the index of the sublattice $A(\mathbb{Z}^{r})$ in $\mathbb{Z}^{r}$ is t
he finite number
$$[\mathbb{Z}^{r} : A(\mathbb{Z}^{r})] = |\det A|.$$
(This is the standard lattice-index formula; equivalently, by Smith normal form,
 $A = U D V$ with $U, V \in \mathrm{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{Z})$ and $D = \operatorname{
diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{r})$, $d_{i} \neq 0$, and the index is $|d_{1}\cdots d_{r}|
 = |\det A|$.)

Surjectivity of $f$ means $A(\mathbb{Z}^{r}) = \mathbb{Z}^{r}$, so the index is
$1$:
$$|\det A| = [\mathbb{Z}^{r} : A(\mathbb{Z}^{r})] = 1.$$

**Step 3: $A$ is invertible over $\mathbb{Z}$, hence $f$ is injective.** Since $
\det A = \pm 1$, the classical adjugate formula gives
$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}\operatorname{adj}(A) = \pm\,\operatorname{adj}(A) \i
n M_{r}(\mathbb{Z}).$$
Thus $A \in \mathrm{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{Z})$, so $A$ is invertible as an endomorphis
m of $\mathbb{Z}^{r}$. In particular, $\ker f = \ker(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$, so $f
$ is injective.

**Conclusion.** A surjective endomorphism $f$ of $G \cong \mathbb{Z}^{r}$ is rep
resented by an integer matrix with $|\det A| = 1$, hence is invertible over $\ma
thbb{Z}$, hence is also injective. Therefore $f$ is an isomorphism. $\blacksquar
e$

**Remark.** Both hypotheses are essential:
- *Torsion-free* is needed: on $G = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ (finitely generated,
 but with torsion), the map $x \mapsto 0$ — or more interestingly, on the Prüfer
-type finite case — surjective non-injective endomorphisms exist (e.g. multiplic
ation by $p$ is surjective but not injective on $\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}$ for
 appropriate choices, or the shift on a suitable torsion group).
- *Finitely generated* is needed: on the non-finitely-generated torsion-free abe
lian group $\mathbb{Q}$, multiplication by $2$ is a surjective but non-injective
... (in fact it is bijective on $\mathbb{Q}$); a better example is the group $\m
athbb{Z}[1/p]$, on which multiplication by $p$ is surjective with nontrivial ker
nel $0$ — actually bijective too. The cleanest counterexample without finite gen
eration is the additive group of $\mathbb{Q}$: the map $x \mapsto 2x$ is an auto
morphism, so one should instead use, e.g., the direct sum $\bigoplus_{n\ge 1}\ma
thbb{Z}$ (free abelian of countable rank, torsion-free, not finitely generated),
 where the shift map $(a_{1},a_{2},\dots)\mapsto(a_{2},a_{3},\dots)$ is surjecti
ve with kernel $\mathbb{Z}$, hence not injective.

So with both hypotheses in place, the answer is **yes**.
