## Answer

A standard example is the quotient group $G = \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$, the additi
ve group of rational numbers modulo the integers.

### Proof

**Construction.** Let $G = \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$, where $\mathbb{Q}$ is the add
itive group of rationals and $\mathbb{Z}$ is the subgroup of integers. The coset
 of a rational $r$ is denoted $r + \mathbb{Z}$, and the group operation is
$$(r_1 + \mathbb{Z}) + (r_2 + \mathbb{Z}) = (r_1 + r_2) + \mathbb{Z}.$$
The identity is $0 + \mathbb{Z}$, and the inverse of $r + \mathbb{Z}$ is $-r + \
mathbb{Z}$.

**Claim.** For every positive integer $n$, the element $a_n := \tfrac{1}{n} + \m
athbb{Z} \in G$ has order exactly $n$, so the cyclic subgroup $\langle a_n \rang
le$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_n$.

**Verification of the order.**

*Upper bound.* We have
$$n \cdot a_n = \underbrace{a_n + \cdots + a_n}_{n \text{ times}} = \frac{n}{n}
+ \mathbb{Z} = 1 + \mathbb{Z} = 0 + \mathbb{Z},$$
since $1 \in \mathbb{Z}$. Hence the order of $a_n$ divides $n$.

*Lower bound.* Let $k$ be any integer with $0 < k < n$. Then
$$k \cdot a_n = \frac{k}{n} + \mathbb{Z}.$$
Since $0 < k < n$, the fraction $\tfrac{k}{n}$ is **not** an integer, so $\tfrac
{k}{n} + \mathbb{Z} \neq 0 + \mathbb{Z}$. Therefore no positive $k < n$ annihila
tes $a_n$.

Combining both bounds, $\operatorname{ord}(a_n) = n$.

**Isomorphism.** The cyclic subgroup generated by $a_n$ is
$$\langle a_n \rangle = \left\{ \tfrac{k}{n} + \mathbb{Z} : k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1
 \right\},$$
which has exactly $n$ elements. The map
$$\varphi: \mathbb{Z}_n \to \langle a_n \rangle, \qquad \varphi(\bar{k}) = \tfra
c{k}{n} + \mathbb{Z}$$
is well defined (since $\bar{k} = \bar{k'}$ in $\mathbb{Z}_n$ means $n \mid (k -
 k')$, i.e. $\tfrac{k-k'}{n} \in \mathbb{Z}$, so $\tfrac{k}{n} + \mathbb{Z} = \t
frac{k'}{n} + \mathbb{Z}$), is a homomorphism (addition is preserved by construc
tion), is injective (kernel is $\{\bar{0}\}$ by the lower-bound argument above),
 and is surjective onto $\langle a_n \rangle$ by definition. Hence $\varphi$ is
an isomorphism, and
$$\langle a_n \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}_n.$$

**Conclusion.** Since this holds for **every** positive integer $n$, the group
$$\boxed{G = \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}}$$
contains a subgroup isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_n$ for every $n \geq 1$.

**Remark.** Other valid examples include the circle group $S^1 = \{z \in \mathbb
{C} : |z| = 1\}$ under multiplication (the element $e^{2\pi i / n}$ has order $n
$), and the direct sum $\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{Z}_n$ (which contains $
\mathbb{Z}_n$ as the $n$-th summand). The group $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ is the m
ost economical: it is precisely the torsion subgroup of $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$,
 and it is generated by the elements $\tfrac{1}{n} + \mathbb{Z}$ across all $n$.
