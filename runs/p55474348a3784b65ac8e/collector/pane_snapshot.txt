Theorem. Every well-ordered subset of $\mathbb{R}$ (under the usual order) is
countable.

Proof.

Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be a set that is well-ordered by the usual order
$<$. We show $S$ is countable.

Key observation: every non-maximal element has an immediate successor.

Let $x \in S$, and suppose $x$ is not the maximum of $S$. Then the set $$T_x =
{y \in S : y > x}$$ is nonempty. Since $S$ is well-ordered, $T_x$ has a least
element; call it $s(x)$. This $s(x)$ is the immediate successor of $x$ in $S$:
there is no element of $S$ strictly between $x$ and $s(x)$, i.e., $$(x,, s(x))
\cap S = \emptyset.$$ Indeed, any $z \in S$ with $x < z < s(x)$ would belong to
$T_x$ and be smaller than $s(x)$, contradicting the minimality of $s(x)$.

Injecting into $\mathbb{Q}$.

Let $S^* = S \setminus {\max S}$ if $S$ has a maximum, otherwise $S^* = S$.
Every element $x \in S^$ has an immediate successor $s(x)$, and the open
interval $(x, s(x))$ is a nonempty open interval of reals (since $x < s(x)$),
hence contains a rational number. By the Axiom of Choice (countable choice, or
simply by fixing an enumeration of $\mathbb{Q}$ and choosing the least-indexed
rational in each interval), select $$q_x \in (x,, s(x)) \cap \mathbb{Q}, \qquad
x \in S^.$$

The intervals are pairwise disjoint, so the assignment is injective.

Suppose $x, x' \in S^$ with $x < x'$. We claim $s(x) \leq x'$. If instead $x' <
s(x)$, then $x' \in (x, s(x)) \cap S$, contradicting $(x, s(x)) \cap S =
\emptyset$. Hence $s(x) \leq x' < s(x')$, so the intervals $(x, s(x))$ and $(x',
s(x'))$ are disjoint. Therefore $q_x \neq q_{x'}$, and the map $$x \longmapsto
q_x$$ is an injection from $S^$ into $\mathbb{Q}$.

Conclusion.

Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable, $S^$ is countable. The set $S \setminus S^$
contains at most one element (the maximum of $S$, if it exists). Therefore $$S =
S^* \cup (S \setminus S^*)$$ is countable. $\blacksquare$
