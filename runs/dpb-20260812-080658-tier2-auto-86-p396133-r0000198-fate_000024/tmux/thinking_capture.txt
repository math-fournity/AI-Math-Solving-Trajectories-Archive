Prime Avoidance Lemma

Theorem. Let $R$ be a commutative ring with identity, $P_1, \dots, P_m$ prime
ideals of $R$, and $A$ an ideal of $R$ with $A \subseteq P_1 \cup \cdots \cup
P_m$. Then $A \subseteq P_i$ for some $i$.

Proof. We proceed by induction on $m$.

Base case ($m = 1$). Trivial: $A \subseteq P_1$.

Inductive step. Assume the result holds for fewer than $m$ primes. Suppose for
contradiction that $A \not\subseteq P_i$ for every $i$. Then for each $i$ there
exists $a_i \in A$ with $a_i \notin P_i$.

Claim. For each $i$, we may assume $a_i \notin \bigcup_{j \neq i} P_j$.

Proof of Claim. Fix $i$. If $a_i \notin P_j$ for all $j \neq i$, we are done.
Otherwise, by the inductive hypothesis applied to the $m - 1$ primes ${P_j : j
\neq i}$: since $A \subseteq \bigcup_j P_j$ and we may assume $A \not\subseteq
P_i$, in fact $A \subseteq \bigcup_{j \neq i} P_j$ would force $A \subseteq P_j$
for some $j \neq i$ (by induction), contradicting our assumption. So there
exists some element of $A$ outside $\bigcup_{j \neq i} P_j$, but we need more
care.

Clean standard proof (Prime Avoidance).

We prove the following stronger statement by induction on $m$:

│ (PA) If $A \subseteq P_1 \cup \cdots \cup P_m$ where at most two of the $P_i$
│ are non-prime (the rest prime), then $A \subseteq P_i$ for some $i$.

We only need the all-prime case, but the stronger statement makes the induction
work.

Base case $m = 1$: Trivial.

Inductive step. Assume (PA) for $m - 1$. Suppose $A \subseteq P_1 \cup \cdots
\cup P_m$ but $A \not\subseteq P_i$ for any $i$.

Step 1. For each $i$, since $A \not\subseteq P_i$, choose $a_i \in A \setminus
P_i$.

Step 2. For each $i$, the element $a_i$ must lie in $\bigcup_{j \neq i} P_j$
(since $a_i \in A \subseteq \bigcup_j P_j$ but $a_i \notin P_i$).

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Cleanest version.

For each $i \in {1, \dots, m}$, by Step 3 we have $b_i \in P_i$ and $b_i \notin
P_j$ for all $j \neq i$.

Define $$c := \sum_{i=1}^{m} \prod_{j \neq i} b_j.$$

Since each $b_j \in A$ and $A$ is an ideal, each product $\prod_{j \neq i} b_j
\in A$, and the sum $c \in A$.

Claim: $c \notin P_k$ for every $k$.

Proof of claim. Fix $k$. Consider the sum $c = \sum_{i=1}^m \prod_{j \neq i}
b_j$ modulo $P_k$.

  • Term $i = k$: $\prod_{j \neq k} b_j$. For each $j \neq k$, $b_j \notin P_k$
  (by construction). Since $P_k$ is prime, $\prod_{j \neq k} b_j \notin P_k$.
  • Terms $i \neq k$: $\prod_{j \neq i} b_j$. This product includes the factor
  $b_k$ (since $k \neq i$ means $k$ is among the $j \neq i$). Since $b_k \in
  P_k$, the entire product $\prod_{j \neq i} b_j \in P_k$.

Therefore: $$c = \underbrace{\prod_{j \neq k} b_j}{\notin P_k} + \sum{i \neq k}
\underbrace{\prod_{j \neq i} b_j}_{\in P_k}.$$

So $c \notin P_k$, because $c \equiv \prod_{j \neq k} b_j \pmod{P_k}$, and this
residue is nonzero in $R/P_k$ (which is an integral domain since $P_k$ is
prime).

Since $k$ was arbitrary, $c \notin P_k$ for all $k = 1, \dots, m$.

But $c \in A \subseteq P_1 \cup \cdots \cup P_m$, so $c \in P_k$ for some $k$.
Contradiction.

Therefore our assumption that $A \not\subseteq P_i$ for all $i$ is false, and $A
\subseteq P_i$ for some $i$. $\blacksquare$

Summary of the argument:

  1. Induction on $m$ reduces the problem: assuming the result for $m-1$ primes
  lets us find, for each $i$, an element $b_i \in A$ lying in $P_i$ but in no
  other $P_j$.
  2. The cleverly constructed sum $c = \sum_i \prod_{j \neq i} b_j$ lies in $A$
  (ideal closure) but, by primality, escapes every $P_k$ — the "$i = k$" term
  survives mod $P_k$ while all other terms vanish.
  3. This contradicts $A \subseteq \bigcup_i P_i$, forcing $A \subseteq P_i$ for
  some $i$.
