## Proof

**Definition.** A function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is *unbounded at* $x$ if
$f$ is unbounded on every neighborhood of $x$; equivalently, for every $\delta>0
$, $\sup_{y\in(x-\delta,x+\delta)}|f(y)|=\infty$. Let $U(f)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}: f
\text{ is unbounded at } x\}$.

**Answer: Yes.** For every closed set $E\subseteq\mathbb{R}$, there exists $f:\m
athbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ with $U(f)=E$. Moreover, closedness of $E$ is necessary s
ince $U(f)$ is always closed.

---

### Step 1: $U(f)$ is always closed (necessity)

Let $x_n\in U(f)$ with $x_n\to x$. For any $\delta>0$, pick $n$ large enough tha
t $|x_n-x|<\delta/2$. Since $x_n\in U(f)$, $f$ is unbounded on $(x_n-\delta/2,\,
x_n+\delta/2)\subseteq(x-\delta,\,x+\delta)$. Hence $f$ is unbounded on $(x-\del
ta,x+\delta)$, so $x\in U(f)$. Thus $U(f)$ is closed. $\checkmark$

---

### Step 2: Construction for arbitrary closed $E$ (sufficiency)

Let $E\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ be closed. Decompose $E=\operatorname{int}(E)\sqcup\p
artial E$, where $\partial E=E\setminus\operatorname{int}(E)$.

**Building blocks:**

- **On $\mathbb{R}\setminus E$:** set $f(x)=\dfrac{1}{d(x,E)}$, which is finite
since $x\notin E\Rightarrow d(x,E)>0$.

- **On $\operatorname{int}(E)$:** enumerate $\mathbb{Q}\cap\operatorname{int}(E)
=\{r_1,r_2,r_3,\dots\}$ (countable). Set $f(r_n)=n$ and $f(x)=0$ for $x\in\opera
torname{int}(E)\setminus\mathbb{Q}$.

- **On $\partial E$:** set $f(x)=0$.

These three pieces are disjoint and cover $\mathbb{R}$, so $f$ is well-defined.

---

### Step 3: Verification that $U(f)=E$

**Points outside $E$ are not in $U(f)$:** Let $x\notin E$. Since $\mathbb{R}\set
minus E$ is open, choose $\delta>0$ with $(x-\delta,x+\delta)\Subset\mathbb{R}\s
etminus E$. Then $d(\cdot,E)\ge\varepsilon>0$ on this interval, so $f=1/d(\cdot,
E)\le 1/\varepsilon$ there. Hence $f$ is bounded near $x$, so $x\notin U(f)$. $\
checkmark$

**Boundary points of $E$ are in $U(f)$:** Let $x\in\partial E$. For any $\delta>
0$, the interval $(x-\delta,x+\delta)$ contains points $y\notin E$ (by definitio
n of boundary). As $y\to x$ with $y\notin E$, $d(y,E)\to 0$, so $f(y)=1/d(y,E)\t
o\infty$. Thus $f$ is unbounded on every neighborhood of $x$, giving $x\in U(f)$
. $\checkmark$

**Interior points of $E$ are in $U(f)$:** Let $x\in\operatorname{int}(E)$. For a
ny $\delta>0$, the set $(x-\delta,x+\delta)\cap\operatorname{int}(E)$ is open an
d contains $x$, hence contains an open interval $J\subseteq\operatorname{int}(E)
$ around $x$. The interval $J$ contains infinitely many rationals from $\mathbb{
Q}\cap\operatorname{int}(E)$, in particular $r_n$ for arbitrarily large $n$. At
those points $f(r_n)=n\to\infty$, so $f$ is unbounded on $J\subseteq(x-\delta,x+
\delta)$. Hence $x\in U(f)$. $\checkmark$

Combining the three cases: $U(f)=\partial E\cup\operatorname{int}(E)=E$. $\black
square$

### Conclusion

For every closed subset $E$ of $\mathbb{R}$, there exists a function $f:\mathbb{
R}\to\mathbb{R}$ whose set of unboundedness points is exactly $E$. The closednes
s of $E$ is both sufficient (by the construction above) and necessary (since $U(
f)$ is always closed).
