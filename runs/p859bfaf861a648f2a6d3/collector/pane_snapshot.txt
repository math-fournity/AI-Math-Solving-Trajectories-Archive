## Proof

Let $u \in (0,1)$ and set $u = e^{-t}$ with $t = -\ln u > 0$. As $u \to 1^-$, we
 have $t \to 0^+$. Define

$$S(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} e^{-t n^2}.$$

We determine the asymptotic behavior of $S(t)$ as $t \to 0^+$.

### Step 1. Reduction to a Jacobi theta function

Recall the Jacobi theta function

$$\vartheta_3(0, q) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} q^{n^2} = 1 + 2\sum_{n=1}^{\inft
y} q^{n^2}.$$

With $q = e^{-t}$,

$$S(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} e^{-t n^2} = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(\vartheta_3(0, e^{-t}
) - 1\bigr). \tag{1}$$

### Step 2. The Jacobi imaginary transformation

The classical modular identity (Jacobi's imaginary transformation) states

$$\vartheta_3(0, e^{-t}) = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{t}}\;\vartheta_3\!\left(0, e^{-\pi^2
/t}\right). \tag{2}$$

This follows from the Poisson summation formula applied to $f(x) = e^{-t x^2}$:

$$\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-t n^2} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{t}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\
infty} e^{-\pi^2 n^2 / t}.$$

### Step 3. Asymptotics as $t \to 0^+$

As $t \to 0^+$, the quantity $\pi^2/t \to +\infty$, so $e^{-\pi^2/t} \to 0$ fast
er than any power of $t$. Therefore

$$\vartheta_3\!\left(0, e^{-\pi^2/t}\right) = 1 + 2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} e^{-\pi^2
 n^2/t} = 1 + 2e^{-\pi^2/t} + O\!\left(e^{-4\pi^2/t}\right) = 1 + O\!\left(e^{-\
pi^2/t}\right).$$

Substituting into (2):

$$\vartheta_3(0, e^{-t}) = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{t}}\left(1 + O\!\left(e^{-\pi^2/t}\r
ight)\right).$$

The correction is *exponentially small* in $t$, hence negligible compared to eve
ry power of $t$. From (1),

$$S(t) = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{t}}\left(1 + O\!\left(e^{-\pi^2/t}\right)\r
ight) - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{t}} - \frac{1}{2} + O\!\left(\
sqrt{\frac{\pi}{t}}\,e^{-\pi^2/t}\right).$$

Since $\sqrt{\pi/t}\,e^{-\pi^2/t} \to 0$ and the constant $-1/2$ is lower order
than $\sqrt{\pi/t} \to \infty$, the leading behavior is

$$S(t) \sim \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{t}}, \qquad t \to 0^+. \tag{3}$$

### Step 4. Cross-check via integral approximation

For an independent confirmation, compare the sum with the Gaussian integral:

$$\int_0^{\infty} e^{-t x^2}\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{t}}.$$

The sum $S(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} e^{-t n^2}$ is a right-endpoint Riemann sum
for this integral on intervals of width $1$. As $t \to 0^+$ the integrand varies
 on the scale $x \sim 1/\sqrt{t} \gg 1$, so the mesh size $1$ is fine relative t
o the variation scale, and the sum approaches the integral. More precisely, by t
he Euler–Maclaurin formula the difference between the sum and the integral is bo
unded by a constant (the $-1/2$ boundary term plus exponentially small correctio
ns, consistent with Step 3). This independently yields the same leading term (3)
.

### Step 5. Returning to the variable $u$

Since $t = -\ln u$ and $u \to 1^-$,

$$-\ln u = (1 - u) + \frac{(1-u)^2}{2} + \cdots \sim 1 - u,$$

so $t \sim 1 - u$ as $u \to 1^-$. Substituting into (3):

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u^{n^2} \sim \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{-\ln u}} \sim \fr
ac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{1 - u}}, \qquad u \to 1^-.$$

---

### Conclusion

As $u \to 1^-$,

$$\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u^{n^2} \sim \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{1-u}}.}$$

Equivalently, with $t = -\ln u$, the precise asymptotic (including the exponenti
ally small error from the modular transformation) is

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} e^{-t n^2} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{t}} - \frac{1}{2}
 + O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{t}}\,e^{-\pi^2/t}\right), \qquad t \to 0^+.$$
