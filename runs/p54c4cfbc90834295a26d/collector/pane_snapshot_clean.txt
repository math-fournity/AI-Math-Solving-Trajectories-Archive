## Proof

**Answer: Yes, such a real number $r$ exists.**

Define the set
$$S = \left\{ \sum_{i=0}^{n}\bigl(c_i\, a^{i} + d_i\, b^{i}\bigr) \;\middle|\; c
_i, d_i \in \mathbb{Q},\; n \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} \right\}.$$

Observe that every element of $S$ can be rewritten as $p(a) + q(b)$, where $p, q
 \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ are polynomials with rational coefficients (take $p(x) = \su
m_{i=0}^{n} c_i x^{i}$ and $q(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} d_i x^{i}$, padding with zero
coefficients as needed). Conversely, every such pair $(p, q)$ gives an element o
f $S$. Hence

$$S = \{\, p(a) + q(b) : p, q \in \mathbb{Q}[x] \,\}.$$

**Step 1: $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ is countable.**
A polynomial with rational coefficients of degree $d$ is determined by a $(d{+}1
)$-tuple of rationals. Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable, $\mathbb{Q}^{d+1}$ is co
untable for each $d$, and $\mathbb{Q}[x] = \bigcup_{d=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{Q}^{d+
1}$ is a countable union of countable sets, hence countable.

**Step 2: $S$ is countable.**
The map $\varphi : \mathbb{Q}[x] \times \mathbb{Q}[x] \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by
 $\varphi(p, q) = p(a) + q(b)$ has image exactly $S$. Since $\mathbb{Q}[x] \time
s \mathbb{Q}[x]$ is a Cartesian product of two countable sets, it is countable.
The image of a countable set under any function is countable. Therefore $S$ is c
ountable.

(Note: the transcendence of $a$ and $b$ is not even needed for the countability
argument — it guarantees the evaluation maps $p \mapsto p(a)$ and $q \mapsto q(b
)$ are injective, so $\mathbb{Q}[a] \cong \mathbb{Q}[x]$ and $\mathbb{Q}[b] \con
g \mathbb{Q}[x]$ as rings, but countability of $S$ holds regardless.)

**Step 3: $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable, so $S \subsetneq \mathbb{R}$.**
By Cantor's theorem, $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable. Since $S$ is countable, $S$ ca
nnot equal $\mathbb{R}$. Therefore there exists $r \in \mathbb{R}$ with $r \noti
n S$.

**Conclusion.** For any two transcendental numbers $a$ and $b$, the set of all f
inite sums $\sum_{i=0}^{n}(c_i a^{i} + d_i b^{i})$ with rational coefficients is
 countable, while $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable. Hence there always exists a real
number $r$ that cannot be expressed in the given form. $\blacksquare$
