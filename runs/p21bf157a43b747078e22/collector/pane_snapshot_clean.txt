# Proof

**Answer: Yes, $f$ must be smooth.** This is the **Myers–Steenrod theorem** (193
9): every distance-preserving map of a Riemannian manifold is a smooth Riemannia
n isometry (hence a diffeomorphism).

Below is a self-contained proof.

---

## Step 1. $f$ is a homeomorphism

Since $f$ preserves distances, $f$ and $f^{-1}$ are both Lipschitz, hence contin
uous. So $f$ is a homeomorphism of $M$.

## Step 2. $f$ maps geodesics to geodesics

Let $\gamma:[0,1]\to M$ be a minimizing geodesic segment parametrized by arc len
gth, of length $\ell=d(\gamma(0),\gamma(1))$. The length of a curve is a supremu
m over partitions of sums of distances:
$$L(\gamma)=\sup_{\mathcal P}\sum_i d\bigl(\gamma(t_{i-1}),\gamma(t_i)\bigr).$$
Since $f$ preserves every pairwise distance, $L(f\circ\gamma)=L(\gamma)=\ell$. B
ut also $d(f(\gamma(0)),f(\gamma(1)))=d(\gamma(0),\gamma(1))=\ell$, so $f\circ\g
amma$ is minimizing, hence a geodesic. Thus **$f$ sends geodesic segments to geo
desic segments**.

## Step 3. The induced map on tangent spaces

Fix $p\in M$ and $r>0$ smaller than the injectivity radius at $p$, so $\exp_p:B(
0,r)\subset T_pM\to M$ is a diffeomorphism onto its image. For $v\in B(0,r)$, th
e geodesic $t\mapsto\exp_p(tv)$ is mapped by $f$ to a geodesic through $f(p)$, s
o there is a unique $A_p(v)\in T_{f(p)}M$ with
$$f\bigl(\exp_p(v)\bigr)=\exp_{f(p)}\bigl(A_p(v)\bigr),\qquad v\in B(0,r).$$
Moreover $\|A_p(v)\|=\|v\|$, because
$$\|A_p(v)\|=d\bigl(f(p),\exp_{f(p)}(A_p(v))\bigr)=d\bigl(f(p),f(\exp_p(v))\bigr
)=d\bigl(p,\exp_p(v)\bigr)=\|v\|.$$

**Homogeneity.** Since $f(\exp_p(tv))=\exp_{f(p)}(t\,A_p(v))$ for $t\in[0,1]$ (b
oth sides are the same geodesic through $f(p)$), we have
$$A_p(tv)=t\,A_p(v),\qquad t\in[0,1],\;v\in B(0,r). \tag{$\ast$}$$

## Step 4. $A_p$ preserves inner products (hence is linear)

In normal coordinates at $p$ we have $g_{ij}(x)=\delta_{ij}+O(|x|^2)$, so for $v
,w$ small (with $\varepsilon=\max(\|v\|,\|w\|)$):
$$d\bigl(\exp_p(v),\exp_p(w)\bigr)^2=\|v-w\|^2+O(\varepsilon^4).$$
Applying this at both $p$ and $f(p)$, and using that $f$ preserves distances tog
ether with $\|A_p(v)\|,\|A_p(w)\|\le\varepsilon$:
$$\|A_p(v)-A_p(w)\|^2=\|v-w\|^2+O(\varepsilon^4).$$
Expanding both sides via $\|A_p(v)\|=\|v\|$, $\|A_p(w)\|=\|w\|$ gives
$$\bigl\langle A_p(v),A_p(w)\bigr\rangle=\langle v,w\rangle+O(\varepsilon^4). \t
ag{$\ast\ast$}$$

Now fix arbitrary $v,w\in T_pM$ and scale: set $v\leadsto tv$, $w\leadsto tw$ wi
th $t\to0^+$. By homogeneity $(\ast)$, $A_p(tv)=t\,A_p(v)$ and $A_p(tw)=t\,A_p(w
)$. Substituting into $(\ast\ast)$:
$$t^2\bigl\langle A_p(v),A_p(w)\bigr\rangle=t^2\langle v,w\rangle+O(t^4),$$
so $\langle A_p(v),A_p(w)\rangle=\langle v,w\rangle+O(t^2)$. Letting $t\to0$:
$$\bigl\langle A_p(v),A_p(w)\bigr\rangle=\langle v,w\rangle\qquad\forall\,v,w.$$

Thus $A_p$ preserves the inner product. A map between inner-product spaces prese
rving the inner product is linear: indeed
$$\bigl\|A_p(v+w)-A_p(v)-A_p(w)\bigr\|^2=0$$
by expanding using the preserved inner products (parallelogram/polarization), gi
ving $A_p(v+w)=A_p(v)+A_p(w)$; together with homogeneity $(\ast)$ this yields li
nearity. Hence **$A_p:T_pM\to T_{f(p)}M$ is a linear isometry**.

## Step 5. $f$ is smooth

We have established
$$f\circ\exp_p=\exp_{f(p)}\circ A_p,\qquad A_p\text{ linear}.$$
In the normal-coordinate chart $\phi_p=\exp_p^{-1}$ at $p$ and $\psi_{f(p)}=\exp
_{f(p)}^{-1}$ at $f(p)$, the local representative of $f$ is
$$\psi_{f(p)}\circ f\circ\phi_p^{-1}=A_p,$$
a **linear** (hence smooth) map. Since $p$ was arbitrary, $f$ is smooth in a nei
ghborhood of every point of $M$, hence **smooth on $M$**.

Moreover $f^*g=g$ because each $A_p=df_p$ is a linear isometry of $(T_pM,g_p)\to
(T_{f(p)}M,g_{f(p)})$, so $f$ is in fact a Riemannian isometry and a diffeomorph
ism. $\blacksquare$

---

**Remark.** The hypothesis that $d$ is induced by a Riemannian metric $g$ is ess
ential: for a general metric $d$ inducing the smooth topology, a distance-preser
ving homeomorphism need not be smooth (e.g. on $\mathbb{R}$ with a non-smooth Fi
nsler-type metric, or more dramatically on metric spaces whose metric is not Rie
mannian). The Riemannian origin of $d$ is precisely what forces geodesics to be
rigid enough for the Myers–Steenrod mechanism to operate.
