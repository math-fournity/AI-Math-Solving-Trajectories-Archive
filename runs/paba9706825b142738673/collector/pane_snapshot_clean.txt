# Proof

**Claim:** Yes, there exists a smooth orthonormal basis for $L^2(T)$ with respec
t to Lebesgue measure on the unit circle $T$.

---

## Construction: The Fourier Basis

Let $T = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| = 1\}$ be the unit circle, parametrized by $z
= e^{i\theta}$, $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$. Equip $T$ with (normalized) Lebesgue mea
sure $d\theta/(2\pi)$, so that $L^2(T)$ is the space of measurable functions $f
: T \to \mathbb{C}$ with

$$\|f\|_2^2 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} |f(e^{i\theta})|^2\, d\theta < \infty.
$$

Define the family of functions

$$e_n(e^{i\theta}) := e^{in\theta}, \qquad n \in \mathbb{Z}.$$

## Step 1: Each $e_n$ is smooth on $T$

The map $e^{i\theta} \mapsto e^{in\theta} = (e^{i\theta})^n$ is the restriction
of the holomorphic (hence $C^\infty$) function $z \mapsto z^n$ on $\mathbb{C} \s
etminus\{0\}$ to $T$. For $n < 0$, we write $z^n = \overline{z}^{|n|}$, which is
 the restriction of the anti-holomorphic (hence $C^\infty$) function $z \mapsto
\bar{z}^{|n|}$. In either case, $e_n$ is $C^\infty$ on $T$.

## Step 2: Orthonormality

For $m, n \in \mathbb{Z}$:

$$\langle e_m, e_n \rangle = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} e^{im\theta}\,\overline
{e^{in\theta}}\, d\theta = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} e^{i(m-n)\theta}\, d\thet
a.$$

- If $m = n$: the integrand is $1$, so $\langle e_n, e_n\rangle = 1$.
- If $m \neq n$: $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} e^{i(m-n)\theta}\,d\theta = \frac{
1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{e^{i(m-n)\cdot 2\pi} - 1}{i(m-n)} = 0$, since $m - n \in \mat
hbb{Z}$.

Thus $\{e_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}$ is an orthonormal set.

## Step 3: Completeness (it is a basis)

We must show that the closed linear span of $\{e_n\}_{n\mathbb{Z}}$ is all of $L
^2(T)$, i.e., if $f \in L^2(T)$ satisfies $\langle f, e_n\rangle = 0$ for all $n
 \in \mathbb{Z}$, then $f = 0$ a.e.

This is the classical **uniqueness theorem for Fourier series**. We recall the s
tandard argument:

**Density of trigonometric polynomials in $C(T)$.** By the Stone–Weierstrass the
orem, the algebra generated by $\{1, e^{i\theta}, e^{-i\theta}\}$ (i.e., trigono
metric polynomials) is dense in $C(T)$ with respect to the uniform norm, since i
t separates points, contains constants, and is closed under conjugation.

**Density of $C(T)$ in $L^2(T)$.** Continuous functions are dense in $L^2(T)$ (b
y Lusin's theorem / standard measure theory: simple functions are dense, and sim
ple functions are approximated in $L^2$ by continuous functions via regularity o
f Lebesgue measure).

**Conclusion.** If $\langle f, e_n \rangle = 0$ for all $n$, then $\langle f, p
\rangle = 0$ for every trigonometric polynomial $p$ (by linearity). By density a
nd continuity of the inner product, $\langle f, g \rangle = 0$ for every $g \in
C(T)$, and then for every $g \in L^2(T)$. Taking $g = f$ gives $\|f\|_2^2 = 0$,
hence $f = 0$ a.e.

Therefore $\{e_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}$ is a **complete** orthonormal system — an
 orthonormal basis of $L^2(T)$.

## Conclusion

The Fourier basis $\{e_n(e^{i\theta}) = e^{in\theta}\}_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}$ is an
 orthonormal basis for $L^2(T)$ with respect to Lebesgue measure, and every elem
ent is $C^\infty$ (smooth) on $T$. Hence such a basis exists. $\blacksquare$
