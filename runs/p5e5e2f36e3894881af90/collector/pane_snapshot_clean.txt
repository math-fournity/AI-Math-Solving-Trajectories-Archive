## Answer

**No.** Goursat's theorem fails when "holomorphic" is replaced by "$\mathbb{R}^2
$-differentiable." A single counterexample suffices.

---

### Statement of Goursat's Theorem

Let $U\subseteq\mathbb{C}$ be open and $f:U\to\mathbb{C}$ be **complex-different
iable** at every point of $U$. Then for every closed triangle $T\subset U$,

$$\oint_{\partial T} f(z)\,dz = 0.$$

The remarkable feature of Goursat's proof is that **no continuity of $f'$ is ass
umed**—only pointwise complex differentiability.

### The Replacement Hypothesis

"$\mathbb{R}^2$-differentiable" means: $f:U\to\mathbb{C}$ admits a **real-linear
** approximation at each point, i.e., $f(z+h)-f(z) = L_z(h) + o(\|h\|)$ where $L
_z:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2$ is some real-linear map. This is strictly weaker
 than complex differentiability, which requires $L_z$ to be **complex-linear** (
equivalently, the Cauchy–Riemann equations hold).

### Counterexample

Take $f(z)=\bar{z}=x-iy$. This map is real-linear, hence $\mathbb{R}^2$-differen
tiable everywhere (in fact $C^\infty$). Let $T$ be the triangle with vertices $0
,1,i$, oriented counterclockwise ($0\to 1\to i\to 0$).

**Edge 1** ($0\to 1$): $z(t)=t$, $dz=dt$, $\bar z=t$, $t\in[0,1]$.
$$I_1=\int_0^1 t\,dt=\tfrac12.$$

**Edge 2** ($1\to i$): $z(t)=1-t+it$, $dz=(-1+i)\,dt$, $\bar z=1-t-it$, $t\in[0,
1]$.
$$\bar z\,dz=(1-t-it)(-1+i)=(-1+2t)+i,$$
$$I_2=\int_0^1(-1+2t+i)\,dt=\bigl[-t+t^2+it\bigr]_0^1=i.$$

**Edge 3** ($i\to 0$): $z(t)=i(1-t)$, $dz=-i\,dt$, $\bar z=-i(1-t)$, $t\in[0,1]$
.
$$\bar z\,dz=(-i(1-t))(-i)=-(1-t),$$
$$I_3=\int_0^1 -(1-t)\,dt=-\tfrac12.$$

**Total:**
$$\oint_{\partial T}\bar z\,dz = I_1+I_2+I_3 = \tfrac12 + i - \tfrac12 = i \neq
0.$$

Thus a smooth ($\mathbb{R}^2$-differentiable) function can have a nonzero integr
al over a triangle boundary, so the conclusion of Goursat's theorem **fails**.

### Why Goursat's Proof Cannot Survive the Weakening

Goursat's subdivision argument hinges on the derivative being **complex-linear**
: the approximation $f(z+h)-f(z)=f'(z)\,h+o(|h|)$ has its linear part given by *
*complex multiplication**, which commutes with rotation. When one bisects a tria
ngle and estimates the integral over small sub-triangles, the complex-linearity
forces the linear contribution to telescope to zero (by cancellation of oriented
 edges), leaving only the $o(|h|)$ remainder, which is driven to zero by iterati
on.

For a merely real-differentiable $f$, the linear part $L_z$ is an arbitrary real
-linear map $\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2$. Such a map decomposes as $L_z(h)=\alp
ha h+\beta\bar h$ (with $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}$). The $\alpha h$ part behave
s as in the holomorphic case, but the $\beta\bar h$ part does **not** telescope
to zero under subdivision—it contributes a nonzero residue. In the counterexampl
e above, $f(z)=\bar z$ has $\alpha=0,\beta=1$, and the entire integral comes fro
m this $\bar h$ component.

Equivalently, the real-differentiable analogue of Cauchy's theorem is **Green's
theorem** ($\oint P\,dx+Q\,dy=\iint(Q_x-P_y)\,dA$), which (a) requires $C^1$ reg
ularity, not mere differentiability, and (b) gives a nonzero right-hand side in
general. Goursat's achievement—dropping the continuity hypothesis on the derivat
ive—has **no real counterpart**; the complex-linear structure is exactly what ma
kes it possible.

### Conclusion

Goursat's theorem does **not** hold under the hypothesis of $\mathbb{R}^2$-diffe
rentiability. The function $f(z)=\bar z$ is a smooth counterexample: it is $\mat
hbb{R}^2$-differentiable everywhere yet $\oint_{\partial T}\bar z\,dz=i\neq 0$ f
or the triangle $T=\operatorname{conv}\{0,1,i\}$.
