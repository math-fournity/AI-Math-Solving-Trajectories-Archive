**Answer: Yes.** A Lévy process which is a.s. non‑negative for all times is nece
ssarily a subordinator, hence a.s. nondecreasing.

---

### Proof

Let $X$ be a Lévy process on $[0,\infty)$ with Lévy–Khintchine triplet $(b,\sigm
a^{2},\nu)$ (truncation $h(x)=x\,\mathbf 1_{\{|x|\le 1\}}$), so that for real $u
$
$$
\mathbb E\big[e^{iuX(1)}\big]=\exp\!\Big(iub-\tfrac12\sigma^{2}u^{2}+\int_{\math
bb R}\big(e^{iux}-1-iux\,\mathbf 1_{\{|x|\le 1\}}\big)\,\nu(dx)\Big).
$$

**Step 0 — reduce to a fixed‑time statement.** The hypothesis
$$
\mathbb P\big(X(t)\ge 0\ \text{for all }t\ge 0\big)=1
$$
implies, for every *fixed* $t\ge 0$, that $\mathbb P(X(t)\ge 0)=1$. Hence the la
w of $X(t)$ is supported on $[0,\infty)$, and the Laplace transform
$$
L_t(\lambda):=\mathbb E\big[e^{-\lambda X(t)}\big]\in(0,1],\qquad \lambda\ge 0,
$$
is finite for all $\lambda\ge 0$. By the Lévy–Khintchine formula for the Laplace
 transform (valid whenever $L_1(\lambda)<\infty$, which holds here), for every $
\lambda\ge 0$
$$
\log L_1(\lambda)=-\lambda b+\tfrac12\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}+\int_{\mathbb R}\big(
e^{-\lambda x}-1+\lambda x\,\mathbf 1_{\{|x|\le 1\}}\big)\,\nu(dx).
$$
Define the **Laplace exponent**
$$
\Phi(\lambda):=-\log L_1(\lambda)=\lambda b-\tfrac12\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}-\int_{
\mathbb R}\big(e^{-\lambda x}-1+\lambda x\,\mathbf 1_{\{|x|\le 1\}}\big)\,\nu(dx
).
$$
Since $L_1(\lambda)\le 1$, we have the key constraint
$$
\Phi(\lambda)\ge 0\qquad\text{for all }\lambda\ge 0. \tag{$\star$}
$$
Also $L_t(\lambda)=L_1(\lambda)^{t}=e^{-t\Phi(\lambda)}$.

---

**Step 1 — the Lévy measure has no negative part: $\nu((-\infty,0))=0$.**

Suppose $\nu((-\infty,0))>0$. For every $x<0$ the integrand $e^{-\lambda x}-1+\l
ambda x\,\mathbf 1_{\{|x|\le 1\}}=e^{\lambda|x|}-1-\lambda|x|\,\mathbf 1_{\{|x|\
le 1\}}$ tends to $+\infty$ as $\lambda\to\infty$, and it is increasing in $\lam
bda$. By monotone convergence,
$$
\int_{x<0}\big(e^{-\lambda x}-1+\lambda x\,\mathbf 1_{\{|x|\le 1\}}\big)\,\nu(dx
)\xrightarrow[\lambda\to\infty]{}+\infty
$$
(super‑exponentially, since $e^{\lambda|x|}$ dominates). The remaining part of t
he integral grows at most polynomially in $\lambda$ (it is $O(\lambda^{2})$ near
 $0$ and $O(\lambda)$ elsewhere). Therefore the whole integral $I(\lambda):=\int
(\cdots)\,\nu(dx)\to+\infty$ faster than any polynomial, while $\lambda b-\tfrac
12\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}=O(\lambda^{2})$. Consequently
$$
\Phi(\lambda)=\lambda b-\tfrac12\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}-I(\lambda)\xrightarrow[\la
mbda\to\infty]{}-\infty,
$$
contradicting $(\star)$. Hence $\nu((-\infty,0))=0$ (and $\nu(\{0\})=0$ always),
 so **$\nu$ is supported on $(0,\infty)$**.

---

**Step 2 — no Gaussian part: $\sigma=0$.**

With $\nu$ on $(0,\infty)$,
$$
I(\lambda)=\int_{0<x\le 1}(e^{-\lambda x}-1+\lambda x)\,\nu(dx)+\int_{x>1}(e^{-\
lambda x}-1)\,\nu(dx).
$$
The first integrand is $\ge 0$ (since $e^{-y}-1+y\ge 0$ for $y\ge 0$) and the se
cond is $\le 0$. In particular $I(\lambda)\ge -\nu((1,\infty))$, so $I(\lambda)$
 is bounded below. Thus
$$
\Phi(\lambda)=\lambda b-\tfrac12\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}-I(\lambda)\le \lambda b-\t
frac12\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}+\nu((1,\infty)).
$$
If $\sigma>0$, the right‑hand side $\to-\infty$, contradicting $(\star)$. Hence
**$\sigma=0$**.

---

**Step 3 — finite variation and non‑negative drift.**

Now
$$
\Phi(\lambda)=\lambda b-I(\lambda),\qquad
\frac{I(\lambda)}{\lambda}=\int_{0<x\le 1}\!\Big(x-\frac{1-e^{-\lambda x}}{\lamb
da}\Big)\,\nu(dx)+\int_{x>1}\frac{e^{-\lambda x}-1}{\lambda}\,\nu(dx).
$$
For $x>0$, $(1-e^{-\lambda x})/\lambda\downarrow 0$ and $(e^{-\lambda x}-1)/\lam
bda\to 0$ as $\lambda\to\infty$; by monotone convergence
$$
\frac{I(\lambda)}{\lambda}\xrightarrow[\lambda\to\infty]{}\int_{0}^{1}x\,\nu(dx)
\in[0,\infty].
$$
Since $\Phi(\lambda)\ge 0$ for all $\lambda$, we have $\liminf_{\lambda\to\infty
}\Phi(\lambda)/\lambda\ge 0$, i.e.
$$
b-\int_{0}^{1}x\,\nu(dx)\ge 0.
$$
This forces $\int_{0}^{1}x\,\nu(dx)<\infty$ (otherwise the left side is $-\infty
$). Together with $\nu((1,\infty))<\infty$ (true for any Lévy measure) this give
s
$$
\int_{(0,\infty)}(1\wedge x)\,\nu(dx)<\infty,
$$
i.e. **$X$ is of finite variation**, and the **drift**
$$
d:=b-\int_{0}^{1}x\,\nu(dx)\ge 0.
$$

---

**Step 4 — conclusion.**

We have shown:
- $\sigma=0$ (no Brownian part),
- $\nu$ is supported on $(0,\infty)$ (no negative jumps),
- $\int(1\wedge x)\,\nu(dx)<\infty$ (finite variation),
- $d\ge 0$.

These are precisely the defining conditions of a **subordinator**. Consequently
$X$ admits the representation
$$
X(t)=d\,t+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{(0,\infty)}x\,N(ds,dx),
$$
where $N$ is a Poisson random measure with intensity $ds\,\nu(dx)$ on $(0,\infty
)$. Both terms are nondecreasing in $t$: $d\,t$ because $d\ge 0$, and the integr
al because it is a sum of positive jumps with absolutely convergent total variat
ion. Therefore, almost surely, $t\mapsto X(t)$ is nondecreasing.

---

Thus the hypothesis "$X(t)\ge 0$ for all $t\ge 0$ a.s." does imply that $X$ is n
ondecreasing a.s.
