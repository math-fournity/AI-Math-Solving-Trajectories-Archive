## Proof

We prove the statement is **true**: under the hypotheses $f, f', xf \in L^2(\mat
hbb{R})$, we have $\lim_{|x|\to\infty} x|f(x)|^2 = 0$.

**Step 1. Setup and regularity of $f$.**

Since $f, f' \in L^2(\mathbb{R})$, we have $f \in H^1(\mathbb{R})$. By Sobolev e
mbedding in one dimension, $f$ is (locally) absolutely continuous, continuous, a
nd $f(x) \to 0$ as $|x| \to \infty$.

Define
$$g(x) := x\,|f(x)|^2.$$
Since $f$ is locally absolutely continuous and $x$ is smooth, $g$ is locally abs
olutely continuous. Computing the derivative (valid a.e.):
$$g'(x) = |f(x)|^2 + 2x\,\operatorname{Re}\!\big(\overline{f(x)}\,f'(x)\big).$$

**Step 2. Show $g' \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$.**

We bound each term. First,
$$\int_{\mathbb{R}} |f(x)|^2\,dx = \|f\|_2^2 < \infty.$$

Second, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,
$$\int_{\mathbb{R}} \big|x\,\overline{f(x)}\,f'(x)\big|\,dx \le \Big(\int_{\math
bb{R}} x^2|f(x)|^2\,dx\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}} |f'(x)|^2\,dx\Big)^{1/2}
 = \|xf\|_2\,\|f'\|_2 < \infty,$$
where we used $xf \in L^2$ and $f' \in L^2$.

Therefore $g' \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$.

**Step 3. Existence of limits at $\pm\infty$.**

Since $g$ is locally absolutely continuous and $g' \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, for any
 $x$,
$$g(x) = g(0) + \int_0^x g'(t)\,dt.$$
Because $g' \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, the integrals $\int_0^{\infty} g'(t)\,dt$ and
$\int_{-\infty}^{0} g'(t)\,dt$ converge. Hence the finite limits
$$L_+ := \lim_{x\to+\infty} g(x) = g(0) + \int_0^{\infty} g'(t)\,dt, \qquad L_-
:= \lim_{x\to-\infty} g(x) = g(0) - \int_{-\infty}^{0} g'(t)\,dt$$
both exist.

**Step 4. The limits must be zero.**

Note that $g(x) = x|f(x)|^2 \ge 0$ for $x > 0$, so $L_+ \ge 0$. Likewise $g(x) \
le 0$ for $x < 0$, so $L_- \le 0$.

Suppose for contradiction that $L_+ > 0$. Then there exists $R > 0$ such that fo
r all $x \ge R$,
$$x|f(x)|^2 \ge \frac{L_+}{2}, \quad\text{i.e.,}\quad |f(x)|^2 \ge \frac{L_+}{2x
}.$$
Multiplying by $x^2$:
$$x^2|f(x)|^2 \ge \frac{L_+\,x}{2}, \qquad x \ge R.$$
Integrating,
$$\int_R^{\infty} x^2|f(x)|^2\,dx \ge \frac{L_+}{2}\int_R^{\infty} x\,dx = +\inf
ty,$$
contradicting $xf \in L^2(\mathbb{R})$, which requires $\int_{\mathbb{R}} x^2|f(
x)|^2\,dx < \infty$. Hence $L_+ = 0$.

Similarly, suppose $L_- < 0$. Then there exists $R > 0$ such that for all $x \le
 -R$,
$$x|f(x)|^2 \le \frac{L_-}{2} < 0, \quad\text{i.e.,}\quad |f(x)|^2 \ge \frac{|L_
-|}{2|x|}.$$
Multiplying by $x^2$:
$$x^2|f(x)|^2 \ge \frac{|L_-|\,|x|}{2}, \qquad x \le -R.$$
Integrating,
$$\int_{-\infty}^{-R} x^2|f(x)|^2\,dx \ge \frac{|L_-|}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{-R} |x|
\,dx = +\infty,$$
again contradicting $xf \in L^2(\mathbb{R})$. Hence $L_- = 0$.

**Conclusion.**

Combining Steps 3 and 4,
$$\lim_{x\to+\infty} x|f(x)|^2 = 0 \quad\text{and}\quad \lim_{x\to-\infty} x|f(x
)|^2 = 0,$$
which is precisely
$$\lim_{|x|\to\infty} x|f(x)|^2 = 0. \qquad\blacksquare$$
