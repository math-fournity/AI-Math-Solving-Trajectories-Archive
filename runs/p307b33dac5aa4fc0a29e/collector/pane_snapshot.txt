## Solution

**Claim.** The constant function $f \equiv 0$ on $[0,1]\times[0,1]$ is continuou
s and equals almost everywhere the function
$$
g(x,y) = \begin{cases} 1, & (x,y)\in \mathbb{Q}^2,\\[4pt] 0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
$$
which is discontinuous at every point of $[0,1]\times[0,1]$.

---

### Proof

**Step 1: $f$ is continuous.** The function $f(x,y)=0$ is a constant function on
 $[0,1]^2$, hence continuous (in fact uniformly continuous).

**Step 2: $g$ is discontinuous at every point.** Let $(x_0,y_0)\in[0,1]^2$ be ar
bitrary. We show $g$ is discontinuous at $(x_0,y_0)$.

In every open ball $B$ centered at $(x_0,y_0)$:
- There exists a point $(p,q)\in B$ with $p,q\in\mathbb{Q}$ (since $\mathbb{Q}^2
$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}^2$), so $g(p,q)=1$.
- There exists a point $(r,s)\in B$ with $r\notin\mathbb{Q}$ (since $\mathbb{R}\
setminus\mathbb{Q}$ is dense), so $g(r,s)=0$.

Thus within every neighborhood of $(x_0,y_0)$, $g$ takes both values $0$ and $1$
. Regardless of the value of $g(x_0,y_0)\in\{0,1\}$, choosing $\varepsilon = \tf
rac{1}{2}$ shows that no $\delta>0$ can make $|g(x,y)-g(x_0,y_0)|<\varepsilon$ f
or all $(x,y)$ within distance $\delta$. Hence $g$ is discontinuous at $(x_0,y_0
)$. Since $(x_0,y_0)$ was arbitrary, $g$ is **nowhere continuous**.

**Step 3: $f = g$ almost everywhere.** The two functions differ exactly on the s
et
$$
\{(x,y)\in[0,1]^2 : f(x,y)\ne g(x,y)\} = \mathbb{Q}^2 \cap [0,1]^2.
$$
The set $\mathbb{Q}^2\cap[0,1]^2$ is countable (a product of two countable sets)
, and every countable subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$ has two-dimensional Lebesgue meas
ure zero. Therefore
$$
f = g \quad \text{a.e. on } [0,1]^2. \qquad \blacksquare
$$

### PROOF COMPLETE
