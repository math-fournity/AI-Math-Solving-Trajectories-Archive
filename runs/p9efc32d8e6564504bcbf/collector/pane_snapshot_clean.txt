## Proof

**Claim:** $f$ is constant.

**Step 1: Setup and key observation.**

Since $g$ admits a primitive $G$ (i.e., $G' = g$), the function $g$ is a derivat
ive. Derivatives are Baire class 1 functions (as pointwise limits of the continu
ous functions $g_n(x) = n(G(x+1/n)-G(x))$). By Baire's theorem, the set $D$ of c
ontinuity points of $g$ is a **dense** $G_\delta$ subset of $\mathbb{R}$.

Since $f$ is convex on $\mathbb{R}$, it is continuous, and its left and right de
rivatives $f'_-(x)$ and $f'_+(x)$ exist everywhere, with $f'_-(x) \leq f'_+(x)$,
 and $f'_+$ is non-decreasing.

**Step 2: At every continuity point of $g$, both one-sided derivatives of $f$ va
nish.**

Let $x_0 \in D$, so $g$ is continuous at $x_0$. Let $I(x_0) = (x_0 - \delta_0, x
_0 + \delta_0)$ be the interval guaranteed by the hypothesis. Given any $\vareps
ilon > 0$, by continuity of $g$ at $x_0$, there exists $\delta_1 > 0$ such that
$|g(t) - g(x_0)| < \varepsilon/2$ whenever $|t - x_0| < \delta_1$.

Set $\delta = \min(\delta_0, \delta_1)$. For all $u, v \in (x_0 - \delta, x_0 +
\delta)$ with $u < x_0 < v$:
$$|g(u) - g(v)| \leq |g(u) - g(x_0)| + |g(x_0) - g(v)| < \varepsilon,$$
and by hypothesis:
$$\left|\frac{f(v) - f(u)}{v - u}\right| \leq |g(u) - g(v)| < \varepsilon. \tag{
$\ast$}$$

**Bound on $f'_-(x_0)$:** Fix $u \in (x_0 - \delta, x_0)$. Inequality $(\ast)$ h
olds for all $v \in (x_0, x_0 + \delta)$. Since $f$ is continuous (convex), lett
ing $v \to x_0^+$:
$$\left|\frac{f(x_0) - f(u)}{x_0 - u}\right| \leq \varepsilon.$$
This holds for all $u \in (x_0 - \delta, x_0)$. Letting $u \to x_0^-$:
$$|f'_-(x_0)| \leq \varepsilon.$$

**Bound on $f'_+(x_0)$:** Fix $v \in (x_0, x_0 + \delta)$. Inequality $(\ast)$ h
olds for all $u \in (x_0 - \delta, x_0)$. Letting $u \to x_0^-$ (using continuit
y of $f$):
$$\left|\frac{f(v) - f(x_0)}{v - x_0}\right| \leq \varepsilon.$$
This holds for all $v \in (x_0, x_0 + \delta)$. Letting $v \to x_0^+$:
$$|f'_+(x_0)| \leq \varepsilon.$$

Since $\varepsilon > 0$ was arbitrary:
$$f'_-(x_0) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad f'_+(x_0) = 0 \quad \text{for every } x_0
 \in D.$$

**Step 3: $f'_+ \equiv 0$ on all of $\mathbb{R}$.**

The right derivative $f'_+$ of a convex function is **non-decreasing**. The set
$D$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$, and $f'_+(d) = 0$ for all $d \in D$.

For any $x \in \mathbb{R}$:
- Choose $d_1 \in D$ with $d_1 < x$. Then $0 = f'_+(d_1) \leq f'_+(x)$ (non-decr
easing, $d_1 < x$).
- Choose $d_2 \in D$ with $d_2 > x$. Then $f'_+(x) \leq f'_+(d_2) = 0$ (non-decr
easing, $x < d_2$).

Therefore $f'_+(x) = 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Step 4: $f$ is constant.**

For a convex function, the right derivative $f'_+$ is non-decreasing and Lebesgu
e measurable, and the fundamental theorem of calculus holds:
$$f(b) - f(a) = \int_a^b f'_+(t)\,dt \quad \text{for all } a < b.$$
Since $f'_+(t) = 0$ for all $t$, we get $f(b) = f(a)$ for all $a, b \in \mathbb{
R}$. Hence $f$ is constant.

**Step 5: Verification.**

Any constant function $f \equiv c$ is convex, and the inequality $|f(u) - f(v)|
= 0 \leq (v-u)|g(u)-g(v)|$ holds trivially for any $g$. Thus every constant func
tion satisfies the hypotheses.

**Conclusion:** $f$ is a constant function.
