## Proof

**Setup.** Three infinite cylinders of radius 1, with axes lying in the $xy$-pla
ne, pairwise at angle $\pi/3$. Three lines through the origin at angles $0,\pi/3
,2\pi/3$ realize this (the angle between the lines at $0$ and $2\pi/3$ is $\min(
2\pi/3,\pi/3)=\pi/3$). A cylinder with axis direction $\mathbf{u}=(\cos\theta,\s
in\theta,0)$ is the set of points $\mathbf{p}=(x,y,z)$ with perpendicular distan
ce $\le 1$:
$$|\mathbf{p}|^2-(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{u})^2 \le 1.$$

**Polar form of the constraints.** Write $x=r\cos\varphi,\ y=r\sin\varphi$. The
in-plane perpendicular coordinate to the line at angle $\theta$ is $v_\theta = -
x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta = r\sin(\varphi-\theta)$, so the cylinder constraint is
$v_\theta^2+z^2\le 1$. For $\theta=0,\pi/3,2\pi/3$:
$$v_1 = r\sin\varphi,\qquad v_2 = r\sin(\varphi-\pi/3),\qquad v_3 = -r\sin(\varp
hi+\pi/3).$$
The intersection requires $z^2 \le 1 - M$ where $M = r^2\max(\sin^2\varphi,\ \si
n^2(\varphi-\pi/3),\ \sin^2(\varphi+\pi/3))$.

**Symmetry reduction.** The three lines divide the plane into 6 sectors of angle
 $\pi/3$. The quantity $M$ is invariant under the dihedral group $D_3$ (order 6)
, which permutes the three lines and acts transitively on the 6 sectors. Hence
$$V = 6\int_{\text{one sector}}\int 2\sqrt{1-M}\,dx\,dy.$$
Take the sector $\varphi\in[0,\pi/3]$. In this sector $|\sin(\varphi+\pi/3)|$ is
 the largest of the three absolute values, since
$$\sin(\varphi+\pi/3)-\sin\varphi = \cos(\varphi+\pi/6)\ge 0,\qquad \sin(\varphi
+\pi/3)-\sin(\pi/3-\varphi)=\sin\varphi\ge 0.$$
So $M = r^2\sin^2(\varphi+\pi/3)$ here, and the admissible range is $0\le r\le \
csc(\varphi+\pi/3)$.

**Inner $r$-integral.** With $s=\sin(\varphi+\pi/3)$:
$$\int_0^{1/s} 2r\sqrt{1-r^2s^2}\,dr = \frac{1}{s^2}\int_0^1 u^{1/2}\,du = \frac
{2}{3s^2}\quad(u=1-r^2s^2).$$

**Outer $\varphi$-integral.**
$$V = 6\int_0^{\pi/3}\frac{2}{3\sin^2(\varphi+\pi/3)}\,d\varphi = 4\int_0^{\pi/3
}\csc^2(\varphi+\pi/3)\,d\varphi = 4\Big[-\cot(\varphi+\pi/3)\Big]_0^{\pi/3}.$$
$$=4\big(-\cot(2\pi/3)+\cot(\pi/3)\big)=4\Big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3
}\Big)=\frac{8}{\sqrt3}=\frac{8\sqrt3}{3}.$$

**Verification of method.** Applying the identical procedure to two perpendicula
r cylinders (axes at $0,\pi/2$) recovers the classical Steinmetz volume $16/3$,
confirming the setup.

$$\boxed{V=\dfrac{8\sqrt{3}}{3}}$$
