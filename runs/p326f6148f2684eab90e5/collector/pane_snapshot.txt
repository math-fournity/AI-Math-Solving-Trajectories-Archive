**IMO 1986, Problem 1.**

Let $d$ be a positive integer, $d \notin \{2, 5, 13\}$. We must show that among
the four elements of $\{2, 5, 13, d\}$, there exist distinct $a, b$ with $ab - 1
$ **not** a perfect square.

**Observation.** The three "fixed" pairs among $\{2, 5, 13\}$ all give squares:
$$2\cdot5 - 1 = 9 = 3^2, \qquad 2\cdot13 - 1 = 25 = 5^2, \qquad 5\cdot13 - 1 = 6
4 = 8^2.$$
So if the claim failed, the only non-square products $ab-1$ would have to come f
rom pairs involving $d$. Thus the claim is equivalent to:

> It is impossible that all three of $2d-1,\; 5d-1,\; 13d-1$ are simultaneously
perfect squares.

We prove this by contradiction, working modulo $16$.

---

**Assume** $2d-1 = x^2$, $5d-1 = y^2$, $13d-1 = z^2$ for non-negative integers $
x, y, z$.

**Step 1: $d$ is odd.**
Since $2d - 1 = x^2$: if $x$ were even, $x^2 \equiv 0 \pmod{4}$, giving $2d \equ
iv 1 \pmod{4}$, impossible (left side even). Hence $x$ is odd, $x^2 \equiv 1 \pm
od{4}$, so $2d \equiv 2 \pmod{4}$, i.e. $d \equiv 1 \pmod{2}$. **$d$ is odd.**

**Step 2: $y$ and $z$ are even; write $y = 2a$, $z = 2b$.**
Since $d$ is odd, $5d$ and $13d$ are odd, so $5d - 1$ and $13d - 1$ are even. A
square that is even is divisible by $4$, so $y, z$ are even. Set $y = 2a,\; z =
2b$, giving
$$5d - 1 = 4a^2, \qquad 13d - 1 = 4b^2.$$

**Step 3: Narrow $d \bmod 16$.**
From Step 1, $d$ is odd. Also $d \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ (since $x$ odd gives $x^2 \e
quiv 1 \pmod{8}$, so $2d - 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{8}$, i.e. $d \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$). Th
us
$$d \bmod 16 \in \{1, 5, 9, 13\}.$$

Now use $5d - 1 = 4a^2$. Since $a^2 \bmod 4 \in \{0, 1\}$, we have $4a^2 \bmod 1
6 \in \{0, 4\}$. Compute $5d - 1 \bmod 16$ for each candidate:

| $d \bmod 16$ | $5d - 1 \bmod 16$ | in $\{0, 4\}$? |
|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| $1$ | $4$ | ✓ |
| $5$ | $24 \equiv 8$ | ✗ |
| $9$ | $44 \equiv 12$ | ✗ |
| $13$ | $64 \equiv 0$ | ✓ |

So $d \equiv 1$ or $13 \pmod{16}$.

**Step 4: Eliminate the remaining cases with $13d - 1 = 4b^2$.**
Again $4b^2 \bmod 16 \in \{0, 4\}$. Compute $13d - 1 \bmod 16$:

| $d \bmod 16$ | $13d - 1 \bmod 16$ | in $\{0, 4\}$? |
|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| $1$ | $13 - 1 = 12$ | ✗ |
| $13$ | $169 - 1 = 168 \equiv 8$ | ✗ |

Both surviving cases are eliminated: $12 \notin \{0, 4\}$ and $8 \notin \{0, 4\}
$.

---

**Conclusion.** The assumption that $2d-1$, $5d-1$, and $13d-1$ are all perfect
squares forces $d \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ (hence $d \bmod 16 \in \{1,5,9,13\}$), then
 forces $d \equiv 1$ or $13 \pmod{16}$ (from $5d-1 = 4a^2$), and finally rules o
ut both $d \equiv 1$ and $d \equiv 13 \pmod{16}$ (from $13d-1 = 4b^2$). This is
a contradiction.

Therefore at least one of $2d-1$, $5d-1$, $13d-1$ is **not** a perfect square. S
electing the corresponding pair $(a, b) \in \{(2, d), (5, d), (13, d)\}$ — which
 are distinct since $d \notin \{2, 5, 13\}$ — yields distinct $a, b \in \{2, 5,
13, d\}$ with $ab - 1$ not a perfect square. $\blacksquare$
