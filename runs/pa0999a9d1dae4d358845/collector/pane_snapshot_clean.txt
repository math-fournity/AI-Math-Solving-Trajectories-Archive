## Answer

**No.** The condition is **not sufficient**. The existence of such a plane $P$ d
oes not force $f$ to be differentiable at $(x,y)$.

### Counterexample

Let $f(x,y) = |x|$, and consider the point $p = (0,0,f(0,0)) = (0,0,0)$ on the s
urface $S = \{(x,y,|x|)\}$.

**Claim 1.** $f$ is not differentiable at $(0,0)$.

Indeed $f(h,0) = |h|$, so $\dfrac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h} = \dfrac{|h|}{h}$, which equ
als $+1$ for $h>0$ and $-1$ for $h<0$. The limit does not exist, so $f$ is not (
even partially) differentiable in $x$ at $0$, hence not differentiable at $(0,0)
$.

**Claim 2.** There exists a plane $P$ through $p$ such that for every smooth cur
ve $\gamma:I\to S$ through $p$, the tangent line to $\gamma$ at $p$ lies in $P$.

Take $P = \{(X,Y,Z): Z = 0\}$, the plane $z=0$ through the origin.

Let $\gamma(t) = (x(t),\, y(t),\, |x(t)|)$ be any smooth curve on $S$ with $\gam
ma(0) = (0,0,0)$, i.e. $x(0)=0$, $y(0)=0$. Smoothness of $\gamma$ forces the thi
rd component $t\mapsto |x(t)|$ to be smooth at $0$.

We show $x'(0)=0$. Suppose for contradiction that $x'(0)=a\neq 0$. Then $x(t) =
at + O(t^{2})$, so for all sufficiently small $t\neq 0$, $x(t)$ has the same sig
n as $at$, and consequently
$$|x(t)| = |a|\,|t| + O(t^{2}),$$
which is not differentiable at $t=0$ — contradicting the smoothness of $|x(t)|$.
 Hence $x'(0)=0$.

Since $x(0)=0$ and $x'(0)=0$, we have $x(t) = O(t^{2})$, and therefore $|x(t)| =
 O(t^{2})$ as well. Thus
$$\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}|x(t)| = 0.$$

So every tangent vector at $p$ has the form
$$\gamma'(0) = \bigl(x'(0),\, y'(0),\, \tfrac{d}{dt}|_{0}|x(t)|\bigr) = (0,\, b,
\, 0),\qquad b = y'(0).$$

Every such vector lies in the direction space of $P$ (its $z$-component is $0$),
 and therefore the tangent line $\{p + t\,\gamma'(0)\}$ lies in $P$.

Thus the plane $P:\, z=0$ satisfies the stated condition, yet $f$ is not differe
ntiable at $(0,0)$.

### Why the condition is too weak

The hypothesis only constrains the **tangent vectors of smooth curves** that act
ually exist on $S$. When $f$ is non-differentiable, the "bad" directions cannot
be realized as tangent directions of any *smooth* curve through $p$ (the composi
tion $t\mapsto f(x(t),y(t))$ fails to be smooth along them), so those directions
 simply do not appear among the $\gamma'(0)$. The remaining tangent vectors may
collapse into a low-dimensional — here even one-dimensional — set that trivially
 fits inside some plane $P$.

This is strictly weaker than the usual textbook definition of a tangent plane, w
hich additionally requires the plane to **approximate the surface**:
$$\frac{f(x+h,\, y+k) - f(x,y) - \alpha h - \beta k}{\sqrt{h^{2}+k^{2}}} \to 0,$
$
i.e. it constrains the surface itself, not merely the smooth curves lying on it.
 That stronger condition *is* equivalent to differentiability. The curve-based c
ondition in the problem is not.

### Conclusion

The existence of a plane $P$ through $p$ containing every tangent line of every
smooth curve $\gamma$ on $S$ through $p$ is **not sufficient** for $f$ to be dif
ferentiable at $(x,y)$, as demonstrated by $f(x,y)=|x|$ at $(0,0)$.
