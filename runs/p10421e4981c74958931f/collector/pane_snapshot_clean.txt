## Solution

Define
$$f(x) = \int_0^1 \frac{e^{-x^2(1+t)}}{1+t}\,dt.$$

Then $f(0) = \int_0^1 \frac{1}{1+t}\,dt = \ln 2$, and the given limit is
$$\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{f(x)-f(0)}{x} = f'(0).$$

**Computing $f'(x)$.** Differentiating under the integral sign (justified since
the integrand is smooth in $(x,t)$ on a neighborhood of $x=0$, $t\in[0,1]$):
$$f'(x) = \int_0^1 \frac{1}{1+t}\cdot\bigl(-2x(1+t)\bigr)\,e^{-x^2(1+t)}\,dt = -
2x\int_0^1 e^{-x^2(1+t)}\,dt.$$

Evaluating at $x=0$:
$$f'(0) = -2\cdot 0\cdot\int_0^1 e^{0}\,dt = 0.$$

**Verification via Taylor expansion.** Expanding the integrand for small $x$:
$$\frac{1}{x(1+t)}\Bigl(e^{-x^2(1+t)}-1\Bigr) = \frac{1}{x(1+t)}\Bigl[-x^2(1+t)+
\tfrac{x^4(1+t)^2}{2}-\cdots\Bigr] = -x+\frac{x^3(1+t)}{2}-\cdots$$

Integrating over $t\in[0,1]$:
$$\int_0^1\!\left(-x+\frac{x^3(1+t)}{2}-\cdots\right)dt = -x+\frac{3x^3}{4}-\cdo
ts \;\xrightarrow{x\to 0}\; 0.$$

Both methods agree.

$$\boxed{\lim_{x \to 0} \int_0^1 \frac{1}{x} \left( e^{-x^2(1+t)}(1+t)^{-1} - (1
+t)^{-1} \right) dt = 0.}$$
