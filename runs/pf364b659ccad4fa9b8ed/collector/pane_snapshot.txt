## Proof

The answer is **NO**. We provide an explicit counterexample.

### Construction (Barratt–Milnor)

Let $p = (0,0,0) \in \mathbb{R}^3$. For each positive integer $k$, let $S_k$ be
the 2-sphere in $\mathbb{R}^3$ of radius $1/k$ centered at $(1/k, 0, 0)$. One ve
rifies:

- **$p \in S_k$ for every $k$**: the distance from $p$ to the center $(1/k,0,0)$
 is $1/k$, equal to the radius.
- **$S_k \cap S_j = \{p\}$ for $k \neq j$**: solving the two sphere equations si
multaneously forces $x=0$ (by subtracting), then $y^2+z^2=0$, so $y=z=0$.

Define
$$X = \{p\} \cup \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} S_k \;\subset\; \mathbb{R}^3.$$

### $X$ is compact

$X$ is bounded (contained in the ball of radius $2$). $X$ is closed: any converg
ent sequence in $X$ either eventually lies in a single $S_k$ (which is closed) o
r has a subsequence with points on $S_{k_i}$ for $k_i \to \infty$, in which case
 the radii $1/k_i \to 0$ and the points converge to $p \in X$.

### The minimal embedding dimension is $n = 3$

$X \subset \mathbb{R}^3$, so $X$ embeds in $\mathbb{R}^3$. We show $X$ does **no
t** embed in $\mathbb{R}^2$. The sphere $S_1 \subset X$ is homeomorphic to $S^2$
. If $X$ embedded in $\mathbb{R}^2$, then $S_1 \cong S^2$ would embed in $\mathb
b{R}^2$. But $S^2$ cannot embed in $\mathbb{R}^2$: by **invariance of domain**,
any embedding of the compact 2-manifold $S^2$ into $\mathbb{R}^2$ would have ope
n image; but compact subsets of the Hausdorff space $\mathbb{R}^2$ are closed, s
o the image would be both open and closed in the connected space $\mathbb{R}^2$,
 hence all of $\mathbb{R}^2$—contradicting compactness of $S^2$. Therefore $n =
3$.

### $H_m(X) \neq 0$ for infinitely many $m > 3$

This is the theorem of Barratt and Milnor (*"An example of anomalous singular ho
mology,"* Proc. Amer. Math. Soc., 1962). They prove that for this space $X$, the
 singular homology group $H_q(X;\mathbb{Q})$ is nonzero for infinitely many valu
es of $q > 2$ (in particular, for infinitely many $q > 3$).

Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is torsion-free, the universal coefficient theorem gives
$$H_q(X;\mathbb{Q}) \cong H_q(X;\mathbb{Z}) \otimes \mathbb{Q},$$
so the nonvanishing of $H_q(X;\mathbb{Q})$ implies $H_q(X;\mathbb{Z})$ has a non
zero free part, hence $H_q(X;\mathbb{Z}) \neq 0$, for infinitely many $q > 3 = n
$.

### Why higher homology survives (intuition)

The spheres $S_k$ accumulate at $p$. A singular simplex $\sigma: \Delta^m \to X$
 can have its image meeting **infinitely many** $S_k$ simultaneously (by mapping
 different regions of $\Delta^m$ to different spheres, all glued through $p$). T
his allows construction of singular $m$-cycles for $m > 2$ that "thread through"
 multiple spheres. These cycles cannot be boundaries of $(m+1)$-chains, because
any such chain would need to coherently fill across infinitely many accumulating
 spheres—an impossibility forced by the wild topology at $p$. This is the "anoma
ly": the covering dimension of $X$ is $2$, so Čech cohomology vanishes above deg
ree $2$, but **singular** homology does not.

### Conclusion

We have exhibited a compact space $X$ with minimal embedding dimension $n = 3$ s
uch that $H_m(X) \neq 0$ for infinitely many $m > n$. Therefore, the statement *
*"for all $m > n$, $H_m(X) = 0$"** is **false** in general.

**Remark.** The statement *is* true for "tame" spaces (CW complexes, manifolds,
ANRs): if such a space embeds in $\mathbb{R}^n$, its covering dimension is at mo
st $n$, and for CW complexes $H_m = 0$ for $m > \dim(X) \leq n$. The counterexam
ple exploits the failure of this dimension bound for singular homology of wild c
ompact spaces.
