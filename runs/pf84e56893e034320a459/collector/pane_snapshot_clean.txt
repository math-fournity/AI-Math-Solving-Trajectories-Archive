**Answer: Yes, such a functional exists.**

**Proof.**

Recall that a bounded linear functional $f \in X'$ is said to *attain its norm*
if there exists $x \in X$ with $\|x\| = 1$ such that $f(x) = \|f\|$. Equivalentl
y (normalizing any nonzero maximizer), $f$ attains its norm iff there exists a *
*nonzero** $x \in X$ with
$$f(x) = \|f\|\cdot\|x\|.$$
(The element $x=0$ trivially satisfies the displayed equality, so the meaningful
 content of the question is whether a *nonzero* such $x$ exists; this is precise
ly the notion of norm-attainment.)

We use the following classical theorem:

> **James' Theorem (1964).** A Banach space $X$ is reflexive if and only if ever
y continuous linear functional $f \in X'$ attains its norm on the closed unit ba
ll $B_X = \{x : \|x\|\le 1\}$.

The "only if" direction (reflexivity $\Rightarrow$ every functional attains its
norm) admits a short proof: if $X$ is reflexive, the closed unit ball $B_X$ is w
eakly compact (by the Banach–Alaoglu theorem combined with the canonical embeddi
ng being surjective). Each $f \in X'$ is weakly continuous, so $f$ attains its s
upremum on the weakly compact set $B_X$; i.e., $f$ attains its norm.

The "if" direction (every functional attains its norm $\Rightarrow$ reflexivity)
 is the deep part, due to James.

Now apply the contrapositive of the "if" direction: since $X$ is **not reflexive
**, it is **not** the case that every $f \in X'$ attains its norm. Therefore the
re exists some $f \in X'$ that does **not** attain its norm.

For this $f$, there is no $x \in X$ with $\|x\| = 1$ and $f(x) = \|f\|$. Equival
ently, there is no **nonzero** $x \in X$ satisfying $f(x) = \|f\|\cdot\|x\|$ (if
 such a nonzero $x$ existed, normalizing $x/\|x\|$ would give a unit-norm maximi
zer, contradicting non-attainment).

Hence such a linear functional $f$ exists. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The element $x = 0$ always satisfies $f(x) = \|f\|\cdot\|x\|$ trivia
lly, so the question is necessarily interpreted as asking for a *nontrivial* (no
nzero) attaining element, which is the standard notion of a norm-attaining funct
ional. Under this (the only meaningful) reading, James' theorem gives the affirm
ative answer.
