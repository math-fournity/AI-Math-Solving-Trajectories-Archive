Solution

Answer: $n$ works if and only if $n$ is a power of $2$, i.e., $n \in {1, 2, 4,
8, 16, 32, \dots}$.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Key Observations

Since $2^n - 1$ is odd, we work with odd primes. Write $-9 = -1 \cdot 3^2$. For
an odd prime $p \neq 3$:

$$\left(\frac{-9}{p}\right) = \left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)\left(\frac{9}{p}\right)
= \left(\frac{-1}{p}\right).$$

This equals $1$ iff $p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, and $-1$ iff $p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$.

For $p = 3$: $-9 \equiv 0 \pmod{3}$, so we need $3 \mid m$, say $m = 3m'$,
giving $m^2 + 9 = 9(m'^2 + 1)$. If $v_3(2^n - 1) = a$, we need $3^a \mid 9(m'^2
+ 1)$, i.e., $3^{a-2} \mid m'^2 + 1$ (when $a \geq 3$). But $-1$ is not a QR mod
$3$, so we need $a \leq 2$.

Conditions for solvability:

  • (A) No prime $p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$, $p \neq 3$, divides $2^n - 1$.
  • (B) $v_3(2^n - 1) \leq 2$.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Necessity: $n$ must be a power of $2$

Odd $n \geq 3$ fails. For odd $n \geq 3$: $2^n - 1 \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$ (since $n
\geq 2$). Also $3 \nmid 2^n - 1$ (since $\operatorname{ord}_3(2) = 2$ and $n$ is
odd). Since $2^n - 1 > 1$ and $\equiv 3 \pmod{4}$, at least one prime factor $p$
satisfies $p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$, and $p \neq 3$. Condition (A) fails.

Even $n$ with odd factor $d > 1$ fails. Write $n = 2^a \cdot d$ with $d$ odd, $d
\geq 3$. Then $2^d - 1 \mid 2^n - 1$ (since $d \mid n$). As shown above, $2^d -
1$ has a prime factor $p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$, $p \neq 3$. This $p$ divides $2^n -
1$, so condition (A) fails.

Therefore $n$ must be a power of $2$.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Sufficiency: Every power of $2$ works

Let $n = 2^a$ with $a \geq 0$.

Case $a = 0$ ($n = 1$): $2^1 - 1 = 1$ divides everything. ✓

Case $a \geq 1$: Let $p$ be a prime dividing $2^{2^a} - 1$.

  • $\operatorname{ord}_p(2)$ divides $2^a$, so $\operatorname{ord}_p(2) = 2^b$
  for some $0 \leq b \leq a$.
  • $b = 0$ is impossible ($p \mid 2^1 - 1 = 1$).
  • $b = 1$: $\operatorname{ord}_p(2) = 2$, so $p \mid 2^2 - 1 = 3$, giving $p =
  3$.
  • $b \geq 2$: Then $4 \mid \operatorname{ord}_p(2) \mid p - 1$, so $p \equiv 1
  \pmod{4}$.

So every prime $p \neq 3$ dividing $2^{2^a} - 1$ satisfies $p \equiv 1
\pmod{4}$. Condition (A) holds.

For $p = 3$: $v_3(2^{2^a} - 1) = 1 + v_3(2^{a-1}) = 1$ (using $v_3(2^n - 1) = 1
+ v_3(n/2)$ for even $n$, and $v_3(2^{a-1}) = 0$). So $v_3 = 1 \leq 2$.
Condition (B) holds.

Constructing $m$ via CRT: Write $2^{2^a} - 1 = 3 \cdot \prod p_i^{e_i}$ where
each $p_i \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$.

  • Mod $3$: Need $m^2 \equiv 0 \pmod{3}$, i.e., $m \equiv 0 \pmod{3}$. Solution
  exists.
  • Mod $p_i^{e_i}$: Since $p_i \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ and $p_i \neq 3$, we have $
  \left(\frac{-9}{p_i}\right) = \left(\frac{-1}{p_i}\right) = 1$. Since $p_i
  \nmid (-9)$, by Hensel's lemma $-9$ is a QR mod $p_i^{e_i}$. Solution exists.

By CRT, there exists $m$ with $m^2 \equiv -9 \pmod{2^{2^a} - 1}$, i.e., $2^{2^a}
- 1 \mid m^2 + 9$. ✓
