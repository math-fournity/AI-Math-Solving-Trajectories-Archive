## Proof

**Example.** Let $\mathbb{H}$ be the ring of real quaternions:
$$\mathbb{H} = \{a + bi + cj + dk : a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{R}\},$$
with the usual addition and Hamilton's multiplication rules $i^2=j^2=k^2=ijk=-1$
, $ij=k$, $jk=i$, $ki=j$, and $ji=-k$, $kj=-i$, $ik=-j$.

**Claim.** $\mathbb{H}$ is a non-commutative ring with multiplicative identity t
hat has no non-trivial maximal ideals.

---

**Step 1: $\mathbb{H}$ is a ring with identity $1$.** Addition is componentwise
(over $\mathbb{R}^4$), making $(\mathbb{H},+)$ an abelian group. Multiplication
is $\mathbb{R}$-bilinear and associative (a standard verification from the defin
ing relations). The element $1 = 1+0i+0j+0k$ satisfies $1\cdot q = q\cdot 1 = q$
 for all $q\in\mathbb{H}$.

**Step 2: $\mathbb{H}$ is non-commutative.** We have $ij = k$ but $ji = -k \neq
k$, so $ij \neq ji$.

**Step 3: $\mathbb{H}$ is a division ring (every nonzero element is a unit).** F
or $q = a+bi+cj+dk \neq 0$, define its conjugate $\bar{q} = a-bi-cj-dk$ and its
norm $N(q) = q\bar{q} = a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2 \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$. Then
$$q^{-1} = \frac{\bar{q}}{N(q)}$$
satisfies $q\cdot q^{-1} = q^{-1}\cdot q = 1$. Hence every nonzero element is in
vertible.

**Step 4: The only (two-sided) ideals of $\mathbb{H}$ are $\{0\}$ and $\mathbb{H
}$.** Let $I \subseteq \mathbb{H}$ be a nonzero two-sided ideal, and pick $0 \ne
q q \in I$. Since $q$ is a unit (Step 3), for any $r \in \mathbb{H}$ we have $r
= (rq^{-1})q \in I$ (using that $I$ is a left ideal) — or equivalently $r = q(q^
{-1}r) \in I$ (using that $I$ is a right ideal). Thus $I = \mathbb{H}$.

**Step 5: $\mathbb{H}$ has no non-trivial maximal ideals.** Since $\mathbb{H}$ i
s unital, maximal (two-sided) ideals exist by Zorn's lemma. The only proper idea
l is $\{0\}$ (Step 4), so $\{0\}$ is the unique maximal ideal. The ideal $\{0\}$
 is the **trivial** ideal. Therefore every maximal ideal of $\mathbb{H}$ is triv
ial, i.e., $\mathbb{H}$ has **no non-trivial maximal ideals**.

---

**Conclusion.** The real quaternion ring $\mathbb{H}$ is a non-commutative ring
with multiplicative identity $1$, and its only maximal ideal is the trivial idea
l $\{0\}$. Hence $\mathbb{H}$ is a specific example of a non-commutative unital
ring lacking non-trivial maximal ideals.
