Yes, a singular algebraic variety $A$ can be birationally equivalent to a smooth
 algebraic variety $B$ over $\mathbb{C}$. In fact, this is always possible: ever
y algebraic variety over $\mathbb{C}$ admits a resolution of singularities.

**Proof.**

Let $A$ be any (possibly singular) algebraic variety over $\mathbb{C}$. By Hiron
aka's theorem on resolution of singularities (1964), there exists a smooth algeb
raic variety $B$ over $\mathbb{C}$ together with a proper birational morphism
$$f: B \to A$$
such that $f$ is an isomorphism over the smooth locus $A_{\mathrm{sm}}$ of $A$.
Concretely, $B$ is obtained from $A$ by a sequence of blowups along smooth cente
rs contained in the singular locus of $A$ (and its successive strict transforms)
.

Since $f$ is birational, $B$ and $A$ are birationally equivalent by definition:
there exist dense open subsets $U \subseteq A$ and $V \subseteq B$ with $f|_V: V
 \to U$ an isomorphism. In particular, $f$ induces an isomorphism of function fi
elds
$$f^*: \mathbb{C}(A) \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;} \mathbb{C}(B),$$
which is the defining condition for birational equivalence of varieties over $\m
athbb{C}$.

Moreover, $B$ is smooth (nonsingular) by construction. Therefore:

- If $A$ is singular, then $A$ and $B$ are **not isomorphic** (since smoothness
is an isomorphism invariant), yet they **are birationally equivalent**. This giv
es a concrete example of the phenomenon in question.
- If $A$ is already smooth, we may simply take $B = A$.

Thus a singular variety can indeed be birationally equivalent to a smooth variet
y over $\mathbb{C}$. The resolution $f: B \to A$ provides the birational map, an
d $B$ is smooth while $A$ may be singular.

**Remark.** This is not a vacuous or rare phenomenon. A classic concrete example
 is the nodal cubic curve $A: y^2 = x^2(x+1)$ in $\mathbb{A}^2_\mathbb{C}$, whic
h has a singular node at the origin. Its normalization $\widetilde{A} \cong \mat
hbb{A}^1_\mathbb{C}$ is smooth, and the normalization map $\nu: \widetilde{A} \t
o A$ is birational (it is bijective on a dense open set and induces an isomorphi
sm of function fields). Here $B = \widetilde{A} \cong \mathbb{A}^1$ is smooth wh
ile $A$ is singular, yet they are birationally equivalent.

The key point is that **birational equivalence is a much coarser relation than i
somorphism**: it only requires agreement on dense open sets, so singularities—wh
ich are confined to proper closed subsets—can be "resolved" without changing the
 birational class. Hironaka's resolution theorem guarantees this can always be d
one over fields of characteristic zero, and in particular over $\mathbb{C}$.
