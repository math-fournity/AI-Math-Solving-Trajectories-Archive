Yes, the statement is **true**. It is Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange form of
 the remainder, expanded about the endpoint $b$ and evaluated at $a$. Here is th
e proof.

---

**Theorem.** Let $f \in \mathcal{C}^n([a,b])$ and suppose $f$ is $n+1$ times dif
ferentiable on $(a,b)$. Then there exists $c$ strictly between $a$ and $b$ such
that
$$
f(a)=f(b)+f'(b)(a-b)+\cdots+\frac{f^{(n)}(b)}{n!}(a-b)^n+\frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+
1)!}(a-b)^{n+1}.
$$

**Proof.** The case $a=b$ is vacuous (the interval $(a,b)$ is empty), so assume
$a\neq b$. Set
$$
P(x)=f(b)+f'(b)(x-b)+\frac{f''(b)}{2!}(x-b)^2+\cdots+\frac{f^{(n)}(b)}{n!}(x-b)^
n,
$$
the Taylor polynomial of $f$ of degree $n$ centered at $b$, and let
$$
g(x)=f(x)-P(x).
$$
Since $P^{(k)}(b)=f^{(k)}(b)$ for $k=0,1,\dots,n$, we have
$$
g^{(k)}(b)=0,\qquad k=0,1,\dots,n. \tag{1}
$$
Also $P^{(n+1)}\equiv 0$, so
$$
g^{(n+1)}(x)=f^{(n+1)}(x)\quad\text{on }(a,b). \tag{2}
$$
The desired conclusion is equivalent to
$$
g(a)=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}(a-b)^{n+1}
$$
for some $c$ between $a$ and $b$.

Define the constant
$$
\lambda=\frac{g(a)}{(a-b)^{n+1}},
$$
and consider the auxiliary function
$$
\varphi(x)=g(x)-\lambda\,(x-b)^{n+1}.
$$
Because $(x-b)^{n+1}$ and its first $n$ derivatives all vanish at $x=b$, equatio
n (1) gives
$$
\varphi^{(k)}(b)=0,\qquad k=0,1,\dots,n. \tag{3}
$$
Moreover,
$$
\varphi(b)=g(b)=0,\qquad \varphi(a)=g(a)-\lambda(a-b)^{n+1}=g(a)-g(a)=0. \tag{4}
$$

We now apply Rolle's theorem repeatedly. From $\varphi(b)=\varphi(a)=0$ and the
continuity of $\varphi$ on the closed interval between $a$ and $b$ together with
 differentiability on its interior, Rolle's theorem yields a point $c_1$ strictl
y between $a$ and $b$ with $\varphi'(c_1)=0$. Since also $\varphi'(b)=0$ by (3),
 applying Rolle's theorem to $\varphi'$ on the interval between $b$ and $c_1$ yi
elds $c_2$ with $\varphi''(c_2)=0$. Continuing inductively — at each stage using
 $\varphi^{(k)}(b)=0$ from (3) together with the zero $\varphi^{(k)}(c_k)=0$ jus
t produced — after $n+1$ applications we obtain a point $c=c_{n+1}$ strictly bet
ween $a$ and $b$ such that
$$
\varphi^{(n+1)}(c)=0. \tag{5}
$$
(The hypotheses $f\in\mathcal C^n([a,b])$ and $f^{(n+1)}$ exists on $(a,b)$ guar
antee that each $\varphi^{(k)}$, for $k\le n$, is continuous on the closed inter
val and differentiable on its interior, so every application of Rolle's theorem
is legitimate; the final step only requires $\varphi^{(n)}$ to be differentiable
 on the interior, which follows from the existence of $f^{(n+1)}$.)

Computing $\varphi^{(n+1)}$ directly:
$$
\varphi^{(n+1)}(x)=g^{(n+1)}(x)-\lambda\,(n+1)!=f^{(n+1)}(x)-\lambda\,(n+1)!,
$$
using (2). Substituting $x=c$ and (5) gives
$$
0=f^{(n+1)}(c)-\lambda\,(n+1)!,
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
\lambda=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}.
$$
Recalling the definition of $\lambda$,
$$
\frac{g(a)}{(a-b)^{n+1}}=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!},
$$
and therefore
$$
g(a)=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}(a-b)^{n+1}.
$$
Unfolding $g(a)=f(a)-P(a)$ yields exactly
$$
f(a)=f(b)+f'(b)(a-b)+\cdots+\frac{f^{(n)}(b)}{n!}(a-b)^n+\frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+
1)!}(a-b)^{n+1},
$$
with $c\in(a,b)$. $\blacksquare$

---

**Remark.** This is precisely Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange form of the rem
ainder, with the roles of the expansion center and evaluation point assigned to
$b$ and $a$ respectively. The hypothesis $f\in\mathcal C^n([a,b])$ (continuity o
f $f^{(n)}$ up to the endpoints) together with the existence of $f^{(n+1)}$ on $
(a,b)$ is exactly the standard hypothesis under which the Lagrange remainder is
valid; expanding about an endpoint rather than an interior point changes nothing
, since Rolle's theorem only requires the closed interval between the two points
.
