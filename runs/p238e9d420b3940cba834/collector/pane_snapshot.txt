## Proof

ext{homo}$, $\text{homo}\circ\text{anti}=\text{anti}$, $\text{anti}\circ\text{ho

**Claim.** The direct product $A \times B$ exists in $\bar{\mathcal{G}}$ if and
only if at least one of $A, B$ is abelian. When it exists, it is the usual direc
t product with standard projection homomorphisms.

### Part 1: If at least one of $A, B$ is abelian, the product exists.

Without loss of generality, assume $A$ is abelian. Take $P = A \times B$ with st
andard projections $\pi_A, \pi_B$ (both homomorphisms). Since $A$ is abelian, ev
ery homomorphism $f: X \to A$ is also an anti-homomorphism (because $f(x)f(y) =
f(y)f(x)$), and vice versa. So every morphism $f: X \to A$ in $\bar{\mathcal{G}}
$ is simultaneously a homomorphism and an anti-homomorphism.

Given any $X$ with morphisms $f: X \to A$ and $g: X \to B$, define $h: X \to A \
times B$ by $h(x) = (f(x), g(x))$. This is the unique set map satisfying $\pi_A
\circ h = f$ and $\pi_B \circ h = g$.

- If $g$ is a homomorphism: $h$ is a homomorphism (both components are homomorph
isms; $f$ is a homomorphism since it's both types). So $h$ is a morphism in $\ba
r{\mathcal{G}}$. ✓
- If $g$ is an anti-homomorphism: $h$ is an anti-homomorphism (both components a
re anti-homomorphisms; $f$ is anti since $A$ is abelian). So $h$ is a morphism i
n $\bar{\mathcal{G}}$. ✓

Uniqueness holds because $\pi_A, \pi_B$ are jointly injective. The case $B$ abel
ian is symmetric.

### Part 2: If both $A, B$ are non-abelian, the product does not exist.

Suppose both $A$ and $B$ are non-abelian. Let $P$ be any group with any morphism

Since $A$ is non-abelian, there exist $a_1, a_2 \in A$ with $a_1 a_2 \neq a_2 a_
1$. Since $B$ is non-abelian, there exist $b_1, b_2 \in B$ with $b_1 b_2 \neq b_
2 b_1$. Set $X = F_2 = \langle x, y \rangle$ (free group on two generators). We
construct four pairs of morphisms:

1(y)=b_2$ (not anti).

2. **(H, A):** $f_2 = f_1$ (homo, not anti); $g_2: X \to B$ anti-homomorphism wi
th $g_2(x)=b_1, g_2(y)=b_2$ (not homo since $b_1b_2 \neq b_2b_1$).

3. **(A, H):** $f_3: X \to A$ anti-homomorphism with $f_3(x)=a_1, f_3(y)=a_2$ (n
ot homo); $g_3 = g_1$ (homo, not anti).

4. **(A, A):** $f_3$ (anti, not homo); $g_2$ (anti, not homo).

For each pair, the universal property requires a morphism $h: X \to P$ with $\pi
_A \circ h = f_i$ and $\pi_B \circ h = g_i$. The type of $\pi_A \circ h$ is $\al
pha$ if $h$ is a homomorphism, and the opposite of $\alpha$ if $h$ is an anti-ho

**Type compatibility analysis.** For each pair, we need at least one choice of $
h$'s type to match both $f_i$'s and $g_i$'s types:

| Pair | $h$ homo requires | $h$ anti requires |
|------|-------------------|-------------------|
| (H, H) | $\alpha=H,\ \beta=H$ | $\alpha=A,\ \beta=A$ |
| (H, A) | $\alpha=H,\ \beta=A$ | $\alpha=A,\ \beta=H$ |
| (A, H) | $\alpha=A,\ \beta=H$ | $\alpha=H,\ \beta=A$ |

Pairs (H,H) and (A,A) require $\alpha = \beta$ (both projections same type).
Pairs (H,A) and (A,H) require $\alpha \neq \beta$ (projections opposite types).

These conditions are **mutually contradictory**: we cannot have $\alpha = \beta$

\pi_B$ satisfies the universal property for all four pairs. The product does not
