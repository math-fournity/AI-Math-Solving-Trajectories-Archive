## Solution

**Yes, $h$ remains a homeomorphism.**

**Setup.** Let $h: \mathbb{R}^\omega \to \mathbb{R}^\omega$ be defined by
$$h(x_1, x_2, \dots) = (a_1 x_1,\, a_2 x_2,\, \dots),$$
where each $a_i \neq 0$ (the sign is now unrestricted). We equip $\mathbb{R}^\om
ega$ with the **box topology**, whose basic open sets are $\prod_{i} U_i$ with e
ach $U_i$ open in $\mathbb{R}$.

**Proof.**

**Step 1: $h$ is a bijection.** Since every $a_i \neq 0$, the map $h$ is injecti
ve (if $h(x)=h(y)$ then $a_i x_i = a_i y_i$ for all $i$, so $x_i=y_i$). It is su
rjective because for any $y \in \mathbb{R}^\omega$, the point $x = (y_1/a_1,\, y
_2/a_2,\, \dots)$ satisfies $h(x)=y$. The inverse is
$$h^{-1}(y_1, y_2, \dots) = (y_1/a_1,\, y_2/a_2,\, \dots).$$

**Step 2: $h$ is continuous.** Let $V = \prod_i V_i$ be a basic box-open set. Th
en
$$h^{-1}(V) = \prod_i \{x_i \in \mathbb{R} : a_i x_i \in V_i\} = \prod_i \tfrac{
1}{a_i}V_i,$$
where $\tfrac{1}{a_i}V_i = \{x/a_i : x \in V_i\}$. Since multiplication by $1/a_
i$ is a homeomorphism of $\mathbb{R}$ (for any nonzero $a_i$, positive or negati
ve), each $\tfrac{1}{a_i}V_i$ is open in $\mathbb{R}$. Therefore $h^{-1}(V) = \p
rod_i \tfrac{1}{a_i}V_i$ is a product of open sets, hence **box-open**. So $h$ i
s continuous.

**Step 3: $h^{-1}$ is continuous.** The inverse $h^{-1}$ has exactly the same fo
rm as $h$—it is the coordinate-wise multiplication map $y_i \mapsto (1/a_i)\,y_i
$. By the identical argument as in Step 2 (with $a_i$ replaced by $1/a_i$, which
 is also nonzero), the preimage of any box-open set $\prod_i U_i$ under $h^{-1}$
 is $\prod_i a_i U_i$, a product of open sets, hence box-open. So $h^{-1}$ is co
ntinuous.

**Conclusion.** $h$ is a continuous bijection with continuous inverse, so $h$ is
 a homeomorphism in the box topology for **any** choice of nonzero $a_i$, regard
less of sign.

**Why the sign condition is irrelevant.** The map $x \mapsto ax$ is a homeomorph
ism of $\mathbb{R}$ for every $a \neq 0$—whether $a > 0$ or $a < 0$. A negative
$a_i$ simply reflects the $i$-th coordinate, which is perfectly continuous with
continuous inverse. The box topology treats each coordinate independently (a box
-open set places no constraint relating different coordinates), so there is no c
oupling between coordinates that could be disrupted by varying signs or unbounde
d magnitudes.

**Contrast with the uniform topology.** This stands in sharp contrast to the uni
form topology on $\mathbb{R}^\omega$ (induced by $\rho(x,y) = \sup_i \min(|x_i -
 y_i|, 1)$), where $h$ is a homeomorphism only when the sequence $(a_i)$ is **bo
unded** and **bounded away from $0$** (i.e., $0 < m \le a_i \le M$). The uniform
 metric couples all coordinates through the supremum, so unbounded $a_i$ or $a_i
 \to 0$ can destroy continuity of $h$ or $h^{-1}$. The box topology, being finer
 and coordinate-wise, imposes no such restriction—making $h$ a homeomorphism und
er the sole requirement $a_i \neq 0$.
