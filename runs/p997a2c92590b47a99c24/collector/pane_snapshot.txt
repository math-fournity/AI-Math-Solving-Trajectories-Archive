## Solution

The statement is **false**. No such function $h \in L^1(\lambda)$ exists.

### Proof

Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists $h \in L^1(\lambda)$ with
$$m(E) = \int_E h\,d\lambda \quad \text{for every measurable } E \subseteq [0,1]
.$$

**Step 1: Characterize $L^1(\lambda)$.** Since $\lambda$ is counting measure, fo
r any nonnegative measurable $f$,
$$\int f\,d\lambda = \sum_{x \in [0,1]} f(x),$$
where the integral (equivalently the sum over an uncountable index set) is finit
e only if $f$ is supported on at most countably many points. In particular, $h \
in L^1(\lambda)$ means $h$ is nonzero on at most countably many points and $\sum
_{x} |h(x)| < \infty$.

**Step 2: Evaluate on singletons.** For any $x \in [0,1]$, take $E = \{x\}$. The
n
$$m(\{x\}) = \int_{\{x\}} h\,d\lambda = h(x).$$
But Lebesgue measure of a singleton is $m(\{x\}) = 0$, so $h(x) = 0$ for every $
x \in [0,1]$.

**Step 3: Derive the contradiction.** Since $h(x)=0$ for every $x$, we have $h \
equiv 0$. Taking $E = [0,1]$,
$$m([0,1]) = \int_{[0,1]} h\,d\lambda = 0,$$
but $m([0,1]) = 1$. Contradiction.

### Remark on why Radon–Nikodym does not apply

One might be tempted to invoke the Radon–Nikodym theorem. Indeed $m \ll \lambda$
 holds trivially, since $\lambda(E)=0$ only when $E=\varnothing$ (counting measu
re vanishes only on the empty set), and $m(\varnothing)=0$. However, the Radon–N
ikodym theorem requires the dominating measure $\lambda$ to be **$\sigma$-finite
**. The counting measure on $[0,1]$ is not $\sigma$-finite: a set has finite cou
nting measure only if it is finite, and $[0,1]$ cannot be written as a countable
 union of finite sets (such a union would be countable, whereas $[0,1]$ is uncou
ntable). Hence the theorem's hypotheses fail, and — as the argument above shows
— the conclusion fails as well.
