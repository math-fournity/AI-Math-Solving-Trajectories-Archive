## Proof

**Step 1: Evaluate the integral in closed form.**

For $\operatorname{Re}(a)>-1$,
$$\int_{0}^{\infty} t^{a} e^{-3t}\,dt = 3^{-(a+1)}\,\Gamma(a+1),$$
obtained by the substitution $u=3t$. Setting $a=x^{2}+1$ (so $a+1=x^{2}+2$) give
s
$$g(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{x^{2}+1}e^{-3t}\,dt=3^{-(x^{2}+2)}\,\Gamma(x^{2}+2).$
$

**Step 2: Locate the singularities of $g$.**

The factor $3^{-(x^{2}+2)}=e^{-(x^{2}+2)\ln 3}$ is an entire function of $x$. He
nce the only singularities of $g$ come from the poles of $\Gamma(x^{2}+2)$.

The Gamma function $\Gamma(z)$ is meromorphic on $\mathbb{C}$ with simple poles
at $z=0,-1,-2,\dots$ and no other singularities. Thus $\Gamma(x^{2}+2)$ has pole
s exactly where
$$x^{2}+2\in\{0,-1,-2,\dots\},\qquad\text{i.e.}\quad x^{2}=-(n+2),\ n=0,1,2,\dot
s$$
The corresponding points are
$$x=\pm i\sqrt{n+2},\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots$$
At each such point the prefactor $3^{-(x^{2}+2)}=3^{-(-(n+2))}=3^{n+2}\neq 0$, s
o each pole of $\Gamma(x^{2}+2)$ is a genuine (simple) pole of $g$. Therefore th
e singularities of $g$ are precisely
$$\{\pm i\sqrt{n+2}:n=0,1,2,\dots\}.$$

**Step 3: Distance of the nearest singularity from the origin.**

The distances of these singularities from $0$ are $\sqrt{n+2}$, $n=0,1,2,\dots$,
 i.e.
$$\sqrt{2},\ \sqrt{3},\ \sqrt{4}=2,\ \sqrt{5},\dots$$
The nearest singularities to $0$ are $\pm i\sqrt{2}$, both at distance $\sqrt{2}
$.

**Step 4: Analyticity in a neighborhood of $0$.**

At $x=0$, $x^{2}+2=2$ and $\Gamma(2)=1! =1$ is finite, so $g(0)=3^{-2}\cdot 1=\t
frac{1}{9}$, and $g$ is analytic at $0$. More generally, inside the open disk $|
x|<\sqrt{2}$ we have $x^{2}+2\notin\{0,-1,-2,\dots\}$ (the closest hit, $x^{2}+2
=0$, occurs only at $|x|=\sqrt{2}$, on the boundary), so $\Gamma(x^{2}+2)$ is an
alytic throughout $|x|<\sqrt{2}$. Combined with the entire prefactor, $g$ is ana
lytic on the disk $|x|<\sqrt{2}$.

**Step 5: Radius of convergence.**

Since $g$ is analytic on $|x|<\sqrt{2}$ and has (non-removable) poles at $x=\pm
i\sqrt{2}$ lying on the circle $|x|=\sqrt{2}$, the Taylor series of $g$ about $0
$ converges exactly on the largest disk centered at $0$ free of singularities. B
y the standard characterization of the radius of convergence of a Taylor series
(it equals the distance from the center to the nearest singularity), the radius
of convergence is
$$R=\sqrt{2}.$$

**Conclusion.** The function $g(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{x^{2}+1}e^{-3t}\,dt=3^{-(
x^{2}+2)}\Gamma(x^{2}+2)$ is analytic in a neighborhood of $0$ (in particular on
 $|x|<\sqrt{2}$), so it admits a power-series expansion about $0$. The radius of
 convergence of this series is

$$\boxed{R=\sqrt{2}}.$$
