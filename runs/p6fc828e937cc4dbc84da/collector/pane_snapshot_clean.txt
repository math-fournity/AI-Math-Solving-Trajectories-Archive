## Proof

**Claim.** If $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is uniformly continuous and Lebesgue i
ntegrable (i.e., $\int_{\mathbb{R}} |f|\,dm < \infty$), then $\lim_{x\to\infty}
f(x)=0$.

**Proof.** Suppose, for contradiction, that $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)\neq 0$. Then
there exists $\varepsilon>0$ and a sequence $(x_n)_{n\geq 1}$ with $x_n\to\infty
$ such that
$$|f(x_n)|\ge \varepsilon \quad\text{for all } n.$$

Since $f$ is uniformly continuous, there exists $\delta>0$ such that for all $x,
y\in\mathbb{R}$,
$$|x-y|<\delta \implies |f(x)-f(y)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.$$

Hence for every $n$ and every $x\in(x_n-\delta,\,x_n+\delta)$,
$$|f(x)|\ge |f(x_n)|-|f(x)-f(x_n)| > \varepsilon - \frac{\varepsilon}{2} = \frac
{\varepsilon}{2}.$$

Since $x_n\to\infty$, we may extract a subsequence $(x_{n_k})_{k\ge 1}$ such tha
t the intervals $I_k:=(x_{n_k}-\delta,\,x_{n_k}+\delta)$ are pairwise disjoint (
e.g., choose $x_{n_{k+1}}-x_{n_k}>2\delta$, which is possible since $x_n\to\inft
y$).

On each $I_k$ we have $|f|\ge \varepsilon/2$, so
$$\int_{I_k}|f|\,dm \ge \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdot m(I_k)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\
cdot 2\delta = \varepsilon\delta.$$

Since the $I_k$ are pairwise disjoint and all lie in $\mathbb{R}$, by countable
additivity and monotonicity of the integral,
$$\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f|\,dm \ge \int_{\bigcup_k I_k}|f|\,dm = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}
\int_{I_k}|f|\,dm \ge \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon\delta = \infty.$$

This contradicts the assumption that $f$ is Lebesgue integrable ($\int_{\mathbb{
R}}|f|\,dm<\infty$).

Therefore $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=0$. $\blacksquare$
