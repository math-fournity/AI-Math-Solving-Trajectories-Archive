Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether $\Delta u \in L^p(S^1)$ wit
h the distributional relationship implies $u \in W^{2,p}(S^1)$.

**Answer: Yes.**

**Proof.**

Parametrize $S^1$ by $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$ with periodic identification. The La
place–Beltrami operator on $S^1$ is $\Delta = \frac{d^2}{d\theta^2}$.

We are given that $u$ is a distribution on $S^1$ whose distributional Laplacian
$\Delta u =: f$ belongs to $L^p(S^1)$, where $1 < p < \infty$. The distributiona
l relationship
$$\int_{S^1} \phi\, \Delta u = \int_{S^1} u\, \Delta\phi \quad \forall\, \phi \i
n C^\infty(S^1)$$
is precisely the statement that $f = \Delta u$ in the distributional sense, i.e.
, $u'' = f$.

**Step 1 (Zero mean of $f$).** Taking $\phi \equiv 1 \in C^\infty(S^1)$, we obta
in
$$\int_0^{2\pi} f(\theta)\,d\theta = \int_{S^1} \Delta u = \int_{S^1} u \cdot \D
elta(1) = 0.$$

**Step 2 (First integration).** Define $F(\theta) = \int_0^\theta f(t)\,dt$. Sin
ce $f \in L^p([0,2\pi])$, the function $F$ is absolutely continuous with $F' = f
$ a.e., so $F \in W^{1,p}([0,2\pi])$. By Step 1, $F(2\pi) = F(0) = 0$, so $F$ ex
tends to a periodic function in $W^{1,p}(S^1) \subset L^p(S^1)$.

**Step 3 ($u' \in L^p$).** Since $u'' = f = F'$ in the distributional sense, we
have $(u' - F)' = 0$ distributionally. A distribution on $S^1$ with zero derivat
ive is a constant, so $u' = F + C$ for some constant $C$. Since $F \in L^p(S^1)$
 and constants lie in $L^p(S^1)$, we conclude $u' \in L^p(S^1)$.

**Step 4 ($u \in L^p$).** Define $G(\theta) = \int_0^\theta u'(t)\,dt$. Since $u
' \in L^p(S^1)$, $G$ is absolutely continuous with $G' = u'$ a.e., so $G \in W^{
1,p}([0,2\pi])$. Since $u$ is a distribution on $S^1$ (hence periodic), $u'$ is
periodic, and its zeroth Fourier mode vanishes (as $\widehat{u'}(0) = i\cdot 0 \
cdot \hat{u}(0) = 0$), giving $\int_0^{2\pi} u' = 0$ and thus $G(2\pi) = G(0)$.
So $G$ extends to a periodic function in $W^{1,p}(S^1) \subset L^p(S^1)$. Now $(
u - G)' = u' - u' = 0$ distributionally, so $u = G + D$ for some constant $D$, g
iving $u \in L^p(S^1)$.

**Step 5 (Conclusion).** We have shown:
- $u \in L^p(S^1)$ (Step 4),
- $u' \in L^p(S^1)$ (Step 3),
- $u'' = f \in L^p(S^1)$ (given).

Therefore $u \in W^{2,p}(S^1)$.

**Remark.** The argument is elementary because on the one-dimensional manifold $
S^1$, the Laplacian is simply $d^2/d\theta^2$, and its inverse is given by itera
ted integration—a bounded, indeed compact, operator from $L^p$ to $W^{1,p}$. The
 hypothesis $1 < p < \infty$ is not needed in dimension one (the result holds fo
r all $1 \leq p \leq \infty$); it would become essential in higher dimensions wh
ere inverting $\Delta$ requires the Calderón–Zygmund theory of singular integral
s.
