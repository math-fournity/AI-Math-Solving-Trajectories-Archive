## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, there exists an eigenfunction of the linear transformation $h(f)
 = f \circ g$ on the space of analytic functions, for every continuously differe
ntiable $g:[0,1]\to[0,1]$.

**Proof.**

Consider any nonzero constant function $f(x) = c$, where $c \in \mathbb{R}\setmi
nus\{0\}$. This function is analytic on $[0,1]$ (it admits a power series expans
ion $f(x)=c$ converging everywhere).

For any continuously differentiable $g:[0,1]\to[0,1]$, we compute the action of
$h$:

$$h(f)(x) = f(g(x)) = c = f(x) \quad \text{for all } x \in [0,1].$$

Therefore $h(f) = 1 \cdot f$, which means $f$ is an eigenfunction of $h$ with ei
genvalue $\lambda = 1$.

Since a nonzero constant analytic function exists for **every** continuously dif
ferentiable $g:[0,1]\to[0,1]$, the composition operator $h$ always possesses at
least one eigenfunction. $\blacksquare$

**Remark (non-constant eigenfunctions).** The existence of non-constant eigenfun
ctions depends on $g$:

- **$g(x)=x$ (identity):** Every analytic function is an eigenfunction with $\la
mbda=1$.
- **$g(x)=1-x$:** Applying $h$ twice gives $h^2(f)=f$, so $\lambda^2=1$, i.e., $
\lambda=\pm 1$. Non-constant eigenfunctions exist: e.g., $f(x)=x-\tfrac{1}{2}$ s
atisfies $f(1-x)=-f(x)$ (eigenvalue $-1$), and $f(x)=(x-\tfrac{1}{2})^2$ satisfi
es $f(1-x)=f(x)$ (eigenvalue $1$).
- **$g(x)=x^2$:** Only constant eigenfunctions exist. Indeed, $f(x^2)=\lambda f(
x)$. If $\lambda=1$, then $f(x)=f(x^{1/2^n})\to f(1)$ by continuity, forcing $f$
 constant. If $\lambda\neq 1$, then $f(0)=f(1)=0$; substituting $x=e^{-t}$ and s
etting $F(t)=f(e^{-t})$ yields $F(2t)=\lambda F(t)$ with $F(0)=0$. Analyticity o
f $f$ near $x=1$ gives $F(t)\sim \alpha t^k$ near $t=0$ for some $k\geq 1$, and
iterating $F(t)=\lambda^n F(t/2^n)\sim \alpha t^k(\lambda/2^k)^n$ forces $\lambd
a=2^k$ and $F(t)=\alpha t^k$, i.e., $f(x)=\alpha(-\ln x)^k$, which is **not** an
alytic at $x=0$. Hence no non-constant analytic eigenfunction exists.

Thus, while non-constant eigenfunctions may or may not exist depending on $g$, *
*an eigenfunction always exists** — namely, any nonzero constant function with e
igenvalue $1$.
