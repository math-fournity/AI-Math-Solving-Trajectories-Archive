Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether there exists a bounded oper
ator on $l_2$ and a vector $x \neq 0$ such that the resolvent function $R(\lambd
a, T)x = (T - \lambda I)^{-1}x$ has a pole at an isolated point of $\sigma(T)$.

**Answer: Yes.**

**Proof.**

Consider the bounded operator $T: l_2 \to l_2$ defined on the standard orthonorm
al basis $\{e_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ by
$$Te_n = \frac{1}{n}\, e_n, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$$

This is a compact, self-adjoint (diagonal) operator with $\|T\| = 1$.

**Spectrum.** The spectrum of a diagonal operator on $l_2$ is the closure of its
 eigenvalue set:
$$\sigma(T) = \left\{\frac{1}{n} : n \geq 1\right\} \cup \{0\}.$$
Each point $\frac{1}{n}$ is an isolated point of $\sigma(T)$ (an eigenvalue of m
ultiplicity one), and $0$ is the unique accumulation point.

**Resolvent at an isolated spectral point.** Take $x = e_1 \neq 0$. For $\lambda
 \in \rho(T)$,
$$R(\lambda, T)\,x = (T - \lambda I)^{-1} e_1 = \frac{1}{1 - \lambda}\, e_1.$$

This is an $l_2$-valued function with a **simple pole** at $\lambda = 1$, which
is an isolated point of $\sigma(T)$.

More generally, for any $n \geq 1$ and $x = e_n$, the resolvent function
$$R(\lambda, T)\,e_n = \frac{1}{1/n - \lambda}\, e_n$$
has a simple pole at $\lambda = 1/n$, an isolated point of the spectrum.

**Remark on the general mechanism.** Whenever $\lambda_0$ is an isolated point o
f $\sigma(T)$, the Riesz projection $P = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint R(\lambda,T)\,d\l
ambda$ decomposes $l_2 = P(l_2) \oplus (I-P)(l_2)$, and $T|_{P(l_2)} - \lambda_0
 I$ is quasinilpotent. When this restriction is **nilpotent** (in particular, wh
enever $P(l_2)$ is finite-dimensional, as for eigenvalues of finite algebraic mu
ltiplicity), the Laurent expansion of the resolvent at $\lambda_0$ terminates on
 the negative-power side, yielding a pole. For any $x$ with $Px \neq 0$, the vec
tor-valued resolvent $R(\lambda,T)x$ then exhibits this pole. The diagonal opera
tor example above is the simplest instance: each isolated eigenvalue has finite
(one-dimensional) Riesz range, so the resolvent has a pole there for the corresp
onding eigenvector.
