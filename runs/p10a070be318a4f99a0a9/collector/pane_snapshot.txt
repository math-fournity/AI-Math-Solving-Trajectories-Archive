## Proof

**Key identity.** Since $1+iy = \sqrt{1+y^2}\,e^{i\arctan y}$, we get $\frac{e^{
ix\arctan y}}{(1+y^2)^{x/2}} = \frac{(1+iy)^x}{(1+y^2)^x} = \frac{1}{(1-iy)^x}$.
 Taking imaginary parts:

$$\frac{\sin(x\arctan y)}{(1+y^2)^{x/2}} = \operatorname{Im}\!\left[(1-iy)^{-x}\
right]$$

So the integral is $I(x) = \operatorname{Im}\!\left[\int_0^\infty \frac{(1-iy)^{
-x}}{e^{\pi y}+1}\,dy\right]$.

**Contour integral.** Define $h(z) = \frac{(1-iz)^{-x}}{e^{\pi z}+1}$. The branc
h point of $(1-iz)^{-x}$ is at $z=-i$; the branch cut runs from $-i$ downward al
ong the imaginary axis, entirely in the lower half-plane. Thus $h$ is meromorphi
c in the upper half-plane with poles at $z=(2k+1)i$, $k=0,1,2,\ldots$ (zeros of
$e^{\pi z}+1$).

Integrate $h$ over the contour: real axis from $-R$ to $R$, then the semicircle
$|z|=R$ in the upper half-plane. For $\operatorname{Re}(x)>1$, the semicircle in
tegral vanishes (exponential decay on the right, algebraic decay $R^{1-\operator
name{Re}(x)}$ on the left).

**Residues.** At $z=(2k+1)i$: since $e^{(2k+1)\pi i}=-1$, the residue of $\frac{
1}{e^{\pi z}+1}$ is $-\frac{1}{\pi}$, and $1-i(2k+1)i = 2k+2$, so:

$$\operatorname{Res}_{z=(2k+1)i} h(z) = -\frac{(2k+2)^{-x}}{\pi}$$

Summing: $\sum_{k=0}^\infty \operatorname{Res} = -\frac{1}{\pi\cdot 2^x}\sum_{k=
1}^\infty k^{-x} = -\frac{\zeta(x)}{\pi\cdot 2^x}$.

By the residue theorem: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(t)\,dt = 2\pi i\cdot\left(-\f
rac{\zeta(x)}{\pi\cdot 2^x}\right) = -\frac{2i\,\zeta(x)}{2^x}$.

**Evaluating the real-line integral.** Split at $t=0$ and substitute $t\mapsto -
t$ on $(-\infty,0)$:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(t)\,dt = \int_0^\infty \frac{(1-it)^{-x}}{e^{\pi t}+
1}\,dt + \int_0^\infty \frac{(1+it)^{-x}}{e^{-\pi t}+1}\,dt$$

Using $\frac{1}{e^{-\pi t}+1} = 1 - \frac{1}{e^{\pi t}+1}$:

$$= \int_0^\infty (1+it)^{-x}\,dt + \int_0^\infty \frac{(1-it)^{-x}-(1+it)^{-x}}
{e^{\pi t}+1}\,dt$$

The first integral (for $\operatorname{Re}(x)>1$): $\int_0^\infty (1+it)^{-x}\,d
t = \frac{-i}{x-1}$.

For the second: $(1-it)^{-x} - (1+it)^{-x} = 2i\,\operatorname{Im}[(1-it)^{-x}]
= \frac{2i\sin(x\arctan t)}{(1+t^2)^{x/2}}$, so the second integral equals $2i\,
I(x)$.

**Combining:**

$$\frac{-i}{x-1} + 2i\,I(x) = -\frac{2i\,\zeta(x)}{2^x}$$

$$\boxed{I(x) = \frac{1}{2(x-1)} - \frac{\zeta(x)}{2^x}}$$

**Analytic continuation.** The integral $I(x)$ converges for all $x\in\mathbb{R}
$ (exponential decay at $\infty$, integrable at $0$) and is entire in $x$. The r

ta(x)}{2^x}\sim\frac{1}{2(x-1)}$ and the pole cancels. Both sides are entire and

**Solving the equation.** The equation $I(x) = \frac{1}{2(x-1)}$ becomes:

$$\frac{1}{2(x-1)} - \frac{\zeta(x)}{2^x} = \frac{1}{2(x-1)} \implies \frac{\zet

(since $2^x \neq 0$). Note $x=1$ is excluded (LHS undefined).
