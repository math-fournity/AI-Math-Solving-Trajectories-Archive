## Proof (via contour integration)

$$f(z) = \frac{e^{iz}}{1+z^2}.$$
Its poles are the zeros of $1+z^2$, namely $z = i$ and $z = -i$, both simple. Le
t $\Gamma_R$ be the closed contour consisting of the segment $[-R, R]$ on the re
al axis followed by the upper semicircle $C_R = \{Re^{i\theta} : 0 \le \theta \l
e \pi\}$. For $R > 1$, the only pole inside $\Gamma_R$ is $z = i$.

**Step 2: Residue at $z = i$.** Since the pole is simple,
$$\operatorname{Res}_{z=i} f(z) = \lim_{z \to i} (z-i)\frac{e^{iz}}{(z-i)(z+i)}
= \frac{e^{i \cdot i}}{2i} = \frac{e^{-1}}{2i}.$$

By the residue theorem,
$$\oint_{\Gamma_R} f(z)\,dz = 2\pi i \cdot \frac{e^{-1}}{2i} = \frac{\pi}{e}.$$

**Step 3: The semicircle contribution vanishes.** On $C_R$, write $z = Re^{i\the
ta}$ with $\theta \in [0,\pi]$, so $\operatorname{Im} z = R\sin\theta \ge 0$. He

$$|e^{iz}| = e^{-\operatorname{Im} z} = e^{-R\sin\theta} \le 1.$$
Therefore
$$\left|\int_{C_R} f(z)\,dz\right| \le \int_0^\pi \frac{e^{-R\sin\theta}}{|1+R^2
 e^{2i\theta}|}\,R\,d\theta \le \frac{R}{R^2-1}\int_0^\pi e^{-R\sin\theta}\,d\th
eta.$$
Splitting $[0,\pi]$ into $[0,\pi/2]$ and $[\pi/2,\pi]$ and using $\sin\theta \ge
 \frac{2\theta}{\pi}$ on $[0,\pi/2]$ (Jordan's inequality),
$$\int_0^\pi e^{-R\sin\theta}\,d\theta = 2\int_0^{\pi/2} e^{-R\sin\theta}\,d\the
ta \le 2\int_0^{\pi/2} e^{-2R\theta/\pi}\,d\theta = \frac{\pi}{R}\left(1 - e^{-R
}\right) \le \frac{\pi}{R}.$$
So
$$\left|\int_{C_R} f(z)\,dz\right| \le \frac{R}{R^2-1}\cdot\frac{\pi}{R} = \frac
{\pi}{R^2-1} \xrightarrow{R\to\infty} 0.$$

**Step 4: Take the limit.** Letting $R \to \infty$,
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{e^{ix}}{1+x^2}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{e}.$$
