Claim. Yes, the action of $G$ on $V$ is trivial.

Let $\dim_{\mathbb{Q}_\ell} V = n$, and let $\rho: G \to \mathrm{GL}(V) \cong
\mathrm{GL}n(\mathbb{Q}\ell)$ be the continuous representation. We prove $\rho$
is the trivial homomorphism.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Step 1. The image $H := \rho(G)$ is a pro-$p$-group, and is conjugate into $
\mathrm{GL}n(\mathbb{Z}\ell)$.

Since $G$ is a pro-$p$-group (compact) and $\rho$ is continuous, $H = \rho(G)$
is compact (continuous image of compact is compact) and is a quotient of a pro-
$p$-group, hence a pro-$p$-group.

Any compact subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}n(\mathbb{Q}\ell)$ is conjugate into $
\mathrm{GL}n(\mathbb{Z}\ell)$. Indeed, let $L_0 = \mathbb{Z}\ell^n$. Since $H$
is compact, the entries of every $h \in H$ are bounded, so there exists $m \geq
0$ with $H \cdot L_0 \subseteq \ell^{-m} L_0$. The $\mathbb{Z}\ell$-lattice $
$L := \sum_{h \in H} h \cdot L_0 \subseteq \ell^{-m} L_0$$ is a finitely
generated $\mathbb{Z}\ell$-submodule (hence a lattice, being of full rank) and
is $H$-stable by construction. Choosing a $\mathbb{Z}\ell$-basis of $L$
conjugates $H$ into $\mathrm{GL}n(\mathbb{Z}\ell)$.

Thus, after conjugation, $H \subseteq \mathrm{GL}n(\mathbb{Z}\ell)$.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Step 2. $H$ is finite.

Consider the reduction homomorphism $$\pi: \mathrm{GL}n(\mathbb{Z}\ell) \to
\mathrm{GL}n(\mathbb{F}\ell),$$ with kernel $K = 1 + \ell, M_n(\mathbb{Z}
_\ell)$, which is a pro-$\ell$ group. The kernel of $\pi|_H : H \to \mathrm{GL}
n(\mathbb{F}\ell)$ is $H \cap K$. This group is simultaneously a pro-$p$-group
(subgroup of the pro-$p$-group $H$) and a pro-$\ell$-group (subgroup of the pro-
$\ell$-group $K$). Since $p \neq \ell$, the only such group is trivial: $$H \cap
K = {1}.$$ Therefore $H$ embeds into the finite group $\mathrm{GL}
n(\mathbb{F}\ell)$, so $H$ is finite. Being a finite $p$-group (quotient of a
pro-$p$-group), $|H|$ is a power of $p$, in particular prime to $\ell$.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Step 3. Each element of $H$ is trivial, using unipotency and finiteness.

Let $\sigma \in H$. By hypothesis there exists $n$ with $(\sigma - 1)^n = 0$, so
$\sigma$ is unipotent: all its eigenvalues (over $\overline{\mathbb{Q}_\ell}$)
equal $1$.

On the other hand, $\sigma$ has finite order (since $H$ is finite), say $|
\sigma| = p^k$ for some $k \geq 0$. The polynomial $x^{p^k} - 1 \in
\mathbb{Q}\ell[x]$ is separable because $\ell \neq p$ (its derivative $p^k
x^{p^k - 1}$ does not vanish at any root). Hence $\sigma$ is diagonalizable over
$\overline{\mathbb{Q}\ell}$, with eigenvalues that are $p^k$-th roots of unity.
But unipotency forces every eigenvalue to be $1$. A diagonalizable operator all
of whose eigenvalues are $1$ is the identity: $$\sigma = \mathrm{Id}.$$

Since $\sigma \in H$ was arbitrary, $H = {1}$, i.e., $\rho(G) = {1}$.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Conclusion. The action of $G$ on $V$ is trivial. $\blacksquare$
