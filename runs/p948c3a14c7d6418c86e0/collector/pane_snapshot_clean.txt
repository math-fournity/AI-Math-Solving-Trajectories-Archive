## Solution

We claim the only polynomial is the zero polynomial.

**Notation.** Let $P\in\mathbb Z[x]$ satisfy $n\mid P(2^n)$ for every positive i
nteger $n$.

**Key observation.** Fix $k\ge 0$ and let $p$ be any odd prime. Set $n=2^{k}p$,
which is a positive integer, so $n\mid P(2^n)$, in particular
$$p\mid P\bigl(2^{\,2^{k}p}\bigr).$$
Compute $2^{\,2^{k}p}$ modulo $p$. By Fermat's little theorem, for any integer $
a$ we have $a^{p}\equiv a\pmod p$; taking $a=2^{2^{k}}$,
$$2^{\,2^{k}p}=\bigl(2^{2^{k}}\bigr)^{p}\equiv 2^{2^{k}}\pmod p.$$
Since $P$ has integer coefficients, congruences are preserved under evaluation:
$$P\bigl(2^{\,2^{k}p}\bigr)\equiv P\bigl(2^{2^{k}}\bigr)\pmod p.$$
Hence $p\mid P\bigl(2^{2^{k}}\bigr)$.

This holds for **every** odd prime $p$. An integer divisible by infinitely many
distinct primes must be $0$, so
$$P\bigl(2^{2^{k}}\bigr)=0\qquad\text{for all }k\ge 0.$$

**Infinitely many roots.** The numbers
$$2^{2^{0}},\;2^{2^{1}},\;2^{2^{2}},\;2^{2^{3}},\;\dots \;=\;2,\;4,\;16,\;256,\;
65536,\;\dots$$
are strictly increasing, hence pairwise distinct. Thus $P$ has infinitely many d
istinct roots. A nonzero polynomial over $\mathbb Z$ (equivalently over $\mathbb
 Q$) has only finitely many roots, so
$$P\equiv 0.$$

**Verification.** The zero polynomial satisfies $n\mid 0 = P(2^n)$ for every pos
itive integer $n$.

Therefore the unique polynomial with integer coefficients satisfying the conditi
on is

$$\boxed{P(x)\equiv 0}.$$
