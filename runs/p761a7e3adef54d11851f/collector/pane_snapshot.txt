## Proof

**Setup.** The $n$-point Gauss–Legendre quadrature rule in one dimension, on the
 reference interval $[-1,1]$, has the form
$$Q_n[g]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i\,g(t_i),$$
where $t_1,\dots,t_n$ are the roots of the Legendre polynomial $P_n$ and the wei
ghts $w_i$ are the corresponding Gaussian weights. Its classical degree of accur
acy is $2n-1$: it integrates every polynomial of degree $\le 2n-1$ exactly, and
it fails on some polynomial of degree $2n$ (e.g. $t^{2n}$, since $Q_n[t^{2n}]\ne
\int_{-1}^{1}t^{2n}\,dt$).

For the rectangle $[a,b]\times[p,q]$ we apply the **product (tensor) rule**: map
 $[a,b]\ni x \mapsto t\in[-1,1]$ and $[p,q]\ni y\mapsto s\in[-1,1]$ affinely, an
d approximate
$$I[f]=\int_a^b\!\int_p^q f(x,y)\,dx\,dy \;\approx\; \frac{(b-a)(q-p)}{4}\sum_{i
=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n w_i w_j\, f\!\bigl(x(t_i),y(s_j)\bigr)=:Q_{n,n}[f].$$

**Claim.** The degree of accuracy of $Q_{n,n}$ on bivariate polynomials (measure
d by total degree) is exactly $2n-1$.

**Exactness up to total degree $2n-1$.** Let $f(x,y)$ be any polynomial of total
 degree $\le 2n-1$, so
$$f(x,y)=\sum_{\alpha+\beta\le 2n-1} c_{\alpha\beta}\,x^\alpha y^\beta.$$
By linearity it suffices to check each monomial $x^\alpha y^\beta$ with $\alpha+
\beta\le 2n-1$. The key observation is that $\alpha+\beta\le 2n-1$ forces **both
** $\alpha\le 2n-1$ **and** $\beta\le 2n-1$ (since $\alpha,\beta\ge 0$). The dou
ble integral separates:
$$\int_a^b\!\int_p^q x^\alpha y^\beta\,dx\,dy = \left(\int_a^b x^\alpha\,dx\righ
t)\!\left(\int_p^q y^\beta\,dy\right).$$
After the affine change of variables each factor is a constant times $\int_{-1}^
1 t^\alpha\,dt$ and $\int_{-1}^1 s^\beta\,dt$ respectively. Since $\alpha\le 2n-
1$ and $\beta\le 2n-1$, the one-dimensional $n$-point Gauss–Legendre rule integr
ates each factor exactly:
$$\int_a^b x^\alpha\,dx = \frac{b-a}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\,x(t_i)^\alpha,\qquad \i
nt_p^q y^\beta\,dy = \frac{q-p}{2}\sum_{j=1}^n w_j\,y(s_j)^\beta.$$
Multiplying these two exact identities gives
$$\int_a^b\!\int_p^q x^\alpha y^\beta\,dx\,dy = \frac{(b-a)(q-p)}{4}\sum_{i,j} w
_i w_j\, x(t_i)^\alpha y(s_j)^\beta = Q_{n,n}[x^\alpha y^\beta].$$
Summing over all monomials yields $Q_{n,n}[f]=I[f]$. Hence $Q_{n,n}$ is exact fo
r every polynomial of total degree $\le 2n-1$.

**Failure at total degree $2n$.** Consider the monomial $f(x,y)=x^{2n}$ (i.e. $\
alpha=2n,\beta=0$, total degree $2n$). Then
$$I[f]=\int_a^b x^{2n}\,dx\cdot(q-p),\qquad Q_{n,n}[f]=\frac{b-a}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n
 w_i\,x(t_i)^{2n}\cdot(q-p).$$
This reduces to the one-dimensional rule applied to $x^{2n}$, a polynomial of de
gree $2n$. The $n$-point Gauss–Legendre rule is **not** exact for degree $2n$ (i
t has only $2n$ free parameters $\{t_i,w_i\}$ and is already determined by exact
ness on degrees $0,\dots,2n-1$; the moment $\int_{-1}^1 t^{2n}dt=\frac{2}{2n+1}$
 is not matched by $\sum_i w_i t_i^{2n}$). Therefore $Q_{n,n}[x^{2n}]\ne I[x^{2n
}]$, and the rule fails on a polynomial of total degree $2n$.

**Conclusion.** The product $n\times n$ Gauss–Legendre rule on $[a,b]\times[p,q]
$ integrates every bivariate polynomial of total degree $\le 2n-1$ exactly, and
fails on at least one polynomial of total degree $2n$. Its degree of accuracy is
 therefore

$$\boxed{2n-1}.$$
