## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. For any Lebesgue measurable set $E$ with $m(E) > 0$ and any $0 <
 \alpha < 1$, there exists an open interval $I$ such that $m(E \cap I) > \alpha
\cdot m(I)$.

**Proof by contradiction.** Suppose, for some fixed $0 < \alpha < 1$, that for *
every* open interval $I$ we have
$$m(E \cap I) \le \alpha \cdot m(I). \tag{$\star$}$$

**Step 1: Reduce to the bounded case.** Since $E = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} (E \ca
p [-n, n])$ and $m(E) > 0$, there exists $N$ with $m(E \cap [-N,N]) > 0$. Replac
ing $E$ by $E \cap [-N, N]$, we may assume $E$ is bounded, so $0 < m(E) < \infty
$.

**Step 2: Approximate $E$ from outside by an open set.** By outer regularity of
Lebesgue measure, for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists an open set $O \supse
teq E$ with
$$m(O) < m(E) + \varepsilon.$$

**Step 3: Decompose $O$ into disjoint open intervals.** Every open subset of $\m
athbb{R}$ is a countable union of pairwise disjoint open intervals: $O = \bigsqc
up_{j} I_j$.

**Step 4: Apply the contradiction hypothesis to each $I_j$.** Since each $I_j$ i
s an open interval, hypothesis $(\star)$ gives $m(E \cap I_j) \le \alpha \cdot m
(I_j)$. Summing over $j$ (using countable additivity and the fact that the $I_j$
 are disjoint):
$$m(E) = m(E \cap O) = \sum_{j} m(E \cap I_j) \le \sum_{j} \alpha \cdot m(I_j) =
 \alpha \sum_{j} m(I_j) = \alpha \cdot m(O).$$

**Step 5: Derive the contradiction.** Combining with Step 2:
$$m(E) \le \alpha \cdot m(O) < \alpha\bigl(m(E) + \varepsilon\bigr) = \alpha \cd
ot m(E) + \alpha\,\varepsilon.$$

Rearranging:
$$m(E)(1 - \alpha) < \alpha\,\varepsilon.$$

This holds for **every** $\varepsilon > 0$. Letting $\varepsilon \to 0^+$:
$$m(E)(1 - \alpha) \le 0.$$

But $m(E) > 0$ and $1 - \alpha > 0$ (since $\alpha < 1$), so the left-hand side
is strictly positive — a contradiction.

**Conclusion.** The hypothesis $(\star)$ is false, so there must exist at least
one open interval $I$ with $m(E \cap I) > \alpha \cdot m(I)$. $\blacksquare$
