## Proof

We are given a sequence $(u_n)$ bounded in $H^1(\mathbb{R}^N)$. We examine each
of the five claimed convergence results. We show that **(1), (2), (3) hold** whi
le **(4) and (5) do not hold in general**.

Notation: $2^* = \frac{2N}{N-2}$ for $N \geq 3$ and $2^* = \infty$ for $N = 1, 2
$.

---

### Claim (1): $u_n \rightharpoonup u$ weakly in $H^1(\mathbb{R}^N)$, up to a su
bsequence. **(TRUE)**

$H^1(\mathbb{R}^N)$ is a Hilbert space, hence **reflexive**. By the Banach–Alaog
lu theorem (in the reflexive setting, equivalently the Eberlein–Šmulian theorem)
, every bounded sequence in a reflexive Banach space admits a weakly convergent
subsequence. Since $(u_n)$ is bounded in $H^1(\mathbb{R}^N)$, there exist $u \in
 H^1(\mathbb{R}^N)$ and a subsequence (still denoted $u_n$) such that
$$u_n \rightharpoonup u \quad \text{weakly in } H^1(\mathbb{R}^N).$$
In particular, $u_n \rightharpoonup u$ weakly in $L^2$ and $\nabla u_n \righthar
poonup \nabla u$ weakly in $(L^2)^N$. $\checkmark$

---

### Claim (2): $u_n \to u$ in $L^s_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^N)$ for all $2 \leq
 s < 2^*$, up to a subsequence. **(TRUE)**

This is the **Rellich–Kondrashov compactness theorem** combined with a diagonal
argument.

**Step 1 (Local compactness on a fixed ball).** Let $B_R = B_R(0)$ be a ball of
radius $R$. The restriction $u_n|_{B_R}$ is bounded in $H^1(B_R)$. By the Rellic
h–Kondrashov theorem, the embedding
$$H^1(B_R) \hookrightarrow L^s(B_R)$$
is **compact** for every $1 \leq s < 2^*$. Hence, from the weakly convergent sub
sequence of Claim (1), we can extract a further subsequence converging **strongl
y** in $L^s(B_R)$ for any fixed $s \in [1, 2^*)$.

**Step 2 (Countable family of exponents and balls).** Let $s_k = 2 + (2^* - 2)(1
 - 2^{-k})$ (or any enumeration of a countable dense subset of $[2, 2^*)$) and $
R_k = k$. We apply a **diagonal extraction**: from the subsequence converging in
 $L^{s_1}(B_1)$, extract a further subsequence converging in $L^{s_1}(B_2)$ and
$L^{s_2}(B_2)$, and so on. The diagonal subsequence $(u_{n_k})$ converges strong
ly in $L^{s_j}(B_R)$ for every $j$ and every $R$.

**Step 3 (All $s \in [2, 2^*)$ and all compact sets).** For any $s \in [2, 2^*)$
 and any compact $K \Subset \mathbb{R}^N$, pick $j$ with $s \leq s_j < 2^*$ and
$R$ with $K \subset B_R$. Since $B_R$ is bounded, $L^{s_j}(B_R) \hookrightarrow
L^s(K)$ continuously (when $s \leq s_j$), so strong convergence in $L^{s_j}(B_R)
$ implies strong convergence in $L^s(K)$. Thus $u_{n_k} \to u$ in $L^s_{\mathrm{
loc}}(\mathbb{R}^N)$ for all $2 \leq s < 2^*$. $\checkmark$

### Claim (3): $u_n(x) \to u(x)$ for a.e. $x \in \mathbb{R}^N$, up to a subseque
nce. **(TRUE)**

From Claim (2), the (already extracted) subsequence converges strongly in $L^2_{
\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^N)$. A standard theorem in measure theory states: **str
ong convergence in $L^p$ implies convergence in measure, and convergence in meas
ure implies the existence of a further subsequence converging almost everywhere.
**

Concretely, restrict to $B_1(0)$: $u_{n_k} \to u$ in $L^2(B_1)$, so $u_{n_k} \to
 u$ in measure on $B_1$, hence a further subsequence converges a.e. on $B_1$. Re
peating the diagonal argument over $B_R(0)$, $R = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$, we obtain a
subsequence converging a.e. on every $B_R$, hence a.e. on $\mathbb{R}^N = \bigcu
p_R B_R$. $\checkmark$

### Claim (4): $\nabla u_n(x) \to \nabla u(x)$ for a.e. $x \in \mathbb{R}^N$, up
 to a subsequence. **(FALSE in general)**

Weak convergence $\nabla u_n \rightharpoonup \nabla u$ in $L^2$ (from Claim 1) d
oes **not** imply convergence in measure, and hence does not guarantee an a.e.-c
onvergent subsequence. We provide an explicit counterexample.

**Counterexample ($N = 1$).** Let $\phi \in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ with $\phi \
equiv 1$ on $[0, 2\pi]$ and $\operatorname{supp}\phi \subset (-1, 2\pi+1)$. Defi
ne
$$u_n(x) = -\frac{\phi(x)\cos(nx)}{n}.$$

- **Boundedness in $H^1(\mathbb{R})$:** $\|u_n\|_{L^2} \leq \|\phi\|_{L^2}/n \to
 0$, and
$$u_n'(x) = \phi(x)\sin(nx) - \frac{\cos(nx)}{n}\phi'(x).$$
The second term has $L^2$-norm $O(1/n) \to 0$. The first term satisfies $\|\phi\
sin(n\cdot)\|_{L^2} \leq \|\phi\|_{L^2}$, uniformly bounded. Hence $(u_n)$ is bo
unded in $H^1(\mathbb{R})$.

- **Weak limit:** $u_n \to 0$ strongly in $L^2$, and $\phi\sin(n\cdot) \righthar
poonup 0$ weakly in $L^2$ by the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma (since $\sin(nx)$ oscill
ates rapidly against any $L^2$ test function). Thus $u_n \rightharpoonup 0$ in $
H^1$, so $u = 0$ and $\nabla u = 0$.

- **No a.e. convergent subsequence of $\nabla u_n$:** On $[0, 2\pi]$, where $\ph
i \equiv 1$, we have $u_n'(x) = \sin(nx) + O(1/n)$. It suffices to show that **n
o subsequence of $\sin(n_k x)$ converges to $0$ a.e. on $[0, 2\pi]$**.

  Suppose for contradiction that $\sin(n_k x) \to 0$ a.e. on $[0, 2\pi]$ for som
e subsequence. Then $\sin^2(n_k x) \to 0$ a.e., and $0 \leq \sin^2(n_k x) \leq 1
$. By the **dominated convergence theorem**,
  $$\int_0^{2\pi} \sin^2(n_k x)\,dx \to 0.$$
  But a direct computation gives $\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2(n_k x)\,dx = \pi$ for ever
y $k$ (since $n_k$ is a positive integer), a contradiction.

  Hence no subsequence of $\nabla u_n = u_n'$ converges to $\nabla u = 0$ a.e. C
laim (4) fails. $\boldsymbol{\times}$

### Claim (5): $\nabla u_n \to \nabla u$ in $(L^s_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^N))^
N$ for all $2 \leq s < 2^*$, up to a subsequence. **(FALSE in general)**

The same counterexample disproves this. With $u = 0$ and $\nabla u_n = \phi\sin(
n\cdot) + O(1/n)$, strong convergence $\nabla u_n \to 0$ in $L^s_{\mathrm{loc}}$
 would require $\|\phi\sin(n_k\cdot)\|_{L^s(K)} \to 0$ for every compact $K$ and
 some subsequence.

On $K = [0, 2\pi]$ (where $\phi \equiv 1$), by the equidistribution/Riemann–Lebe
sgue averaging principle,
$$\int_0^{2\pi}|\sin(n_k x)|^s\,dx \to \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|
\sin t|^s\,dt\,dx = \int_0^{2\pi}|\sin t|^s\,\frac{dt}{2\pi}\cdot 2\pi = \int_0^
{2\pi}|\sin t|^s\,dt > 0$$
(indeed the integral $\int_0^{2\pi}|\sin t|^s dt$ is a positive constant indepen
dent of $n_k$). Thus $\|\sin(n_k\cdot)\|_{L^s(0,2\pi)} \not\to 0$ for any subseq
uence, so $\nabla u_n \not\to 0$ in $L^s_{\mathrm{loc}}$.

**Conceptual reason:** The Rellich–Kondrashov theorem provides compactness of th
e embedding $H^1 \hookrightarrow L^s$ (for the **functions**), but grants **no c
ompactness for the gradients**. Gradients of a bounded $H^1$ sequence only conve
rge weakly in $L^2$; there is no mechanism promoting this to strong $L^s$ conver
gence. $\boldsymbol{\times}$
