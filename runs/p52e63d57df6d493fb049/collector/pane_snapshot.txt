**Answer: Yes.**

**Proof.**

Let $A$ be a self-adjoint linear operator on a finite-dimensional Euclidean spac
e $V$. Since $A$ is self-adjoint, it is diagonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$, and it
s eigenspaces are mutually orthogonal. Let $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$ be the
**distinct** eigenvalues of $A$, so that

$$V = V_{\lambda_1} \oplus V_{\lambda_2} \oplus \cdots \oplus V_{\lambda_k}, \qq
uad V_{\lambda_i} = \ker(A - \lambda_i I),$$

an orthogonal direct sum decomposition.

Fix an index $j \in \{1, \dots, k\}$ and let $P_j$ denote the orthogonal project
or onto $V_{\lambda_j}$. We claim $P_j$ is a polynomial in $A$.

Since the eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$ are pairwise distinct, we ma
y apply Lagrange interpolation. Define the polynomial

$$p_j(x) = \prod_{\substack{i=1 \\ i \neq j}}^{k} \frac{x - \lambda_i}{\lambda_j
 - \lambda_i}.$$

By construction:
- $p_j(\lambda_j) = 1$,
- $p_j(\lambda_i) = 0$ for every $i \neq j$.

Now evaluate $p_j(A)$. For any vector $v \in V_{\lambda_i}$ (any eigenspace), $A
v = \lambda_i v$, hence $p_j(A)v = p_j(\lambda_i) v$. Therefore:

- If $v \in V_{\lambda_j}$, then $p_j(A)v = p_j(\lambda_j)\, v = v$, so $p_j(A)$
 acts as the identity on $V_{\lambda_j}$.
- If $v \in V_{\lambda_i}$ with $i \neq j$, then $p_j(A)v = p_j(\lambda_i)\, v =
 0$, so $p_j(A)$ annihilates every other eigenspace.

Since $V$ is the direct sum of the eigenspaces, $p_j(A)$ is precisely the linear
 map that is the identity on $V_{\lambda_j}$ and zero on $\bigoplus_{i \neq j} V
_{\lambda_i}$. This is exactly the projector onto $V_{\lambda_j}$ along the comp
lementary sum of the remaining eigenspaces. Because the eigenspaces of a self-ad
joint operator are mutually orthogonal, this projector is in fact the **orthogon
al** projector onto $V_{\lambda_j}$.

Thus $P_j = p_j(A)$, a polynomial in $A$. Since $j$ was arbitrary, every orthogo
nal projector onto an eigenspace of $A$ is a polynomial in $A$. $\blacksquare$
