## Proof

**Yes**, such a positive integer $n$ exists.

### Setup and Key Idea

Choose $n = m^2$ to be a perfect square, where $m$ is a large positive integer s
elected so that $\frac{10^{200}}{2m}$ is slightly greater than $1$. Then $\sqrt{
n} = m$ is an integer (200th digit = 0), and for $j = 1, \ldots, 999$, the numbe
rs $m^2 + j$ are not perfect squares (since the next square $(m+1)^2 = m^2 + 2m+
1 > m^2 + 999$ when $m > 499$), so $\sqrt{m^2+j}$ is irrational with a well-defi
ned 200th digit.

### Taylor Expansion

For $j \geq 1$:
$$\sqrt{m^2 + j} = m\sqrt{1 + \frac{j}{m^2}} = m + \frac{j}{2m} - \frac{j^2}{8m^
3} + \frac{j^3}{16m^5} - \cdots$$

Since $0 < \frac{j}{2m} < \frac{1000}{2m} < 1$ (for $m$ large), the integer part
 is $m$ and:
$$\{\sqrt{m^2+j}\} = \frac{j}{2m} - R_j, \quad \text{where } R_j = \frac{j^2}{8m
^3} - \frac{j^3}{16m^5} + \cdots > 0$$

(The series for $R_j$ is alternating with decreasing terms when $j \ll m^2$, so
$R_j > 0$.)

Multiplying by $10^{200}$:
$$10^{200}\{\sqrt{m^2+j}\} = \frac{j \cdot 10^{200}}{2m} - 10^{200}R_j = j\gamma
 - \varepsilon_j$$

where $\gamma = \frac{10^{200}}{2m}$ and $\varepsilon_j = 10^{200}R_j > 0$.

### Bounding the Error

With $m \approx 5 \times 10^{199}$:
$$\varepsilon_j < \frac{j^2 \cdot 10^{200}}{4m^3} < \frac{10^6 \cdot 10^{200}}{4
 \cdot (4.99 \times 10^{199})^3} < 2 \times 10^{-394}$$

This is astronomically small.

### Choosing $m$

Select $m$ satisfying:
1. $\frac{500 \times 10^{200}}{1001} < m < 5 \times 10^{199}$, ensuring $\gamma
= \frac{10^{200}}{2m} \in (1, 1 + \frac{1}{1000})$, i.e., $\gamma = 1 + \theta$
with $0 < \theta < \frac{1}{1000}$.
2. $m$ has a prime factor $p > 999$ with $p \neq 5$ (e.g., $p = 1009$).

**Existence:** Take $m = 1009k$ for integer $k$. The interval $\left(\frac{4.995
 \times 10^{199}}{1009}, \frac{5 \times 10^{199}}{1009}\right)$ has length $\app
rox 5 \times 10^{193}$, containing vast numbers of integers. Choosing $k$ not di
visible by $5$ (at most $1/5$ are excluded) gives a valid $m$ with prime factor
$1009 > 999$.

### The 200th Digit Computation

**For $j = 0$:** $\sqrt{n} = m$ is an integer, so the 200th digit is $\boxed{0}$
.

**For $j = 1, \ldots, 999$:** We compute $\lfloor j\gamma - \varepsilon_j \rfloo
r \bmod 10$.

Since $m$ has prime factor $p > 999$ with $p \neq 2, 5$, the denominator $D$ of
$\gamma = \frac{10^{200}}{2m}$ in lowest terms satisfies $D \geq p > 999$. For $
1 \leq j \leq 999 < D$, since $\gcd(j, D) < D$, we have $j\gamma \notin \mathbb{
Z}$, so $\{j\gamma\} \geq \frac{1}{D} \geq \frac{1}{2m} > 10^{-200}$.

Since $\{j\gamma\} > 10^{-200} \gg 10^{-394} > \varepsilon_j$:
$$\lfloor j\gamma - \varepsilon_j \rfloor = \lfloor j\gamma \rfloor$$

Now $\gamma = 1 + \theta$ with $0 < \theta < \frac{1}{1000}$, so:
$$\lfloor j\gamma \rfloor = \lfloor j + j\theta \rfloor = j + \lfloor j\theta \r
floor = j$$
since $j\theta < 999 \times \frac{1}{1000} < 1$.

Therefore the 200th digit of $\sqrt{m^2 + j}$ is $j \bmod 10$.

### Conclusion

The 200th digits after the decimal point of $\sqrt{n}, \sqrt{n+1}, \ldots, \sqrt
{n+999}$ are:
$$0 \bmod 10,\; 1 \bmod 10,\; 2 \bmod 10,\; \ldots,\; 999 \bmod 10$$

which is $0, 1, 2, \ldots, 9, 0, 1, 2, \ldots, 9, \ldots$ — the sequence $j \bmo
d 10$ for $j = 0, 1, \ldots, 999$.

Over 1000 consecutive integers $j = 0, \ldots, 999$, the value $j \bmod 10$ cycl
es through $\{0, 1, \ldots, 9\}$ exactly 100 times, so **each digit from 0 to 9
occurs exactly 100 times**.
