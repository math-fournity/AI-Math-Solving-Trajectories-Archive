## Proof

**Claim.** The smallest constant is $c = \dfrac{1}{12}$.

---

### Step 1. Reformulation as a quadrature error

Set $h = x - y \neq 0$ (the case $x = y$ is trivial). By the Fundamental Theorem
 of Calculus,

$$
f(x) - f(y) = \int_y^x f'(s)\,ds,
$$

so

$$
\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{x-y} = \frac{1}{x-y}\int_y^x f'(s)\,ds
$$

is the average of $f'$ over $[y,x]$. Therefore the left–hand side of the desired
 inequality is precisely the **error of the trapezoidal rule** applied to $g :=
f'$ on the interval $[y,x]$ (or $[x,y]$):

$$
E := \frac{1}{x-y}\int_y^x f'(s)\,ds - \frac{f'(x)+f'(y)}{2}
= \frac{1}{x-y}\left[\int_y^x f'(s)\,ds - \frac{(x-y)\bigl(f'(x)+f'(y)\bigr)}{2}
\right].
$$

---

### Step 2. The trapezoidal-rule error identity

Since $f \in C^4([0,1])$, we have $g = f' \in C^3([0,1]) \subset C^2([0,1])$. Th
e classical trapezoidal-rule error formula (obtained, e.g., by two integrations
by parts of $(s-y)(s-x)$ against $g''$) states: for $g \in C^2([a,b])$ there exi
sts $\eta \in (a,b)$ with

$$
\int_a^b g(s)\,ds - \frac{(b-a)\bigl(g(a)+g(b)\bigr)}{2} = -\frac{(b-a)^3}{12}\,
g''(\eta).
$$

Applying this with $a = \min(x,y)$, $b = \max(x,y)$, $g = f'$ (so $g'' = f'''$),
 and $b - a = |x-y|$:

$$
\int_{\min(x,y)}^{\max(x,y)} f'(s)\,ds - \frac{|x-y|\bigl(f'(x)+f'(y)\bigr)}{2}
= -\frac{|x-y|^3}{12}\,f'''(\eta)
$$

for some $\eta$ between $x$ and $y$. Dividing both sides by $x - y$ (note $\frac
{|x-y|^3}{x-y} = |x-y|^2\cdot\operatorname{sgn}(x-y)$, and the sign is absorbed
into the choice of $\eta$):

$$
\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{x-y} - \frac{f'(x)+f'(y)}{2} = -\frac{(x-y)^2}{12}\,f'''(\eta).
$$

Taking absolute values and letting $M := \max_{\xi\in[0,1]}|f'''(\xi)|$:

$$
\left|\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{x-y} - \frac{f'(x)+f'(y)}{2}\right|
= \frac{|x-y|^2}{12}\,|f'''(\eta)|
\le \frac{1}{12}\,|x-y|^2\,M.
$$

Hence $c = \dfrac{1}{12}$ is **sufficient**.

---

### Step 3. Sharpness — $c = \tfrac{1}{12}$ is necessary

Take $f(x) = x^3 \in C^4([0,1])$ (indeed $C^\infty$). Then

$$
f'(x) = 3x^2,\qquad f''(x) = 6x,\qquad f'''(x) = 6,
$$

so $M = \max_{[0,1]}|f'''| = 6$. For any $x \neq y$:

$$
\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{x-y} = \frac{x^3 - y^3}{x-y} = x^2 + xy + y^2,
$$

$$
\frac{f'(x)+f'(y)}{2} = \frac{3x^2 + 3y^2}{2}.
$$

Their difference is

$$
x^2 + xy + y^2 - \frac{3x^2+3y^2}{2}
= xy - \frac{x^2+y^2}{2}
= -\frac{(x-y)^2}{2}.
$$

Thus

$$
\left|\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{x-y} - \frac{f'(x)+f'(y)}{2}\right| = \frac{|x-y|^2}{2}.
$$

The inequality demands

$$
\frac{|x-y|^2}{2} \le c\,|x-y|^2 \cdot 6 = 6c\,|x-y|^2,
$$

i.e. $c \ge \dfrac{1}{12}$. Therefore no constant smaller than $\dfrac{1}{12}$ c
an work.

---

### Conclusion

The constant $c = \dfrac{1}{12}$ is both sufficient (Step 2, via the trapezoidal
-rule error identity) and necessary (Step 3, via the sharp example $f(x)=x^3$).
Hence the smallest constant is

$$
\boxed{c = \dfrac{1}{12}}.
$$
