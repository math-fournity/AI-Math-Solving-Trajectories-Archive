## Answer

All monic polynomials $f \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ with $f(\mathbb{Z})$ closed under mu
ltiplication are exactly

$$f(x) = (x+c)^d, \qquad c \in \mathbb{Z},\ d \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0},$$

where $d=0$ gives the constant polynomial $f \equiv 1$.

## Proof

**Sufficiency.** For $f(x)=(x+c)^d$ with $d\ge 1$, since $n\mapsto n+c$ is a bij
ection of $\mathbb{Z}$,
$$f(\mathbb{Z})=\{(n+c)^d:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}=\{m^d:m\in\mathbb{Z}\},$$
and $m^d\cdot n^d=(mn)^d$, so $f(\mathbb{Z})$ is closed under multiplication. Fo
r $d=0$, $f\equiv 1$ and $f(\mathbb{Z})=\{1\}$.

**Necessity.** Let $f(x)=x^d+a_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+a_0$ be monic and suppose $f(
\mathbb{Z})$ is closed under multiplication.

*Case $d=0$.* Then $f\equiv 1$, which is $(x+c)^0$.

*Case $d\ge 1$.* We proceed in two steps.

**Step 1: $f(m)$ is a perfect $d$-th power for every $m\in\mathbb{Z}$.**

First suppose $d$ is even. Then $f(t)\to +\infty$ as $|t|\to\infty$, so $f$ is b
ounded below on $\mathbb{Z}$. Fix $m$. For all large $n>0$, $f(n)>0$, and by clo
sure $f(m)f(n)=f(k)$ for some $k\in\mathbb{Z}$. If $f(m)<0$, then $f(m)f(n)\to -
\infty$, contradicting that $f(k)$ is bounded below. Hence $f(m)\ge 0$ for all $
m$.

For any $m$ (any $d\ge 1$), let $\alpha_m$ denote the real $d$-th root of $f(m)$
 (non-negative when $d$ is even; unique real root when $d$ is odd). If $f(m)=0$
set $\alpha_m=0\in\mathbb{Z}$. Assume $f(m)\ne 0$. By closure, for every $n\in\m
athbb{Z}$ there exists $k(n,m)\in\mathbb{Z}$ with
$$f(k(n,m))=f(m)\,f(n)=\alpha_m^d\,f(n).$$
As $n\to+\infty$, $f(n)\to+\infty$, so $|k(n,m)|\to\infty$, and comparing leadin
g terms $k(n,m)^d\sim \alpha_m^d n^d$ gives $k(n,m)/n\to\alpha_m$. Writing $k(n,
m)=\alpha_m n(1+\varepsilon_n)$ with $\varepsilon_n\to 0$ and expanding
$$f(k)=k^d\Bigl(1+\frac{a_{d-1}}{k}+O(k^{-2})\Bigr),\qquad f(n)=n^d\Bigl(1+\frac
{a_{d-1}}{n}+O(n^{-2})\Bigr),$$
the equation $f(k)=\alpha_m^d f(n)$ yields, after matching the $n^d$ and $n^{d-1
}$ terms,
$$k(n,m)=\alpha_m n+\beta_m+O(1/n),\qquad \beta_m=\frac{a_{d-1}(\alpha_m-1)}{d}.
$$
Thus $k(n,m)-\alpha_m n\to\beta_m$. Since $k(n,m)\in\mathbb{Z}$, the sequence $\
{\alpha_m n\}$ (fractional parts) converges to $\{-\beta_m\}$. If $\alpha_m\noti
n\mathbb{Q}$, Weyl's equidistribution theorem makes $\{\alpha_m n\}$ dense in $[
0,1]$, a contradiction. Hence $\alpha_m\in\mathbb{Q}$; combined with $\alpha_m^d
=f(m)\in\mathbb{Z}$ this forces $\alpha_m\in\mathbb{Z}$.

Therefore $f(m)=\alpha_m^d$ with $\alpha_m\in\mathbb{Z}$ for every $m\in\mathbb{
Z}$.

**Step 2: $f(x)=(x+c)^d$.**

For $n\to+\infty$, $f(n)>0$ and
$$\alpha_n=f(n)^{1/d}=\bigl(n^d+a_{d-1}n^{d-1}+O(n^{d-2})\bigr)^{1/d}=n+\frac{a_
{d-1}}{d}+O(1/n).$$
Since $\alpha_n-n\in\mathbb{Z}$ (both are integers) and $\alpha_n-n\to \frac{a_{
d-1}}{d}$, the limit $c:=\frac{a_{d-1}}{d}$ is an integer, and $\alpha_n-n=c$ fo
r all sufficiently large $n$. Hence
$$f(n)=(n+c)^d\quad\text{for all sufficiently large }n.$$
The polynomial $f(x)-(x+c)^d$ has infinitely many integer roots, so it is identi
cally zero:
$$f(x)=(x+c)^d.$$

Combining both cases, every monic $f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ with $f(\mathbb{Z})$ close
d under multiplication is of the form $(x+c)^d$ with $c\in\mathbb{Z}$, $d\ge 0$.
 $\square$
