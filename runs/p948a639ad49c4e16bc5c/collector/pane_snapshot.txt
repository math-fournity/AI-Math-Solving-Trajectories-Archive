**Answer: Yes, $[k(x):k(q(x))] = d$.**

**Proof.**

Since $d = \deg r \ge \deg s \ge 0$ and $q = r/s$ is nonzero with $\gcd(r,s)=1$,
 if $d \ge 1$ then $r$ is nonconstant, so $q$ is a nonconstant rational function
, hence transcendental over $k$. (The case $d=0$ forces $r,s$ both constant, mak
ing $q$ constant and $[k(x):k(q(x))]=[k(x):k]=\infty$; this degenerate case is e
xcluded by the meaningful formulation of the problem, so we assume $d \ge 1$.)

**Step 1: $x$ is algebraic over $k(q(x))$ of degree at most $d$.**

Set $q := q(x) = r(x)/s(x)$. Consider the polynomial
$$F(T) := r(T) - q\, s(T) \in k(q)[T].$$
Since $\deg r = d \ge \deg s$, the leading term of $F$ (as a polynomial in $T$)
is the leading term of $r(T)$, so $\deg_T F = d$. Substituting $T = x$:
$$F(x) = r(x) - q\cdot s(x) = r(x) - \frac{r(x)}{s(x)}\cdot s(x) = 0.$$
Thus $x$ is a root of $F$, so $x$ is algebraic over $k(q)$ and $[k(x):k(q)] \le
d$.

**Step 2: $F(T)$ is irreducible over $k(q)$, hence $[k(x):k(q)] = d$.**

Since $q$ is transcendental over $k$, the assignment $t \mapsto q$ gives an isom
orphism $k(t) \xrightarrow{\sim} k(q)$. Under this, $F(T)$ corresponds to
$$\widetilde{F}(T) := r(T) - t\, s(T) \in k(t)[T].$$
It suffices to show $\widetilde{F}$ is irreducible in $k(t)[T]$.

View $\widetilde{F} = -s(T)\cdot t + r(T)$ as an element of $k[T][t] = k[t,T]$,
i.e., as a polynomial in $t$ of degree $1$ over the UFD $k[T]$. A degree-$1$ pol
ynomial $a\,t + b \in R[t]$ over a UFD $R$ is irreducible iff $\gcd(a,b) = 1$ in
 $R$: if a nonunit $c \in R$ divides both $a$ and $b$, then $at+b = c(a't+b')$ i
s a nontrivial factorization; conversely, any factorization $at+b = gh$ in $R[t]
$ forces one factor to have degree $0$ in $t$, hence to divide both $a$ and $b$,
 so it must be a unit when $\gcd(a,b)=1$.

Here $a = -s(T)$ and $b = r(T)$, and $\gcd(r(T), s(T)) = 1$ in $k[T]$ by hypothe
sis. Therefore $\widetilde{F}$ is irreducible in $k[T][t] = k[t,T]$.

Now regard $\widetilde{F}$ as a polynomial in $T$ over $R = k[t]$. Since $\widet
ilde{F}$ is irreducible in $k[t,T]$, it cannot have a nonunit factor in $k[t]$ (
such a factor $g(t)$ of positive degree would give a nontrivial factorization $\
widetilde{F} = g(t)\cdot h$ in $k[t,T]$, since $\deg_T \widetilde{F} = d \ge 1$
makes $h$ a nonunit). Hence $\widetilde{F}$ is primitive in $k[t][T]$. By **Gaus
s's Lemma**, a primitive polynomial over a UFD is irreducible in $R[T]$ iff it i
s irreducible over the fraction field. Therefore $\widetilde{F}$ is irreducible
in $k(t)[T]$.

**Step 3: Conclusion.**

Via the isomorphism $k(t) \cong k(q)$, the polynomial $F(T) = r(T) - q\,s(T)$ is
 irreducible in $k(q)[T]$, has degree $d$, and has $x$ as a root. Hence $F$ is t
he minimal polynomial of $x$ over $k(q)$, giving
$$[k(x) : k(q(x))] = \deg F = d.$$

**Remark.** The hypothesis that $k$ is algebraically closed is not needed; the r
esult holds for any field $k$. The essential ingredients are $\gcd(r,s)=1$ and $
d = \deg r \ge \deg s$ (so that $\deg_T F = d$ and $\widetilde{F}$ is linear in
$t$ with coprime coefficients).
