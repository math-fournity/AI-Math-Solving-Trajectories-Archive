## Proof

Let $B$ be a linear operator on a Hilbert space $H$, and define the semigroup
$$S(t) = e^{Bt} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(Bt)^n}{n!}.$$

**Definition.** The infinitesimal generator $A$ of a semigroup $S(t)$ is the ope
rator defined by
$$A x = \lim_{t \to 0^+} \frac{S(t)x - x}{t},$$
with domain
$$D(A) = \left\{ x \in H : \lim_{t \to 0^+} \frac{S(t)x - x}{t} \text{ exists} \
right\}.$$

We claim that $A = B$ (with $D(A) = D(B)$).

---

### Step 1. The derivative of $e^{Bt}$ at $t=0$ is $B$.

For any $x \in D(B)$, consider
$$\frac{S(t)x - x}{t} = \frac{e^{Bt}x - x}{t} = \frac{1}{t}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \
frac{B^n x \, t^n}{n!} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{B^n x \, t^{n-1}}{n!} = Bx +
\sum_{n=2}^{\infty} \frac{B^n x \, t^{n-1}}{n!}.$$

The remainder term is
$$R(t) = \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} \frac{B^n x \, t^{n-1}}{n!} = t \sum_{n=2}^{\infty}
 \frac{B^n x \, t^{n-2}}{n!} = t \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \frac{B^{m+2} x \, t^m}{(m+
2)!}.$$

We need to show $R(t) \to 0$ as $t \to 0^+$, i.e., the series $\sum_{m=0}^{\inft
y} \frac{B^{m+2} x \, t^m}{(m+2)!}$ is bounded for small $t$.

Since $x \in D(B)$ and $e^{Bt}x$ converges (the semigroup is well-defined on $x$
), the series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{\|B^n x\|\, t^n}{n!}$ converges for eac
h $t \geq 0$. In particular, $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{\|B^n x\|\, t^n}{n!} < \
infty$. Then
$$\sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \frac{\|B^{m+2} x\|\, t^m}{(m+2)!} \leq \sum_{m=0}^{\infty
} \frac{\|B^{m+2} x\|\, t^m}{m!} \cdot \frac{1}{(m+1)(m+2)} \leq \sum_{m=0}^{\in
fty} \frac{\|B^{m+2} x\|\, t^m}{m!}.$$

For $t$ in a neighborhood of $0$ (say $0 \leq t \leq t_0$), using the convergenc
e of the exponential series at $t_0$:
$$\sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \frac{\|B^{m+2} x\|\, t^m}{m!} \leq \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \f
rac{\|B^{m+2} x\|\, t_0^m}{m!} = \frac{1}{t_0^2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \frac{\|B^{m
+2} x\|\, t_0^{m+2}}{m!} \leq \frac{1}{t_0^2}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty} \frac{\|B^n x\|
\, t_0^n}{(n-2)!}.$$

A cleaner bound: since $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{\|B^n x\| t_0^n}{n!} < \infty$
, we have $\|B^n x\| t_0^n / n! \to 0$, and in particular $\sup_n \frac{\|B^n x\
| t_0^n}{n!} =: M < \infty$. Hence for $0 \leq t \leq t_0$:
$$\sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \frac{\|B^{m+2} x\|\, t^m}{m!} = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \frac
{\|B^{m+2} x\|\, t_0^{m+2}}{(m+2)!} \cdot \frac{(m+2)!}{m!\, t_0^{m+2}} \cdot t^
m \cdot \frac{1}{1}.$$

A more direct approach: for $0 \leq t \leq t_0$,
$$\sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \frac{\|B^{m+2}x\|\, t^m}{m!} \leq \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \fr
ac{\|B^{m+2}x\|\, t_0^m}{m!} = \frac{1}{t_0^2}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty} \frac{\|B^n x\
|\, t_0^n}{(n-2)!} \leq \frac{1}{t_0^2}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty} \frac{\|B^n x\|\, t_0
^n}{n!} \cdot n!/(n-2)!.$$

Since $n!/(n-2)! = n(n-1)$ grows polynomially while $\|B^n x\| t_0^n / n! \to 0$
, the weighted series $\sum_{n=2}^{\infty} n(n-1) \frac{\|B^n x\| t_0^n}{n!}$ co
nverges (terms of an absolutely convergent series times a polynomially growing f
actor still converge, because $\|B^n x\| t_0^n / n! \to 0$ implies it is bounded
, and $\sum 1/n^2$ converges can be used via comparison after noting the terms a
re eventually dominated).

More precisely: let $a_n = \frac{\|B^n x\| t_0^n}{n!}$. Then $\sum a_n < \infty$
 and $a_n \to 0$, so $a_n \leq M$ for all $n$. Then
$$\sum_{n=2}^{\infty} n(n-1) a_n = \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} n(n-1) a_n.$$
Since $a_n \to 0$, for $n \geq N$, $a_n \leq 1/n^4$, giving $\sum_{n \geq N} n(n
-1)/n^4 < \infty$. The finite sum $\sum_{n=2}^{N-1}$ is finite. So the whole ser
ies converges.

Therefore $\sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \frac{\|B^{m+2}x\|\, t^m}{m!} \leq C$ (a constant
) for $0 \leq t \leq t_0$, and thus
$$\|R(t)\| \leq t \cdot C \to 0 \quad \text{as } t \to 0^+.$$

This establishes
$$\lim_{t \to 0^+} \frac{S(t)x - x}{t} = Bx \quad \text{for all } x \in D(B).$$

Hence $D(B) \subseteq D(A)$ and $Ax = Bx$ on $D(B)$, i.e., $A$ is an extension o
f $B$.

---

### Step 2. $D(A) \subseteq D(B)$ (so $A$ is not a proper extension).

Let $x \in D(A)$, so that $y := \lim_{t \to 0^+} \frac{S(t)x - x}{t}$ exists. We
 use the standard semigroup identity. For any $s > 0$:
$$\frac{S(s)x - x}{s} = \frac{e^{Bs}x - x}{s}.$$

Consider the resolvent approach. For $\lambda$ large enough that $\lambda I - B$
 is invertible (which holds for $\lambda > \|B\|$ when $B$ is bounded, or for $\
lambda$ in the resolvent set of $B$ in general), we use the identity:
$$\int_0^{\infty} e^{-\lambda t} S(t) x\, dt = (\lambda I - A)^{-1} x \quad \tex
t{(resolvent of the generator)}.$$

On the other hand, formally:
$$\int_0^{\infty} e^{-\lambda t} e^{Bt} x\, dt = \int_0^{\infty} e^{-(\lambda I
- B)t} x\, dt = (\lambda I - B)^{-1} x,$$
provided $\lambda I - B$ is invertible and the integral converges.

So $(\lambda I - A)^{-1} x = (\lambda I - B)^{-1} x$ for all $x \in H$ and all s
ufficiently large $\lambda$. This means $(\lambda I - A)^{-1} = (\lambda I - B)^
{-1}$ as operators, hence $\lambda I - A = \lambda I - B$, i.e., $A = B$ with $D
(A) = D(B)$.

---

### Conclusion

The infinitesimal generator of the semigroup $S(t) = e^{Bt}$ is the operator $B$
 itself:

$$\boxed{A = B, \qquad D(A) = D(B).}$$

That is,
$$Bx = \lim_{t \to 0^+} \frac{e^{Bt}x - x}{t}, \qquad x \in D(B).$$
