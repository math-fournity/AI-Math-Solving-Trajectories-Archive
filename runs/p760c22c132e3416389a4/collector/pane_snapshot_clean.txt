Yes. Every infinite-dimensional vector space has an infinite linearly independen
t subset that spans the space — that is, an infinite **Hamel basis**. The proof
uses Zorn's Lemma (equivalent to the Axiom of Choice).

## Proof

Let $V$ be an infinite-dimensional vector space over a field $F$.

**Step 1: Every vector space has a basis (Hamel basis).**

Consider the collection $\mathcal{L}$ of all linearly independent subsets of $V$
, partially ordered by inclusion. $\mathcal{L}$ is nonempty (the empty set is li
nearly independent, or any singleton $\{v\}$ with $v \neq 0$).

Let $\mathcal{C} = \{L_i\}_{i \in I}$ be a chain (totally ordered subset) in $\m
athcal{L}$. We claim $U = \bigcup_{i \in I} L_i$ is linearly independent, hence
an upper bound of $\mathcal{C}$ in $\mathcal{L}$.

To verify linear independence of $U$: take any finite subset $\{u_1, \dots, u_n\
} \subseteq U$ and suppose $\sum_{k=1}^n a_k u_k = 0$ for scalars $a_k \in F$. E
ach $u_k$ lies in some $L_{i_k}$. Since $\mathcal{C}$ is a chain, among $L_{i_1}
, \dots, L_{i_n}$ there is a largest one, say $L_{i_m}$, containing all the othe
rs. Thus $\{u_1, \dots, u_n\} \subseteq L_{i_m}$. Since $L_{i_m}$ is linearly in
dependent, $a_1 = \cdots = a_n = 0$. So $U$ is linearly independent.

Therefore every chain in $\mathcal{L}$ has an upper bound in $\mathcal{L}$. By *
*Zorn's Lemma**, $\mathcal{L}$ has a maximal element $B$.

**Step 2: $B$ spans $V$.**

Suppose for contradiction that $\operatorname{span}(B) \subsetneq V$. Then there
 exists $v \in V \setminus \operatorname{span}(B)$. Consider $B' = B \cup \{v\}$
. Any finite linear relation $\sum a_k b_k + c v = 0$ with $b_k \in B$ forces $c
 = 0$ (otherwise $v = -\frac{1}{c}\sum a_k b_k \in \operatorname{span}(B)$, cont
radiction), and then $a_k = 0$ by independence of $B$. So $B'$ is linearly indep
endent, contradicting maximality of $B$. Hence $\operatorname{span}(B) = V$, and
 $B$ is a basis.

**Step 3: $B$ is infinite.**

Since $V$ is infinite-dimensional, it is not spanned by any finite set. If $B$ w
ere finite, then $\operatorname{span}(B) = V$ would make $V$ finite-dimensional,
 contradicting the hypothesis. Therefore $B$ is infinite.

**Step 4: $B$ is the desired subset.**

$B$ is linearly independent (by construction), infinite (Step 3), and spans $V$
(Step 2). Thus $B$ is an infinite linearly independent subset that spans $V$.

## Remark on the role of the Axiom of Choice

The existence of a basis for **every** vector space is equivalent (over ZF) to t
he Axiom of Choice. In particular, it is consistent with ZF that there exists a
vector space with no basis. So the statement "every infinite-dimensional vector
space has an infinite linearly independent spanning set" is true in ZFC (Zermelo
–Fraenkel set theory with the Axiom of Choice), which is the standard foundation
 for mathematics, but it is not provable in ZF alone.
