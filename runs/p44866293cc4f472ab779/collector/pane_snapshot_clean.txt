## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. The set $\{f^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is linearly independent.

**Setup.** Suppose for contradiction that the set is linearly dependent. Then th
ere exists a nontrivial finite linear relation
$$\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k\, f^{(k)}(x) = 0 \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathbb{R}, \t
ag{$\star$}$$
with $a_N \neq 0$ (if the lowest index appearing is $m > 0$, we relabel so the s
um starts at $k=0$ by replacing $f$ with $f^{(m)}$; the argument below applies t
o $f^{(m)}$, and boundedness of $f$ will constrain $f^{(m)}$ as explained in Cas
e 2).

Equation $(\star)$ says $f$ satisfies a linear ODE with **constant coefficients*
*. Let $P(\lambda) = \sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k \lambda^k$ be the characteristic polynom
ial, with distinct roots $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_s$ of multiplicities $m_1, \
dots, m_s$. The general solution is
$$f(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{s} p_j(x)\, e^{\lambda_j x},$$
where $p_j$ is a polynomial of degree $< m_j$.

---

**Step 1: Boundedness forces all exponents to be purely imaginary and all polyno
mial factors to be constant.**

Since $f$ is bounded on $\mathbb{R}$:

- If $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_j) > 0$: $|p_j(x) e^{\lambda_j x}| \to \infty$ a
s $x \to +\infty$, so $p_j \equiv 0$.
- If $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_j) < 0$: $|p_j(x) e^{\lambda_j x}| \to \infty$ a
s $x \to -\infty$, so $p_j \equiv 0$.
- If $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_j) = 0$ and $\lambda_j = i\beta_j \neq 0$: $e^{i
\beta_j x}$ is bounded, but $|x|^m |e^{i\beta_j x}| = |x|^m \to \infty$ for $m \
geq 1$, so $p_j$ must be constant (degree $0$), i.e., $m_j = 1$.
- If $\lambda_j = 0$: $p_j(x) = x^m$ is bounded only if $m = 0$, so $p_j$ is con
stant.

**Case 1: $a_0 \neq 0$ (i.e., $\lambda = 0$ is not a root).** Then all $\lambda_
j$ are nonzero purely imaginary, and
$$f(x) = c_0 + \sum_{j} \bigl(A_j \cos(\beta_j x) + B_j \sin(\beta_j x)\bigr),$$
a **trigonometric polynomial** (plus constant).

**Case 2: $a_0 = 0$.** Write $P(\lambda) = \lambda^m Q(\lambda)$ with $Q(0) \neq
 0$. Then $g := f^{(m)}$ satisfies $Q(D)\,g = 0$, and by the same boundedness an
alysis applied to $g$ (using that $f$, an $m$-fold antiderivative of $g$, is bou
nded — any term $e^{\lambda x}$ with $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) \neq 0$ in $g$
produces an unbounded contribution to $f$ after integration, and a constant term
 in $g$ produces a polynomial in $f$), we conclude $g = f^{(m)}$ is a trigonomet
ric polynomial with zero mean. Integrating $m$ times, $f$ is a trigonometric pol
ynomial plus a polynomial of degree $\leq m$; boundedness kills the polynomial p
art. So again $f$ is a **trigonometric polynomial** (plus constant).

---

**Step 2: A trigonometric polynomial cannot be strictly monotonically increasing
.**

In both cases, $f$ is a trigonometric polynomial:
$$f(x) = c_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{r}\bigl(A_j \cos(\beta_j x) + B_j \sin(\beta_j x)\big
r).$$
Its derivative is
$$f'(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{r} \beta_j\bigl(-A_j \sin(\beta_j x) + B_j \cos(\beta_j x)
\bigr),$$
also a trigonometric polynomial. The **Bohr mean** (uniform average) of $f'$ is
$0$, since every $\sin$ and $\cos$ term has zero mean.

Since $f$ is strictly increasing, $f'(x) \geq 0$ for all $x$, and $f'$ is not id
entically zero. But $f'$ is an **almost periodic function** (a finite trigonomet
ric polynomial is almost periodic). A classical fact: *if an almost periodic fun
ction $h$ satisfies $h \geq 0$ everywhere and has Bohr mean $0$, then $h \equiv
0$.*

*Proof of the fact:* If $h(x_0) = \delta > 0$, by continuity $h \geq \delta/2$ o
n an interval $I$ around $x_0$. By almost periodicity, the set of $\tau$ with $\
sup_x |h(x+\tau) - h(x)| < \delta/4$ is relatively dense, so translates of $I$ a
ppear with positive density. On each such translate, $h \geq \delta/4$, forcing
the Bohr mean to be positive — contradiction. $\square$

Applying this to $h = f'$: $f' \geq 0$ and $\operatorname{mean}(f') = 0$ imply $
f' \equiv 0$, contradicting strict monotonicity.

**(Alternative contradiction via the finite-zeros hypothesis):** A nonconstant t
rigonometric polynomial $f^{(n)}$ is periodic (or almost periodic) and oscillate
s, so it has **infinitely many zeros** — contradicting the hypothesis that each
$f^{(n)}$ vanishes at only finitely many points. If all $f^{(n)}$ are constant,
then $f$ is a polynomial, and boundedness forces $f$ to be constant, contradicti
ng strict monotonicity.

---

**Conclusion.** In all cases, the assumption of a nontrivial linear relation $(\
star)$ leads to $f$ being a trigonometric polynomial, which contradicts either s
trict monotonicity or the finite-zeros condition. Therefore no nontrivial finite
 linear relation among $\{f^{(n)}\}$ exists, and the set $\{f^{(n)}\}_{n \in \ma
thbb{N}}$ is **linearly independent**. $\blacksquare$
