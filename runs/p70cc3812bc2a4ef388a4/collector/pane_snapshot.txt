# Bruck–Ryser Theorem for Finite Projective Planes

## Statement

**Theorem (Bruck–Ryser, 1949).** *If a finite projective plane of order $n$ exis
ts and $n \equiv 1$ or $2 \pmod{4}$, then $n$ must be expressible as the sum of
two integer squares, i.e., there exist integers $a, b$ such that*
$$n = a^2 + b^2.$$

Equivalently (in contrapositive form): *if $n \equiv 1$ or $2 \pmod{4}$ and $n$
is **not** a sum of two squares, then no projective plane of order $n$ exists.*

## Derivation from the Bruck–Ryser–Chowla theorem

A projective plane of order $n$ is a symmetric $(v, k, \lambda)$-design with par
ameters
$$v = n^2 + n + 1, \qquad k = n + 1, \qquad \lambda = 1, \qquad k - \lambda = n.
$$

The Bruck–Ryser–Chowla theorem states that for a symmetric $(v,k,\lambda)$-desig
n:

- If $v$ is **even**, then $k - \lambda$ must be a perfect square.
- If $v$ is **odd**, then the Diophantine equation
$$(k-\lambda)\,x^2 + (-1)^{(v-1)/2}\,\lambda\, y^2 = z^2$$
must have a nontrivial integer solution $(x, y, z) \neq (0,0,0)$.

**Step 1: $v$ is always odd.** Since $v = n^2+n+1 = n(n+1)+1$ and $n(n+1)$ is th
e product of two consecutive integers (hence always even), $v$ is always odd. So
 only the odd case applies.

**Step 2: Compute the exponent.** We have
$$\frac{v-1}{2} = \frac{n^2+n}{2} = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}.$$

- If $n \equiv 0 \pmod{4}$: $n(n+1)/2 = (4m)(4m+1)/2 = 2m(4m+1)$, which is even.
 So $(-1)^{(v-1)/2} = +1$.
- If $n \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$: $n(n+1)/2 = (4m+3)(4m+4)/2 = (4m+3)\cdot 2(m+1)$, wh
ich is even. So $(-1)^{(v-1)/2} = +1$.
- If $n \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$: $n(n+1)/2 = (4m+1)(4m+2)/2 = (4m+1)(2m+1)$, which is
 odd. So $(-1)^{(v-1)/2} = -1$.
- If $n \equiv 2 \pmod{4}$: $n(n+1)/2 = (4m+2)(4m+3)/2 = (2m+1)(4m+3)$, which is
 odd. So $(-1)^{(v-1)/2} = -1$.

**Step 3: The critical case $n \equiv 1$ or $2 \pmod{4}$.** Here $(-1)^{(v-1)/2}
 = -1$, and substituting $k - \lambda = n$, $\lambda = 1$, the Diophantine equat
ion becomes
$$n\,x^2 - y^2 = z^2, \qquad \text{i.e.,} \qquad n\,x^2 = y^2 + z^2.$$

Setting $x = 1$ (which is permitted since we seek a nontrivial solution), this r
equires
$$n = y^2 + z^2$$
for some integers $y, z$. Hence **$n$ must be a sum of two squares**.

**Step 4: The non-restrictive case $n \equiv 0$ or $3 \pmod{4}$.** Here $(-1)^{(
v-1)/2} = +1$, and the equation becomes
$$n\,x^2 + y^2 = z^2,$$
which always admits the trivial nonzero solution $x = 0,\; y = z = 1$. Thus no r
estriction on $n$ arises in these cases.

## Conclusion

The Bruck–Ryser theorem gives the following necessary condition for the existenc
e of a finite projective plane of order $n$:

> **If $n \equiv 1$ or $2 \pmod{4}$, then $n$ must be representable as a sum of
two squares of integers: $n = a^2 + b^2$.**

### Notable applications

- **$n = 6$:** $6 \equiv 2 \pmod{4}$, and $6$ is not a sum of two squares (since
 $6 = 2 \cdot 3$ and $3 \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$ appears to an odd power). Hence **no
projective plane of order $6$ exists**.
- **$n = 14$:** $14 \equiv 2 \pmod{4}$, and $14 = 2 \cdot 7$ with $7 \equiv 3 \p
mod{4}$ to an odd power, so $14$ is not a sum of two squares. Hence **no project
ive plane of order $14$ exists**.
- **$n = 10$:** $10 \equiv 2 \pmod{4}$, but $10 = 1^2 + 3^2$, so the Bruck–Ryser
 condition is **satisfied**. (Nonexistence of the order-$10$ plane was establish
ed later by computer, showing the condition is necessary but not sufficient.)
- **$n = 12$:** $12 \equiv 0 \pmod{4}$, so Bruck–Ryser imposes no restriction. (
Nonexistence remains open for many such orders.)

The theorem is a **necessary** condition, not sufficient: satisfying it does not
 guarantee that a projective plane of order $n$ exists.
