## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, such a polynomial always exists for any finite set of points $x_
1, \ldots, x_m \in \mathbb{R}^d$.

**Construction.** Define

$$p(x) \;=\; -\prod_{i=1}^{m}\;\sum_{j=1}^{d}\bigl(x_j - x_{i,j}\bigr)^2,$$

where $x = (x_1, \ldots, x_d)$ and $x_i = (x_{i,1}, \ldots, x_{i,d})$.

**$p$ is a polynomial.** Each factor $q_i(x) := \sum_{j=1}^{d}(x_j - x_{i,j})^2$
 is a polynomial of degree $2$ in the $d$ indeterminates $x_1, \ldots, x_d$. The
 product of $m$ polynomials is a polynomial, so $p$ is a polynomial of degree $2
m$.

**Vanishing at the given points.** For any $i \in \{1, \ldots, m\}$, evaluating
at $x = x_i$:

$$q_i(x_i) = \sum_{j=1}^{d}(x_{i,j} - x_{i,j})^2 = 0.$$

Since $q_i(x_i) = 0$, the entire product $\prod_{k=1}^{m} q_k(x_i)$ contains a z
ero factor, hence

$$p(x_i) = -\prod_{k=1}^{m} q_k(x_i) = 0. \qquad\checkmark$$

**Strict negativity elsewhere.** Let $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with $x \notin \{x_1,
\ldots, x_m\}$. Then for every $i$, $x \neq x_i$, so at least one coordinate dif
fers: there exists $j$ with $x_j \neq x_{i,j}$, giving $(x_j - x_{i,j})^2 > 0$.
All other squared terms are $\geq 0$, so

$$q_i(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{d}(x_j - x_{i,j})^2 > 0 \quad \text{for every } i = 1, \l
dots, m.$$

Therefore the product $\prod_{i=1}^{m} q_i(x)$ is a product of strictly positive
 real numbers, hence strictly positive, and

$$p(x) = -\prod_{i=1}^{m} q_i(x) < 0. \qquad\checkmark$$

**Conclusion.** The polynomial $p(x) = -\prod_{i=1}^{m}\|x - x_i\|^2$ (written o
ut in coordinates as above) satisfies $p(x_i) = 0$ for all $i$ and $p(x) < 0$ fo
r all $x \notin \{x_1, \ldots, x_m\}$. Such a polynomial can always be construct
ed. $\blacksquare$
