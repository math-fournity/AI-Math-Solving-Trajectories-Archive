### Step 1. Rewrite the system as a single complex ODE

Let $z = x + iy$, so $\bar z = x - iy$ and $\dot z = \dot x + i\dot y$. Using th
e given equations,

$$
\dot x + i\dot y = \frac{(x+iy)^3 + (x-iy)^3}{2} + i\cdot\frac{(x+iy)^3 - (x-iy)
^3}{2i}.
$$

Since $i\cdot\frac{1}{2i} = \frac{1}{2}$, the right-hand side becomes

$$
\frac{(x+iy)^3 + (x-iy)^3}{2} + \frac{(x+iy)^3 - (x-iy)^3}{2} = (x+iy)^3 = z^3.
$$

Hence the system is equivalent to the single complex ODE

$$
\boxed{\dot z = z^3.}
$$

(One may verify directly: $\dot x = x^3 - 3xy^2$, $\dot y = 3x^2 y - y^3$, which
 are the real and imaginary parts of $z^3$.)

### Step 2. Convert to polar coordinates

Write $z = r e^{i\theta}$, so $\dot z = (\dot r + i r\dot\theta)e^{i\theta}$. Th
e ODE $\dot z = z^3 = r^3 e^{i3\theta}$ gives

$$
\dot r + i r\dot\theta = r^3 e^{i2\theta} = r^3(\cos 2\theta + i\sin 2\theta).
$$

Equating real and imaginary parts:

$$
\dot r = r^3\cos 2\theta, \qquad \dot\theta = r^2\sin 2\theta.
$$

### Step 3. General trajectory (relation between $r$ and $\theta$)

For trajectories with $\sin 2\theta \neq 0$,

$$
\frac{dr}{d\theta} = \frac{\dot r}{\dot\theta} = \frac{r^3\cos 2\theta}{r^2\sin
2\theta} = r\cot 2\theta.
$$

Separating variables, $\dfrac{dr}{r} = \cot 2\theta\,d\theta$, and integrating:

$$
\ln r = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln|\sin 2\theta| + \text{const}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
r^2 = C\,\sin 2\theta \quad (C \in \mathbb{R}).
$$

These are lemniscate-like curves, each confined to a sector where $\sin 2\theta$
 has constant sign, and all passing through the origin $r = 0$.

### Step 4. Apply the condition that the trajectory passes through $(1,0)$

The point $(x,y) = (1,0)$ corresponds to polar coordinates

$$
r = 1, \qquad \theta = 0.
$$

At $\theta = 0$ we have $\sin 2\theta = 0$, so this point does **not** lie on an
y curve of the family $r^2 = C\sin 2\theta$ (which would force $r = 0$ there). I
nstead, $\theta = 0$ is an **invariant line** of the system: the $\theta$-equati
on

$$
\dot\theta = r^2\sin 2\theta
$$

is smooth (hence its solutions are unique), and $\theta(t) \equiv 0$ is a soluti
on. By uniqueness, any trajectory starting at $\theta(0) = 0$ satisfies $\theta(
t) = 0$ for all $t$.

With $\theta \equiv 0$, the radial equation reduces to

$$
\dot r = r^3, \qquad r(0) = 1,
$$

whose solution is $r(t) = (1 - 2t)^{-1/2}$, defined for $t < \tfrac{1}{2}$, with
 $r \to 0$ as $t \to -\infty$ and $r \to +\infty$ as $t \to \tfrac{1}{2}^-$.

### Step 5. Equation of the trajectory

The image of the solution in the phase plane is the set $\{(r,\theta) : \theta =
 0,\ r > 0\}$, i.e. the positive $x$-axis. Therefore, the equation of the trajec
tory in polar coordinates is

$$
\boxed{\theta = 0.}
$$

Equivalently, in Cartesian form, $y = 0$ with $x > 0$. This is the separatrix (i
nvariant ray) of the system $\dot z = z^3$ along which trajectories flow outward
 from the origin toward infinity in finite time.
