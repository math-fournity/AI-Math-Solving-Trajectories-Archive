## Proof

**Construction.** Let $\{q_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ be an enumeration (without repeti
tion) of all rational numbers in $[0,1]$. Define

$$g(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n}\,|x - q_n|, \qquad x \in [0,1].$$

Since $|x - q_n| \le 1$ for $x, q_n \in [0,1]$ and $\sum 1/2^n = 1 < \infty$, th
e series converges uniformly, so $g$ is continuous. Each $|x-q_n|$ is convex, he
nce $g$ is convex.

Set $c = 1 + g(1) - g(0)$ and define

$$f(x) = \frac{x + g(x) - g(0)}{c}.$$

**$c > 0$:** We have $g(1)-g(0) = \sum \frac{1}{2^n}(|1-q_n|-|q_n|) = \sum \frac
{1}{2^n}(1-2q_n) = 1 - 2\sum \frac{q_n}{2^n}$. Since $q_n \in [0,1]$ and $q_n <
1$ for infinitely many $n$, we get $\sum \frac{q_n}{2^n} < 1$, so $g(1)-g(0) > -
1$ and $c > 0$.

---

**Condition 1.** $f(0) = \frac{0+g(0)-g(0)}{c} = 0$ and $f(1) = \frac{1+g(1)-g(0
)}{c} = \frac{c}{c} = 1$. ✓

---

**$f$ is strictly increasing.** Since $g$ is convex, $f$ is convex. The right de
rivative of $g$ at $x$ is

$$g'_+(x) = \sum_{q_n \le x}\frac{1}{2^n} - \sum_{q_n > x}\frac{1}{2^n} = 2\!\su
m_{q_n \le x}\frac{1}{2^n} - 1.$$

For every $x \in [0,1)$, the set $\{n : q_n \le x\}$ is nonempty (it contains th
e index with $q_n = 0$), so $\sum_{q_n \le x} 1/2^n > 0$, giving $g'_+(x) > -1$
and thus $f'_+(x) = \frac{1+g'_+(x)}{c} > 0$.

For a convex function, $f(y) \ge f(x) + f'_+(x)(y-x)$ whenever $y > x$. Since $f
'_+(x) > 0$ for all $x \in [0,1)$, we obtain $f(y) > f(x)$ for all $0 \le x < y
\le 1$. Hence $f$ is strictly increasing, and since $f(0)=0,\; f(1)=1$, it maps
$[0,1]$ onto $[0,1]$. ✓

---

**Condition 2 (no interval where $f'$ exists everywhere and is continuous).** We
 show $f$ is **not differentiable at any rational** $q_n$.

At $x = q_n$, the term $\frac{1}{2^n}|x - q_n|$ has left derivative $-1/2^n$ and
 right derivative $+1/2^n$. Every other term $\frac{1}{2^m}|x - q_m|$ ($m \ne n$
) is differentiable at $q_n$ (since $q_m \ne q_n$). By the dominated convergence
 argument given below, the sum $\sum_{m \ne n} \frac{1}{2^m}|x - q_m|$ is differ
entiable at $q_n$. Therefore $g$ has a kink at $q_n$: its left and right derivat
ives differ by $2/2^n > 0$, so $g$ (and hence $f$) is not differentiable at $q_n
$.

Since the rationals are dense in $[0,1]$, every non-degenerate interval $A \subs
et [0,1]$ contains some $q_n$ at which $f'$ does not exist. Hence there is **no*
* interval on which $f'$ exists everywhere (let alone is continuous and finite).
 ✓

---

**Condition 3 (dense set where $f'$ exists, is positive and finite).** Let $x_0
\in (0,1)$ be **irrational**, so $x_0 \ne q_n$ for all $n$. Consider the differe
nce quotient:

$$\frac{g(x_0+h) - g(x_0)}{h} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n}\cdot \frac{|x_
0+h-q_n| - |x_0-q_n|}{h}.$$

For each $n$, $|x - q_n|$ is differentiable at $x_0$ with derivative $\operatorn
ame{sgn}(x_0 - q_n)$. Moreover, the difference quotient of each term is bounded
by $1$ in absolute value (Lipschitz property). Since $\sum 1/2^n < \infty$, the
**dominated convergence theorem for series** allows us to interchange limit and
summation:

$$g'(x_0) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{g(x_0+h)-g(x_0)}{h} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1
}{2^n}\operatorname{sgn}(x_0 - q_n) = \sum_{q_n < x_0}\frac{1}{2^n} - \sum_{q_n
> x_0}\frac{1}{2^n}.$$

Since $x_0$ is irrational, no $q_n$ equals $x_0$, so $\sum_{q_n < x_0} 1/2^n + \
sum_{q_n > x_0} 1/2^n = 1$. Setting $S(x_0) = \sum_{q_n < x_0} 1/2^n$, we get $g
'(x_0) = 2S(x_0) - 1$, and therefore

$$f'(x_0) = \frac{1 + g'(x_0)}{c} = \frac{2\,S(x_0)}{c}.$$

Since $x_0 \in (0,1)$, there exist rationals less than $x_0$, so $S(x_0) > 0$, g
iving $f'(x_0) > 0$. Also $S(x_0) \le 1$, so $f'(x_0) \le 2/c < \infty$.

The set $B = \{x_0 \in (0,1) : x_0 \text{ irrational}\}$ is dense in $[0,1]$, an
d for every $x_0 \in B$, the derivative $f'(x_0)$ exists, is positive, and is fi
nite. ✓

---

**Conclusion.** The function $f$ is continuous, strictly increasing, maps $[0,1]
$ to $[0,1]$ with $f(0)=0$, $f(1)=1$, is non-differentiable at every rational (s
o $f'$ fails to exist on every interval), and is differentiable with positive fi
nite derivative at every irrational in $(0,1)$ (a dense set). All three conditio
ns are satisfied, so **such a function exists**. $\blacksquare$
