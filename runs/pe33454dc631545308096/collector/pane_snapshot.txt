## Proof

We seek the smallest $n$ for which there exists a **set** (so the weights are di
stinct) of $n$ positive integer crate-weights such that, after removing any one
crate, the remaining crates can be split between two delivery trucks of equal to
tal weight. A "delivery truck" must actually carry crates, so each side of the s
plit must be nonempty; this rules out $n\le 2$ trivially (removing a crate leave
s fewer than two crates to fill two trucks).

Let the weights be $a_1,\dots,a_n$, total $S$.

**Parity reduction.** For every $i$, the remaining sum $S-a_i$ must be even, so
every $a_i$ has the same parity as $S$. If all $a_i$ are even, divide every weig
ht by $2$; the equal-split property is preserved and the weights stay distinct.
Repeating, we may assume the weights are all **odd**. Then $S=n\cdot(\text{odd})
$ has the parity of $n$, and $S-a_i$ even forces $n$ to be **odd**. Hence every
even $n$ is impossible.

**$n=3$ is impossible.** Removing one crate leaves two distinct crates; splittin
g them into two nonempty equal trucks forces the two remaining weights to be equ
al, contradicting distinctness.

**$n=5$ is impossible.** Write the five distinct odd weights in increasing order
 $a<b<c<d<e$. A 4-element set of distinct positive integers is bisectable only i
n two ways: either (pairing) the smallest plus the largest equals the sum of the
 two middle elements, or (dominant) the largest equals the sum of the other thre
e. Consider the three 4-subsets that contain both $a$ and $e$, namely those obta
ined by removing $b$, $c$, or $d$:

- removing $b$: $\{a,c,d,e\}$ needs $a+e=c+d$ or $e=a+c+d$;
- removing $c$: $\{a,b,d,e\}$ needs $a+e=b+d$ or $e=a+b+d$;
- removing $d$: $\{a,b,c,e\}$ needs $a+e=b+c$ or $e=a+b+c$.

Two pairing-type conditions cannot both hold (e.g. $a+e=c+d$ and $a+e=b+d$ give
$c=b$; the other pairs similarly force $d=b$ or $d=c$), and two dominant-type co
nditions cannot both hold (e.g. $e=a+c+d$ and $e=a+b+d$ give $c=b$; the others f
orce $d=b$ or $d=c$). So among these three subsets at most one can be pairing-ty
pe and at most one dominant-type — at most two satisfiable, yet all three must b
e bisectable. Contradiction. So $n=5$ is impossible.

Together with the parity ruling out $n=4,6$, every $n<7$ is impossible.

**$n=7$ is attainable.** Take the weights $\{1,3,5,7,9,11,13\}$, with total $S=4
9$. For each removal the remaining six crates have even sum and split into two n
onempty equal halves:

| removed | remaining sum | half | one side | other side |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1  | 48 | 24 | $\{13,11\}$ | $\{3,5,7,9\}$ |
| 3  | 46 | 23 | $\{13,9,1\}$ | $\{5,7,11\}$ |
| 5  | 44 | 22 | $\{13,9\}$ | $\{1,3,7,11\}$ |
| 7  | 42 | 21 | $\{13,5,3\}$ | $\{1,9,11\}$ |
| 9  | 40 | 20 | $\{13,7\}$ | $\{1,3,5,11\}$ |
| 11 | 38 | 19 | $\{13,5,1\}$ | $\{3,7,9\}$ |
| 13 | 36 | 18 | $\{11,7\}$ | $\{1,3,5,9\}$ |

Each row gives two nonempty groups of equal weight. Hence a valid set of $7$ cra
tes exists.

Since every $n<7$ is impossible and $n=7$ is achievable, the smallest possible n
umber of crates is

$$\boxed{7}.$$

### PROOF COMPLETE
