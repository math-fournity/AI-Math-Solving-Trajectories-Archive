## Proof

**Convention.** We use $\hat f(\xi) = \int_{\mathbb R^n} f(x)\,e^{-2\pi i x\cdot
 \xi}\,dx$. With this convention the Gaussian $\phi(x)=e^{-\pi|x|^2}$ satisfies
$\widehat\phi=\phi$.

---

**Setup.** Since $f\in L^1(\mathbb R^n)$, $\hat f$ is continuous, bounded, and v
anishes at infinity (Riemann–Lebesgue). We are given $\hat f\ge 0$ pointwise and
 that $f$ is continuous at $0$. We must show $\hat f\in L^1(\mathbb R^n)$.

For $\varepsilon>0$ define the approximate identity
$$\phi_\varepsilon(x)=\varepsilon^{-n}e^{-\pi|x|^2/\varepsilon^2},\qquad \wideha
t{\phi_\varepsilon}(\xi)=e^{-\pi\varepsilon^2|\xi|^2}.$$

---

**Step 1. A Fubini identity.** Consider
$$I_\varepsilon \;:=\; \int_{\mathbb R^n}\hat f(\xi)\,e^{-\pi\varepsilon^2|\xi|^
2}\,d\xi.$$
The integrand is non-negative, so $I_\varepsilon\in[0,\infty]$. To evaluate it,
write $\hat f$ as the Fourier integral and apply Fubini–Tonelli. The double inte
gral is absolutely summable since
$$\iint |f(x)|\,e^{-\pi\varepsilon^2|\xi|^2}\,dx\,d\xi \;=\; \|f\|_1\cdot\vareps
ilon^{-n}<\infty.$$
Hence
$$I_\varepsilon \;=\; \int_{\mathbb R^n} f(x)\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb R^n} e^{-2\pi i
 x\cdot\xi}\,e^{-\pi\varepsilon^2|\xi|^2}\,d\xi\Bigr)dx \;=\; \int_{\mathbb R^n}
 f(x)\,\phi_\varepsilon(x)\,dx \;=\; (f*\phi_\varepsilon)(0),$$
using that $\phi_\varepsilon$ is even.

---

**Step 2. Limit as $\varepsilon\to 0$ from the $f$–side.** The family $\{\phi_\v
arepsilon\}_{\varepsilon>0}$ is a standard approximate identity: $\int\phi_\vare
psilon=1$, $\phi_\varepsilon\ge 0$, and $\phi_\varepsilon$ concentrates at the o
rigin. Since $f$ is continuous at $0$ and $f\in L^1$,
$$(f*\phi_\varepsilon)(0)\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{} f(0).$$
Thus $I_\varepsilon\to f(0)$, a **finite** real number.

---

**Step 3. Limit as $\varepsilon\to 0$ from the $\hat f$–side.** For every $\xi$,
 $e^{-\pi\varepsilon^2|\xi|^2}\uparrow 1$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow 0$. Since $\
hat f\ge 0$, the monotone convergence theorem gives
$$I_\varepsilon \;=\; \int \hat f(\xi)\,e^{-\pi\varepsilon^2|\xi|^2}\,d\xi \;\up
arrow\; \int_{\mathbb R^n}\hat f(\xi)\,d\xi \in [0,\infty].$$

---

**Step 4. Conclusion.** Combining Steps 2 and 3,
$$\int_{\mathbb R^n}\hat f(\xi)\,d\xi \;=\; \lim_{\varepsilon\to 0} I_\varepsilo
n \;=\; f(0) \;<\; \infty.$$
Therefore $\hat f\in L^1(\mathbb R^n)$, and in fact $\|\hat f\|_1 = f(0)$.

**Answer: Yes.** The hypotheses $f\in L^1$, $f$ continuous at $0$, and $\hat f\g
e 0$ imply $\hat f\in L^1$.
