# Solution

**Claim.** $H \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C} \;\cong\; M_{2}(\mathbb{C})$, the
$\mathbb{C}$-algebra of $2\times 2$ complex matrices.

## Setup

Let $H$ be the Hamiltonian quaternions, an $\mathbb{R}$-algebra with basis $\{1,
i,j,k\}$ and relations
$$i^{2}=j^{2}=k^{2}=-1,\qquad ij=k=-ji,\quad jk=i=-kj,\quad ki=j=-ik.$$
Let $\iota\in\mathbb{C}$ denote the complex imaginary unit. The tensor product $
H\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}$ is naturally a **$\mathbb{C}$-algebra** (scalar
s act on the second factor), and as a $\mathbb{C}$-vector space it has dimension
 $\dim_{\mathbb{R}} H = 4$, with $\mathbb{C}$-basis $\{1\otimes 1,\; i\otimes 1,
\; j\otimes 1,\; k\otimes 1\}$.

## Constructing the map

Define an $\mathbb{R}$-algebra homomorphism $\varphi:H\to M_{2}(\mathbb{C})$ on
the quaternion basis by
$$
\varphi(1)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
\varphi(i)=\begin{pmatrix}\iota&0\\0&-\iota\end{pmatrix},\quad
\varphi(j)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\varphi(k)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\iota\\\iota&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Verification of the quaternion relations.**
- $\varphi(i)^{2}=\operatorname{diag}(\iota^{2},(-\iota)^{2})=\operatorname{diag
}(-1,-1)=-I$.
- $\varphi(j)^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\
\0&-1\end{pmatrix}=-I$.
- $\varphi(k)^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\iota\\\iota&0\end{pmatrix}^{2}=\begin{pmatri
x}\iota^{2}&0\\0&\iota^{2}\end{pmatrix}=-I$.
- $\varphi(i)\varphi(j)=\operatorname{diag}(\iota,-\iota)\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&
0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\iota\\\iota&0\end{pmatrix}=\varphi(k)=\varphi(i
j)$, and $\varphi(j)\varphi(i)=-\varphi(k)=\varphi(-k)=\varphi(ji)$.
- The remaining relations $jk=i$, $ki=j$ (and their signed reverses) follow by t
he same direct multiplication.

Hence $\varphi$ is a well-defined $\mathbb{R}$-algebra homomorphism.

## Extending to $\mathbb{C}$

By the universal property of scalar extension, $\varphi$ extends uniquely to a *
*$\mathbb{C}$-algebra** homomorphism
$$\Phi:H\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow M_{2}(\mathbb{C}),\qquad \
Phi(q\otimes z)=z\,\varphi(q),$$
where the central element $1\otimes\iota$ maps to $\iota\cdot I$.

## Surjectivity

The four image matrices are
$$I,\quad \operatorname{diag}(\iota,-\iota),\quad \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{p
matrix},\quad \begin{pmatrix}0&\iota\\\iota&0\end{pmatrix}.$$
From $I$ and $\operatorname{diag}(\iota,-\iota)$ we obtain, by $\mathbb{C}$-line
ar combinations,
$$e_{11}=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(I-\iota\,\operatorname{diag}(\iota,-\iota)\bigr)=\beg
in{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad e_{22}=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(I+\iota\,\opera
torname{diag}(\iota,-\iota)\bigr)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$
From $\varphi(j)$ and $\varphi(k)$ we obtain
$$e_{12}=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\varphi(j)-\iota\,\varphi(k)\bigr)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1
\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad e_{21}=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(-\varphi(j)-\iota\,\varphi(k)
\bigr)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.$$
(For instance, $\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\begin{psmallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{psmallmatrix
}-\iota\begin{psmallmatrix}0&\iota\\\iota&0\end{psmallmatrix}\bigr)=\tfrac{1}{2}
\begin{psmallmatrix}0&1+1\\-1-1&0\end{psmallmatrix}=\begin{psmallmatrix}0&1\\0&0
\end{psmallmatrix}$.)

Thus all four matrix units $e_{11},e_{22},e_{12},e_{21}$ lie in the image, so $\
Phi$ is **surjective**.

## Conclusion by dimension count

Both $H\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}$ and $M_{2}(\mathbb{C})$ are $4$-dimension
al over $\mathbb{C}$. A surjective $\mathbb{C}$-linear map between finite-dimens
ional $\mathbb{C}$-vector spaces of equal dimension is an isomorphism. Therefore
 $\Phi$ is a **$\mathbb{C}$-algebra isomorphism**:
$$\boxed{\,H\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}\;\cong\;M_{2}(\mathbb{C})\,}.$$

## Remark (structural viewpoint)

$H$ is a central simple $\mathbb{R}$-algebra, split by the extension $\mathbb{R}
\subset\mathbb{C}$ (equivalently, $\mathbb{C}$ is a splitting field since it is
algebraically closed). The general splitting principle for a central simple alge
bra of degree $n$ over a splitting field gives $M_{n}(F)$; here $\deg H=2$, reco
vering $M_{2}(\mathbb{C})$. Concretely, passing from $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{C}
$ provides a square root of $-1$ in the center, which "untwists" the quaternion
relations into the standard matrix-unit relations, exactly as the explicit map $
\Phi$ above demonstrates.
