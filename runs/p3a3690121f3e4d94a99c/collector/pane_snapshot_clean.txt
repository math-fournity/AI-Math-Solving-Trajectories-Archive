## Answer

**Yes**, such polynomials $m, n \in k[x, y, z]$ exist.

**Justification.**

The condition that $f$ and $g$ "do not share a single zero" means that there is
no point $(a, b, c) \in k^3$ with $f(a,b,c) = 0$ and $g(a,b,c) = 0$. In algebrai
c-geometric language, this says the affine variety
$$V(f, g) = \{(a,b,c) \in k^3 : f(a,b,c) = g(a,b,c) = 0\}$$
is empty.

Now consider the ideal $J = (f, g) \subseteq k[x, y, z]$. We wish to show $1 \in
 J$, i.e., $J = k[x,y,z]$, which is exactly the statement that there exist $m, n
 \in k[x,y,z]$ with $mf + ng = 1$.

Since $k$ is algebraically closed, we may apply the **Weak Hilbert Nullstellensa
tz**:

> *If $k$ is algebraically closed and $I \subsetneq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is a pro
per ideal, then $V(I) \neq \emptyset$.*

Taking the contrapositive: if $V(I) = \emptyset$, then $I = k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$
(i.e., $I$ is not proper, so $1 \in I$).

Applying this to $J = (f, g)$: we have $V(J) = V(f, g) = \emptyset$, so by the W
eak Nullstellensatz,
$$(f, g) = k[x, y, z].$$

Therefore $1 \in (f, g)$, which means there exist polynomials $m, n \in k[x, y,
z]$ such that
$$mf + ng = 1.$$

**Remark on the role of algebraic closedness.** The hypothesis that $k$ is algeb
raically closed is essential. For example, over $k = \mathbb{R}$ (not algebraica
lly closed), take $f = x^2 + 1$ and $g = y$ in $\mathbb{R}[x, y]$. Then $V(f, g)
 = \emptyset$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ (since $x^2 + 1 = 0$ has no real solution), yet
$(f, g) \neq \mathbb{R}[x, y]$, since $\mathbb{R}[x,y]/(x^2+1, y) \cong \mathbb{
C} \neq 0$. So no such $m, n$ exist in this case. The Nullstellensatz—and hence
the conclusion—relies crucially on $k$ being algebraically closed.
