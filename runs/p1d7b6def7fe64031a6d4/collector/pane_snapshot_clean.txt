## Proof

The answer is **no**. It is not guaranteed that every pair of consecutive primes
 after a certain point differ by at most 6. In fact, prime gaps are **unbounded*
*: for any positive integer $N$, there exist two consecutive primes whose differ
ence exceeds $N$.

### Construction

Fix any positive integer $N$. Consider the $N$ consecutive integers

$$
(N+1)! + 2,\quad (N+1)! + 3,\quad (N+1)! + 4,\quad \ldots,\quad (N+1)! + (N+1).
$$

For each $k$ with $2 \le k \le N+1$, the integer $(N+1)! + k$ is divisible by $k
$, since $k \mid (N+1)!$ (as $k \le N+1$) and $k \mid k$. Moreover, $(N+1)! + k
> k > 1$, so $(N+1)! + k$ is **composite**.

Thus all $N$ of these integers are composite. They form a run of $N$ consecutive
 composite numbers. Let $p$ be the largest prime strictly less than $(N+1)!+2$ a
nd $q$ be the smallest prime strictly greater than $(N+1)!+(N+1)$. Then $p$ and
$q$ are consecutive primes (no prime lies between them, since every integer in t
he interval $[(N+1)!+2,\,(N+1)!+(N+1)]$ is composite), and

$$
q - p \ge (N+1)! + (N+1) - (N+1)! + 1 \ge N+1.
$$

More precisely, $q - p > N$ since there are at least $N$ composite integers stri
ctly between $p$ and $q$.

### Conclusion

Since $N$ was arbitrary, prime gaps can be made arbitrarily large. In particular
, taking $N = 6$ (or any $N \ge 6$) yields a pair of consecutive primes differin
g by more than 6, and taking $N$ arbitrarily large yields gaps exceeding any fix
ed bound. Therefore there is **no** point after which all consecutive primes dif
fer by at most 6.

The statement is **false**.
