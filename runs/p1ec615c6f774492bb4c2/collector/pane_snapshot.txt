Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether $|S_N(f,0)| \to \infty$ is
possible for continuous $2\pi$-periodic $f$.

**Answer: The statement is TRUE.** For any continuous $2\pi$-periodic $f$, $|S_N
(f,0)|$ does not diverge to infinity.

**Proof.**

We use two classical facts and a key observation linking them.

**Fact 1 (Fejér's Theorem).** For any continuous $2\pi$-periodic $f$, the Cesàro
 means of the partial sums converge to $f(0)$:
$$\sigma_N(f,0) := \frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{k=0}^{N} S_k(f,0) \longrightarrow f(0) \qu
ad \text{as } N\to\infty.$$

**Fact 2 (Riemann–Lebesgue Lemma).** For any $f \in L^1(\mathbb{T})$ (in particu
lar, for continuous $f$), the Fourier coefficients satisfy $\hat{f}(k) \to 0$ as
 $|k|\to\infty$.

Since $S_N(f,0) = \sum_{k=-N}^{N} \hat{f}(k)$, the consecutive difference is:
$$S_N(f,0) - S_{N-1}(f,0) = \hat{f}(N) + \hat{f}(-N) \longrightarrow 0 \quad \te
xt{as } N\to\infty. \tag{$\ast$}$$

**Key Observation.** If $|S_N(f,0)| \to \infty$ and $(\ast)$ holds, then $S_N(f,
0)$ is eventually of constant sign, and hence $S_N(f,0) \to +\infty$ or $S_N(f,0
) \to -\infty$.

*Proof of the Key Observation.* Suppose $|S_N(f,0)| \to \infty$. Fix any $M > 0$
. Then:
- There exists $N_0$ such that $|S_N(f,0)| > M$ for all $N \geq N_0$.
- By $(\ast)$, there exists $N_1$ such that $|S_N(f,0) - S_{N-1}(f,0)| < 2M$ for
 all $N \geq N_1$.

For $N \geq \max(N_0, N_1)$, if $S_{N-1}(f,0)$ and $S_N(f,0)$ had **opposite sig
ns**, then:
$$|S_N(f,0) - S_{N-1}(f,0)| = |S_N(f,0)| + |S_{N-1}(f,0)| > M + M = 2M,$$
contradicting $|S_N(f,0) - S_{N-1}(f,0)| < 2M$.

Therefore, for all $N \geq \max(N_0, N_1)$, consecutive partial sums share the s
ame sign. By induction, $S_N(f,0)$ has **constant sign** for all sufficiently la
rge $N$. Combined with $|S_N(f,0)| \to \infty$, this gives either $S_N(f,0) \to
+\infty$ or $S_N(f,0) \to -\infty$. $\square$

**Completing the proof.** Suppose for contradiction that $|S_N(f,0)| \to \infty$
 for some continuous $f$. By the Key Observation, $S_N(f,0) \to +\infty$ or $S_N
(f,0) \to -\infty$.

**Case 1:** $S_N(f,0) \to +\infty$. A standard result on Cesàro means states tha
t if a sequence $a_N \to +\infty$, then its Cesàro mean $\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{k=0}
^{N} a_k \to +\infty$ as well. (Indeed, for any $K>0$, eventually $a_k > K$, so
the average is eventually at least $\frac{N-N_0}{N}\cdot K \to K$; since $K$ is
arbitrary, the mean diverges.) Applying this to $a_N = S_N(f,0)$:
$$\sigma_N(f,0) = \frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{k=0}^{N} S_k(f,0) \longrightarrow +\infty,$
$
contradicting Fejér's theorem, which gives $\sigma_N(f,0) \to f(0) < \infty$.

**Case 2:** $S_N(f,0) \to -\infty$. By the same argument, $\sigma_N(f,0) \to -\i
nfty$, again contradicting Fejér's theorem.

Both cases lead to contradictions. Therefore, $|S_N(f,0)| \not\to \infty$ for an
y continuous $2\pi$-periodic function $f$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** Note that $|S_N(f,0)| \not\to \infty$ does **not** mean $S_N(f,0)$ i
s bounded — by the Banach–Steinhaus theorem (since the Lebesgue constants $\|S_N
(\cdot,0)\| \sim \frac{4}{\pi^2}\log N \to \infty$), there exist continuous $f$
with $\sup_N |S_N(f,0)| = \infty$. The partial sums can be unbounded, but they c
annot **diverge to infinity**: the combination of Fejér's theorem (Cesàro summab
ility) and the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma (vanishing increments) forces sign changes
 whenever the partial sums grow large, preventing $|S_N|$ from tending to infini
ty.
