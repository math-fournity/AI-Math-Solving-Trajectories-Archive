## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. The Galois group of an irreducible quintic over $\mathbb{Q}$ wit
h exactly one real root and two complex conjugate pairs of roots always contains
 a double transposition.

**Setup.** Let $f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ be an irreducible quintic whose five roots
are
$$r,\quad z,\quad \bar z,\quad w,\quad \bar w,$$
where $r \in \mathbb{R}$ and $z, w \in \mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}$, with $z\n
eq \bar z$ and $w \neq \bar w$ (the two non-real conjugate pairs are distinct).
Let $K$ be the splitting field of $f$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, and let $G = \mathrm{Ga
l}(K/\mathbb{Q})$ be the Galois group. Since $f$ is irreducible, $G$ acts transi
tively on the five roots, so $G \leq S_5$ is a transitive subgroup.

**Complex conjugation.** Consider the automorphism $c : \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C
}$ given by complex conjugation, $c(\alpha) = \bar\alpha$. Since $c$ fixes every
 element of $\mathbb{Q}$, and since $K$ is a normal extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ (b
eing a splitting field), the restriction $c|_K$ is a $\mathbb{Q}$-automorphism o
f $K$, i.e., $c|_K \in G$.

**Action on the roots.** The real root $r$ is fixed by $c$: $c(r) = \bar r = r$.
 Each non-real root is sent to its complex conjugate:
$$c(z) = \bar z, \quad c(\bar z) = z, \quad c(w) = \bar w, \quad c(\bar w) = w.$
$
Labeling the roots so that the permutation induced by $c|_K$ is written in cycle
 notation, we get
$$c|_K = (z\;\bar z)(w\;\bar w),$$
a product of two disjoint transpositions fixing the remaining point $r$.

**Conclusion.** An element of $S_5$ that is a product of two disjoint transposit
ions is precisely a **double transposition** (cycle type $(2,2,1)$). Since $c|_K
 \in G$ and $c|_K$ is a double transposition, the Galois group $G$ always contai
ns a double transposition.

The argument uses only that the polynomial has one real root and two non-real co
njugate pairs; irreducibility ensures $G$ is a transitive subgroup of $S_5$ (and
 guarantees the splitting field is the relevant Galois extension), but the key p
oint is simply that complex conjugation, which necessarily lies in $G$, acts as
a double transposition on the roots.
