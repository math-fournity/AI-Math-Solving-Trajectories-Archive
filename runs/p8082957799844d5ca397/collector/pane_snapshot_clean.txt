## Proof

### Part 1: The mapping degree can be defined

Since $X$ and $Y$ are compact, connected, oriented $n$-manifolds, their top sing
ular homology groups satisfy $H_n(X;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ and $H_n(Y;\ma
thbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$. Any continuous map $f: X \to Y$ induces a homomorphi
sm
$$f_* : H_n(X;\mathbb{Z}) \longrightarrow H_n(Y;\mathbb{Z}),$$
which is multiplication by a uniquely determined integer $\deg(f)$. This is the
**mapping degree**, and it is well-defined for every continuous map—hence in par
ticular for every analytic map.

Equivalently, by Sard's theorem regular values $y \in Y$ exist and are dense. Fo
r any regular value $y$, the fiber $f^{-1}(y)$ is finite (since $f$ is proper, a
s $X$ is compact, and $f$ is a local diffeomorphism near each preimage), and
$$\deg(f) = \sum_{x \in f^{-1}(y)} \operatorname{sgn}(\det df_x),$$
which is independent of the choice of regular value. **The degree is always defi
ned.**

### Part 2: No uniform bound $d$ need exist

We construct an explicit counterexample: a surjective real analytic map between
compact, connected, orientable manifolds of equal dimension that possesses an **
infinite fiber**.

**Setup.** Let $X = Y = S^2 = \{(x,y,z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 : x^2+y^2+z^2=1\}$, whi
ch is compact, connected, and orientable. Define
$$f: S^2 \to S^2, \qquad f(x,y,z) = (2xz,\; 2yz,\; 2z^2 - 1).$$

**$f$ is real analytic.** The map $f$ is the restriction of a polynomial map $\m
athbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ to $S^2$, hence real analytic.

**$f$ maps $S^2$ to $S^2$.** If $x^2+y^2+z^2=1$, then
$$(2xz)^2 + (2yz)^2 + (2z^2-1)^2 = 4z^2(x^2+y^2) + 4z^4 - 4z^2 + 1 = 4z^2(1-z^2)
 + 4z^4 - 4z^2 + 1 = 1. \quad\checkmark$$

**$f$ is surjective.** For any $(a,b,c) \in S^2$ with $c > -1$, set $z = \sqrt{(
c+1)/2}$, $x = a/(2z)$, $y = b/(2z)$. Then
$$x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = \frac{a^2+b^2}{4z^2} + z^2 = \frac{1-c^2}{2(c+1)} + \frac{c+
1}{2} = \frac{1-c}{2} + \frac{c+1}{2} = 1, \quad\checkmark$$
and $f(x,y,z) = (a,b,c)$. For the south pole $(0,0,-1)$: any point $(x,y,0)$ on
the equator satisfies $f(x,y,0) = (0,0,-1)$.

**$f$ has an infinite fiber.** The fiber over the south pole is
$$f^{-1}(0,0,-1) = \{(x,y,0) \in S^2 : x^2 + y^2 = 1\},$$
which is the **equator**—a circle, hence an uncountably infinite set.

**The degree of $f$ is $2$.** The north pole $(0,0,1)$ is a regular value. Its p
reimages are $f^{-1}(0,0,1) = \{(0,0,1),\, (0,0,-1)\}$ (the north and south pole
s). At each preimage $p$, the ambient Jacobian $DF_p = \operatorname{diag}(\pm 2
, \pm 2, \pm 4)$ restricts to an invertible map on the tangent plane $T_p S^2$ (
the $xy$-plane) with positive determinant $\det = 4 > 0$ in both cases. Thus eac
h preimage contributes $+1$, giving $\deg(f) = 2$.

**Conclusion of Part 2.** The map $f$ is surjective, real analytic, has well-def
ined degree $2$, yet the fiber over the south pole is infinite. Therefore **no i
nteger $d$ can uniformly bound the number of preimages** of all points in $Y$.

### Remark on the holomorphic case

The situation differs for **complex analytic (holomorphic)** maps. If $X = Y = \
mathbb{CP}^n$ and $f$ is holomorphic, then every fiber $f^{-1}(y)$ is a compact
complex analytic subvariety of $\mathbb{CP}^n$. By the dimension theorem for hol
omorphic maps between equidimensional varieties, $\dim f^{-1}(y) \leq 0$, so eac
h fiber is a **finite** set of points. Moreover, the sum of scheme-theoretic mul
tiplicities over any fiber equals $\deg(f)$, giving $|f^{-1}(y)| \leq \deg(f)$ f
or all $y \in Y$. Thus for holomorphic maps, such a bound $d = \deg(f)$ **does**
 exist.

The counterexample above exploits the fact that real analytic maps (unlike holom
orphic maps) can collapse positive-dimensional submanifolds to points without vi
olating analyticity—our map collapses the entire equator (a 1-dimensional subman
ifold) to the south pole.

### Final Answer

The mapping degree of a surjective analytic map $f: X \to Y$ between compact, co
nnected, orientable manifolds of equal dimension **can always be defined** (via
the induced map on top homology). However, there does **not** in general exist a
n integer $d$ uniformly bounding the cardinality of all fibers. The map $f: S^2
\to S^2$, $f(x,y,z) = (2xz, 2yz, 2z^2-1)$, is a surjective real analytic map of
degree $2$ with an infinite fiber (the equator maps to the south pole), providin
g an explicit counterexample.
