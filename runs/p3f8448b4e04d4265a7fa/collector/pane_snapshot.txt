## Proof

Model the party as an undirected graph $G$ on the $2n+1$ people, where two peopl
e are adjacent iff they know each other (acquaintance is mutual — the standard i
nterpretation for party problems). The hypothesis says:

> For every $n$-element subset $S \subseteq V(G)$, there is a vertex $v \notin S
$ adjacent to every vertex of $S$.

We must show that some vertex is **universal** (adjacent to all the other $2n$ p
eople).

**Assume for contradiction** that no vertex is universal.

### Constructing the complement graph

Let $H = \overline{G}$ be the complement graph: $u,v$ are adjacent in $H$ iff th
ey do **not** know each other. Since no one knows everyone, every person fails t
o know at least one other person. Hence every vertex of $H$ has at least one nei
ghbor — **$H$ has no isolated vertex**.

### A lemma (Ore, 1962)

**Lemma.** *If a graph $H$ on $N$ vertices has no isolated vertex, then $H$ has
a dominating set of size at most $N/2$.* (A set $D$ is *dominating* if every ver
tex not in $D$ has a neighbor in $D$.)

*Proof of Lemma.* Let $D$ be an inclusion-wise minimal dominating set of $H$. We
 claim $V(H)\setminus D$ is also a dominating set.

A set $D'$ is dominating iff every vertex outside $D'$ has a neighbor in $D'$. T
aking $D' = V\setminus D$, the vertices outside $D'$ are exactly the vertices of
 $D$, so we must show: every $u \in D$ has a neighbor in $V\setminus D$.

Suppose some $u \in D$ has **no** neighbor in $V\setminus D$. Then all neighbors
 of $u$ lie in $D$. By minimality of $D$, the vertex $u$ has a *private neighbor
* $p$ with respect to $D$: a vertex $p \in N[u]$ (the closed neighborhood of $u$
) that is **not** dominated by $D\setminus\{u\}$.

- If $p = u$: then $u$ has no neighbor in $D\setminus\{u\}$, and by assumption n
o neighbor in $V\setminus D$ either. So $u$ is isolated in $H$ — contradicting t
hat $H$ has no isolated vertex.
- If $p \neq u$: then $p \in V\setminus D$ and $p$ is adjacent to $u$, contradic
ting that $u$ has no neighbor in $V\setminus D$.

Both cases are impossible, so every $u \in D$ has a neighbor in $V\setminus D$.
Hence $V\setminus D$ is a dominating set.

Since $D$ and $V\setminus D$ are both dominating and partition $V$, the smaller
of the two has size $\le N/2$. Therefore $\gamma(H) \le N/2$. $\square$

### Applying the lemma

Apply the lemma to $H$ with $N = 2n+1$: $H$ has a dominating set $D$ with
$$|D| \le \frac{2n+1}{2} = n + \tfrac{1}{2},$$
so, being an integer, $|D| \le n$.

Extend $D$ to a set $S$ with $|S| = n$ by adding arbitrary vertices (possible si
nce $2n+1 > n$). A superset of a dominating set is still dominating, so **$S$ is
 a dominating set of $H$ of size exactly $n$**.

### Reaching the contradiction

Translate back to $G$. Since $S$ dominates $H$, every vertex $v \notin S$ has a
neighbor $w \in S$ **in $H$**, which means $v$ and $w$ do **not** know each othe
r in $G$. Therefore $v$ does **not** know all members of $S$.

This holds for **every** $v \notin S$. So no person outside the $n$-person group
 $S$ knows all of them — directly contradicting the hypothesis that *every* $n$-
person group has an external person who knows all its members.

The contradiction refutes our assumption. Therefore **some person knows everyone
 in the party**. $\blacksquare$
