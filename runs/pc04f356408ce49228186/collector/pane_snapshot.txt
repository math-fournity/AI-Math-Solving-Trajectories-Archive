## Proof

The answer is **no**: $p(x)f(x)$ need not belong to $L^2(\mathbb{R})$.

**Counterexample.** Take the polynomial $p(x) = x$ and define

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{1+|x|}.$$

**Step 1: $f \in L^2(\mathbb{R})$.** We have

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}} |f(x)|^2\,dx = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \frac{dx}{(1+|x|)^2} = 2\in
t_0^\infty \frac{dx}{(1+x)^2} = 2\left[-\frac{1}{1+x}\right]_0^\infty = 2 < \inf
ty.$$

So $f \in L^2(\mathbb{R})$.

**Step 2: $xf \notin L^2(\mathbb{R})$.** We have

$$|xf(x)|^2 = \frac{x^2}{(1+|x|)^2}.$$

As $|x| \to \infty$, $\frac{x^2}{(1+|x|)^2} \to 1$. Therefore there exists $R >
0$ such that for $|x| \geq R$,

$$\frac{x^2}{(1+|x|)^2} \geq \frac{1}{2}.$$

Consequently,

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}} |xf(x)|^2\,dx \geq \int_{|x|\geq R} \frac{x^2}{(1+|x|)^2}\,d
x \geq \int_{|x|\geq R} \frac{1}{2}\,dx = \infty.$$

Hence $xf \notin L^2(\mathbb{R})$, i.e., $p(x)f(x) \notin L^2(\mathbb{R})$.

**Step 3: There exists a sequence $\{f_n\} \subset \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$ with
 $f_n \to f$ in $L^2$.** The Schwartz space $\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$ contains t
he compactly supported smooth functions $C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R})$, and $C_c^\inft
y(\mathbb{R})$ is dense in $L^2(\mathbb{R})$. Explicitly, let $\varphi \in C_c^\
infty(\mathbb{R})$ be a standard mollifier with $\int \varphi = 1$, and let $\ch
i_n \in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ be a smooth cutoff with $\chi_n \equiv 1$ on $[-
n,n]$ and $\chi_n \equiv 0$ outside $[-(n+1), n+1]$. Set

$$f_n = (\chi_n \cdot f) * \varphi_{1/n}, \qquad \varphi_{1/n}(x) = n\,\varphi(n
x).$$

Each $f_n \in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R}) \subset \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$, and by st
andard mollification and truncation arguments, $f_n \to f$ in $L^2(\mathbb{R})$.

**Conclusion.** We have constructed $f \in L^2(\mathbb{R})$ and a sequence $\{f_
n\} \subset \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$ converging to $f$ in $L^2$, yet for the pol
ynomial $p(x) = x$, the product $p(x)f(x) = xf(x)$ does not lie in $L^2(\mathbb{
R})$.

The essential reason is that $L^2$-convergence controls only the unweighted norm
 $\|f_n - f\|_2$, providing no control over the decay of $f$ at infinity. Polyno
mial weights amplify the tail behavior, and the tail of an arbitrary $L^2$ limit
 need not decay fast enough to keep $p\cdot f$ square-integrable. $\blacksquare$
