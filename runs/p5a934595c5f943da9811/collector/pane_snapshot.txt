# General Solution of Laplace's Equation in Two Dimensions

## Problem

Find the general solution of the two-dimensional Laplace's equation

$$\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} = 0.$$

## Proof

We derive the general solution by separation of variables in two coordinate syst
ems, each yielding a complete family whose linear combinations form the general
solution.

### 1. Cartesian coordinates

Assume a separable solution $u(x,y)=X(x)Y(y)$. Substituting into $\Delta u=0$ an
d dividing by $XY$ gives

$$\frac{X''(x)}{X(x)} = -\frac{Y''(y)}{Y(y)}.$$

The left side depends only on $x$, the right only on $y$, so both equal a consta
nt $\lambda$ (the **separation constant**). This yields the two ODEs

$$X''-\lambda X = 0,\qquad Y''+\lambda Y = 0.$$

The character of the solutions depends on the sign of $\lambda$.

**Case $\lambda = k^2>0$ ($k>0$).** Then
$$X(x)=A\cosh kx + B\sinh kx,\qquad Y(y)=C\cos ky + D\sin ky,$$
so
$$u_k(x,y)=(A_k\cosh kx+B_k\sinh kx)(C_k\cos ky+D_k\sin ky).$$

**Case $\lambda = -k^2<0$ ($k>0$).** Then
$$X(x)=A\cos kx + B\sin kx,\qquad Y(y)=C\cosh ky + D\sinh ky,$$
so
$$u_k(x,y)=(A_k\cos kx+B_k\sin kx)(C_k\cosh ky+D_k\sin ky).$$

**Case $\lambda=0$.** Then $X''=0$, $Y''=0$, giving the linear (affine) harmonic
 functions
$$u_0(x,y)=a+bx+cy+dxy.$$

Because Laplace's equation is linear, any convergent linear combination (or inte
gral) of these separable solutions is again harmonic. Conversely, completeness o
f the trigonometric/hyperbolic bases on rectangular domains guarantees that ever
y harmonic function on a rectangle is representable by such a superposition. Hen
ce the **general solution in Cartesian form** is

$$\boxed{\,u(x,y)=a+bx+cy+dxy+\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\bigl[(A(k)\cosh kx+B(k)\sinh k
x)(C(k)\cos ky+D(k)\sin ky)\\ +(E(k)\cos kx+F(k)\sin kx)(G(k)\cosh ky+H(k)\sinh
ky)\bigr]\,dk\,}$$

with the integral replaced by a sum $\sum_k$ when boundary conditions force a di
screte spectrum (e.g. on a finite rectangle). The constants and the spectral fun
ctions $A,\dots,H$ are determined by boundary data.

### 2. Polar coordinates (the more canonical "general solution")

Write the equation in polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$, with $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r
\sin\theta$:

$$\Delta u = u_{rr}+\frac{1}{r}u_r+\frac{1}{r^2}u_{\theta\theta}=0.$$

Set $u(r,\theta)=R(r)\Theta(\theta)$. Substituting and multiplying by $r^2/(R\Th
eta)$:

$$\frac{r^2R''+rR'}{R} = -\frac{\Theta''}{\Theta} = \mu,$$

a constant. The angular equation $\Theta''+\mu\Theta=0$ with the physical requir
ement that $u$ be single-valued ($\Theta$ $2\pi$-periodic) forces $\mu=n^2$ for
$n=0,1,2,\dots$, giving

$$\Theta_n(\theta)=A_n\cos n\theta + B_n\sin n\theta,\qquad n\ge 1,\qquad \Theta
_0(\theta)=\tfrac12 A_0.$$

The radial equation $r^2R''+rR'-n^2R=0$ is a Cauchy–Euler equation. For $n\ge1$
its two independent solutions are $r^n$ and $r^{-n}$; for $n=0$ they are $1$ and
 $\ln r$. Thus

$$R_0(r)=a_0+b_0\ln r,\qquad R_n(r)=a_n r^n + b_n r^{-n},\quad n\ge1.$$

Superposing all separated solutions (linearity of $\Delta$) and using completene
ss of the Fourier basis on $[0,2\pi]$ — which guarantees that every $2\pi$-perio
dic angular dependence can be expanded in $\{\cos n\theta,\sin n\theta\}$ — give
s the **general solution in polar form**:

$$\boxed{\,u(r,\theta)=a_0+b_0\ln r+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(a_n r^n+b_n r^{-n}\
bigr)\cos n\theta+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(c_n r^n+d_n r^{-n}\bigr)\sin n\theta\
,}$$

where the series converges on any annulus on which $u$ is harmonic, and the coef
ficients are determined by boundary (or asymptotic) conditions.

### 3. Why this is "the" general solution

* **Completeness.** The trigonometric system $\{1,\cos n\theta,\sin n\theta\}$ i
s complete in $L^2[0,2\pi]$; hence every harmonic function's angular dependence
is captured. The radial ODE is second-order, so $r^n,r^{-n}$ (and $1,\ln r$) spa
n all radial factors. Therefore no harmonic function (regular on an annulus) is
omitted.
* **Exclusion of the singular modes.** On a domain containing the origin and on
which $u$ is required to be bounded, the terms $b_0\ln r$ and $b_n r^{-n},d_n r^
{-n}$ are discarded, leaving the familiar **interior** solution $u=a_0+\sum_{n\g
e1}r^n(a_n\cos n\theta+c_n\sin n\theta)$. On an exterior domain the growing term
s $a_n r^n$ are discarded instead. The boxed formula with all terms retained is
the general solution on an annulus $r_1<r<r_2$.
* **Equivalence of the two forms.** The Cartesian and polar representations are
related by the identities $\cosh kx\cos ky$, $\cos kx\cosh ky$, etc., and by $r^
n\cos n\theta=\operatorname{Re}(x+iy)^n$, $\ln r=\tfrac12\ln(x^2+y^2)$. They are
 two Fourier-type expansions of the same harmonic function in different bases; e
ither one, with arbitrary coefficients, constitutes the general solution.

### 4. Compact complex form (optional but standard)

Writing $z=x+iy$, a function $u$ is harmonic iff locally $u=\operatorname{Re} f(
z)$ for some holomorphic $f$. Expanding $f$ in a Laurent series $f(z)=\sum_{n=-\
infty}^{\infty}\alpha_n z^n$ (with the $n=0$ term augmented by $b_0\ln|z|$ to al
low a logarithmic harmonic term) and taking the real part recovers exactly the p
olar series above, since $\operatorname{Re}(z^n)=r^n\cos n\theta$ and $\operator
name{Re}(z^{-n})=r^{-n}\cos n\theta$. This gives the same general solution in th
e compact form

$$u(r,\theta)=\operatorname{Re}\!\left(\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\alpha_n z^n\rig
ht)+b_0\ln|z|.$$

## Summary

The general solution of the two-dimensional Laplace equation is the arbitrary li
near superposition of all separated harmonic solutions. In polar coordinates it
reads

$$u(r,\theta)=a_0+b_0\ln r+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl[(a_n r^n+b_n r^{-n})\cos n\t
heta+(c_n r^n+d_n r^{-n})\sin n\theta\bigr],$$

with the coefficients fixed by boundary conditions; singular ($r^{-n}$, $\ln r$)
 terms are dropped on domains where $u$ must be regular. Equivalently, in Cartes
ian coordinates it is a superposition of products of trigonometric and hyperboli
c functions together with the affine term $a+bx+cy+dxy$. Both forms are complete
 and equivalent, related by the standard identities between $\{r^n\cos n\theta\}
$ and $\{\cosh kx\cos ky,\cos kx\cosh ky\}$.
