Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether differentiating the coeffic
ients $a_k(s)$ with respect to the parameter $s$ preserves a lower bound on the
radius of convergence.

**Answer: No.** The radius of convergence of $\sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k'(s)\,x^k$ ne
ed not be at least $R$.

---

## Counterexample

Let $R = 1$ and let the open interval be $I = (-1, 1)$. Define

$$a_k(s) = \frac{1}{2^k}\,\sin(k!\,s), \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$

Each $a_k(s)$ is differentiable (in fact $C^\infty$) on all of $\mathbb{R}$, hen
ce on $I$.

### The series $\sum a_k(s)\,x^k$ has radius of convergence $\geq 2 > R = 1$ for
 every $s \in I$.

For every $s$ and every $k$:

$$|a_k(s)| = \frac{|\sin(k!\,s)|}{2^k} \leq \frac{1}{2^k}.$$

Therefore

$$\limsup_{k\to\infty} |a_k(s)|^{1/k} \leq \limsup_{k\to\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2
^k}\right)^{1/k} = \frac{1}{2}.$$

By the Cauchy–Hadamard theorem, the radius of convergence of $\sum a_k(s)\,x^k$
is

$$\rho(s) = \frac{1}{\limsup_{k\to\infty}|a_k(s)|^{1/k}} \geq \frac{1}{1/2} = 2
> 1 = R,$$

for every $s \in I$. ✓

### The series $\sum a_k'(s)\,x^k$ has radius of convergence $0$ at $s = 0$.

Differentiating:

$$a_k'(s) = \frac{k!}{2^k}\,\cos(k!\,s).$$

Evaluating at $s = 0$:

$$a_k'(0) = \frac{k!}{2^k}\,\cos(0) = \frac{k!}{2^k}.$$

Hence

$$|a_k'(0)|^{1/k} = \frac{(k!)^{1/k}}{2}.$$

By Stirling's approximation, $k! \sim \sqrt{2\pi k}\,(k/e)^k$, so $(k!)^{1/k} \s
im k/e \to \infty$. Therefore

$$\limsup_{k\to\infty} |a_k'(0)|^{1/k} = \infty,$$

and the radius of convergence of $\sum a_k'(0)\,x^k$ is

$$\rho'(0) = \frac{1}{\limsup_{k\to\infty}|a_k'(0)|^{1/k}} = \frac{1}{\infty} =
0 < 1 = R.$$

---

## Conclusion

The radius of convergence of $\sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k'(s)\,x^k$ is **not** necessa
rily at least $R$. The counterexample above shows it can be as small as $0$: the
 coefficients $a_k(s) = \frac{1}{2^k}\sin(k!\,s)$ are uniformly bounded by $1/2^
k$ (giving radius $\geq 2 > R$ for the original series at every $s$), yet differ
entiation with respect to $s$ introduces a factor of $k!$, which grows super-exp
onentially and drives the radius of the differentiated series to $0$.

The essential reason is that **differentiability of $a_k$ in $s$ provides no uni
form control over the growth of $a_k'(s)$ as $k \to \infty$**. A single derivati
ve in $s$ can amplify the $k$-th coefficient by a factor (here $k!$) that is sup
er-exponential in $k$, overwhelming the exponential decay ($2^{-k}$) that guaran
teed convergence of the original series.
