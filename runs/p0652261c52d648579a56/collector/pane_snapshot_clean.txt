## Determination

**No** — there is no (unital, associative) ring $R$ whose additive group $(R,+)$
 is isomorphic to the circle group $S^{1}$.

*(If one adopts the convention that "ring" need not possess a multiplicative ide
ntity, then the zero-multiplication ring on any abelian group gives a trivial "y
es"; the interesting and standard interpretation — rings with $1$ — yields the n
egative answer below.)*

---

## Proof

**1. The torsion subgroup of $S^{1}$.**
Via $t\mapsto e^{2\pi i t}$ we have an isomorphism of abelian groups $S^{1}\cong
 \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$. The torsion subgroup of $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ is $\ma
thbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$, and
$$\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}\;\cong\;\bigoplus_{p\ \text{prime}} C_{p^{\infty}},$$
where $C_{p^{\infty}}$ is the Prüfer $p$-group. In particular the torsion subgro
up $T:=\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ of $S^{1}$ is **nonzero**.

Since $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ is divisible and divisible groups are injective $\
mathbb{Z}$-modules, the short exact sequence
$$0\to T\to S^{1}\to S^{1}/T\to 0$$
splits. The quotient $S^{1}/T\cong \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}$ is a torsion-free divi
sible group, i.e. a $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space $V$ (of dimension $\mathfrak{c}$).
 Hence, as abelian groups,
$$S^{1}\;\cong\;T\oplus V,\qquad T=\bigoplus_{p}C_{p^{\infty}},\quad V\text{ a }
\mathbb{Q}\text{-vector space}.$$

**2. A tensor-product vanishing.**
We claim $T\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} S^{1}=0$ and $S^{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} T=0$.

It suffices to show $C_{p^{\infty}}\otimes C_{q^{\infty}}=0$ for all primes $p,q
$ and $C_{p^{\infty}}\otimes V=0$.

- *$C_{p^{\infty}}\otimes C_{q^{\infty}}=0$:* Take $a\in C_{p^{\infty}}$ of orde
r $p^{m}$ and $b\in C_{q^{\infty}}$. Since $C_{q^{\infty}}$ is divisible, $b=p^{
m}c$ for some $c$. Then
$$a\otimes b=a\otimes p^{m}c=p^{m}a\otimes c=0\otimes c=0.$$
- *$C_{p^{\infty}}\otimes V=0$:* Take $a$ of order $p^{m}$ and $v\in V$. Since $
V$ is a $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space, $v=p^{m}v'$ for some $v'\in V$, and
$$a\otimes v=a\otimes p^{m}v'=p^{m}a\otimes v'=0.$$

Summing over the prime components gives $T\otimes T=0$, $T\otimes V=0$, hence $T
\otimes S^{1}=0$; by symmetry $S^{1}\otimes T=0$.

**3. The torsion part is annihilated by every ring structure.**
Suppose $R$ is any (associative) ring with $(R,+)\cong S^{1}$, and identify $(R,
+)=S^{1}$, so $T\subseteq R$ is the torsion subgroup. Multiplication is a $\math
bb{Z}$-bilinear map $\mu:R\times R\to R$, hence factors through $R\otimes_{\math
bb{Z}} R$. The restriction $\mu|_{T\times R}$ factors through $T\otimes R=0$, so
$$T\cdot R=0.$$
Likewise the restriction $\mu|_{R\times T}$ factors through $R\otimes T=0$, so
$$R\cdot T=0.$$
Thus **every element of $T$ is annihilated (on both sides) by every element of $
R$** — regardless of which ring structure is chosen.

**4. Contradiction with the existence of $1$.**
If $R$ is unital with identity $1\in R$, then for every $t\in T$,
$$t=t\cdot 1=0,$$
since $t\in T$ and $T\cdot R=0$. Hence $T=0$.

But $T=\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}\neq 0$. This is a contradiction.

**5. Conclusion.**
No associative unital ring $R$ can have $(R,+)\cong S^{1}$: the nonzero divisibl
e torsion subgroup $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}\le S^{1}$ is forced into the two-sided
 annihilator by the vanishing of the relevant tensor products, which is incompat
ible with the existence of a multiplicative identity. $\blacksquare$
