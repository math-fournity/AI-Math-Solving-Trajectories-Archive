## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, $f$ is necessarily holomorphic on $D$ (in particular, on $\opera
torname{int} D$).

We prove this by Morera's theorem. Recall:

> **Morera's Theorem.** Let $\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}$ be open and $g:\Omega\to
\mathbb{C}$ continuous. If $\int_{\partial T} g(z)\,dz = 0$ for every closed tri
angle $T\subseteq\Omega$, then $g$ is holomorphic on $\Omega$.

We apply this with $\Omega = \operatorname{int} D$ and $g = f|_{\operatorname{in
t}D}$, which is continuous since $f$ is continuous on $D$.

---

**Setup.** Let $C\subset D$ be a continuously embedded (Jordan) arc from $-1$ to
 $1$, with $C\subset D$ and $C\cap\partial D = \{-1,1\}$. By the Jordan arc theo
rem, $D\setminus C$ has exactly two connected components, $D_1$ and $D_2$, each
open in $\mathbb{C}$ (their interiors), with $\overline{D_1}\cup\overline{D_2}=D
$ and $\overline{D_1}\cap\overline{D_2}=C$. By hypothesis, $f$ is holomorphic on
 $D_1$ and on $D_2$, and continuous on all of $D$.

---

**Key step: vanishing of triangle integrals.** Let $T\subseteq\operatorname{int}
D$ be any closed triangle. We show $\int_{\partial T} f\,dz = 0$.

The arc $C$ meets $T$ in a closed subset of $T$. Since $C$ is a Jordan arc (a ho
meomorphic image of $[0,1]$) and $T$ is compact, $C\cap T$ is a finite union of
cross-cuts of $T$: it consists of finitely many sub-arcs of $C$, each with endpo
ints on $\partial T$, and possibly sub-arcs with one or both endpoints in $\oper
atorname{int}T$ (but since $C$ enters and exits $T$ through $\partial T$, and $C
$ is embedded, the intersection $C\cap\operatorname{int}T$ is a finite union of
arcs each connecting two points of $\partial T$). These cross-cuts partition $T$
 into finitely many closed regions

$$T = R_1 \cup R_2 \cup \cdots \cup R_m,$$

where each $R_j$ has piecewise-smooth boundary (composed of line segments from $
\partial T$ and sub-arcs of $C$), and each $\operatorname{int} R_j$ lies entirel
y in $D_1$ or entirely in $D_2$.

*Justification of finiteness and the cross-cut structure:* $C$ is the image of a
n embedding $\gamma:[0,1]\to D$. The preimage $\gamma^{-1}(T)$ is a compact subs
et of $[0,1]$. Since $\gamma$ is an embedding and $T$ is a polygon, $\gamma^{-1}
(\operatorname{int}T)$ is an open subset of $[0,1]$, hence a finite union of dis
joint open intervals (as $\gamma$ crosses each side of $T$ at most finitely many
 times — each side is a line segment and $C$ meets it in finitely many points be
cause $C$ is a Jordan arc, which intersects a line in finitely many components;
a standard result for Jordan arcs). Each such interval maps to a sub-arc of $C$
crossing $T$, forming a cross-cut. (If $C\cap T = \varnothing$, then $T\subseteq
 D_i$ for some $i$ and the result is immediate from Cauchy–Goursat.)

---

**Applying Cauchy's theorem on each piece.** For each $j$, $\operatorname{int}R_
j \subseteq D_i$ for some $i\in\{1,2\}$. Since $f$ is holomorphic on $D_i$ and c
ontinuous on $\overline{R_j}\subseteq D$, the general Cauchy theorem (valid for
regions with piecewise-$C^1$ boundary, by approximating with polygons or by the
standard extension via Green/Cauchy–Goursat) gives

$$\int_{\partial R_j} f(z)\,dz = 0.$$

*Detail on the general Cauchy theorem here:* $R_j$ is a compact region whose bou
ndary is a finite union of line segments and $C^1$ arcs (sub-arcs of $C$). Since
 $f$ is holomorphic on a neighborhood of $R_j$ within $D_i$ (namely $\operatorna
me{int}R_j$ plus a collar inside $D_i$), and $f$ is continuous on $\overline{R_j
}$, the integral vanishes. Concretely, one triangulates $R_j$ into small triangl
es each lying in $D_i$; on each, Cauchy–Goursat gives zero; summing, the interna
l edges cancel, leaving $\int_{\partial R_j}f=0$. The continuity of $f$ on $\ove
rline{R_j}$ ensures the boundary integral is well-defined and the approximation
is valid.

---

**Summing over all pieces.** Sum the identities $\int_{\partial R_j}f\,dz=0$ ove
r $j=1,\dots,m$:

$$0 = \sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{\partial R_j} f\,dz.$$

Each boundary $\partial R_j$ consists of:
- segments lying on $\partial T$ (traversed once, in the direction inherited fro
m $\partial T$), and
- sub-arcs of $C$.

Every sub-arc of $C$ appears in exactly two adjacent regions $R_j, R_k$, travers
ed in **opposite** directions. Since $f$ is **continuous on $C$** (hence single-
valued there — the same value is taken from both sides), the two contributions c
ancel:

$$\int_{\text{arc}} f\,dz \big|_{R_j} + \int_{\text{arc}} f\,dz \big|_{R_k} = 0.
$$

The remaining contributions are exactly the segments of $\partial T$, which reas
semble to $\int_{\partial T}f\,dz$. Therefore

$$\int_{\partial T} f(z)\,dz = 0.$$

---

**Conclusion via Morera.** The above holds for every closed triangle $T\subseteq
\operatorname{int}D$. Since $f$ is continuous on $\operatorname{int}D$, Morera's
 theorem implies $f$ is holomorphic on $\operatorname{int}D$.

Since $f$ is holomorphic on $\operatorname{int}D$ and continuous on $D$, $f$ is
holomorphic on $D$ (in the sense that it is holomorphic on the interior and exte
nds continuously to the boundary; in particular, $f$ is holomorphic at every int
erior point of $D$, which is the content of the question).

---

**Remark on the necessity of continuity.** The continuity of $f$ across $C$ is e
ssential. Without it, one could take $f\equiv 0$ on $D_1$ and $f\equiv 1$ on $D_
2$; this is holomorphic on each half but not even continuous on $D$, hence not h
olomorphic. The continuity hypothesis is precisely what makes the boundary integ
rals along $C$ cancel, which is the mechanism driving Morera's theorem.
