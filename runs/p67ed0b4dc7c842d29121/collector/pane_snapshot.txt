## Solution

**Claim:** The minimum number of zeros of $f$ in $(a,b)$ is $\boxed{n+1}$.

### Part 1: Lower bound — $f$ has at least $n+1$ zeros

Suppose $f$ is not identically zero (if $f \equiv 0$, it has infinitely many zer
os and we are done). Let $y_1 < y_2 < \cdots < y_r$ be the points in $(a,b)$ whe
re $f$ **changes sign** (i.e., $f$ has a zero at $y_j$ and $f$ takes opposite si
gns on the two sides of $y_j$). These are a subset of all zeros of $f$.

**Claim:** $r \geq n+1$.

Suppose for contradiction that $r \leq n$. Define the polynomial
$$q(t) = \prod_{j=1}^{r}(t - y_j),$$
which has degree $r \leq n$. Since $q(t) = c_0 + c_1 t + \cdots + c_r t^r$ is a
polynomial of degree $\leq n$, by linearity of the integral and the hypothesis $
\int_a^b t^k f(t)\,dt = 0$ for $k = 0, 1, \ldots, n$:
$$\int_a^b q(t)\,f(t)\,dt = \sum_{k=0}^{r} c_k \int_a^b t^k f(t)\,dt = 0. \tag{1
}$$

Now analyze the sign of $q(t)f(t)$ on $[a,b]$. The points $y_1, \ldots, y_r$ par
tition $[a,b]$ into $r+1$ subintervals. On each open subinterval, $f$ has consta
nt sign (no sign changes occur there, by definition of the $y_j$'s as the comple
te set of sign-changing zeros). Likewise, $q$ has constant sign on each subinter
val, and $q$ changes sign at each $y_j$ (since each factor $(t - y_j)$ changes s
ign there).

At each $y_j$, **both** $f$ and $q$ change sign, so their product $q(t)f(t)$ doe
s **not** change sign. Between consecutive $y_j$'s, both $f$ and $q$ maintain co
nstant sign. Therefore $q(t)f(t)$ has **constant sign** on all of $[a,b]$.

Since $qf$ is continuous and of constant sign on $[a,b]$, the integral $\int_a^b
 q(t)f(t)\,dt$ can be zero only if $q(t)f(t) \equiv 0$ on $[a,b]$. But $q$ is a
polynomial with only finitely many zeros, so $q(t) \neq 0$ except at $y_1, \ldot
s, y_r$. Thus $f(t) = 0$ for all $t \in [a,b] \setminus \{y_1,\ldots,y_r\}$, and
 by continuity $f \equiv 0$ on $[a,b]$—contradicting our assumption that $f$ is
not identically zero.

Therefore $r \geq n+1$, and since every sign-changing zero is a zero, $f$ has at
 least $n+1$ zeros in $(a,b)$.

### Part 2: Upper bound — $n+1$ zeros is achievable

We construct a continuous (in fact polynomial) function satisfying the moment co
nditions with exactly $n+1$ zeros.

Let $P_{n+1}(x)$ be the **Legendre polynomial** of degree $n+1$, which satisfies
 the orthogonality relation:
$$\int_{-1}^{1} P_{n+1}(x)\,g(x)\,dx = 0 \quad \text{for every polynomial } g \t
ext{ of degree } \leq n.$$
It is well known that $P_{n+1}$ has exactly $n+1$ simple roots, all lying in $(-
1, 1)$.

Define the affine map $L: [a,b] \to [-1,1]$ by $L(t) = \frac{2t - a - b}{b - a}$
, and set
$$f(t) = P_{n+1}(L(t)).$$

**Zeros:** $f(t) = 0 \iff L(t) \in \{\text{roots of } P_{n+1}\}$. Since $L$ maps
 $(a,b)$ bijectively onto $(-1,1)$, the $n+1$ simple roots of $P_{n+1}$ in $(-1,
1)$ correspond to exactly $n+1$ simple zeros of $f$ in $(a,b)$.

**Moment conditions:** For each $k \in \{0, 1, \ldots, n\}$, substitute $x = L(t
)$, i.e., $t = \frac{b-a}{2}x + \frac{a+b}{2}$, $dt = \frac{b-a}{2}dx$:
$$\int_a^b t^k f(t)\,dt = \frac{b-a}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(\tfrac{b-a}{2}x + \tfr
ac{a+b}{2}\right)^k P_{n+1}(x)\,dx.$$
The integrand's polynomial factor $\left(\tfrac{b-a}{2}x + \tfrac{a+b}{2}\right)
^k$ has degree $k \leq n$ in $x$. By the orthogonality of $P_{n+1}$ to all polyn
omials of degree $\leq n$, this integral equals $0$.

Thus $f(t) = P_{n+1}(L(t))$ is a continuous function on $[a,b]$ with $\int_a^b t
^k f(t)\,dt = 0$ for all $k = 0, \ldots, n$, and exactly $n+1$ zeros in $(a,b)$.

### Conclusion

The lower bound shows any such $f$ has at least $n+1$ zeros, and the constructio

$ in $(a,b)$ is $n+1$**.
