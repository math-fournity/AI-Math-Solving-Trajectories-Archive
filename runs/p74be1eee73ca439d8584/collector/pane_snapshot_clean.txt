## Proof

**Claim.** Yes, a complete orthonormal sequence with a countable number of vecto
rs can exist in $\mathcal{L}_2(-\pi,\pi)$. The apparent paradox — that each vect
or "has uncountably many components" — does not prevent this. We resolve the par
adox and give an explicit construction.

---

### 1. What "components" means, and why the paradox is illusory

The worry in the problem is this: a vector $f \in \mathcal{L}_2(-\pi,\pi)$ is a
(class of) function(s) defined on the uncountable set $(-\pi,\pi)$, so it seems
to have one "component" $f(t)$ for each $t \in (-\pi,\pi)$ — uncountably many co
mponents. How, then, can a *countable* orthonormal sequence be complete, i.e., s
pan the whole space?

The resolution has three layers.

**(a) Elements of $\mathcal{L}_2$ are equivalence classes, not point-valued vect
ors.** An element of $\mathcal{L}_2(-\pi,\pi)$ is an equivalence class of square
-integrable functions under the relation $f \sim g \iff f = g$ a.e. The value $f
(t)$ at any single point $t$ is not even well-defined on the equivalence class.
So point values are *not* "components" in any linear-algebraic sense. The "uncou
ntably many components" intuition is borrowed from $\mathbb{R}^n$ (where compone
nts are coordinates against a fixed basis) and does not transfer to function spa
ce.

**(b) "Components" against an orthonormal basis are Fourier coefficients, which
are countable.** Once an orthonormal system $\{e_n\}$ is fixed, the genuine "com
ponents" of $f$ are the inner products $\langle f, e_n \rangle$ — and there is o
ne per basis vector. If the basis is countable, every vector has only countably
many genuine components. The uncountability of the domain is irrelevant to the n
umber of coordinates.

**(c) Separability is the structural reason.** The real question is whether $\ma
thcal{L}_2(-\pi,\pi)$ admits a countable *complete* orthonormal system. This is
equivalent to separability, and $\mathcal{L}_2(-\pi,\pi)$ is separable. We prove
 both directions.

### 2. $\mathcal{L}_2(-\pi,\pi)$ is separable

Consider the set $\mathcal{P}$ of polynomials with rational coefficients restric
ted to $(-\pi,\pi)$:
$$\mathcal{P} = \left\{ \sum_{k=0}^{N} q_k t^k \;\middle|\; N \in \mathbb{N},\;
q_k \in \mathbb{Q} \right\}.$$
This is a countable set (countable union of countable sets). We claim it is dens
e in $\mathcal{L}_2(-\pi,\pi)$.

- **Step 1: Continuous functions are dense.** For any $f \in \mathcal{L}_2(-\pi,
\pi)$ and $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $g \in C_c(-\pi,\pi)$ (continuous, com
pactly supported in the open interval) with $\|f - g\|_2 < \varepsilon/2$. This
is a standard measure-theory fact: simple functions are dense in $\mathcal{L}_2$
, and simple functions are approximated by continuous ones (e.g., via Lusin's th
eorem / mollification).

- **Step 2: Polynomials approximate continuous functions.** By the Weierstrass a
pproximation theorem (applied to the compact closure $[-\pi+\delta, \pi-\delta]$
 containing the support of $g$), for any $\delta > 0$ there is a polynomial $p$
with $|g(t) - p(t)| < \delta$ uniformly on the support. Choosing $\delta$ small
enough gives $\|g - p\|_2 < \varepsilon/2$.

- **Step 3: Rational-coefficient polynomials approximate real-coefficient polyno
mials.** Given a real-coefficient polynomial $p(t) = \sum a_k t^k$ on the bounde
d interval $(-\pi,\pi)$, approximate each $a_k$ by a rational $q_k$ close enough
 that $\|p - \tilde{p}\|_2 < \varepsilon/2$, where $\tilde{p}(t) = \sum q_k t^k
\in \mathcal{P}$. This is possible because there are finitely many coefficients
and the interval is bounded.

Combining: $\|f - \tilde{p}\|_2 \leq \|f - g\|_2 + \|g - p\|_2 + \|p - \tilde{p}
\|_2 < \varepsilon.$

Hence $\mathcal{P}$ is a countable dense subset, and $\mathcal{L}_2(-\pi,\pi)$ i
s **separable**.

### 3. Separability $\iff$ existence of a countable complete orthonormal system

**($\Leftarrow$)** If $\{e_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ is a complete orthonormal system, t
hen the set of finite linear combinations $\sum_{n=1}^{N} q_n e_n$ with $q_n \in
 \mathbb{Q} + i\mathbb{Q}$ (rational real and imaginary parts) is countable and
dense (by completeness, every $f$ is the $\mathcal{L}_2$-limit of its Fourier pa
rtial sums, and rational coefficients approximate the real ones). So the space i
s separable.

**($\Rightarrow$)** If the space is separable with countable dense subset $\{g_n
\}$, apply the Gram–Schmidt process: define $e_1 = g_{n_1}/\|g_{n_1}\|$ where $g
_{n_1}$ is the first nonzero element, and inductively orthogonalize each subsequ
ent $g_n$ against the previously constructed $e_k$'s, discarding any that become
 zero. This produces a countable (finite or infinite) orthonormal sequence $\{e_
n\}$. It is complete: if $\langle f, e_n \rangle = 0$ for all $n$, then $\langle
 f, g_n \rangle = 0$ for all $n$ (each $g_n$ is a finite linear combination of $
e_k$'s), so by density $\|f\| = 0$, i.e., $f = 0$.

Since $\mathcal{L}_2(-\pi,\pi)$ is infinite-dimensional (it contains the linearl
y independent set $\{1, t, t^2, \ldots\}$), the Gram–Schmidt process produces an
 *infinite* countable orthonormal sequence. Combining with Section 2:

> **$\mathcal{L}_2(-\pi,\pi)$ admits a countable complete orthonormal sequence.*
*

### 4. Explicit example: the trigonometric system

The classical concrete instance is the trigonometric orthonormal system:
$$e_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}, \qquad e_{2n-1} = \frac{\cos(nt)}{\sqrt{\pi}}, \q
quad e_{2n} = \frac{\sin(nt)}{\sqrt{\pi}}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$$

- **Orthonormality** is verified by direct integration using the standard identi
ties $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos(mt)\cos(nt)\,dt = \pi\delta_{mn}$, $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi
}\sin(mt)\sin(nt)\,dt = \pi\delta_{mn}$, $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos(mt)\sin(nt)\,dt
= 0$, and orthogonality of $1$ against $\cos(nt), \sin(nt)$.

- **Completeness** is the content of the Riesz–Fischer theorem combined with the
 density of trigonometric polynomials (e.g., via Fejér's theorem: the Cesàro mea
ns of the Fourier series of any $f \in \mathcal{L}_2$ converge to $f$ in $\mathc
al{L}_2$-norm). Equivalently, if $\langle f, e_n\rangle = 0$ for all $n$, then a
ll Fourier coefficients of $f$ vanish, so $f = 0$ a.e.

This is a **countably infinite** complete orthonormal sequence in $\mathcal{L}_2
(-\pi,\pi)$.

### 5. Resolution of the paradox, summarized

| Apparent obstacle | Why it fails to obstruct |
|---|---|
| "Each vector has uncountably many components (point values)" | $\mathcal{L}_2$
 elements are a.e.-equivalence classes; point values are not well-defined and ar
e not "components." |
| "Uncountably many components need uncountably many basis vectors" | The genuin
e components are Fourier coefficients $\langle f, e_n\rangle$ — one per basis ve
ctor — which are countable when the basis is countable. |
| "How can countable span uncountable?" | Separability: a countable dense set ex
ists (rational-coefficient polynomials), and density — not cardinality of the do
main — is what completeness of an orthonormal system requires. |

The uncountability of the *domain* $(-\pi,\pi)$ governs the cardinality of point
 evaluations, which are not the structure of $\mathcal{L}_2$. The structure that
 matters — the Hilbert-space topology — is governed by separability, which holds
 because square-integrable functions on a finite-measure interval can be approxi
mated in norm by countably many building blocks (polynomials with rational coeff
icients, or equivalently trigonometric polynomials with rational coefficients).
A countable complete orthonormal sequence therefore exists, and the trigonometri
c system is an explicit one.

$\blacksquare$
