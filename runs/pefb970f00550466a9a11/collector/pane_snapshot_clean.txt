## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. If $G$ is analytic and bounded in $\mathbb{C}^+$ with $|G(z)| \l
eq C$ for all $z \in \mathbb{C}^+$, and $G$ has no poles on $\mathbb{R}$, then $
G(x)$ is bounded on $\mathbb{R}$ with $|G(x)| \leq C$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$
 (i.e., $C_o = C$ suffices).

**Key observation.** The condition "$G(x)$ has no poles on $\mathbb{R}$" means t
hat $G$ extends holomorphically across the real line — at each point $x_0 \in \m
athbb{R}$, $G$ is analytic in a full neighborhood of $x_0$ (not merely from abov
e). In particular, $G$ is **continuous** at every point of $\mathbb{R}$.

**Argument.** Fix an arbitrary $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}$. Since $G$ is holomorphic at
 $x_0$, it is continuous there. Consider the vertical approach from within the u
pper half-plane: the points $z_y = x_0 + iy$ for $y > 0$ all lie in $\mathbb{C}^
+$ and satisfy $z_y \to x_0$ as $y \to 0^+$. By continuity of $G$ at $x_0$:

$$G(x_0) = \lim_{y \to 0^+} G(x_0 + iy).$$

Since each $x_0 + iy \in \mathbb{C}^+$, the boundedness hypothesis gives $|G(x_0
 + iy)| \leq C$ for every $y > 0$. Taking the limit and using continuity of the
modulus:

$$|G(x_0)| = \lim_{y \to 0^+} |G(x_0 + iy)| \leq C.$$

Since $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}$ was arbitrary, we conclude

$$|G(x)| \leq C \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathbb{R}.$$

**Remark on the sharpness of the bound.** The constant $C_o = C$ is optimal: it
is the same bound that holds in the interior. No larger constant is needed, and
in general no smaller constant suffices (consider, e.g., $G(z) = e^{iz}$, which
satisfies $|G(z)| = e^{-\operatorname{Im} z} \leq 1$ in $\mathbb{C}^+$ and $|G(x
)| = 1$ on $\mathbb{R}$, attaining the bound).

**Remark on the role of "no poles."** The hypothesis that $G$ has no poles on $\
mathbb{R}$ is essential for the argument, as it guarantees continuity at every r
eal point. Without it, $G$ could have a pole at some $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}$, makin
g $|G(x_0)| = \infty$. Note also that an essential singularity at a real point i
s ruled out implicitly: if $G$ is bounded by $C$ in $\mathbb{C}^+$ near $x_0$ an
d $x_0$ is an isolated singularity, then by the Riemann removable singularity th
eorem (applied to the restriction from a punctured neighborhood), $x_0$ must be
a removable singularity — not an essential one. Thus "no poles" together with bo
undedness from above forces $G$ to extend analytically across $\mathbb{R}$, and
the continuity argument applies. $\blacksquare$
