## Baire Category Theorem

**Theorem (Baire, complete metric space form).** Let $(X, d)$ be a complete, non
-empty metric space. Then $X$ cannot be written as a countable union of closed s
ets each having empty interior. Equivalently, if
$$X = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} F_n, \qquad F_n \subseteq X \text{ closed},$$
then at least one $F_n$ has non-empty interior: there exists some $n$ and some n
on-empty open ball $B \subseteq F_n$.

A set whose complement contains a dense open set is called *meagre* (or *of the
first category*); a set which is not meagre is *of the second category*. In this
 language the theorem says: **every complete non-empty metric space is of the se
cond category in itself.**

---

### Proof

We argue by contradiction. Suppose, towards a contradiction, that
$$X = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} F_n,$$
where each $F_n$ is closed and $\operatorname{int}(F_n) = \emptyset$.

**Key observation.** Since $\operatorname{int}(F_n) = \emptyset$, the complement
 $U_n := X \setminus F_n$ is open and *dense* in $X$. Indeed, $U_n$ is open beca
use $F_n$ is closed, and it is dense because its closure $\overline{U_n}$ is the
 complement of $\operatorname{int}(F_n)$, which is all of $X$:
$$\overline{U_n} = X \setminus \operatorname{int}(F_n) = X \setminus \emptyset =
 X.$$

So the theorem reduces to showing:

> **Claim.** In a complete metric space, the intersection of countably many dens
e open sets is dense (and in particular, non-empty).

We prove the Claim, which immediately yields the theorem: if every $U_n$ is dens
e and open, then $\bigcap_n U_n$ is dense, hence non-empty (since $X \neq \empty
set$). But
$$\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} U_n = \bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} (X \setminus F_n) = X \set
minus \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} F_n = X \setminus X = \emptyset,$$
contradicting non-emptiness. So it suffices to prove the Claim.

---

**Proof of the Claim (nested-ball construction).**

Let $G_1, G_2, G_3, \dots$ be a sequence of dense open subsets of $X$. We must s
how $G := \bigcap_n G_n$ is dense. It is enough to show that $G$ meets every non
-empty open ball, so let $B_0 = B(x_0, r_0)$ be any non-empty open ball in $X$;
we will produce a point of $G$ lying in $B_0$.

We construct inductively a sequence of open balls
$$B_n = B(x_n, r_n), \qquad 0 < r_n \le \tfrac{1}{n} \quad (n \ge 1),$$
with the properties:
1. $\overline{B_n} \subseteq B_{n-1}$, and
2. $\overline{B_n} \subseteq G_n$.

**Base step ($n=1$).** Since $G_1$ is dense and open, $G_1 \cap B_0$ is a non-em
pty open set. Pick $x_1 \in G_1 \cap B_0$. Since $G_1 \cap B_0$ is open, there e
xists $r_1 > 0$ with $r_1 \le 1$ small enough that
$$\overline{B(x_1, r_1)} \subseteq G_1 \cap B_0 \subseteq B_0.$$
(Choose $r_1 < \min\{1,\, \operatorname{dist}(x_1, X \setminus (G_1 \cap B_0))\}
$; this distance is positive because $x_1$ lies in the open set $G_1 \cap B_0$.)
 Then $\overline{B_1} \subseteq B_0$ and $\overline{B_1} \subseteq G_1$.

**Inductive step.** Suppose $B_{n-1} = B(x_{n-1}, r_{n-1})$ has been constructed
. Since $G_n$ is dense and open, $G_n \cap B_{n-1}$ is non-empty and open. Pick
$x_n \in G_n \cap B_{n-1}$ and choose $r_n > 0$ with $r_n \le \tfrac{1}{n}$ smal
l enough that
$$\overline{B(x_n, r_n)} \subseteq G_n \cap B_{n-1}.$$
Then $\overline{B_n} \subseteq B_{n-1}$ and $\overline{B_n} \subseteq G_n$, comp
leting the induction.

**Extracting the limit.** The closed balls $\overline{B_n} = \overline{B(x_n, r_
n)}$ form a *nested* sequence:
$$\overline{B_1} \supseteq \overline{B_2} \supseteq \overline{B_3} \supseteq \cd
ots,$$
and their diameters satisfy
$$\operatorname{diam}(\overline{B_n}) \le 2 r_n \le \frac{2}{n} \to 0.$$

Since $(X, d)$ is **complete**, Cantor's intersection theorem applies: a nested
sequence of non-empty closed sets in a complete metric space whose diameters ten
d to zero has exactly one point in its intersection. Thus there exists a unique
$$p \in \bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} \overline{B_n}.$$

**Verifying $p \in G \cap B_0$.** For each $n \ge 1$, we have $p \in \overline{B
_n} \subseteq G_n$, so $p \in G_n$ for all $n$, i.e. $p \in \bigcap_n G_n = G$.
Also $p \in \overline{B_1} \subseteq B_0$. Therefore $p \in G \cap B_0$, proving
 $G$ meets the arbitrary ball $B_0$. Hence $G$ is dense. $\blacksquare$

### Consequences and explanation

1. **Reformulation as "second category".** A meagre set is a countable union of
nowhere-dense sets (closed sets with empty interior are the prototype). The theo
rem says a complete non-empty metric space is *not* meagre in itself — it is "la
rge" in the sense of Baire category.

2. **Generic existence.** Since $\bigcap_n U_n$ is not merely non-empty but *den
se*, "most" points of $X$ (in the topological/Baire sense) lie outside every $F_
n$. This is why the theorem underwrites generic-existence arguments: a property
holding on a countable intersection of dense open sets holds on a *residual* (co
meagre) set.

3. **Typical applications.**
   - *Irrationals are not meagre-representable from rationals:* $\mathbb{R}$ is
complete, and $\mathbb{Q}$ is a countable union of singleton closed sets (each w
ith empty interior), so $\mathbb{Q}$ is meagre; consequently $\mathbb{R} \setmin
us \mathbb{Q}$ is non-empty (indeed residual).
   - *Continuous nowhere-differentiable functions exist:* in $C([0,1])$ with the
 sup norm (complete), the set of functions differentiable at some point is a cou
ntable union of closed nowhere-dense sets, hence meagre; its complement is resid
ual and non-empty.
   - *Uniform boundedness principle (Banach–Steinhaus):* the Baire theorem appli
ed to a complete Banach space yields the uniform boundedness of a pointwise-boun
ded family of operators.

4. **Role of completeness.** Completeness is essential: $\mathbb{Q}$ with the us
ual metric is *not* complete, and $\mathbb{Q} = \bigcup_{q \in \mathbb{Q}} \{q\}
$ is a countable union of closed sets with empty interior covering the whole spa
ce. The Cantor intersection step is exactly where completeness is invoked; witho
ut it the nested balls may "converge" to a point outside the space.

5. **Why "non-empty".** The empty space is vacuously complete, and $\emptyset =
\bigcup_n \emptyset$ is a trivial countable union of closed empty-interior sets
covering it. The non-emptiness hypothesis excludes this degenerate case, ensurin
g the dense intersection produced by the Claim is genuinely non-empty.
