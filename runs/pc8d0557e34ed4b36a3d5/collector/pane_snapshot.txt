# Proof: Probability of Randomly Selecting a Transcendental Number

## Problem

What is the probability of randomly selecting digits to form a decimal represent
ation of a transcendental number?

## Setup

"Randomly selecting digits to form a decimal representation" means we generate a
n infinite sequence of digits $d_1, d_2, d_3, \ldots$, each chosen independently
 and uniformly from $\{0, 1, 2, \ldots, 9\}$, forming the real number

$$x = 0.d_1 d_2 d_3 \cdots \in [0, 1].$$

This process induces the **Lebesgue measure** (uniform probability) on $[0,1]$.
(The only ambiguity arises from numbers with dual decimal representations, e.g.,
 $0.1000\ldots = 0.0999\ldots$; these are exactly the rationals of the form $k/1
0^n$, a countable — hence measure-zero — set, so they do not affect the probabil
ity.)

## Key Facts

**Fact 1: The set of algebraic numbers is countable.**

An algebraic number is a real (or complex) number that is a root of a non-zero p
olynomial with integer coefficients. The set of all polynomials with integer coe
fficients is countable (each polynomial has finitely many coefficients, each fro
m $\mathbb{Z}$, and a countable union of countable sets is countable). Each poly
nomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ roots. A countable union of finite sets is
countable. Therefore, the set $\mathbb{A}$ of algebraic numbers is **countable**
.

**Fact 2: A countable subset of $\mathbb{R}$ has Lebesgue measure zero.**

Let $S = \{s_1, s_2, s_3, \ldots\}$ be a countable subset of $\mathbb{R}$. For a
ny $\varepsilon > 0$, cover each $s_i$ with an open interval of length $\varepsi
lon / 2^i$. The total length of the cover is:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \frac{\varepsilon}{2^i} = \varepsilon.$$

Since $\varepsilon > 0$ is arbitrary, the outer measure of $S$ is $0$. Hence $S$
 has Lebesgue measure zero.

**Fact 3: Transcendental numbers exist and form the complement of $\mathbb{A}$ i
n $\mathbb{R}$.**

A transcendental number is, by definition, a real number that is **not** algebra
ic. The set of transcendental numbers is $T = \mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{A}$.
Since $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable and $\mathbb{A}$ is countable, $T$ is uncounta
ble (indeed, of the same cardinality as $\mathbb{R}$).

## Main Argument

Consider the probability space $([0,1], \mathcal{B}, \lambda)$ where $\lambda$ i
s the Lebesgue measure, which is exactly the measure induced by independent unif
orm digit selection.

- The set of algebraic numbers in $[0,1]$ is $\mathbb{A} \cap [0,1]$. Since $\ma
thbb{A}$ is countable (Fact 1), $\mathbb{A} \cap [0,1]$ is countable, and thus h
as Lebesgue measure zero (Fact 2):

$$\lambda(\mathbb{A} \cap [0,1]) = 0.$$

- The set of transcendental numbers in $[0,1]$ is $T \cap [0,1] = [0,1] \setminu
s (\mathbb{A} \cap [0,1])$. By the additivity of Lebesgue measure:

$$\lambda(T \cap [0,1]) = \lambda([0,1]) - \lambda(\mathbb{A} \cap [0,1]) = 1 -
0 = 1.$$

## Conclusion

The probability of randomly selecting digits to form a decimal representation of
 a transcendental number is:

$$\boxed{1}.$$

In other words, a number chosen "at random" (by independently selecting each dec
imal digit uniformly) is transcendental **with probability 1** — it is *almost s
urely* transcendental. The algebraic numbers, despite including all rationals an
d familiar irrationals like $\sqrt{2}$, are so sparse (countable, measure zero)
that the chance of landing on one by random digit selection is exactly $0$.
