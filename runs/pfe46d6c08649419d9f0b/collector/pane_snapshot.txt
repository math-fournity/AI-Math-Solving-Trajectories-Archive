The problem asks for a characterization of irreducible $P(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ su
ch that $P(n)$ is prime for infinitely many $n\in\mathbb{N}$. I must be honest:
**the full characterization is an open problem** (the Bunyakovsky conjecture). W
hat *can* be proved completely is the set of **necessary** conditions; their **s

## Necessary conditions

Let $P\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ be irreducible, $\deg P=d\geq 1$, and suppose $P(n)$ is
prime for infinitely many $n\in\mathbb{N}$.

### (N1) Positive leading coefficient

Write $P(x)=a_{d}x^{d}+\dots+a_{0}$ with $a_{d}\neq 0$. If $a_{d}<0$, then $P(n)
\to-\infty$ as $n\to\infty$, so there exists $N$ with $P(n)<0$ for all $n\geq N$
. Negative integers are not prime, so $P(n)$ is prime for at most finitely many
$n$. Contradiction. Hence

$$\boxed{a_{d}>0.}$$

(If $a_{d}>0$, then $P(n)\to+\infty$, so $P(n)$ is eventually positive and large
 — a prerequisite for primality.)

### (N2) No fixed prime divisor (the values are collectively coprime)

Define $g:=\gcd\{P(n):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$. We claim $g=1$ (e
quivalently, no prime $p$ divides $P(n)$ for every $n$).

Suppose for contradiction that some prime $p$ divides $P(n)$ for all $n\in\mathb
b{Z}$. Then for every $n$, $P(n)\equiv 0\pmod p$. For $P(n)$ to be prime we must
 have $P(n)=p$ (the only positive prime divisible by $p$). But $P(x)-p$ is a non
zero polynomial of degree $d\geq 1$ (nonzero because $P$ is nonconstant), so it
has at most $d$ integer roots. Thus $P(n)=p$ for at most $d$ values of $n$. For
every other $n$ with $P(n)>p$ — and there are infinitely many since $P(n)\to+\in
fty$ — we have $p\mid P(n)$ and $P(n)>p$, so $P(n)$ is composite. Hence only fin
itely many prime values. Contradiction.

Therefore

$$\boxed{\gcd\{P(n):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}=1,}$$

i.e. **there is no prime $p$ dividing $P(n)$ for all $n$**.

*Remark on computability of $g$.* One has $g=\gcd(P(0),P(1),\dots,P(d))$: indeed
 for any $n$, write $n=\sum_{i=0}^{d}c_{i}\binom{n}{i}$ (integer $c_{i}$); using
 finite differences, $P(n)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}(\Delta^{i}P)(0)\binom{n}{i}$, so $P(n)
$ is an integer linear combination of $P(0),\dots,P(d)$. Thus $g$ is effectively
 computable from finitely many values.

### (N3) Irreducibility (given, but let us note its role)

If $P=QR$ with nonconstant $Q,R\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$, then for large $n$ both $|Q(n)
|,|R(n)|>1$, so $P(n)=Q(n)R(n)$ is composite. The problem *assumes* irreducibili
ty, so this is built in. (Strictly, reducibility over $\mathbb{Z}$ is also an ob
struction.)

## The conjectured characterization (Bunyakovsky, 1857)

**Bunyakovsky Conjecture.** *Let $P\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ be irreducible with positiv
e leading coefficient and $\gcd\{P(n):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}=1$. Then $P(n)$ is prime
for infinitely many $n\in\mathbb{N}$.*

This is **open**. The three conditions above are exactly the conjectured necessa
ry-and-sufficient conditions:

> **Conjectured answer.** $P(n)$ is prime for infinitely many $n\in\mathbb{N}$ $
\iff$ $P$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}$, has positive leading coefficient, an
d has no fixed prime divisor ($\gcd\{P(n):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}=1$).

### Why sufficiency is hard / current state

- For $d=1$, $P(x)=ax+b$ with $\gcd(a,b)=1$: this is **Dirichlet's theorem on pr
imes in arithmetic progressions** — proved. So the conjecture is a theorem in de
gree $1$.
- For $d\geq 2$: **completely open**. Even $P(x)=x^{2}+1$ (irreducible, positive
 leading coefficient, $\gcd\{n^{2}+1:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}=1$ since $P(0)=1$) is not
known to take infinitely many prime values. This is Landau's fourth problem (191
2).
- The Bateman–Horn conjecture gives the predicted asymptotic density:
$$\#\{n\leq N:P(n)\text{ prime}\}\sim C_{P}\cdot\frac{N}{\log N},\qquad C_{P}=\f
rac{1}{d}\prod_{p\text{ prime}}\frac{1-\nu_{p}(P)/p}{1-1/p}>0,$$
where $\nu_{p}(P)=\#\{a\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}:P(a)\equiv 0\pmod p\}$. The condition
$\gcd\{P(n)\}=1$ is equivalent to $\nu_{p}(P)<p$ for every $p$, which makes each
 local factor positive and hence $C_{P}>0$.

### Equivalence of "no fixed prime divisor" with the local condition

The condition $\gcd\{P(n):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}=1$ is equivalent to: for every prime
$p$, $P$ does **not** vanish identically as a function $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\t
o\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, i.e. $\nu_{p}(P)<p$. (If $p\mid P(n)\,\forall n$ then
$\nu_{p}(P)=p$; conversely $\nu_{p}(P)=p$ means $p\mid P(n)$ for all $n$.) This
is the "admissibility" condition appearing in Bateman–Horn.

## Summary

**Proved (necessary):** If $P\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is irreducible and $P(n)$ is prim
e for infinitely many $n\in\mathbb{N}$, then
1. $P$ has positive leading coefficient, and
2. $\gcd\{P(n):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}=1$ (no fixed prime divisor).

**Proved (sufficient, degree $1$ only):** For linear $P(x)=ax+b$ with $a>0$ and
$\gcd(a,b)=1$, Dirichlet's theorem gives infinitely many primes — exactly the Bu
nyakovsky conditions, and they are also necessary.

**Open (degree $\geq 2$):** Whether the two conditions above (plus irreducibilit
y) are *sufficient* is the **Bunyakovsky conjecture**, unsolved since 1857, open
 even for $x^{2}+1$.

Hence a complete, proved characterization is **not known**. The conjectured comp
lete answer is:

> $P(n)$ is prime for infinitely many $n\in\mathbb{N}$ **iff** $P$ is irreducibl
e over $\mathbb{Z}$, has positive leading coefficient, and satisfies $\gcd\{P(n)
:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}=1$.

I cannot honestly claim to prove the sufficiency, as it would resolve a major op
en problem. The rigorous, complete *provable* content is the necessity proof abo
ve together with the degree-$1$ sufficiency (Dirichlet).
