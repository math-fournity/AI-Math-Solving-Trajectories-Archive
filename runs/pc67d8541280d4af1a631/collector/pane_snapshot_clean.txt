The problem asks whether there exists a linear transformation on a complex vecto
r space with no eigenvectors at all, even allowing infinite-dimensional spaces.

**Answer: Yes, such a linear transformation exists.**

**Construction.** Let $V$ be the vector space of all complex sequences $v = (x_1
, x_2, x_3, \ldots)$ with $x_i \in \mathbb{C}$, under componentwise addition and
 scalar multiplication. This is an infinite-dimensional complex vector space. De
fine the **right shift operator** $T: V \to V$ by

$$T(x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots) = (0, x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots).$$

It is immediate that $T$ is linear: $T(v+w) = Tv + Tw$ and $T(cv) = c\,Tv$ follo
w directly from the componentwise definition.

**Claim.** $T$ has no eigenvectors.

**Proof of claim.** Suppose, for contradiction, that $v = (x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots
) \neq 0$ is an eigenvector of $T$ with eigenvalue $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$. The
n $Tv = \lambda v$, i.e.,

$$(0, x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots) = (\lambda x_1, \lambda x_2, \lambda x_3, \lambda x
_4, \ldots).$$

Comparing components yields the infinite system:

$$0 = \lambda x_1, \quad x_1 = \lambda x_2, \quad x_2 = \lambda x_3, \quad x_3 =
 \lambda x_4, \quad \ldots$$

**Case 1: $\lambda \neq 0$.** The first equation $0 = \lambda x_1$ forces $x_1 =
 0$. The second equation gives $x_2 = x_1 / \lambda = 0$. The third gives $x_3 =
 x_2 / \lambda = 0$. Proceeding inductively, $x_n = 0$ for every $n \geq 1$. Hen
ce $v = 0$, contradicting that $v$ is an eigenvector.

**Case 2: $\lambda = 0$.** The equation $Tv = 0$ becomes $(0, x_1, x_2, \ldots)
= (0, 0, 0, \ldots)$, so $x_1 = x_2 = x_3 = \cdots = 0$. Again $v = 0$, a contra
diction.

In both cases we reach a contradiction, so no nonzero $v$ satisfies $Tv = \lambd
a v$ for any $\lambda$. Therefore $T$ has no eigenvectors. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** This contrasts sharply with the finite-dimensional case: on a finite
-dimensional complex vector space, every linear transformation has at least one
eigenvector, because the characteristic polynomial factors completely over $\mat
hbb{C}$ (fundamental theorem of algebra), guaranteeing an eigenvalue and hence a
n eigenvector. The example above shows this finite-dimensional result does not e
xtend to infinite dimensions — the right shift operator on the space of all comp
lex sequences is a concrete linear transformation with **no** eigenvectors whats
oever.
