**Answer: Yes, such a function exists.**

## Proof

We construct an explicit example: the standard bump function.

**Construction.** Define $x: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ by
$$
x(t) = \begin{cases} e^{-1/(1-t^2)} & \text{if } |t| < 1, \\ 0 & \text{if } |t|
\ge 1. \end{cases}
$$

Clearly $x$ is nonzero (e.g., $x(0) = e^{-1} \neq 0$). We show $x$ is smooth and
 satisfies the required conditions.

---

**Step 1: $x$ is smooth.**

On the open intervals $(-\infty, -1)$, $(-1, 1)$, and $(1, \infty)$, $x$ is smoo
th (it is either identically zero or a composition of smooth functions). It rema
ins to check smoothness at $t = 1$ and $t = -1$. By symmetry ($x(-t) = x(t)$), i
t suffices to check $t = 1$.

We use the following key lemma.

**Lemma.** For every integer $n \ge 0$,
$$
\lim_{u \to 0^+} \frac{e^{-1/u}}{u^n} = 0.
$$

*Proof of Lemma.* Set $v = 1/u$, so $v \to +\infty$. Then $e^{-1/u}/u^n = v^n e^
{-v}$. Since $e^v = \sum_{j=0}^\infty v^j/j! \ge v^{n+1}/(n+1)!$, we get $v^n e^
{-v} \le (n+1)!/v \to 0$. $\square$

Now set $g(t) = e^{-1/(1-t^2)}$ for $|t|<1$. We claim that for every $k \ge 0$,
the $k$-th derivative of $x$ at $t=1$ (from the left) tends to $0$, which matche
s the right-hand derivative (which is $0$ since $x \equiv 0$ for $t \ge 1$).

For $t \in (-1,1)$, write $u = 1 - t^2$, so $u \to 0^+$ as $t \to 1^-$. The func
tion $g(t) = h(u)$ where $h(u) = e^{-1/u}$. By the chain rule and induction, $g^
{(k)}(t)$ is a finite sum of terms of the form
$$
h^{(j)}(u) \cdot P(u, t)
$$
where $j \le k$ and $P$ is a polynomial in $u$ and $t$. Since $h^{(j)}(u) = R_j(
u)\, e^{-1/u}$ for some rational function $R_j(u)$ (this follows by induction: $
h'(u) = u^{-2} e^{-1/u}$, and each differentiation produces another rational fun
ction times $e^{-1/u}$), each term has the form
$$
R(u)\, e^{-1/u} \cdot P(u,t)
$$
where $R$ is rational and $P$ is polynomial. As $t \to 1^-$, $u \to 0^+$, and $t
$ stays bounded, so $P(u,t)$ stays bounded and $R(u)$ may blow up at most polyno
mially in $1/u$. By the Lemma, $e^{-1/u}$ decays faster than any power of $1/u$,
 so every term $\to 0$.

Therefore $\lim_{t \to 1^-} g^{(k)}(t) = 0$ for every $k \ge 0$.

This means: the left-hand limit of $x^{(k)}$ at $t=1$ is $0$, and the right-hand
 value is $0$. By induction on $k$, this shows $x^{(k)}(1) = 0$ and $x^{(k)}$ is
 continuous at $t=1$ for all $k$. (The base case $k=0$: $x$ is continuous at $1$
 since both sides give $0$. Inductive step: if $x^{(k)}$ is continuous at $1$ wi
th $x^{(k)}(1)=0$, and $\lim_{t\to 1^-} (x^{(k)})'(t) = \lim_{t\to 1^-} g^{(k+1)
}(t) = 0 = \lim_{t\to 1^+} 0$, then $x^{(k)}$ is differentiable at $1$ with $x^{
(k+1)}(1) = 0$, and $x^{(k+1)}$ is continuous.)

The same argument works at $t = -1$ by symmetry. Hence $x \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R
})$.

---

**Step 2: All derivatives are bounded and vanish at infinity.**

Since $x$ is compactly supported (supported on $[-1,1]$), every derivative $D^k
x$ is also compactly supported on $[-1,1]$. Being continuous on the compact set
$[-1,1]$ and zero outside, each $D^k x$ is:

- **Bounded:** $|D^k x(t)| \le \max_{s \in [-1,1]} |D^k x(s)| =: M_k < \infty$ f
or all $t \in \mathbb{R}$.
- **Vanishing at infinity:** For $|t| > 1$, $D^k x(t) = 0$, so $\lim_{s \to \pm\
infty} |D^k x(s)| = 0$ trivially.

---

**Conclusion.** The function $x$ is nonzero, smooth, and for every $k \in \mathb
b{N}_0$, $D^k x$ is bounded on $\mathbb{R}$ and $|D^k x(s)| \to 0$ as $s \to \pm
\infty$. Therefore such a function exists. $\blacksquare$
