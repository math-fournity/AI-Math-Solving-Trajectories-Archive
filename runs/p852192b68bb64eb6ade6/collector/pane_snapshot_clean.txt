## Proof

We seek the smallest integer $n$ for which there exist primes $X_1,\dots,X_n$ wi
th
$$X_1^2+X_2^2+\cdots+X_{n-1}^2=X_n^2,$$
where $X_n$ is the hypotenuse (strictly the largest term, as required by the sta
ndard definition of a Pythagorean $n$-tuple).

### Parity and modular constraints

Let $k$ denote the number of **odd** primes among $X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}$; the remai
ning $n-1-k$ terms equal $2$.

- If $X_n=2$, then $X_n^2=4$, but $X_1^2+\cdots+X_{n-1}^2\ge 4(n-1)\ge 4$, with
equality only when $n=2$ and $X_1=X_2=2$ — a degenerate case where the "hypotenu
se" is not strictly larger. Hence every nontrivial solution has $X_n$ an **odd**
 prime.
- With $X_n$ odd, $X_n^2$ is odd, so the left side must be odd: $k$ is **odd**.
- Reducing mod $4$: every odd prime has square $\equiv 1\pmod 4$, and $2^2\equiv
 0\pmod 4$, so
 $$X_1^2+\cdots+X_{n-1}^2 \equiv k \pmod 4.$$
 Since $X_n^2\equiv 1\pmod 4$, we obtain
 $$k\equiv 1\pmod 4.$$

Thus $k\in\{1,5,9,\dots\}$, and in particular $k\ge 1$.

With exactly $k$ odd primes (each contributing its square) and $n-1-k$ copies of
 $2$ (each contributing $4$), and writing the single distinguished odd prime on
the left as $p$ when $k=1$:
$$X_n^2 = p^2 + 4(n-2)\qquad(\text{when }k=1),$$
i.e. $(X_n-p)(X_n+p)=4(n-2)$, with both factors even (since $X_n,p$ are odd).

### Ruling out $n=3,4,5$

**$n=3$:** $k\le 2$ and $k\equiv 1\pmod 4$ force $k=1$. Then $(X_3-p)(X_3+p)=4$.
 The only even factor pair is $2\times 2$, giving $X_3=2,\ p=0$ — impossible.

**$n=4$:** $k\le 3$ forces $k=1$. Then $(X_4-p)(X_4+p)=8$. The only even factor
pair is $2\times 4$, giving $X_4=3,\ p=1$ — $1$ is not prime. Impossible.

**$n=5$:** $k\le 4$ forces $k=1$. Then $(X_5-p)(X_5+p)=12$. The only even factor
 pair is $2\times 6$, giving $X_5=4,\ p=2$ — but $X_5=4$ is not prime. Impossibl
e.

(The case $n=2$ gives only the degenerate $X_1=X_2=2$, where the hypotenuse is n
ot strictly larger than the leg, so it is not a genuine Pythagorean tuple.)

### A solution for $n=6$

For $n=6$, take $k=1$: one odd prime $p=3$ and four copies of $2$ among the firs
t five terms, and $X_6=5$:
$$3^2+2^2+2^2+2^2+2^2 = 9+4+4+4+4 = 25 = 5^2.$$
All entries $3,2,2,2,2,5$ are prime, and $5$ is strictly the largest. This is a
valid Pythagorean $6$-tuple of primes.

### Conclusion

No genuine Pythagorean $n$-tuple of primes exists for $n\le 5$, and one exists f
or $n=6$. Therefore the smallest such integer is

$$\boxed{n=6}.$$
