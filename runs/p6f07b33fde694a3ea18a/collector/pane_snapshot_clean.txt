## Proof

**Interpretation.** "The unit ball in $L_1([0,1]^2)$, restricted to finite dimen
sions" means: for any finite-dimensional (linear) subspace $V \subseteq L_1([0,1
]^2)$, consider the closed unit ball $B_V = \{f \in V : \|f\|_1 \le 1\}$. We sho
w $B_V$ is compact (in the $L_1$-norm topology).

---

**Theorem.** Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional subspace of $L_1([0,1]^2)$ with $\d
im V = n$. Then $B_V = \{f \in V : \|f\|_1 \le 1\}$ is compact.

**Proof.**

Choose a basis $\{e_1, \dots, e_n\}$ for $V$ and define the linear isomorphism
$$\phi : \mathbb{R}^n \to V, \qquad \phi(a_1,\dots,a_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i\, e_i
.$$

**Step 1: All norms on $V$ are equivalent.** On the finite-dimensional space $V$
, the $L_1$-norm $\|\cdot\|_1$ is a norm. Define a reference norm on $V$ by $\|f
\|_\phi := \|\phi^{-1}(f)\|_2$ (the Euclidean norm of the coordinate vector). Si
nce $V$ is finite-dimensional, all norms on $V$ are equivalent: there exist cons
tants $c, C > 0$ such that
$$c\,\|f\|_\phi \;\le\; \|f\|_1 \;\le\; C\,\|f\|_\phi \qquad \forall\, f \in V.
\tag{$\star$}$$

**Step 2: $\phi^{-1}(B_V)$ is closed in $\mathbb{R}^n$.** The norm $\|\cdot\|_1
: V \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous, so $B_V = \|\cdot\|_1^{-1}([0,1])$ is closed
in $V$. The map $\phi^{-1}$ is continuous (being a linear map between finite-dim
ensional normed spaces), so $\phi^{-1}(B_V)$ is closed in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

**Step 3: $\phi^{-1}(B_V)$ is bounded in $\mathbb{R}^n$.** By the left inequalit
y in $(\star)$, for any $f \in B_V$:
$$\|\phi^{-1}(f)\|_2 = \|f\|_\phi \;\le\; \frac{1}{c}\,\|f\|_1 \;\le\; \frac{1}{
c}.$$
Hence $\phi^{-1}(B_V) \subseteq \{a \in \mathbb{R}^n : \|a\|_2 \le 1/c\}$, which
 is bounded.

**Step 4: Apply Heine–Borel.** $\phi^{-1}(B_V)$ is a closed and bounded subset o
f $\mathbb{R}^n$, hence compact by the Heine–Borel theorem. Since $\phi$ is cont
inuous, $B_V = \phi\bigl(\phi^{-1}(B_V)\bigr)$ is the continuous image of a comp
act set, hence compact in $V$. As $V$ embeds continuously into $L_1([0,1]^2)$, $
B_V$ is compact in $L_1([0,1]^2)$ as well. $\blacksquare$

---

**Remark (context).** This is exactly the content of **Riesz's theorem**: a norm
ed vector space is finite-dimensional *if and only if* its closed unit ball is c
ompact. The full (infinite-dimensional) unit ball of $L_1([0,1]^2)$ is **not** c
ompact — for instance, the sequence $f_n(x,y) = n\,\mathbf{1}_{[0,\,1/n]\times[0
,1]}$ lies in the unit ball ($\|f_n\|_1 = 1$) but has no $L_1$-convergent subseq
uence, since $\|f_n - f_m\|_1 = 2$ for $n \neq m$. Compactness is recovered prec
isely upon restriction to finite-dimensional subspaces, as shown above.
