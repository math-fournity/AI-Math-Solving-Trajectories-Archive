## Arzelà–Ascoli Theorem (compact domain version)

**Theorem.** Let $K$ be a compact metric space (equivalently, a compact subset o
f $\mathbb{R}^n$, or more generally a compact Hausdorff space), and let $C(K)$ d
enote the space of real- (or complex-) valued continuous functions on $K$, equip
ped with the supremum norm $\|f\|_\infty = \sup_{x\in K}|f(x)|$. Let $\mathcal{F
}\subseteq C(K)$.

Then the following are equivalent:

1. **(Precompactness / Relative compactness)** $\mathcal{F}$ is relatively compa
ct in $C(K)$, i.e. its closure $\overline{\mathcal{F}}$ is compact in $C(K)$ (eq
uivalently, every sequence in $\mathcal{F}$ admits a subsequence converging unif
ormly on $K$ to some limit in $C(K)$).

2. **(Pointwise boundedness + equicontinuity)** $\mathcal{F}$ is
   - **pointwise bounded**: for every $x\in K$, $\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|f(x)|<\i
nfty$; and
   - **equicontinuous**: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $\delta>0$ such
that for all $x,y\in K$ with $d(x,y)<\delta$ and for all $f\in\mathcal{F}$, $|f(
x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon$.

In particular, a subset $\mathcal{F}\subseteq C(K)$ is compact if and only if it
 is closed, pointwise bounded, and equicontinuous.

### Remarks on the proof structure

- **$(1)\Rightarrow(2)$: Compactness forces uniform boundedness.** Since $\overl
ine{\mathcal{F}}$ is compact in $C(K)$, it is bounded in norm, giving **uniform*
* boundedness (hence pointwise boundedness). For equicontinuity: compactness of
$\overline{\mathcal{F}}$ gives total boundedness, so for $\varepsilon>0$ there e
xist $f_1,\dots,f_n\in\mathcal{F}$ with every $f\in\mathcal{F}$ within $\varepsi
lon/3$ of some $f_i$. Each $f_i$ is uniformly continuous (continuous on a compac
t set), so there is $\delta_i>0$ with $d(x,y)<\delta_i\Rightarrow |f_i(x)-f_i(y)
|<\varepsilon/3$. Take $\delta=\min_i\delta_i$. Then for any $f\in\mathcal{F}$,
picking $f_i$ with $\|f-f_i\|_\infty<\varepsilon/3$,
$$|f(x)-f(y)|\le |f(x)-f_i(x)|+|f_i(x)-f_i(y)|+|f_i(y)-f(y)|<\varepsilon.$$
So $\mathcal{F}$ is equicontinuous.

- **$(2)\Rightarrow(1)$: The diagonal subsequence argument.** Since $K$ is compa
ct it is separable; let $\{x_1,x_2,\dots\}$ be a countable dense subset. By poin
twise boundedness, $\{f(x_1):f\in\mathcal{F}\}$ is bounded, so a **Cantor diagon
al argument** extracts a subsequence $\{f_{n}\}$ converging pointwise at every $
x_j$. Equicontinuity upgrades this pointwise convergence on a dense set to **uni
form convergence on all of $K$**: for $\varepsilon>0$ pick $\delta$ from equicon
tinuity, cover $K$ by finitely many $\delta$-balls centered at $x_{j_1},\dots,x_
{j_m}$, choose $N$ so that $|f_n(x_{j_k})-f_m(x_{j_k})|<\varepsilon$ for all $k$
 and $n,m\ge N$, and conclude $\|f_n-f_m\|_\infty<3\varepsilon$. Thus $\{f_n\}$
is Cauchy in $C(K)$, hence converges uniformly to a continuous limit. So $\overl
ine{\mathcal{F}}$ is sequentially compact, hence compact.

### Note on the hypotheses

- **Compactness of $K$** is essential: it gives uniform continuity of each $f$ a
nd total boundedness used above.
- **Equicontinuity** (not mere uniform continuity of each member) is the key add
itional ingredient beyond pointwise boundedness; without it, pointwise boundedne
ss alone does not yield a uniformly convergent subsequence.
- For non-compact domains one replaces pointwise boundedness by **uniform bounde
dness** and requires equicontinuity; the conclusion is then local uniform conver
gence on compact subsets.
