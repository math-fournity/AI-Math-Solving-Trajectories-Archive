## Proof

**Yes, it is possible.** We construct an explicit bijection $f:[0,1]\to(0,1)$ th
at is discontinuous at every point of the Cantor set (which is uncountable).

### Setup

Let $C\subset[0,1]$ be the Cantor set, and let $U=[0,1]\setminus C=\bigcup_{n=1}
^{\infty}I_n$ where the $I_n=(a_n,b_n)$ are the disjoint open intervals removed
during the construction of $C$.

Key observations:
- $[0,1]=C\sqcup U$ (disjoint union).
- $(0,1)=(C\setminus\{0,1\})\sqcup U$ (disjoint union), since $U\subset(0,1)$ an
d $C\cap\{0,1\}=\{0,1\}$.
- $C$ and $C\setminus\{0,1\}$ are both uncountable, each of cardinality $\mathfr
ak{c}$.
- $C$ is closed and nowhere dense, so $U$ is open and **dense** in $[0,1]$.

### Constructing a fixed-point-free bijection $\sigma:C\to C\setminus\{0,1\}$

Since $|C\setminus\{0,1\}|=\mathfrak{c}$, partition $C\setminus\{0,1\}$ into two
 disjoint sets $P$ and $Q$ with $|P|=|Q|=\mathfrak{c}$. Pick two distinct points
 $q_1,q_2\in Q$.

- Set $\sigma(0)=q_1$, $\sigma(1)=q_2$.
- Choose a bijection $\beta:P\to Q\setminus\{q_1,q_2\}$ (both have cardinality $
\mathfrak{c}$).
- Choose a bijection $\gamma:Q\to P$ (both have cardinality $\mathfrak{c}$).
- For $x\in P$: $\sigma(x)=\beta(x)$; for $x\in Q$: $\sigma(x)=\gamma(x)$.

Then $\sigma:C\to C\setminus\{0,1\}$ is a bijection. It is **fixed-point-free**:
- $\sigma(0)=q_1\notin\{0,1\}$, so $\sigma(0)\neq 0$; likewise $\sigma(1)\neq 1$
.
- For $x\in P$: $\sigma(x)\in Q$, and $P\cap Q=\emptyset$, so $\sigma(x)\neq x$.
- For $x\in Q$: $\sigma(x)\in P$, and $P\cap Q=\emptyset$, so $\sigma(x)\neq x$.

### Definition of $f$

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}x & \text{if }x\in U,\\ \sigma(x) & \text{if }x\in C.\end{ca
ses}$$

### $f$ is a bijection $[0,1]\to(0,1)$

- $f|_U=\mathrm{id}_U$ maps $U$ bijectively onto $U\subset(0,1)$.
- $f|_C=\sigma$ maps $C$ bijectively onto $C\setminus\{0,1\}\subset(0,1)$.
- Since $[0,1]=C\sqcup U$ and $(0,1)=(C\setminus\{0,1\})\sqcup U$, the images ar
e disjoint and cover $(0,1)$. Hence $f$ is a bijection.

### $f$ is continuous on $U$

Every $y\in U$ lies in some open interval $I_n\subset U$. On $I_n$, $f=\mathrm{i
d}$, which is continuous. Since $I_n$ is open, $f$ is continuous at $y$.

### $f$ is discontinuous at every point of $C$ (uncountably many)

Let $x\in C$. Since $C$ is nowhere dense, $U$ is dense in $[0,1]$, so there exis
ts a sequence $(y_k)\subset U$ with $y_k\to x$. Then:
$$f(y_k)=y_k\longrightarrow x.$$
But $f(x)=\sigma(x)\neq x$ (since $\sigma$ is fixed-point-free). Therefore $f(y_
k)\to x\neq f(x)$, proving $f$ is discontinuous at $x$.

Since this holds for every $x\in C$, and $C$ is uncountable, $f$ is discontinuou
s at uncountably many points.

### Conclusion

The map $f:[0,1]\to(0,1)$ is a bijection that is discontinuous at every point of
 the Cantor set $C$, an uncountable set. Since any closed interval $[a,b]$ ($a<b
$) is homeomorphic to $[0,1]$ and any open interval $(c,d)$ ($c<d$) is homeomorp
hic to $(0,1)$, the result holds for arbitrary closed and open intervals. $\blac
ksquare$
