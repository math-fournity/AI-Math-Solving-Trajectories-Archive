Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether a self-adjoint operator on
a separable Hilbert space with only point spectrum must have an orthonormal basi
s of eigenvectors.

**Answer: Yes.**

**Proof.**

Let $T$ be self-adjoint on a separable Hilbert space $H$ with $\sigma(T) = \sigm
a_p(T)$ (only point spectrum; recall that for self-adjoint operators the residua
l spectrum is always empty, so "only point spectrum" means the continuous spectr
um is also empty).

**Step 1: The set of eigenvalues is countable.**

For a self-adjoint operator, eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues
are orthogonal. In a separable Hilbert space, every orthonormal set is at most c
ountable. Choosing a unit eigenvector for each distinct eigenvalue yields a coun
table orthonormal set, so $\sigma_p(T)$ is at most countable.

**Step 2: The closed span of eigenvectors reduces $T$.**

Let $M = \overline{\operatorname{span}}\{v \in H : Tv = \lambda v \text{ for som
e } \lambda \in \mathbb{R}\}$. Since eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues are mu
tually orthogonal and each eigenspace is $T$-invariant (and $T^*$-invariant, as
$T = T^*$), $M$ is a closed reducing subspace. Thus $H = M \oplus M^\perp$ and $
T = T|_M \oplus S$ where $S := T|_{M^\perp}$ is self-adjoint on $M^\perp$.

**Step 3: $S$ has no eigenvalues.**

If $v \in M^\perp$ satisfies $Sv = \lambda v$, then $Tv = \lambda v$, so $v$ is
an eigenvector of $T$ lying in $M$. Hence $v \in M \cap M^\perp = \{0\}$. So $\s
igma_p(S) = \emptyset$.

**Step 4: Isolated spectral points of a self-adjoint operator are eigenvalues.**

Let $\lambda$ be an isolated point of $\sigma(S)$. Then there exists $\epsilon >
 0$ with $(\lambda-\epsilon, \lambda+\epsilon) \cap \sigma(S) = \{\lambda\}$. Le
t $E$ be the spectral measure of $S$. Since $\lambda \in \sigma(S) = \operatorna
me{supp}(E)$, we have $E((\lambda-\epsilon, \lambda+\epsilon)) \neq 0$. But the
interval minus $\{\lambda\}$ lies in the resolvent set, so $E((\lambda-\epsilon,
\lambda+\epsilon)) = E(\{\lambda\})$. Hence $E(\{\lambda\}) \neq 0$. Since $S\,E
(\{\lambda\}) = \lambda\,E(\{\lambda\})$, the range $\operatorname{ran}\,E(\{\la
mbda\}) = \ker(S - \lambda I)$ is nonzero, so $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $S$.

**Step 5: Contradiction if $M^\perp \neq \{0\}$.**

Suppose $M^\perp \neq \{0\}$. Then $S$ is a self-adjoint operator on a nonzero H
ilbert space, so $\sigma(S) \neq \emptyset$. By Step 4, every isolated point of
$\sigma(S)$ would be an eigenvalue, contradicting Step 3. Therefore $\sigma(S)$
has no isolated points, meaning $\sigma(S)$ is a nonempty perfect subset of $\ma
thbb{R}$, which is uncountable.

Since $M$ reduces $T$, we have $\sigma(T) = \sigma(T|_M) \cup \sigma(S)$, so $\s
igma(S) \subseteq \sigma(T) = \sigma_p(T)$. But $\sigma_p(T)$ is countable (Step
 1) while $\sigma(S)$ is uncountable — contradiction.

**Step 6: Conclusion.**

Therefore $M^\perp = \{0\}$, i.e., $M = H$: the eigenvectors of $T$ span $H$. Si
nce eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal and each eigenspace (be
ing a subspace of a separable space) is separable, we can select a countable ort
honormal basis within each eigenspace. The union over all eigenvalues forms an o
rthonormal basis of $H$ consisting entirely of eigenvectors of $T$. $\blacksquar
e$
