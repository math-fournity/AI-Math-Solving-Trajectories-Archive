**Answer: No.** An integral domain can have a factorization basis without being
a UFD.

## Proof

**Definition.** An integral domain $R$ has a *factorization basis* if every nonz
ero nonunit element of $R$ can be written as a finite product of irreducible ele
ments (atoms). This is the *atomic* property.

**Claim.** There exists an atomic integral domain that is not a UFD.

**Counterexample.** Let $R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}] = \{a + b\sqrt{-5} : a, b \in
 \mathbb{Z}\}$, a subring of $\mathbb{C}$, hence an integral domain.

### Step 1: $R$ has a factorization basis (is atomic)

$R$ is a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module of rank 2 (basis $\{1, \sqrt{-5}\}$). Since $\
mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian and $R$ is finitely generated as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module,
 $R$ is a Noetherian $\mathbb{Z}$-module. Every ideal of $R$ is in particular a
$\mathbb{Z}$-submodule, so $R$ satisfies ACC on all ideals, hence ACC on princip
al ideals.

**Lemma (Noetherian $\Rightarrow$ atomic).** In a domain satisfying ACC on princ
ipal ideals, every nonzero nonunit factors into irreducibles.

*Proof of lemma.* Suppose not. Then there exists a nonzero nonunit $a_0$ that ca
nnot be so factored. Then $a_0$ is not irreducible, so $a_0 = a_1 c_1$ with both
 nonunits. At least one of $a_1, c_1$ cannot be factored into irreducibles; call
 it $a_1$. Then $(a_0) \subsetneq (a_1)$: indeed $a_0 \in (a_1)$, and if $(a_0)
= (a_1)$ then $a_1 = a_0 u$ for a unit $u$, giving $a_0 = a_0 u c_1$, so $uc_1 =
 1$, contradicting $c_1$ being a nonunit. Repeating yields a strictly ascending
chain $(a_0) \subsetneq (a_1) \subsetneq (a_2) \subsetneq \cdots$, contradicting
 ACC. $\square$

By the lemma, $R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is atomic, i.e., has a factorization b
asis.

### Step 2: $R$ is not a UFD

Consider the norm $N(a + b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5b^2 \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$, whic
h is multiplicative: $N(\alpha\beta) = N(\alpha)N(\beta)$. An element $\alpha$ i
s a unit iff $N(\alpha) = 1$ (i.e., $\alpha = \pm 1$).

We exhibit two distinct factorizations of $6$ into irreducibles:
$$6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5}).$$

**$2$ is irreducible:** $N(2) = 4$. If $2 = \alpha\beta$ with nonunits, then $N(
\alpha) = N(\beta) = 2$, requiring $a^2 + 5b^2 = 2$ for integers $a, b$ — imposs
ible. So $2$ is irreducible.

**$3$ is irreducible:** $N(3) = 9$. A nontrivial factorization requires $N(\alph
a) = 3$, i.e., $a^2 + 5b^2 = 3$ — impossible. So $3$ is irreducible.

**$1+\sqrt{-5}$ is irreducible:** $N(1+\sqrt{-5}) = 6$. A nontrivial factorizati
on requires $\{N(\alpha), N(\beta)\} = \{2, 3\}$, but neither $a^2+5b^2=2$ nor $
a^2+5b^2=3$ has integer solutions. So $1+\sqrt{-5}$ is irreducible. By the same
argument (or conjugation), $1-\sqrt{-5}$ is irreducible.

**The factorizations are distinct:** The factors $2, 3$ have norms $4, 9$, while
 $1\pm\sqrt{-5}$ have norm $6$. Since associates have equal norms, $2$ and $3$ a
re not associates of $1\pm\sqrt{-5}$. Thus $6$ has two essentially different fac
torizations into irreducibles.

### Conclusion

$R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is an integral domain with a factorization basis (it
 is atomic, being Noetherian) but is not a UFD (the element $6$ has two distinct
 irreducible factorizations). Therefore, having a factorization basis does **not
** necessarily imply being a UFD. $\blacksquare$
