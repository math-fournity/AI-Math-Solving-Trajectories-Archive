## Proof

Define $f_n(x) = \left(1+\dfrac{x}{n}\right)^{-n}\sin\dfrac{x}{n}$ on $[0,\infty
)$.

**Pointwise limit.** For each fixed $x\ge 0$,
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{x}{n}\right)^{-n}=e^{-x},\qquad \lim_{n\to\inft
y}\sin\frac{x}{n}=0,$$
so $f_n(x)\to e^{-x}\cdot 0 = 0$ pointwise on $[0,\infty)$.

**Integrable dominating function.** We use the classical fact that $\left(1+\dfr
ac{x}{n}\right)^{n}$ is increasing in $n$ for $x\ge 0$ (it increases to $e^x$).
Hence $\left(1+\dfrac{x}{n}\right)^{-n}$ is *decreasing* in $n$. Therefore, for
every $n\ge 2$ and every $x\ge 0$,
$$|f_n(x)|\le \left(1+\frac{x}{n}\right)^{-n}\le \left(1+\frac{x}{2}\right)^{-2}
=:g(x).$$
The function $g(x)=\left(1+\dfrac{x}{2}\right)^{-2}$ is integrable on $[0,\infty
)$:
$$\int_0^\infty \left(1+\frac{x}{2}\right)^{-2}dx = 2\int_1^\infty u^{-2}\,du =
2.$$

**Applying DCT.** The sequence $\{f_n\}_{n\ge 2}$ satisfies:
- $f_n(x)\to 0$ pointwise,
- $|f_n(x)|\le g(x)$ with $g\in L^1([0,\infty))$.

By the Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem,
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^\infty f_n(x)\,dx = \int_0^\infty \lim_{n\to\infty}f_n
(x)\,dx = \int_0^\infty 0\,dx = 0.$$

(The single term $n=1$ does not affect the limit, so restricting to $n\ge 2$ is
harmless.)

**Remark on the naive bound.** A direct bound $|f_n|\le (1+x/n)^{-n}\le (1+x)^{-
1}$ (using $n\ge 1$) fails because $(1+x)^{-1}\notin L^1([0,\infty))$. The key i
s that since $(1+x/n)^{-n}$ is *decreasing* in $n$, we may dominate by the $n=2$
 term $\left(1+\dfrac{x}{2}\right)^{-2}$, which decays like $x^{-2}$ and is inte
grable. This is what makes DCT applicable.

**Conclusion.** The limit is
$$\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{x}{n}\right)^{-n}\sin\fra
c{x}{n}\,dx = 0.}$$
Yes, the evaluation that the limit is $0$ is correct.
