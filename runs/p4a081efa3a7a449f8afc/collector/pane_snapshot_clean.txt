The implication is **false** in general. Interchanging the limit $x\to\infty$ wi
th the summation $\sum_n$ requires additional hypotheses (such as uniform conver
gence of the series in $x$, or a dominating summable sequence). Pointwise conver
gence of each term is not enough. Here is a counterexample.

---

**Counterexample (a "moving bump").**

For $x \ge 1$, define each real-valued term by

$$
s_n(x) \;=\; \begin{cases}
1, & n = \lfloor x \rfloor,\\[2pt]
0, & n \neq \lfloor x \rfloor.
\end{cases}
$$

**Step 1. Each term has a finite limit as $x\to\infty$.**
Fix $n\in\mathbb{N}$. For all $x \ge n+1$ we have $\lfloor x\rfloor \ge n+1 > n$
, so $s_n(x)=0$. Hence

$$
\lim_{x\to\infty} s_n(x) \;=\; 0 \;=:\; s_n .
$$

**Step 2. The limiting series converges.**
Since every $s_n=0$,

$$
S \;=\; \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} s_n \;=\; 0,
$$

which is (trivially) convergent.

**Step 3. The series $S(x)$ converges for every $x\ge 1$.**
For each fixed $x\ge 1$, exactly one index — namely $n=\lfloor x\rfloor\ge 1$ —
satisfies $s_n(x)=1$; all others are $0$. Thus the series has only one nonzero t
erm and

$$
S(x) \;=\; \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} s_n(x) \;=\; 1 \qquad\text{for every }x\ge 1.
$$

**Step 4. The limit of $S(x)$ is not $S$.**
Since $S(x)\equiv 1$,

$$
\lim_{x\to\infty} S(x) \;=\; 1 \;\neq\; 0 \;=\; S .
$$

---

**Conclusion.** All the hypotheses are satisfied — each $s_n(x)\to s_n$ as $x\to
\infty$, and $\sum_n s_n$ is convergent — yet $S(x)\not\to S$. The mass of the s
eries "escapes to infinity": for each fixed $n$ the contribution eventually vani
shes, but at every $x$ there is always *some* (ever-larger) index carrying the v
alue $1$, so the total never tends to $0$.

Therefore the answer is **no**: pointwise convergence of the terms together with
 convergence of $\sum s_n$ does **not** imply $S(x)\to S$. A sufficient addition
al condition would be, for instance, dominated convergence in the form $\sum_n \
sup_{x\ge X}|s_n(x)-s_n|\to 0$ as $X\to\infty$ (i.e. uniform convergence of the
series tail), which rules out the moving-bump phenomenon above.
