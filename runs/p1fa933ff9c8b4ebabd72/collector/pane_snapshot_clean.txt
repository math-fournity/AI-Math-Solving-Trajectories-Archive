# Proof

**The statement is TRUE.**

## Setup and a key observation

Let $X$ be a compact Hausdorff space that is an infinite set. We consider the sp
ace $C(X)$ of continuous real-valued functions on $X$ equipped with two topologi
es:

- **Pointwise convergence topology** $\tau_p$: the coarsest topology making each
 evaluation map $f \mapsto f(x)$ continuous.
- **Compact convergence topology** $\tau_c$: the topology of uniform convergence
 on compact subsets of $X$.

Since $X$ is compact, $\tau_c$ coincides with the topology of uniform convergenc
e on all of $X$, i.e., the topology induced by the sup-norm $\|f\|_\infty = \sup
_{x \in X}|f(x)|$.

**Observation (the hypothesis is automatic).** An infinite discrete space is nev
er compact: the open cover $\{\{x\} : x \in X\}$ admits no finite subcover. Henc
e every infinite compact Hausdorff space is automatically non-discrete, i.e., it
s topology is strictly weaker than the discrete topology. Thus the condition "st
rictly weaker than discrete" is redundant, and it suffices to prove:

> For every infinite compact Hausdorff space $X$, the compact convergence topolo
gy on $C(X)$ is strictly finer than the pointwise convergence topology.

## $\tau_c \supseteq \tau_p$

Every finite subset $F \subseteq X$ is compact. Pointwise convergence is exactly
 uniform convergence on finite sets, while compact convergence is uniform conver
gence on *all* compact sets. Since finite sets are compact, $\tau_c$ is finer th
an $\tau_p$.

## $\tau_c \neq \tau_p$ (strictness)

Consider the open unit ball in the uniform topology:
$$U = \{f \in C(X) : \|f\|_\infty < 1\} = \{f \in C(X) : |f(x)| < 1 \text{ for a
ll } x \in X\}.$$
This is $\tau_c$-open (it is the open ball of radius $1$ around $0$ in the sup-n
orm).

**Claim:** $U$ is not $\tau_p$-open.

Suppose for contradiction that $U$ were $\tau_p$-open. Then, since $0 \in U$, th
ere would exist a basic $\tau_p$-neighborhood of $0$ contained in $U$:
$$V = \{f \in C(X) : |f(x_i)| < \varepsilon_i,\ i = 1, \dots, n\} \subseteq U,$$
for some finite set of points $x_1, \dots, x_n \in X$ and $\varepsilon_1, \dots,
 \varepsilon_n > 0$.

Since $X$ is infinite, there exists a point $y \in X \setminus \{x_1, \dots, x_n
\}$. The set $F = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ is closed (finite subsets of a Hausdorff
space are closed), and $\{y\}$ is closed, with $F \cap \{y\} = \emptyset$. Since
 $X$ is compact Hausdorff, it is **normal**, so by **Urysohn's lemma** there exi
sts a continuous function $g : X \to [0, 2]$ with
$$g|_F = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad g(y) = 2.$$

Then:
- $g(x_i) = 0 < \varepsilon_i$ for every $i$, so $g \in V$.
- $\|g\|_\infty \geq |g(y)| = 2 \geq 1$, so $g \notin U$.

This contradicts $V \subseteq U$. Therefore $U$ is not open in the pointwise con
vergence topology.

## Conclusion

We have shown $\tau_c \supseteq \tau_p$ and, by exhibiting a $\tau_c$-open set t
hat is not $\tau_p$-open, that the inclusion is strict. The hypothesis that $X$
has a topology strictly weaker than the discrete topology is automatically satis
fied by any infinite compact Hausdorff space (since infinite discrete spaces are
 not compact).

Therefore the statement is **true**: for such $X$, the compact convergence topol
ogy is strictly finer than the pointwise convergence topology. $\blacksquare$
