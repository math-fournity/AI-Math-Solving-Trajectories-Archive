## Proof

**Yes**, such functions exist. The classic example demonstrates that $C^\infty$
(infinitely differentiable) functions are not necessarily analytic — their Taylo
r series at a point need not determine the function in any neighborhood.

**Construction.** Define
$$
k(x) = \begin{cases} e^{-1/x^2}, & x \neq 0, \\ 0, & x = 0, \end{cases}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad h(x) = 0.
$$

We work at the point $a = 0$.

**Claim 1: $k$ is infinitely differentiable on all of $\mathbb{R}$.**

For $x \neq 0$, $k$ is a composition of smooth functions, hence smooth. It remai
ns to check smoothness at $x = 0$.

**Claim 2: $k^{(n)}(0) = 0$ for every $n \geq 0$.**

We prove by induction that for every $n \geq 0$,
$$
k^{(n)}(x) = \begin{cases} P_n(1/x)\, e^{-1/x^2}, & x \neq 0, \\ 0, & x = 0, \en
d{cases}
$$
where $P_n$ is a polynomial. This is clear for $n=0$ with $P_0(t) = 1$.

Suppose it holds for $n$. For $x \neq 0$,
$$
k^{(n+1)}(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\!\left[P_n(1/x)\, e^{-1/x^2}\right] = P_n'(1/x)\cdot
\!\left(-\frac{1}{x^2}\right) e^{-1/x^2} + P_n(1/x)\cdot \frac{2}{x^3}\, e^{-1/x
^2}.
$$
This equals $\bigl[-P_n'(1/x)/x^2 + 2P_n(1/x)/x^3\bigr]e^{-1/x^2}$, which is aga
in of the form $P_{n+1}(1/x)\,e^{-1/x^2}$ for a polynomial $P_{n+1}$. So the for
m is preserved.

Now we show $k^{(n)}(0) = 0$ by induction. The base case $k(0) = 0$ is by defini
tion. For the inductive step, assume $k^{(n)}(0) = 0$. Then
$$
k^{(n+1)}(0) = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{k^{(n)}(x) - k^{(n)}(0)}{x - 0} = \lim_{x \t
o 0} \frac{P_n(1/x)\, e^{-1/x^2}}{x}.
$$
Substituting $t = 1/x$ (so $x \to 0^+$ gives $t \to +\infty$, and $x \to 0^-$ gi
ves $t \to -\infty$):
$$
\frac{P_n(1/x)\, e^{-1/x^2}}{x} = t\, P_n(t)\, e^{-t^2}.
$$
Since $t\,P_n(t)$ is a polynomial and $e^{-t^2}$ decays faster than any polynomi
al grows, $\lim_{t \to \pm\infty} t\,P_n(t)\,e^{-t^2} = 0$. Therefore $k^{(n+1)}
(0) = 0$.

By induction, $k^{(n)}(0) = 0$ for all $n \geq 0$.

**Claim 3: All derivatives of $h$ and $k$ agree at $x = 0$.**

Since $h(x) = 0$ is the zero function, $h^{(n)}(0) = 0$ for all $n \geq 0$. By C
laim 2, $k^{(n)}(0) = 0$ for all $n \geq 0$. Hence
$$
h^{(n)}(0) = k^{(n)}(0) = 0 \quad \text{for all } n \geq 0.
$$

**Claim 4: $h$ and $k$ are not equal in any interval around $0$.**

For any $\varepsilon > 0$ and any $x \in (0, \varepsilon)$, we have $k(x) = e^{-
1/x^2} > 0 = h(x)$. Thus $k \neq h$ at every point of $(0, \varepsilon)$. Since
$\varepsilon > 0$ was arbitrary, there is no interval around $0$ on which $h = k
$.

**Conclusion.** The functions $h(x) = 0$ and $k(x) = e^{-1/x^2}$ (with $k(0)=0$)
 are both infinitely differentiable, all their derivatives agree at $x = 0$ (all
 equal $0$), yet they differ at every point of any punctured neighborhood of $0$
. This shows that the Taylor series of a $C^\infty$ function at a point does not
, in general, determine the function — a phenomenon impossible for real-analytic
 functions by the identity theorem.
