## Answer: No, we cannot conclude that $|F(x,y)| < Cxy$.

### Proof

The proposed conclusion fails for two independent reasons.

**Reason 1 (the inequality is impossible as stated).** The phrase "for small $x$
 and $y$" refers to a neighborhood of $(0,0)$, which contains points where $x$ a
nd $y$ have opposite signs — i.e. the second and fourth quadrants. At any such p
oint we have $xy < 0$, so for any positive constant $C$,

$$Cxy < 0 \le |F(x,y)|.$$

Hence the inequality $|F(x,y)| < Cxy$ would require a non‑negative number to be
strictly smaller than a negative number, which is impossible. This holds for **e
very** function $F$, regardless of its derivatives or its behavior. So the concl
usion is false as written.

(If one tried to repair the statement by writing $|F(x,y)| \le C|xy|$, the concl
usion still does **not** follow from the hypotheses as stated, as Reason 2 shows
.)

**Reason 2 (the stated hypotheses are insufficient even for $|F|\le C|xy|$).** T
he hypotheses only constrain $F$ *at the origin*: $F(0,0)=0$ and every partial d
erivative $\partial^{i+j}F/\partial x^i\partial y^j(0,0)$ exists and equals $0$.
 Nothing is assumed about smoothness in a punctured neighborhood, nor about $F$
vanishing on the coordinate axes. The informal phrase "behaves like $xy$" is not
 a quantitative condition, and it does not force $F/(xy)$ to be bounded.

In fact, the bound $|F|\le C|xy|$ becomes true only under **additional** hypothe
ses that are not present. To see what is actually needed, suppose we strengthen
the assumptions to:

- $F$ is $C^\infty$ in a whole neighborhood of $(0,0)$, and
- $F$ vanishes on both coordinate axes: $F(x,0)=F(0,y)=0$.

Then by Hadamard's lemma, $F(x,0)=0$ implies $F(x,y)=y\,A(x,y)$ with $A$ smooth,
 and $F(0,y)=0$ implies $A(0,y)=0$, hence $A(x,y)=x\,B(x,y)$ with $B$ smooth. Th
erefore

$$F(x,y) = xy\,B(x,y),$$

and since $B$ is continuous it is bounded near the origin, say $|B|\le M$. Conse
quently

$$|F(x,y)| \le M\,|xy| \quad\text{for small }x,y.$$

Moreover, the flatness condition (all derivatives of $F$ at $0$ vanish) forces $
B(0,0)=0$, so in fact $F=o(xy)$ — the bound holds with room to spare. But this a
rgument **required** smoothness in a neighborhood and vanishing on the axes, nei
ther of which is among the stated hypotheses.

### Conclusion

The implication is **false**. As literally written, $|F(x,y)|<Cxy$ is impossible
 whenever $xy<0$. Even after correcting the sign to $|F|\le C|xy|$, the conclusi
on does not follow from "all derivatives at the origin vanish" plus the vague co
ndition "behaves like $xy$"; one needs extra assumptions such as smoothness in a
 neighborhood and vanishing on the axes (which, via Hadamard's lemma, would actu
ally yield the stronger statement $F=o(xy)$). The hypotheses given are therefore
 insufficient to support the claimed bound.
