## Proof

Let $M$ be an $n \times n$ random matrix whose entries are i.i.d. complex Gaussi
an random variables (each entry has a continuous joint density on $\mathbb{C} \c
ong \mathbb{R}^2$). We want
$$\Pr(\det M = 0).$$

**Key observation.** The determinant $\det : \mathbb{C}^{n \times n} \to \mathbb
{C}$ is a polynomial in the $n^2$ entries of $M$. It is a *nonzero* polynomial (
e.g., $\det(I_n) = 1$). Its zero set is
$$\Sigma := \{M \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n} : \det M = 0\},$$
the variety of singular matrices.

**Why $\Sigma$ has measure zero.** Viewing $\mathbb{C}^{n \times n} \cong \mathb
b{R}^{2n^2}$, the entries of $M$ have a joint distribution that is absolutely co
ntinuous with respect to Lebesgue measure (each complex Gaussian entry has a den
sity $f(z) = \tfrac{1}{\pi}e^{-|z|^2}$, and the joint density is the product). T
he zero set of any nonzero real/complex polynomial in several variables has Lebe
sgue measure zero. (Proof sketch: fix all but one variable generically so the re
sulting single-variable polynomial is not identically zero; its zero set is fini
te, hence measure zero; integrate/Fubini over the remaining variables, using tha
t the "bad" parameter choices where the polynomial becomes identically zero form
 a lower-dimensional variety, again measure zero.)

Therefore $\Sigma$ is a Lebesgue-null set in $\mathbb{R}^{2n^2}$.

**Conclusion.** Since the distribution of $M$ is absolutely continuous with resp
ect to Lebesgue measure and $\Sigma$ has Lebesgue measure zero,
$$\Pr(\det M = 0) = \Pr(M \in \Sigma) = 0.$$

This holds for every $n \ge 1$ and for any (nondegenerate) Gaussian distribution
 of the entries — real or complex, i.i.d. or with any nonsingular covariance — a
s long as the joint law is absolutely continuous. The essential point is that si
ngularity is the zero locus of a nonzero polynomial, hence a null set, and an ab
solutely continuous measure assigns zero mass to null sets.

$$\boxed{\Pr(\det M = 0) = 0}$$
