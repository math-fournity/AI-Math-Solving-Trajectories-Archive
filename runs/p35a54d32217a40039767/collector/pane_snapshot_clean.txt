## Proof

**Definition.** A *Pisot number* (Pisot–Vijayaraghavan number) is a real algebra
ic integer $\theta > 1$ all of whose Galois conjugates $\theta_2, \dots, \theta_
n$ (the other roots of its minimal polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$) satisfy $|\thet
a_i| < 1$ for $i = 2, \dots, n$.

**Claim.** Every Pisot number $\theta$ has a unique minimal polynomial, and this
 polynomial has the smallest degree among all polynomials in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ hav
ing $\theta$ as a root.

---

### Step 1 — $\theta$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$

By definition a Pisot number is an *algebraic integer*, i.e. a root of some moni
c polynomial in $\mathbb{Z}[x] \subset \mathbb{Q}[x]$. Hence $\theta$ is algebra
ic over $\mathbb{Q}$.

### Step 2 — The minimal polynomial exists and is unique (as a monic polynomial)

Consider the ideal
$$I = \{f(x) \in \mathbb{Q}[x] : f(\theta) = 0\} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}[x].$$

Since $\theta$ is algebraic, $I \neq \{0\}$. Since $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ is a principa
l ideal domain, $I = (m(x))$ for some nonzero polynomial $m(x)$. We may normaliz
e $m$ to be **monic**; call this monic generator $m_\theta(x)$.

*Uniqueness:* Suppose $m_1, m_2$ are both monic generators of $I$. Then $m_1 \mi
d m_2$ and $m_2 \mid m_1$, so $m_1 = c\, m_2$ for some $c \in \mathbb{Q}^\times$
. Since both are monic, comparing leading coefficients gives $c = 1$, hence $m_1
 = m_2$.

### Step 3 — $m_\theta$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$

Suppose $m_\theta(x) = g(x)\,h(x)$ with $\deg g, \deg h < \deg m_\theta$ and $g,
 h \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$. Evaluating at $\theta$: $g(\theta)\,h(\theta) = 0$, so $g
(\theta) = 0$ or $h(\theta) = 0$. Then $g \in I$ or $h \in I$, meaning $m_\theta
 \mid g$ or $m_\theta \mid h$ — contradicting $\deg g, \deg h < \deg m_\theta$.
Hence $m_\theta$ is irreducible.

### Step 4 — $m_\theta$ has the smallest degree

Let $f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ with $f(\theta) = 0$, i.e. $f \in I = (m_\theta)$. The
n $f = m_\theta \cdot q$ for some $q \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$, so
$$\deg f = \deg m_\theta + \deg q \geq \deg m_\theta.$$

Equality holds if and only if $q$ is a nonzero constant, i.e. $f$ is a scalar mu
ltiple of $m_\theta$. In particular, the **only monic** polynomial of degree $\d
eg m_\theta$ having $\theta$ as a root is $m_\theta$ itself.

### Step 5 — Integer coefficients (algebraic integer refinement)

Since $\theta$ is an algebraic integer, its minimal polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$
 in fact lies in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ (Gauss's lemma: a monic irreducible polynomial
in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ having an algebraic integer as a root has integer coefficient
s). Thus $m_\theta \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$, and it is the unique monic polynomial of
least degree in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ (equivalently in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$) having $\theta
$ as a root.

---

### Conclusion

For every Pisot number $\theta$:

1. **Existence and uniqueness:** There is a unique monic polynomial $m_\theta \i
n \mathbb{Z}[x]$ of least degree having $\theta$ as a root. This is the minimal
polynomial of $\theta$.
2. **Minimality of degree:** Every polynomial $f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ (and in part
icular every $f \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$) with $f(\theta) = 0$ satisfies $\deg f \geq
\deg m_\theta$, with equality only for scalar multiples of $m_\theta$.
3. **Divisibility:** $m_\theta \mid f$ for every such $f$.

The Pisot condition (conjugates strictly inside the unit disk) is a *selection c
riterion* determining which algebraic integers count as Pisot numbers; it does n
ot alter the standard minimal-polynomial theory. Once $\theta$ is known to be al
gebraic (which the Pisot definition guarantees), the uniqueness and minimal-degr
ee property of its minimal polynomial follows from the general theory of algebra
ic numbers over $\mathbb{Q}$.

**Therefore, yes: Pisot numbers have unique minimal polynomials, and these polyn
omials have the smallest degree among all polynomials (with rational, or equival
ently integer, coefficients) having the Pisot number as a root.** $\blacksquare$
