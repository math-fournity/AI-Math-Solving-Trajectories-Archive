## Answer

**No.** In general the limit $F$ need not extend continuously to $\partial\Omega
$, so $F\notin A(\Omega)$ in general. Below is an explicit counterexample.

---

### Counterexample

Take $n=1$ and $\Omega=\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}$, so $A(\mathbb{D})=
\mathcal{C}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})\cap\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{D})$.

Consider the holomorphic function
$$
f(z)=\exp\!\Big(-\frac{1+z}{1-z}\Big),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.
$$

**Step 1. $f$ is bounded by $1$ on $\mathbb{D}$.** For $z\in\mathbb{D}$, writing
 $z=x+iy$, a direct computation gives
$$
\operatorname{Re}\frac{1+z}{1-z}=\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{|1-z|^{2}}>0,
$$
hence
$$
|f(z)|=\exp\!\Big(-\operatorname{Re}\frac{1+z}{1-z}\Big)<1.
$$

**Step 2. $f$ does not extend continuously to $\overline{\mathbb{D}}$.** Examine
 the behavior as $z\to 1$.

- Along the real axis $z=1-\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon\downarrow 0$:
  $$
  \frac{1+z}{1-z}=\frac{2-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\to+\infty,\qquad f(1-\vareps
ilon)\to 0.
  $$

- Along $z=1-\varepsilon+i\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon\downarrow 0$ (so $|z|<1$ fo
r small $\varepsilon$):
  $$
  \frac{1+z}{1-z}=\frac{2-\varepsilon+i\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-i\varepsilon}
  =\frac{(2-\varepsilon+i\varepsilon)(\varepsilon+i\varepsilon)}{2\varepsilon^{2
}}
  =\frac{(2-\varepsilon)\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}+i\big((2-\varepsilon)\vareps
ilon+\varepsilon^{2}\big)}{2\varepsilon^{2}}.
  $$
  The real part equals $\frac{(2-\varepsilon)\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}}{2\vare
psilon^{2}}=\frac{2-2\varepsilon}{2\varepsilon}=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon
}\to+\infty$ as well...

  Let me instead use the tangential approach $z=1-\varepsilon+i\sqrt{\varepsilon
}$, $\varepsilon\downarrow 0$ (with $|z|^{2}=(1-\varepsilon)^{2}+\varepsilon=1-\
varepsilon+\varepsilon^{2}<1$). Then
  $$
  \operatorname{Re}\frac{1+z}{1-z}=\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{|1-z|^{2}}=\frac{\varepsilon
-\varepsilon^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}+\varepsilon}=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+
1}\to 1.
  $$
  Hence $|f(z)|\to e^{-1}$, so $f(z)\to e^{-1}\cdot e^{-i\,\operatorname{Im}\fra
c{1+z}{1-z}}$; in particular $|f(z)|\to e^{-1}\neq 0$.

Since approaching $1$ along the real axis gives limit $0$ while the tangential a
pproach gives modulus tending to $e^{-1}\neq 0$, the limit at $z=1$ does not exi
st. Thus $f$ has **no continuous extension** to $\overline{\mathbb{D}}$, i.e. $f
\notin A(\mathbb{D})$.

**Step 3. Construct the approximating sequence $\{F_\nu\}\subset A(\mathbb{D})$.
** Pick $r_\nu\in(0,1)$ with $r_\nu\uparrow 1$ and set
$$
F_\nu(z):=f(r_\nu z),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.
$$

- *Membership in $A(\mathbb{D})$:* For $|z|\le 1$, $|r_\nu z|\le r_\nu<1$, so $r
_\nu z\in\mathbb{D}$ and $F_\nu$ is defined and holomorphic on a neighborhood of
 $\overline{\mathbb{D}}$ (namely $\{|w|<1/r_\nu\}$ contains $\overline{\mathbb{D
}}$). Hence $F_\nu\in\mathcal{O}(\{|z|<1/r_\nu\})\subset A(\mathbb{D})$.

- *Uniform bound:* For $z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}$, $r_\nu z\in\mathbb{D}$, so $
|F_\nu(z)|=|f(r_\nu z)|<1$. Thus $F_\nu(\overline{\mathbb{D}})\subseteq\mathbb{D
}\subset\overline{\mathbb{D}}$.

- *Local uniform convergence to $f$:* Let $K\Subset\mathbb{D}$, so $K\subseteq\{
|z|\le\rho\}$ for some $\rho<1$. For all $\nu$ large enough that $r_\nu\rho<\rho
'<1$, the map $z\mapsto r_\nu z$ sends $K$ into $\{|w|\le\rho'\}$, where $f$ is
uniformly continuous. Since $r_\nu z\to z$ uniformly on $K$ and $f$ is uniformly
 continuous on $\{|w|\le\rho'\}$,
  $$
  \sup_{z\in K}|F_\nu(z)-f(z)|=\sup_{z\in K}|f(r_\nu z)-f(z)|\longrightarrow 0.
  $$
  Hence $F_\nu\to f$ uniformly on compact subsets of $\mathbb{D}$.

By Montel's theorem this (or any) subsequence has a locally uniform limit; here
the full sequence converges locally uniformly to $f$.

**Step 4. Conclusion.** The limit function is $F=f$, which by Step 2 does not ex
tend continuously to $\partial\mathbb{D}$. Therefore $F\notin A(\mathbb{D})$.

---

### Remark on the obstruction

The issue is that local uniform convergence on $\Omega$ controls the behavior on
ly on compact subsets *strictly inside* $\Omega$; it gives **no control near the
 boundary**. The algebra $A(\Omega)$ requires a global continuous extension to $
\overline{\Omega}$, which is a boundary-regularity property not preserved under
locally uniform limits—even when every $F_\nu$ is bounded by $1$ on $\overline{\
Omega}$ and individually extends nicely. The dilation trick $f(r_\nu z)$ always
converts a bounded holomorphic $f$ into an $A(\mathbb{D})$-valued sequence conve
rging locally uniformly to $f$, so *any* bounded holomorphic function failing to
 extend continuously to the boundary yields such a counterexample.

Hence the answer to the question is:

**No, $F$ need not belong to $A(\Omega)$ in general.**
