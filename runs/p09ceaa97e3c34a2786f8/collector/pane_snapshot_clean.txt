## Solution

**Key observation.** The four cases in the definition of $u$ can be unified. Def
ine $r(x) = \max(|x_1|, |x_2|)$. I claim $u(x) = 1 - r(x)$ on all of $\Omega$.

Verification on each sector (the four regions are the open sectors determined by
 the lines $x_2 = \pm x_1$):

- **Right sector** ($x_1 > 0,\; |x_2| < x_1$): here $\max(|x_1|,|x_2|) = x_1$, s
o $1 - r = 1 - x_1 = u$. ✓
- **Left sector** ($x_1 < 0,\; |x_2| < -x_1$): here $\max(|x_1|,|x_2|) = |x_1| =
 -x_1$, so $1 - r = 1 + x_1 = u$. ✓
- **Top sector** ($x_2 > 0,\; |x_1| < x_2$): here $\max(|x_1|,|x_2|) = x_2$, so
$1 - r = 1 - x_2 = u$. ✓
- **Bottom sector** ($x_2 < 0,\; |x_1| < -x_2$): here $\max(|x_1|,|x_2|) = |x_2|
 = -x_2$, so $1 - r = 1 + x_2 = u$. ✓

Thus $u(x) = 1 - \|x\|_\infty$ on $\Omega$.

**$u$ is Lipschitz.** The map $x \mapsto \|x\|_\infty = \max(|x_1|,|x_2|)$ is Li
pschitz with constant $1$ (it is a norm on $\mathbb{R}^2$, and all norms on $\ma
thbb{R}^2$ are Lipschitz-equivalent; directly, $|\|x\|_\infty - \|y\|_\infty| \l
e \|x-y\|_\infty \le \|x-y\|_2$). Hence $u$ is Lipschitz on $\Omega$ with consta
nt $1$.

**Weak derivative.** On each open sector, $u$ is smooth with constant gradient:
- Right: $\nabla u = (-1, 0)$; Left: $\nabla u = (1, 0)$; Top: $\nabla u = (0, -
1)$; Bottom: $\nabla u = (0, 1)$.

The sectors meet along the four rays $\{x_2 = x_1,\, x_1 > 0\}$, $\{x_2 = x_1,\,
 x_1 < 0\}$, $\{x_2 = -x_1,\, x_1 > 0\}$, $\{x_2 = -x_1,\, x_1 < 0\}$, and the o
rigin. These interfaces have **Lebesgue measure zero**.

Since $u$ is **continuous** across each interface (e.g., on $x_2 = x_1 > 0$: fro
m the right sector $u \to 1 - x_1$, from the top sector $u \to 1 - x_2 = 1 - x_1
$), there are no jump discontinuities. A standard result (integration by parts a
cross the measure-zero interfaces produces no boundary terms) then gives that th
e weak gradient $\nabla u$ equals the piecewise constant gradient above, which i
s bounded: $\|\nabla u\|_{L^\infty} = 1$.

**Conclusion.** Since $\Omega = (-1,1)^2$ is bounded:
- $u \in L^\infty(\Omega)$ (as $0 \le u \le 1$), so $u \in L^p(\Omega)$ for ever
y $p \in [1,\infty]$.
- $\nabla u \in L^\infty(\Omega)$ with $\|\nabla u\|_\infty = 1$, so $\nabla u \
in L^p(\Omega)$ for every $p \in [1,\infty]$.

Therefore $u \in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega) \subset W^{1,p}(\Omega)$ for every $p \in
[1, \infty]$.

**Answer:** $u \in W^{1,p}(\Omega)$ for **all** $p \in [1, \infty]$.
