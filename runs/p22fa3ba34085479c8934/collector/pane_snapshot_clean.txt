## Setup

We have $c_n = \int_\Delta (xy(3-x-y))^n\,dxdy$ where $\Delta = \{x>0, y>0, x+y<
3\}$. Define $f(x,y) = xy(3-x-y)$, so $c_n = \int_\Delta e^{n\log f(x,y)}\,dxdy$
.

## Step 1: Locate the unique maximum of $f$ on $\overline{\Delta}$

On the boundary of $\Delta$, $f = 0$ (since one of $x=0, y=0, x+y=3$ holds). In
the interior, $f > 0$.

Critical points of $\log f$:
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\log f = \frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{3-x-y} = 0, \qquad
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\log f = \frac{1}{y} - \frac{1}{3-x-y} = 0.$$

From the first: $x = 3-x-y$. From the second: $y = 3-x-y$. Hence $x = y$ and $3x
 = 3$, giving the unique interior critical point $(x_0, y_0) = (1,1)$, with $f(1
,1) = 1\cdot 1\cdot 1 = 1$.

Since $f \geq 0$ on $\overline\Delta$, $f = 0$ on $\partial\Delta$, and $f(1,1)
= 1 > 0$, the point $(1,1)$ is the unique global maximum with value $M := f(1,1)
 = 1$.

## Step 2: Hessian of $\log f$ at the maximum

Computing second derivatives of $g = \log f = \log x + \log y + \log(3-x-y)$ at
$(1,1)$:

$$g_{xx} = -\frac{1}{x^2} - \frac{1}{(3-x-y)^2}\bigg|_{(1,1)} = -1 - 1 = -2,$$
$$g_{yy} = -\frac{1}{y^2} - \frac{1}{(3-x-y)^2}\bigg|_{(1,1)} = -1 - 1 = -2,$$
$$g_{xy} = -\frac{1}{(3-x-y)^2}\bigg|_{(1,1)} = -1.$$

So the Hessian is $H = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & -1 \\ -1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$, which
is negative definite (eigenvalues $-1$ and $-3$). The maximum is **non-degenerat
e**, with
$$\det(-H) = \det\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = 4 - 1 = 3.$$

## Step 3: Apply the 2D Laplace method

Setting $u = x-1$, $v = y-1$, Taylor expansion gives
$$\log f(1+u, 1+v) = \log(1+u) + \log(1+v) + \log(1-u-v).$$
The linear terms cancel ($u + v - (u+v) = 0$), and the quadratic term is
$$-\frac{u^2}{2} - \frac{v^2}{2} - \frac{(u+v)^2}{2} = -(u^2 + uv + v^2).$$

So near the maximum, $\log f \approx -(u^2 + uv + v^2)$, i.e., $f(1+u,1+v)^n \ap
prox e^{-n(u^2+uv+v^2)}$.

The Gaussian integral: with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 1\end{pmatrix}
$ (so that $u^2+uv+v^2 = (u,v)A(u,v)^T$), $\det A = 3/4$, and
$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^2} e^{-n(u^2+uv+v^2)}\,dudv = \frac{\pi}{n\sqrt{\det A}} = \f
rac{\pi}{n\cdot \sqrt{3}/2} = \frac{2\pi}{n\sqrt{3}}.$$

## Step 4: Justify neglecting the rest

Since $\log f$ has a strict non-degenerate maximum at $(1,1)$ with value $0$ (i.
e., $f = 1$ there) and $\log f < 0$ elsewhere in $\overline\Delta$, for any $\ep
silon > 0$ the contribution from $\overline\Delta \setminus B_\epsilon(1,1)$ is
$O(e^{-n\delta})$ for some $\delta > 0$, which is exponentially negligible. On $
B_\epsilon(1,1)$, standard Taylor-remainder bounds (using the non-degeneracy of
the Hessian) show the integral over $B_\epsilon$ equals the full Gaussian integr
al up to $o(1/n)$ relative error. Therefore

$$\boxed{c_n \sim \frac{2\pi}{n\sqrt{3}} \cdot 1^n \quad \text{as } n\to\infty.}
$$

## Step 5: Determine $a$, $b$, and $l$

We need $d_n = n^a b^n c_n \to l \in \mathbb{R}^+$. Since $c_n \sim \frac{2\pi}{
\sqrt{3}}\, n^{-1}\cdot 1^n$:

- The factor $1^n$ in $c_n$ forces $b = 1$ (any $b > 1$ gives $d_n\to\infty$; an
y $0 < b < 1$ gives $d_n \to 0$).
- The factor $n^{-1}$ forces $a = 1$ to obtain a finite nonzero limit.
- The limit is then $l = \frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{3}}$.

## Result

$$\boxed{a = 1, \qquad b = 1, \qquad l = \frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{3}}.}$$
