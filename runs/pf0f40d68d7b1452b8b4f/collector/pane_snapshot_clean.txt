## Proof

**Setup.** We partition the digits {1,2,3,4,5,6} into three pairs. In each pair
one digit is the tens digit and the other is the ones digit, giving a two-digit
number. We sum the three two-digit numbers and ask how many distinct sums are po
ssible.

**Key observation.** Let $T$ = sum of the three tens-digits and $O$ = sum of the
 three ones-digits. Since every digit is used exactly once,

$$T + O = 1+2+3+4+5+6 = 21.$$

The total sum of the three two-digit codes is

$$S = 10T + O = 10T + (21 - T) = 9T + 21.$$

Thus $S$ depends **only** on $T$, the sum of the three digits chosen to be tens-
digits. The actual pairing (which tens digit pairs with which ones digit) and th
e assignment within pairs do not affect $S$ — only the choice of *which three* d
igits are tens-digits matters.

**Range of $T$.** $T$ is the sum of a 3-element subset of $\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$. The
 minimum is $1+2+3=6$ and the maximum is $4+5+6=15$.

**Every integer from 6 to 15 is attainable:**

| $T$ | Subset |
|-----|--------|
| 6 | {1,2,3} |
| 7 | {1,2,4} |
| 8 | {1,2,5} |
| 9 | {1,3,5} |
| 10 | {2,3,5} |
| 11 | {2,4,5} |
| 12 | {3,4,5} |
| 13 | {3,4,6} |
| 14 | {3,5,6} |
| 15 | {4,5,6} |

(Many other subsets work as well; one witness per value suffices.)

**Counting distinct sums.** Since $S = 9T+21$ is strictly increasing in $T$, eac
h distinct $T$ gives a distinct $S$. The attainable values of $T$ are exactly $\
{6,7,8,\dots,15\}$, which is

$$15 - 6 + 1 = 10$$

values.

**Answer:** $\boxed{10}$ different possible sums can be generated.
