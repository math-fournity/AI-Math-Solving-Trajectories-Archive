**Proof.**

Let $G$ be the graph on the $n \ge 4$ people where an edge means "know each othe
r" (symmetric by hypothesis), and let $H = \bar G$ be its complement ("do not kn
ow each other").

**Uniform vs. mixed.** For a person $x$ seated at a round table, call $x$ *unifo
rm* if $x$ either knows **both** neighbors or knows **neither** neighbor (i.e. t
he two incident table-edges are both in $G$ or both in $H$), and *mixed* otherwi
se (exactly one of the two table-edges lies in $G$).

The hypothesis says: in every cyclic seating of $4$ people, at least one person
is uniform. Equivalently, **there is no cyclic seating of $4$ people in which al
l four are mixed**.

**Alternating 4-cycles.** In a 4-seating $a\text{-}b\text{-}c\text{-}d\text{-}a$
, all four being mixed means each vertex is incident to exactly one $G$-edge and
 one $H$-edge among the two table-edges. Hence the four table-edges alternate $G
,H,G,H$ around the cycle — an *alternating 4-cycle*. So the hypothesis is:

> (†) There is no alternating 4-cycle: no four distinct vertices $a,b,c,d$ with
$ab, cd \in G$ and $bc, da \in H$.

(Any alternating 4-cycle gives a forbidden all-mixed seating, and conversely.)

**Goal:** show $G$ is a *split graph*, i.e. $V$ partitions into a clique $C$ and
 an independent set $I$ (one part possibly empty).

**Main argument.** Let $C$ be a **maximum** clique of $G$, and set $I = V\setmin
us C$. We prove $I$ is independent.

Suppose not: there exist $u, v \in I$ with $uv \in G$. Since $C$ is a *maximum*
clique, $u$ cannot be adjacent to every vertex of $C$ (otherwise $C\cup\{u\}$ wo
uld be a larger clique); so the set $N_C^-(u) = \{c\in C : uc\notin G\}$ is none
mpty. Likewise $N_C^-(v)$ is nonempty.

Pick $c_u \in N_C^-(u)$ and $c_v \in N_C^-(v)$.

*Case 1: $c_u \ne c_v$.* Consider the cyclic seating $u\text{-}v\text{-}c_v\text
{-}c_u\text{-}u$. Its four edges are:
$$uv \in G,\quad v c_v \in H,\quad c_v c_u \in G\ (\text{both in the clique }C),
\quad c_u u \in H.$$
This is an alternating 4-cycle, so every seated person is mixed — contradicting
(†).

*Case 2: $c_u = c_v = c$ for every choice.* Then we must have $N_C^-(u) = N_C^-(
v) = \{c\}$ (a single common vertex): indeed, if $N_C^-(u)$ contained some $c' \
ne c$, choosing $c_u = c'$ and $c_v = c$ would put us back in Case 1; similarly
for $v$. So $u$ and $v$ are each adjacent to **every** vertex of $C\setminus\{c\
}$, and $uv\in G$.

Now form
$$C' \;=\; (C\setminus\{c\}) \;\cup\; \{u, v\}.$$
This is a clique: $C\setminus\{c\}$ is a clique (subset of $C$); $u$ is adjacent
 to all of $C\setminus\{c\}$; $v$ is adjacent to all of $C\setminus\{c\}$; and $
uv\in G$. But
$$|C'| = |C|-1+2 = |C|+1 > |C|,$$
contradicting the maximality of $C$.

Both cases yield a contradiction, so no such $u,v$ exist; $I$ is an independent
set.

**Conclusion.** $V = C \,\cup\, I$ with $C$ a clique, $I$ an independent set (ei
ther possibly empty). Placing the people of $C$ in one group and the people of $
I$ in the other gives the required partition: within the first group everyone kn
ows each other, and within the second group no one knows each other. $\blacksqua
re$
