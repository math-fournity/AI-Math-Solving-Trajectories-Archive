R}\setminus\{0\}$, and has $\lim_{x\to 0} f'(x) = g$, must be $C^1$ on all of $\
mathbb{R}$.

## Proof

The key tool is the fundamental theorem of calculus for Banach-space valued func
tions. We recall two standard facts:

**Fact 1 (FTC for Banach-valued functions).** If $h: [a,b] \to X$ is continuous,
 the Riemann integral $\int_a^b h(t)\,dt$ is well-defined in $X$, and satisfies
the norm inequality $\left\|\int_a^b h(t)\,dt\right\| \leq \int_a^b \|h(t)\|\,dt
$. If $h$ is $C^1$, then $h(b) - h(a) = \int_a^b h'(t)\,dt$.

**Fact 2.** If $h: [a,b] \to X$ is continuous with $\|h(t)\| \leq M$, then $\lef
t\|\int_a^b h(t)\,dt\right\| \leq M|b-a|$.

---

### Step 1: Establishing the integral representation

Fix $x > 0$. For any $\varepsilon \in (0, x)$, $f$ is $C^1$ on $[\varepsilon, x]
$, so by Fact 1:
$$f(x) - f(\varepsilon) = \int_\varepsilon^x f'(t)\,dt.$$

Since $f$ is continuous at $0$, $f(\varepsilon) \to f(0)$ as $\varepsilon \to 0^
+$. We claim the right-hand side converges as $\varepsilon \to 0^+$. Indeed, sin
ce $f'(t) \to g$ as $t \to 0$, there exists $\delta > 0$ and $M > 0$ such that $
\|f'(t)\| \leq M$ for $t \in (0, \delta)$. Hence for $0 < \varepsilon_1 < \varep
silon_2 < \delta$:
$$\left\|\int_{\varepsilon_1}^{\varepsilon_2} f'(t)\,dt\right\| \leq \int_{\vare
psilon_1}^{\varepsilon_2} \|f'(t)\|\,dt \leq M(\varepsilon_2 - \varepsilon_1) \t
o 0,$$
so $\int_\varepsilon^x f'(t)\,dt$ is Cauchy as $\varepsilon \to 0^+$ and converg
es. Taking the limit:
$$f(x) - f(0) = \int_0^x f'(t)\,dt.$$

The case $x < 0$ is identical (working on $[x, \varepsilon]$ with $\varepsilon \
to 0^-$), giving the same formula $f(x) - f(0) = \int_0^x f'(t)\,dt$.

### Step 2: Showing $f'(0) = g$

Write:
$$f(x) - f(0) = \int_0^x f'(t)\,dt = \int_0^x g\,dt + \int_0^x (f'(t) - g)\,dt =
 gx + \int_0^x (f'(t) - g)\,dt.$$

Therefore:
$$\frac{f(x) - f(0) - gx}{x} = \frac{1}{x}\int_0^x (f'(t) - g)\,dt.$$

Since $f'(t) \to g$ as $t \to 0$, for any $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta
 > 0$ such that $\|f'(t) - g\| < \varepsilon$ whenever $0 < |t| < \delta$. For $
0 < |x| < \delta$, applying the norm inequality (Fact 1):
$$\left\|\frac{1}{x}\int_0^x (f'(t) - g)\,dt\right\| \leq \frac{1}{|x|}\int_0^{|
x|} \|f'(t) - g\|\,dt \leq \frac{1}{|x|}\cdot \varepsilon \cdot |x| = \varepsilo
n.$$

(The case $x < 0$ uses $\left\|\int_0^x h(t)\,dt\right\| = \left\|\int_x^0 h(t)\
,dt\right\| \leq \int_x^0 \|h(t)\|\,dt = \int_0^{|x|}\|h(-s)\|\,ds$, giving the
same bound.)

Thus:
$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{f(x) - f(0) - gx}{x} = 0,$$
which means $f$ is (Fréchet) differentiable at $0$ with $f'(0) = g$.

### Step 3: $f'$ is continuous at $0$

By hypothesis, $\lim_{x \to 0} f'(x) = g = f'(0)$. So $f'$ is continuous at $0$.
 Since $f$ is $C^1$ on $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ by assumption, $f'$ exists and
 is continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

### Conclusion

$f$ is differentiable everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$, and $f': \mathbb{R} \to \mathc
al{L}(\mathbb{R}, X) \cong X$ is continuous. Therefore $f$ is of class $C^1$ on
$\mathbb{R}$.
