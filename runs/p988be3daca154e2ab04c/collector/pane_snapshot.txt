## Proof

**Claim.** Yes, an infinite-dimensional vector space can have a linear operator
with infinitely many eigenvalues.

**Construction.**

Let $\mathbb{F}$ be any field, and let $V$ be the vector space of all functions
from $\mathbb{N}$ to $\mathbb{F}$ with finite support; equivalently, $V = \bigop
lus_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \mathbb{F}$, the space of finitely-supported sequences. T
his is an infinite-dimensional vector space with basis $\{e_n : n \in \mathbb{N}
\}$, where $e_n$ is the standard unit vector.

Define $T : V \to V$ by setting, for each basis vector,
$$T(e_n) = n \cdot e_n, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$
and extending linearly. Since every element of $V$ is a *finite* linear combinat
ion of basis vectors, $T$ is well-defined: for $v = \sum_{i=0}^{k} a_i e_i$,
$$T(v) = \sum_{i=0}^{k} a_i \cdot i \cdot e_i,$$
which is again a finitely-supported sequence, hence in $V$. Linearity of $T$ is
immediate from the definition.

**Eigenvalues.** For each $n \in \mathbb{N}$, the basis vector $e_n$ is nonzero
and satisfies
$$T(e_n) = n \cdot e_n,$$
so $e_n$ is an eigenvector of $T$ with eigenvalue $n$. The set $\{0, 1, 2, 3, \d
ots\} \subseteq \mathbb{F}$ (interpreting $n$ as $n \cdot 1_{\mathbb{F}}$) is th
erefore contained in the spectrum of eigenvalues of $T$. This is an infinite set
.

**Remark on the field.** If $\mathbb{F}$ has characteristic $p > 0$, the element
s $n \cdot 1_{\mathbb{F}}$ repeat with period $p$, giving only $p$ distinct valu
es. To handle this, replace the scalar $n$ above by elements of an infinite subs
et of $\mathbb{F}$. If $\mathbb{F}$ itself is finite, pass to an extension: take
 $V$ over $\mathbb{F}(x)$ (the rational function field, which is infinite), and
define $T(e_n) = x^n \cdot e_n$ for $n \in \mathbb{N}$. The elements $\{x^n : n
\in \mathbb{N}\}$ are pairwise distinct in $\mathbb{F}(x)$, yielding infinitely
many distinct eigenvalues. Thus the construction works over every field, provide
d we choose an infinite set of scalars (which always exists in some extension fi
eld, and in the base field whenever the base field is infinite).

**Conclusion.** The operator $T$ defined above is a linear operator on an infini
te-dimensional vector space and possesses infinitely many distinct eigenvalues.
Therefore, an infinite-dimensional vector space **can** have a linear operator w
ith infinitely many eigenvalues.
