**Answer: No.** There is only one such prime (at $n=1$, giving $3$).

## Proof

**Key identity.** For every $n \geq 1$,
$$2^{2^n}-1 \;=\; \prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\bigl(2^{2^k}+1\bigr).$$

This follows from the telescoping factorization
$$x^{2^n}-1 \;=\; (x-1)(x+1)(x^2+1)(x^4+1)\cdots\bigl(x^{2^{n-1}}+1\bigr),$$
obtained by repeatedly applying $a^2-1=(a-1)(a+1)$ to $x^{2^n}-1=(x^{2^{n-1}}-1)
(x^{2^{n-1}}+1)$. Setting $x=2$ and noting the leading factor $(2-1)=1$ gives th
e stated identity. The factors $F_k := 2^{2^k}+1$ are the Fermat numbers.

**Small cases.**
- $n=0$: $2^{2^0}-1 = 2^1-1 = 1$, not prime.
- $n=1$: $2^{2^1}-1 = 2^2-1 = 3$, prime.

**All $n\geq 2$ give composites.** For $n\geq 2$, the identity expresses $2^{2^n
}-1$ as a product of $n\geq 2$ factors
$$F_0\cdot F_1 \cdots F_{n-1},$$
where each $F_k = 2^{2^k}+1 \geq F_0 = 3 > 1$. A product of two or more integers
 each strictly greater than $1$ is composite. Hence $2^{2^n}-1$ is composite for
 every $n\geq 2$.

**Conclusion.** The only value of $n\in\mathbb{N}$ for which $2^{2^n}-1$ is prim
e is $n=1$ (yielding $3$). In particular, there are **not** infinitely many such
 primes — there is exactly one.
