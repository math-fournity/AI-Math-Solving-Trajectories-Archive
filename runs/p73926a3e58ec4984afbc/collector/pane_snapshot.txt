# Solution

**Claim:** The probability is **0**.

## Setup

Let $S$ be an uncountably infinite set. An element $x \in S$ is chosen (accordin
g to some "uniform-like" / non-atomic probability measure on $S$). You submit a
sequence of guesses $g_1, g_2, g_3, \ldots$, forming a countable set

$$G = \{g_1, g_2, g_3, \ldots\} \subseteq S.$$

You succeed if and only if $x \in G$. The probability of success is $P(x \in G)$
.

## Key Theorem

**Theorem (Countable sets have measure zero under non-atomic measures).** Let $\
mu$ be a non-atomic (continuous) probability measure on a measurable space. Then
 every countable set has $\mu$-measure zero.

*Proof.* A non-atomic measure assigns measure zero to every singleton: $\mu(\{s\
}) = 0$ for every $s$. By countable additivity,

$$\mu(G) = \mu\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} \{g_i\}\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty
} \mu(\{g_i\}) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} 0 = 0. \qquad \square$$

## Application

The natural notion of "selecting an element at random from an uncountable set" c
orresponds to a **non-atomic** (continuous) probability measure — one that does
not concentrate positive mass on any single point. This is precisely the class o
f measures for which no individual element is privileged, i.e., the measure-theo
retic formalization of "uniform over an uncountable space."

Under any such measure $\mu$ on $S$:

$$P(\text{success}) = P(x \in G) = \mu(G) = 0.$$

## Why non-atomic is the right interpretation

1. **No uniform distribution exists on a general uncountable set** in the naive
sense (there is no translation-invariant probability measure on all of $\mathbb{
R}$, for instance). The correct generalization of "uniform random element of an
uncountable set" is a **non-atomic probability measure** — one where each indivi
dual element has probability zero of being selected.

2. **If the measure had atoms** (positive mass on specific points), then the pro
blem would reduce to a discrete guessing problem on a countable support, and the
 "uncountable" nature of $S$ would be irrelevant. The interesting and well-posed
 case — the one the question intends — is the non-atomic case.

3. **Concrete example:** If $S = [0,1]$ and $x \sim \mathrm{Uniform}[0,1]$, then
 $G$ is countable, hence Lebesgue measure zero, so $P(x \in G) = 0$.

## Cardinality perspective (intuition)

Even without measure theory: a countable set $G$ has cardinality $\aleph_0$, whi
le $S$ is uncountable ($|S| > \aleph_0$). The "fraction" of $S$ covered by $G$ i
s, in every rigorous sense (Lebesgue measure, Borel measure under any continuous
 distribution), exactly zero. Your countably many guesses exhaust none of the un
countable target.

## Conclusion

$$\boxed{P(\text{correctly guessing}) = 0}$$

A countably infinite set of guesses has measure zero under any continuous (non-a
tomic) probability measure on an uncountable set. No matter how cleverly the cou
ntable sequence of guesses is chosen, the probability of hitting the target is e
xactly **zero**.
