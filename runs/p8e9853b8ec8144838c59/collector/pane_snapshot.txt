## Proof

We are given $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ and $B=(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}$, so $A^{T
}A$ is invertible, meaning $A$ has **full column rank** $m$ (in particular $m\le
 n$). A matrix $C$ satisfies
$$CAC=C,\qquad ACA=A,\qquad (CA)^{T}=CA.$$
We determine whether $C$ must equal $B$.

### Step 1. The three conditions force $CA=I_m$.

From $ACA=A$ we have $A(CA)=A$, i.e. $A(CA-I_m)=0$. Since $A$ has full column ra
nk, the only solution of $AX=0$ is $X=0$. Hence
$$CA-I_m=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad CA=I_m. \tag{$\ast$}$$

### Step 2. Under ($\ast$), conditions (2) and (3) are automatic.

- $(CA)^T = I_m^T = I_m = CA$, so condition (3) holds.
- $CAC = (CA)C = I_m C = C$, so condition (2) holds.

Thus the three given conditions are **equivalent** (under full column rank of $A
$) to the single condition $CA=I_m$; i.e., $C$ is **any left inverse** of $A$.

### Step 3. Left inverses of $A$ are not unique when $n>m$.

When $n=m$ (square invertible), the left inverse is unique and equals $A^{-1}=B$
. But when $n>m$, $A$ has a nontrivial left null space structure: the equation $
CA=I_m$ leaves $n-m$ free parameters in $C$, so there are infinitely many left i
nverses. $B=(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}$ is only the *particular* left inverse selected b
y the Moore–Penrose condition $(AC)^T=AC$ (condition 4), which is **not** among
the hypotheses.

### Step 4. Explicit counterexample.

Take
$$A=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 1},\qquad A^{T}A=1,\q
quad B=(1)^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\end{pmatrix}.$
$

Let $C=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\end{pmatrix}$. Then $C\ne B$. Verify the three conditi
ons:
- $CA = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}=1=I_1$,
so $(CA)^T=CA$. ✓
- $CAC = (CA)C = 1\cdot C = C$. ✓
- $ACA = A(CA) = A\cdot 1 = A$. ✓

Yet $C=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\end{pmatrix}\ne\begin{pmatrix}1&0\end{pmatrix}=B$. (In
deed $AC=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ is not symmetric, so the missing
fourth Moore–Penrose condition fails for this $C$.)

### Conclusion

The three conditions $CAC=C$, $ACA=A$, $(CA)^T=CA$ do **not** uniquely determine
 $C$; they only force $C$ to be a left inverse of $A$. When $n>m$, left inverses
 are not unique, and $B$ is merely the one left inverse that additionally satisf
ies $(AC)^T=AC$. Therefore

$$\boxed{C\text{ is not necessarily equal to }B.}$$
