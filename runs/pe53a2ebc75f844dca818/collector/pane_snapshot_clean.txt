Looking at this problem, I need to find the largest possible area of an isoscele
s trapezoid $ABCD$ with $AD \parallel BC$, where altitudes from $A$ to $CD$ and
from $B$ to $DA$ meet at $X$, with $BX = 3$ and $XQ = 1$.

## Coordinate Setup

Place $D = (0,0)$, $A = (a, 0)$ on the x-axis, with $BC$ at height $h$. Since th
e trapezoid is isosceles, $B$ and $C$ are symmetric about the perpendicular bise
ctor of $AD$:
$$B = \left(\tfrac{a+c}{2},\, h\right), \quad C = \left(\tfrac{a-c}{2},\, h\righ
t),$$
where $c = BC$. Set $d = \frac{a-c}{2}$, so $A = (c+2d,\, 0)$, $C = (d,\, h)$, $
B = (c+d,\, h)$, and $AB = CD = \sqrt{d^2 + h^2}$.

## Locating Q, P, and X

**Point Q:** Since $DA$ is horizontal, $BQ \perp DA$ is vertical, so $Q = (c+d,\
, 0)$.

**Point P:** The foot of perpendicular from $A$ to line $CD$ (direction $(d, h)$
):
$$t = \frac{(A - D)\cdot(d,h)}{d^2+h^2} = \frac{d(c+2d)}{d^2+h^2}, \quad P = (td
,\, th).$$

**Point X:** Line $BQ$ is $x = c+d$. Line $AP$ has direction $(-h, d)$ (perpendi
cular to $CD$). Parametrizing $A + s(-h, d)$ and setting $x = c+d$:
$$c + 2d - sh = c + d \implies s = \frac{d}{h}, \quad X = \left(c+d,\; \frac{d^2
}{h}\right).$$

## Applying the Given Conditions

All of $B$, $X$, $Q$ lie on the vertical line $x = c+d$, with $y$-coordinates $h
$, $d^2/h$, $0$ respectively.

$$XQ = \frac{d^2}{h} = 1 \implies d^2 = h.$$

$$BX = \left|h - \frac{d^2}{h}\right| = |h - 1| = 3.$$

Since $h > 0$: $h - 1 = 3 \implies h = 4$ (the case $h - 1 = -3$ gives $h = -2$,
 impossible). Then $d^2 = 4$, so $d = \pm 2$.

**Validity check:** For $Q$ to lie on segment $DA$ (from $(0,0)$ to $(c+2d, 0)$)
, we need $0 \le c+d \le c+2d$, which requires $d \ge 0$. Thus $d = 2$ (the case
 $d = -2$ either fails this or yields a crossed quadrilateral).

## Maximizing the Area

With $d = 2$, $h = 4$: the area is
$$\text{Area} = \tfrac{1}{2}(a + c)\cdot h = \tfrac{1}{2}(c + 4 + c)\cdot 4 = 4(
c + 2).$$

**Constraint on $c$:** $P$ must lie on segment $CD$, requiring $0 \le t \le 1$:
$$t = \frac{d(c+2d)}{d^2+h^2} = \frac{2(c+4)}{4+16} = \frac{c+4}{10} \le 1 \impl
ies c \le 6.$$

Also $c > 0$ (non-degenerate). The area $4(c+2)$ is maximized at $c = 6$, giving
:

$$\text{Area}_{\max} = 4 \cdot 8 = 32.$$

## Verification at $c = 6$

$A = (10,0)$, $B = (8,4)$, $C = (2,4)$, $D = (0,0)$. Then $AB = CD = \sqrt{20}$
(isosceles ✓), $Q = (8,0)$ on $DA$ ✓, $P = C = (2,4)$ (endpoint of $CD$, $t = 1$
), $AP \perp CD$ since $(8,-4)\cdot(-2,-4) = -16+16 = 0$ ✓, and $X = (8, 1)$ wit
h $BX = 3$, $XQ = 1$ ✓.

The largest possible area of $ABCD$ is $\boxed{32}$.
