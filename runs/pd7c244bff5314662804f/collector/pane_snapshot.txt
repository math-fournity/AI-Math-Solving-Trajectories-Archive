**Problem.** Evaluate $\displaystyle I = \int_{0}^{+\infty} \frac{x - \sin x}{x^
{3}}\, dx$, given the Dirichlet integral $\displaystyle \int_{0}^{+\infty} \frac
{\sin \alpha x}{x}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{2}\operatorname{sgn}\alpha$.

---

### Step 1. Convergence

Near $x = 0$: $x - \sin x = \frac{x^{3}}{6} - \frac{x^{5}}{120} + \cdots$, so $\
frac{x - \sin x}{x^{3}} \to \frac{1}{6}$; the integrand is bounded, hence integr
able near $0$.

As $x \to +\infty$: $\left|\frac{x - \sin x}{x^{3}}\right| \le \frac{x + 1}{x^{3
}} \sim \frac{1}{x^{2}}$, which is integrable. So $I$ converges (absolutely, in
fact).

---

### Step 2. Introduce a parameter

For $a \ge 0$ define
$$I(a) := \int_{0}^{+\infty} \frac{a x - \sin(a x)}{x^{3}}\,dx, \qquad I(0) = 0.
$$
We want $I = I(1)$.

**Scaling check.** Substituting $u = a x$ ($a > 0$):
$$I(a) = \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{u - \sin u}{(u/a)^{3}}\,\frac{du}{a} = a^{2}\in
t_{0}^{\infty}\frac{u - \sin u}{u^{3}}\,du = a^{2} I(1).$$
So $I(a) = a^{2}I(1)$; in particular $I(a)$ is $C^{1}$ in $a$ and $I'(0)=0$.

---

### Step 3. Differentiate under the integral

Formally,
$$I'(a) = \int_{0}^{+\infty} \frac{1 - \cos(a x)}{x^{2}}\,dx.$$

**Justification.** The difference quotient is dominated: for $a \in [0, A]$,
$$\left|\frac{(a+h)x - \sin((a+h)x) - (ax - \sin(ax))}{h\, x^{3}}\right| = \left
|\frac{1 - \cos(\xi x)}{x^{2}}\right| \le \frac{1}{x^{2}} \in L^{1}([1,\infty)),
$$
and near $0$ the integrand $\frac{1-\cos(ax)}{x^{2}} \le \frac{a^{2}}{2}$ is bou
nded. So by dominated convergence, $I'(a) = \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos(ax)}{x
^{2}}\,dx$ is valid (and continuous in $a$).

---

### Step 4. Evaluate $I'(a)$ using the Dirichlet integral

For $a > 0$, substitute $u = a x$:
$$I'(a) = a\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1 - \cos u}{u^{2}}\,du.$$

Now compute $J := \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1 - \cos u}{u^{2}}\,du$ by integration
by parts. Take $f = 1 - \cos u$, $g' = u^{-2}$ (so $g = -1/u$):
$$J = \left[-\frac{1-\cos u}{u}\right]_{0}^{\infty} + \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\si
n u}{u}\,du.$$

Boundary terms: at $u\to\infty$, $\frac{1-\cos u}{u}\to 0$ (bounded numerator);
at $u\to 0$, $1-\cos u \sim u^{2}/2$, so $\frac{1-\cos u}{u}\sim \frac{u}{2}\to
0$. Both vanish.

The remaining integral is the Dirichlet integral with $\alpha = 1$:
$$\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin u}{u}\,du = \frac{\pi}{2}\operatorname{sgn}(1) = \
frac{\pi}{2}.$$

Hence $J = \frac{\pi}{2}$, and
$$I'(a) = a \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2}\,a, \qquad a > 0.$$

By continuity this also gives $I'(0) = 0$, consistent with Step 2.

---

### Step 5. Integrate back

$$I(a) = \int_{0}^{a} I'(t)\,dt + I(0) = \int_{0}^{a}\frac{\pi}{2}\,t\,dt = \fra
c{\pi}{4}\,a^{2}.$$

Setting $a = 1$:
$$\boxed{I = \int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{x - \sin x}{x^{3}}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{4}.}$$

---

**Sanity check (scaling consistency).** From Step 2, $I(a) = a^{2}I(1)$; from St
ep 5, $I(a) = \frac{\pi}{4}a^{2}$. These agree iff $I(1) = \frac{\pi}{4}$. ✓

Numerically, $\pi/4 \approx 0.7854$, and near $0$ the integrand $\approx 1/6$ wh
