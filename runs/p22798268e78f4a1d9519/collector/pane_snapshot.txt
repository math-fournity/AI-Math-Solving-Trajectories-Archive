# Dirichlet Series of the Möbius Function

## Statement

The Dirichlet series of the Möbius function $\mu(n)$ is

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\mu(n)}{n^s} = \frac{1}{\zeta(s)},$$

and it converges (absolutely) for $\Re(s) > 1$.

---

## Proof

### Step 1. Absolute convergence for $\Re(s)>1$

Since $|\mu(n)| \le 1$ for every $n \ge 1$,

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left|\frac{\mu(n)}{n^s}\right| \le \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \f
rac{1}{n^{\Re(s)}}.$$

The right-hand side is the ordinary $p$-series, which converges precisely when $
\Re(s) > 1$. Hence the Dirichlet series of $\mu$ converges absolutely for $\Re(s
) > 1$.

### Step 2. The Euler product of $\zeta(s)$

For $\Re(s) > 1$, the Riemann zeta function has the absolutely convergent Euler
product

$$\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^s} = \prod_{p} \frac{1}{1-p^{-s}},$$

where the product runs over all primes $p$. Each factor is a geometric series $\
sum_{k\ge 0} p^{-ks}$, and absolute convergence justifies rearranging the produc
t into the sum.

### Step 3. Reciprocal via the Euler product

Since each factor $(1-p^{-s})$ is nonzero for $\Re(s)>1$ (as $|p^{-s}|<1$), and
the product converges absolutely, $\zeta(s)\neq 0$ for $\Re(s)>1$, and

$$\frac{1}{\zeta(s)} = \prod_{p} (1 - p^{-s}).$$

Expanding each factor $(1-p^{-s})$ and using absolute convergence to multiply ou
t the product term-by-term, a typical term is

$$\prod_{p \in S} (-p^{-s}) = (-1)^{|S|} \prod_{p\in S} p^{-s} = (-1)^{|S|}\left
(\prod_{p\in S} p\right)^{-s},$$

where $S$ is a finite set of primes. The integer $n = \prod_{p\in S} p$ is squar
efree, and $(-1)^{|S|} = \mu(n)$. Every squarefree $n$ arises uniquely this way
(by the fundamental theorem of arithmetic), and no squarefree $n$ is missed. The
refore

$$\prod_{p}(1-p^{-s}) = \sum_{\substack{n\ge 1 \\ n \text{ squarefree}}} \frac{\
mu(n)}{n^s}.$$

Since $\mu(n) = 0$ whenever $n$ is not squarefree, we may extend the sum over al
l positive integers:

$$\frac{1}{\zeta(s)} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\mu(n)}{n^s}, \qquad \Re(s) > 1
.$$

### Step 4. Divergence for $\Re(s) \le 1$

We show the series does **not** converge (even conditionally) on the line $\Re(s
)=1$ at $s=1$, which already indicates the boundary is sharp. Consider the parti
al sums of $\mu$:

$$M(x) = \sum_{n \le x} \mu(n).$$

If $\sum_{n\ge 1} \mu(n)/n$ converged, then by summation by parts (Abel's summat
ion formula) the partial sums $M(x)$ would satisfy $M(x) = o(x)$, and in fact th
e convergence of $\sum \mu(n)/n$ is equivalent (via the standard Tauberian argum
ent) to the Prime Number Theorem in the form $M(x) = o(x)$. More directly, one c
an argue as follows.

Suppose $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \mu(n)/n^s$ converged at some point with $\Re(s)\le
 1$. By a classical theorem on Dirichlet series (the **abscissa of convergence**
 theorem): if a Dirichlet series $\sum a_n n^{-s}$ converges at a point $s_0$, t
hen it converges in the half-plane $\Re(s) > \Re(s_0)$. Moreover, the abscissa o
f absolute convergence $\sigma_a$ and the abscissa of (ordinary) convergence $\s
igma_c$ satisfy $\sigma_a - \sigma_c \le 1$.

For our series, $\sigma_a = 1$ (Step 1 shows absolute convergence for $\Re(s)>1$
, and divergence of $\sum |\mu(n)|/n^{\sigma}$ for $\sigma \le 1$ follows from $
\sum_{n \text{ squarefree}} 1/n^{\sigma} = \prod_p(1+p^{-\sigma})$ diverging sin
ce $\sum_p p^{-\sigma}$ diverges for $\sigma \le 1$).

A sharper fact pins $\sigma_c = 1$ exactly. The identity $\sum \mu(n) n^{-s} = 1
/\zeta(s)$, valid for $\Re(s)>1$, shows that $1/\zeta(s)$ is the analytic contin
uation of the Dirichlet series into $\Re(s)>1$. The function $1/\zeta(s)$ has a
**pole at $s=1$** (since $\zeta$ has a simple pole there with residue $1$). A Di
richlet series represents a holomorphic function in its half-plane of convergenc
e $\Re(s)>\sigma_c$; if $\sigma_c < 1$, then $1/\zeta(s)$ would be holomorphic a
t $s=1$, contradicting the pole. Hence $\sigma_c \ge 1$, and combined with $\sig
ma_c \le \sigma_a = 1$, we get $\sigma_c = 1$.

Therefore the series **diverges** for every $s$ with $\Re(s) \le 1$ (in particul
ar it cannot converge at any point strictly inside that half-plane, since conver
gence at one point would force convergence in a half-plane reaching past $\Re(s)
=1$, again contradicting the pole).

### Conclusion

$$\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\mu(n)}{n^s} = \frac{1}{\zeta(s)}, \qquad \te
xt{converging (absolutely) for } \Re(s) > 1.}$$

The region of convergence is exactly the half-plane $\Re(s) > 1$; on and to the
left of the line $\Re(s)=1$ the series diverges, because $1/\zeta(s)$ has a pole
 at $s=1$ and a Dirichlet series is holomorphic throughout its open half-plane o
f convergence.
