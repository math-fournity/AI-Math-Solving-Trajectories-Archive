## Proof

Let $G$ be a finite group of order $n$, and let $\phi: G \hookrightarrow S_G \co
ng S_n$ be the right regular (Cayley) action $\phi(g) = [x \mapsto xg]$.

Let $g \in G$ be an element of order $2$, i.e., $g \neq e$ and $g^2 = e$.

**Step 1: $\phi(g)$ has no fixed points.**

A fixed point of $\phi(g)$ is an element $x \in G$ with $xg = x$, which forces $
g = e$. Since $g$ has order $2$, $g \neq e$, so $\phi(g)$ has no fixed points.

**Step 2: $\phi(g)$ is a product of $n/2$ disjoint transpositions.**

Since $g^2 = e$, applying $\phi(g)$ twice gives $\phi(g)^2(x) = (xg)g = xg^2 = x
$, so $\phi(g)^2 = \mathrm{id}$. Thus every cycle of $\phi(g)$ has length $1$ or
 $2$. By Step 1 there are no $1$-cycles (fixed points), so $\phi(g)$ is a produc
t of disjoint $2$-cycles.

By Lagrange's theorem, $2 \mid n$ (since $g$ has order $2$), so $n$ is even. The
 $n$ elements of $G$ are partitioned into $2$-element orbits $\{x, xg\}$, giving
 exactly $n/2$ transpositions:
$$\phi(g) = \prod_{i=1}^{n/2} (a_i\, b_i), \quad \text{disjoint.}$$

**Step 3: Compute the sign.**

The sign of a transposition is $-1$, and the sign is a homomorphism, so
$$\operatorname{sgn}(\phi(g)) = (-1)^{n/2}.$$

**Step 4: Conclude.**

By definition, $\phi(g) \in A_n$ if and only if $\operatorname{sgn}(\phi(g)) = +
1$, i.e.,
$$(-1)^{n/2} = 1 \iff \frac{n}{2} \text{ is even} \iff 4 \mid n.$$

Therefore, $\phi(g) \in A_n$ if and only if $4 \mid n$. $\blacksquare$
