## 1. The Three-Body Problem as a First-Order ODE System

Consider three point masses $m_1, m_2, m_3$ with positions $\mathbf{r}_i \in \ma
thbb{R}^3$ and velocities $\mathbf{v}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$. Newtonian gravity giv
es

$$
m_i \, \ddot{\mathbf{r}}_i \;=\; \sum_{j \neq i} \frac{G\, m_i m_j}{\lVert \math
bf{r}_j - \mathbf{r}_i \rVert^3}\,(\mathbf{r}_j - \mathbf{r}_i), \qquad i = 1,2,
3.
$$

Introducing the state vector

$$
\mathbf{y} \;=\; (\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2, \mathbf{r}_3, \mathbf{v}_1, \mathb
f{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{18},
$$

the system takes the standard first-order form

$$
\dot{\mathbf{y}} \;=\; \mathbf{f}(t, \mathbf{y}),
$$

where $\mathbf{f}$ is smooth (in fact real-analytic) on the open set $\Omega = \
{\mathbf{y} : \mathbf{r}_i \neq \mathbf{r}_j \text{ for } i \neq j\}$, i.e., awa
y from collisions.

## 2. RK4 Is Applicable to Any Smooth First-Order ODE System

The classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method (RK4) is defined for a system $\do
t{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{f}(t,\mathbf{y})$ with step size $h$ by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{k}_1 &= \mathbf{f}(t_n,\, \mathbf{y}_n), \\
\mathbf{k}_2 &= \mathbf{f}\!\bigl(t_n + \tfrac{h}{2},\, \mathbf{y}_n + \tfrac{h}
{2}\mathbf{k}_1\bigr), \\
\mathbf{k}_3 &= \mathbf{f}\!\bigl(t_n + \tfrac{h}{2},\, \mathbf{y}_n + \tfrac{h}
{2}\mathbf{k}_2\bigr), \\
\mathbf{k}_4 &= \mathbf{f}(t_n + h,\, \mathbf{y}_n + h\,\mathbf{k}_3), \\
\mathbf{y}_{n+1} &= \mathbf{y}_n + \tfrac{h}{6}\bigl(\mathbf{k}_1 + 2\mathbf{k}_
2 + 2\mathbf{k}_3 + \mathbf{k}_4\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$

**Theorem (RK4 local truncation error).** If $\mathbf{f}$ is $C^5$ in a neighbor
hood of the solution, then the local truncation error of RK4 is $O(h^5)$ and the
 global error over a fixed interval $[0,T]$ is $O(h^4)$.

Since $\mathbf{f}$ for the three-body problem is real-analytic (hence $C^\infty$
) on $\Omega$, **the hypotheses of the RK4 convergence theorem are satisfied**.
Therefore:

> **RK4 can be applied to the three-body problem, and its numerical solution con
verges to the true solution at fourth-order accuracy on any finite time interval
 $[0,T]$, provided the trajectory stays away from collisions.**

This establishes applicability in the rigorous sense: the method is well-defined
, the iterates exist, and they converge to the true solution as $h \to 0$.

## 3. Qualitative Limitations (Why "Applicable" ≠ "Satisfactory")

Despite being formally applicable, RK4 suffers from three well-known deficiencie
s when applied to the three-body problem:

### 3.1 Chaos and Exponential Error Growth

The three-body problem is **chaotic**: it possesses a positive Lyapunov exponent
 $\lambda > 0$. Any error $\delta\mathbf{y}(0)$ — whether from finite precision,
 truncation, or roundoff — grows as

$$
\lVert \delta\mathbf{y}(t) \rVert \;\sim\; \lVert \delta\mathbf{y}(0) \rVert \,
e^{\lambda t}.
$$

Consequently, the **predictability horizon** is finite: $t_{\text{pred}} \sim \l
ambda^{-1} \log(1/\varepsilon)$ for initial error $\varepsilon$. Beyond this hor
izon, the computed trajectory bears no resemblance to the true one. This is a pr
operty of the *problem*, not of RK4 — **no numerical method** (including symplec
tic or high-order ones) can overcome it. RK4's $O(h^4)$ accuracy delays but cann
ot eliminate the divergence.

### 3.2 Non-Symplecticity and Energy Drift

The three-body system is **Hamiltonian** with conserved total energy

$$
H = \sum_{i} \tfrac{1}{2} m_i \lVert \mathbf{v}_i \rVert^2 - \sum_{i<j} \frac{G\
, m_i m_j}{\lVert \mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j \rVert}.
$$

RK4 is **not a symplectic integrator**: it does not preserve the symplectic stru
cture of Hamiltonian flow. Over long integration times, RK4 exhibits a **secular
 (systematic) drift** in $H$ — the energy error grows roughly linearly with the
number of steps, rather than remaining bounded as it does for symplectic methods
 (leapfrog/Verlet, implicit midpoint, etc.). For long-term studies of the three-
body problem (e.g., stability of hierarchical triples), this drift renders RK4 u
nreliable.

### 3.3 Fixed Step Size and Close Encounters

Standard RK4 uses a **fixed step size** $h$. The three-body problem features **c
lose encounters** (small $\lVert \mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j \rVert$) where the
force diverges as $1/r^2$, requiring arbitrarily small steps for accuracy. A fix
ed $h$ either wastes computation during quiescent phases or fails catastrophical
ly during close approaches. Adaptive RK4 (RK4(5), a.k.a. RKF45 / Dormand–Prince)
 mitigates this but still inherits the non-symplectic energy drift of §3.2.

For **short-term, high-accuracy** integration, RK4 is perfectly adequate. For **
long-term** integration, symplectic methods are strongly preferred. For **event-
driven** problems (close encounters, ejections, collisions), adaptive or regular
ized methods (KS regularization, chain regularization) are preferred.

## 5. Conclusion

**Determination: RK4 can be applied to the three-body problem.** The governing e
quations form a smooth first-order ODE system $\dot{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{f}(t,\
mathbf{y})$ on the collision-free domain $\Omega$, satisfying the $C^5$ regulari
ty required by the RK4 convergence theorem. The method produces fourth-order-acc
urate approximations that converge to the true solution on any finite interval $
[0,T]$ as $h \to 0$.

However, three caveats limit its practical utility:

1. **Chaos** (positive Lyapunov exponent) bounds the predictability horizon for
*any* method, including RK4.
2. **Non-symplecticity** causes secular energy drift over long integration times
, making RK4 unsuitable for long-term dynamical studies where symplectic integra
tors are preferred.
3. **Fixed step size** (in standard RK4) is inefficient for the multiscale dynam
ics of close encounters, motivating adaptive or regularized alternatives.

Thus RK4 is **applicable in principle and effective for short-term integration**
, but **not the method of choice for long-term or high-fidelity studies** of the
 three-body problem.
