**Answer: No.** I'll construct a counterexample.

## Proof

**Setup.** An outer function on $\mathbb{D}$ has the form
$$f(z) = \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} \frac{e^{i\theta}+z}{e^{i\theta
}-z}\,\log w(\theta)\,d\theta\right)$$
with $w \geq 0$, $\log w \in L^1$. Then $|f(z)| = e^{P[\log w](z)}$ (Poisson int
egral), and $|f^*(e^{i\theta})| = w(\theta)$ a.e. A bounded outer function $f \i
n H^\infty$ is **invertible** in $H^\infty$ iff $1/f \in H^\infty$, iff $w$ is b
ounded away from $0$ a.e. (i.e., $\operatorname{ess\,inf} w > 0$). Thus **non-in
vertible** means $\operatorname{ess\,inf} w = 0$.

**Key observation.** Non-invertibility requires $w$ to be arbitrarily small on s
ets of positive measure, but $w$ can still be **strictly positive everywhere**.
If $w > 0$ everywhere and $\log w$ is sufficiently regular, then $|f^*| = w > 0$
 wherever the radial limit exists, so $f$ has no radial limit of $0$.

**Counterexample.** Define $g:[0,2\pi)\to[0,\infty)$ as a step function. For eac
h $n \geq 1$, let $I_n$ be an interval of length $\ell_n = 2^{-n}/n^2$ centered
at $\theta_n = \pi/n$, with the $I_n$ pairwise disjoint (possible since $\sum \e
ll_n < \infty$ and the centers are distinct with $\ell_n$ decreasing exponential
ly). Set
$$g(\theta) = \begin{cases} n & \text{if } \theta \in I_n, \\ 0 & \text{otherwis
e.} \end{cases}$$

**Properties of $g$:**
- $g \in L^1$: $\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi} g = \sum_{n=1}^\infty n\cdot\ell_n =
\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{2^{-n}}{n} < \infty.$
- $g \notin L^\infty$: $g = n$ on $I_n$ with $n\to\infty$.
- $g(\theta) < \infty$ for every $\theta$: disjointness gives each point lies in
 at most one $I_n$.
- The only accumulation point of the $I_n$ is $\theta = 0$ (since $\theta_n = \p
i/n \to 0$), and $g(0)=0$.

**Define the outer function** with $w = e^{-g}$, so $\log w = -g \in L^1$:
$$f(z) = \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} \frac{e^{i\theta}+z}{e^{i\theta
}-z}\,(-g(\theta))\,d\theta\right).$$

**$f$ is bounded:** Since $g \geq 0$, we have $P[g](z) \geq 0$, so $|f(z)| = e^{
-P[g](z)} \leq 1$.

**$f$ is non-invertible in $H^\infty$:** The reciprocal $1/f$ is outer with weig
ht $e^g$. Since $g \notin L^\infty$, we have $e^g \notin L^\infty$, so $1/f \not
in H^\infty$. Equivalently, $\operatorname{ess\,inf} w = e^{-\operatorname{ess\,
sup} g} = 0$.

**$f$ has no radial limit of $0$ anywhere.** We show $|f^*(e^{i\theta})| > 0$ at
 every $\theta$ where the radial limit exists.

Since $g$ is piecewise constant with finitely many discontinuities in any compac
t subinterval of $(0,2\pi)$, the Poisson integral $P[g](re^{i\theta})$ converges
 as $r\to 1^-$ for every $\theta \in (0,2\pi)$: to $g(\theta)$ at continuity poi
nts, and to $\frac{g(\theta^+)+g(\theta^-)}{2}$ at jump points. Both are finite
since $g(\theta) < \infty$ everywhere.

At the accumulation point $\theta = 0$: we prove $P[g](r) \to 0$. For $|\theta|
\leq \pi$ and $r \geq 1/2$, using $1-\cos\theta \geq 2\theta^2/\pi^2$:
$$P_r(\theta) = \frac{1-r^2}{(1-r)^2 + 2r(1-\cos\theta)} \leq \frac{2\delta}{\de
lta^2 + 2\theta^2/\pi^2} \leq \frac{\pi^2\delta}{\theta^2}, \quad \delta = 1-r.$
$

For $\theta \in I_n$ (centered at $\pi/n$, length $2^{-n}/n^2$), we have $\theta
 \geq \pi/(2n)$ for all sufficiently large $n$, so $\theta^2 \geq \pi^2/(4n^2)$.
 Thus:
$$P[g](r) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{n=1}^\infty n\int_{I_n} P_r(\theta)\,d\theta \le
q \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{n=1}^\infty n\cdot\ell_n\cdot\frac{4n^2\delta}{\pi} = \fra
c{2\delta}{\pi^2}\sum_{n=1}^\infty n\cdot 2^{-n} = \frac{4\delta}{\pi}.$$

(The last step uses $\sum_{n=1}^\infty n\,2^{-n} = 2$.) Therefore $P[g](r) \leq
\frac{4(1-r)}{\pi} \to 0$ as $r\to 1^-$, giving $|f^*(1)| = e^0 = 1$.

**Conclusion.** At every $\theta \in [0,2\pi)$, the radial limit of $|f|$ exists
 and equals $e^{-g(\theta)} > 0$. By Fatou's theorem, the radial limit $f^*(e^{i
\theta})$ exists for a.e. $\theta$, and at every such point $|f^*(e^{i\theta})|
= e^{-g(\theta)} > 0$. Hence $f^*(e^{i\theta}) \neq 0$ wherever the radial limit
 exists.

The function $f$ is a non-invertible bounded outer function with **no** radial l
imit of $0$ anywhere. $\blacksquare$
