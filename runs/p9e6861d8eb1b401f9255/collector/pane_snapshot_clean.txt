**Answer: No.** Not every continuous function $f:\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{Q}$ can be
 extended to a continuous function $F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$.

**Counterexample.** Define
$$f:\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{Q},\qquad f(q)=\frac{1}{q^{2}-2}.$$

**Step 1: $f$ is well-defined and maps $\mathbb{Q}$ to $\mathbb{Q}$.**
For $q\in\mathbb{Q}$, we have $q^{2}\in\mathbb{Q}$, so $q^{2}-2\in\mathbb{Q}$. S
ince $\sqrt{2}\notin\mathbb{Q}$, we have $q^{2}\neq 2$ for every $q\in\mathbb{Q}
$, hence $q^{2}-2\neq 0$. Therefore $f(q)=1/(q^{2}-2)\in\mathbb{Q}$.

**Step 2: $f$ is continuous on $\mathbb{Q}$.**
The function $g(x)=x^{2}-2$ is a polynomial, hence continuous on $\mathbb{R}$, a
nd its restriction to $\mathbb{Q}$ is continuous. Since $g(q)\neq 0$ for all $q\
in\mathbb{Q}$, the reciprocal $1/g$ is continuous on the open set $\mathbb{R}\se
tminus\{\pm\sqrt{2}\}$, and in particular its restriction to $\mathbb{Q}$ is con
tinuous (with respect to the subspace topology on $\mathbb{Q}$). Thus $f$ is con
tinuous.

**Step 3: $f$ admits no continuous extension $F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$.**
Suppose, for contradiction, that $F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is continuous and $
F|_{\mathbb{Q}}=f$. Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$ and $F$ is conti
nuous, $F$ is uniquely determined: for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$,
$$F(x)=\lim_{\substack{q\to x\\ q\in\mathbb{Q}}} f(q).$$
In particular, at $x=\sqrt{2}$, we must have
$$F(\sqrt{2})=\lim_{\substack{q\to\sqrt{2}\\ q\in\mathbb{Q}}}\frac{1}{q^{2}-2}.$
$
Take a sequence of rationals $q_{n}\to\sqrt{2}$ with $q_{n}>\sqrt{2}$ (e.g., the
 decimal truncations from above, which are rational). Then $q_{n}^{2}-2\to 0^{+}
$, so
$$f(q_{n})=\frac{1}{q_{n}^{2}-2}\to +\infty.$$
Hence $\lim_{q\to\sqrt{2},\,q\in\mathbb{Q}} f(q)$ does not exist as a finite rea
l number. But $F(\sqrt{2})$ must be a finite real number (since $F:\mathbb{R}\to
\mathbb{R}$), and continuity forces $F(\sqrt{2})$ to equal this limit. This is a
 contradiction.

Therefore no continuous extension $F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ of $f$ exists.

**Conclusion.** There exist continuous functions $\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{Q}$ that
cannot be extended to continuous functions $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$. The obstru
ction is that a continuous function on $\mathbb{Q}$ need not have finite limits
at irrational points (it can blow up near an irrational where the defining expre
ssion has a "pole"), whereas any continuous extension to $\mathbb{R}$ would be f
orced—by density of $\mathbb{Q}$ and continuity—to agree with those limits.
