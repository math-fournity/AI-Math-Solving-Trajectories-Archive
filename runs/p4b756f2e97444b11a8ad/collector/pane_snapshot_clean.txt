## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, such a function exists.

**Example:** Consider $f(x) = -e^{-x}$, defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

**Verification of the three required properties:**

**1. Behavior as $x \to -\infty$ (starts at negative infinity):**

As $x \to -\infty$, we have $-x \to +\infty$, so $e^{-x} \to +\infty$, and there
fore
$$f(x) = -e^{-x} \to -\infty.$$

**2. Behavior as $x \to +\infty$ (approaches zero):**

As $x \to +\infty$, we have $-x \to -\infty$, so $e^{-x} \to 0$, and therefore
$$f(x) = -e^{-x} \to 0.$$

**3. Infinitely many non-zero derivatives:**

The function $f(x) = -e^{-x}$ is smooth ($C^\infty$) on $\mathbb{R}$. Computing
its derivatives:

$$f'(x) = e^{-x}, \quad f''(x) = -e^{-x}, \quad f'''(x) = e^{-x}, \quad f^{(4)}(
x) = -e^{-x}, \dots$$

By induction, for every $n \geq 0$:
$$f^{(n)}(x) = (-1)^{n+1}\, e^{-x}.$$

Since $e^{-x} > 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, each derivative $f^{(n)}$ is a no
n-zero function (it is not identically zero). This holds for **every** $n \geq 0
$, so $f$ has infinitely many non-zero derivatives. $\square$

**Conclusion:** The function $f(x) = -e^{-x}$ satisfies all three conditions sim
ultaneously: it tends to $-\infty$ as $x \to -\infty$, tends to $0$ as $x \to +\
infty$, and possesses infinitely many non-zero derivatives. Therefore, such a fu
nction exists.
