## Proof

**Answer: Yes, such a strictly increasing function $T:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$ e
xists for every irrational $x$.**

Let $x$ be irrational, and let $\frac{p_k}{q_k}$ ($k=0,1,2,\dots$) be the conver
gents of its (infinite) continued fraction expansion. We use three standard fact
s:

1. **Strict growth:** $q_0 < q_1 < q_2 < \cdots$ (the denominators are strictly
increasing).
2. **Convergence rate:** $\left|x - \frac{p_k}{q_k}\right| < \frac{1}{q_k q_{k+1
}}$, hence
$$|\varepsilon_k| := |q_k x - p_k| < \frac{1}{q_{k+1}} \to 0 \quad\text{as } k\t
o\infty.$$
3. **Sign alternation:** The convergents are alternately below and above $x$, so
 the errors $\varepsilon_k = q_k x - p_k$ alternate in sign: $\varepsilon_k\,\va
repsilon_{k+1} < 0$ for all $k$.

Since the signs alternate, infinitely many of the $\varepsilon_k$ are **positive
**. Let $k_1 < k_2 < k_3 < \cdots$ be the (infinite) subsequence of indices with
 $\varepsilon_{k_n} > 0$, i.e.
$$q_{k_n} x - p_{k_n} > 0.$$

For these indices the fractional part is exactly the error:
$$\{q_{k_n} x\} = q_{k_n} x - p_{k_n} = \varepsilon_{k_n}.$$

By fact 2,
$$0 < \{q_{k_n} x\} = \varepsilon_{k_n} < \frac{1}{q_{k_n+1}} \to 0.$$

Now define $T(n) := q_{k_n}$. Since $k_1 < k_2 < \cdots$ and the full sequence $
q_k$ is strictly increasing (fact 1), the subsequence $T(n) = q_{k_n}$ is strict
ly increasing. And we have just shown
$$\{T(n)\,x\} = \{q_{k_n} x\} \to 0 \quad\text{as } n\to\infty.$$

Thus $T:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$ is strictly increasing and satisfies $\{T(n)x\}
\to 0$. $\blacksquare$
