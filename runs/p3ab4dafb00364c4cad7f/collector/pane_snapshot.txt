## Solution

Let $\alpha = 1+\sqrt{3}$ and $\beta = 1/\alpha = (\sqrt{3}-1)/2 \approx 0.366$.
 Note $\alpha$ is irrational, hence so is $\beta$.

**Reduction.** For fixed $n$, writing $p = n-q$ (with $q \in \{0,1,\dots,n\}$, o
r $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$ if $\mathbb{N}$ starts at $1$ — this makes no difference to
the supremum as shown below),

$$
\min_{p+q=n}|p - q\sqrt{3}| = \min_{q}|n - q(1+\sqrt{3})| = \min_{q}|n - q\alpha
| = \alpha\left\|\frac{n}{\alpha}\right\| = \alpha\,\|n\beta\|,
$$

where $\|x\| = \min_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}|x-m|$ is the distance to the nearest intege
r, and the minimizer is $q^* = \lfloor n\beta + \tfrac12\rfloor$ (nearest intege
r to $n\beta$).

**The minimizer is admissible.** Since $\beta \approx 0.366 \in (0,1)$, for $n\g
e 2$ we have $0 < n\beta < n$, and in fact $n\beta < n-1$ for $n\ge 2$ (as $n(1-
\beta) = n\cdot\tfrac{3-\sqrt{3}}{2} > 1$ for $n\ge 2$). Thus $q^* \in \{1,\dots
,n-1\}$, so the constraint $p,q\ge 1$ (when applicable) is satisfied. Hence for
all relevant $n$,

$$
\min_{p+q=n}|p-q\sqrt{3}| = \alpha\,\|n\beta\|.
$$

**Evaluating the supremum.** By Kronecker's theorem (equidistribution of $\{n\be
ta\}$ for irrational $\beta$), the sequence $\{n\beta \bmod 1 : n\ge 1\}$ is den
se in $[0,1)$. The map $x\mapsto \|x\|$ sends $[0,1)$ continuously onto $[0,\tfr
ac12]$, with $\|x\|=\tfrac12$ only at $x=\tfrac12$. Since $\{n\bmod 1\}$ is dens
e, for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $n$ with $\{n\beta\}\in(\tfrac12-\vare
psilon,\,\tfrac12+\varepsilon)\setminus\{\tfrac12\}$, giving $\|n\beta\| > \tfra
c12 - \varepsilon$. Therefore

$$
\sup_{n\ge 1}\|n\beta\| = \tfrac12.
$$

The value $\tfrac12$ is **not attained**: $\|n\beta\|=\tfrac12$ would require $2
n\beta\in\mathbb{Z}$, i.e. $n(\sqrt{3}-1)\in\mathbb{Z}$, i.e. $n\sqrt{3}\in\math
bb{Z}$, impossible for $n\ge 1$ since $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational. So it is a genui
ne supremum, not a maximum.

**Conclusion.**

$$
\sup_{n\ge 1}\min_{\substack{p,q\in\mathbb{N}\\p+q=n}}|p-q\sqrt{3}| = \alpha\cdo
t\tfrac12 = \frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}.
$$

*Numerical check.* $\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}\approx 1.3660$. At $n=15$: $q^*=5$, $|1
5-5(1+\sqrt{3})|=|15-13.660|=1.340$. At $n=56$: $q^*=20$, $|56-20(1+\sqrt{3})|=|
56-54.641|=1.359$. The values approach $1.3660$ from below, consistent with a no
n-attained supremum. $\blacksquare$
