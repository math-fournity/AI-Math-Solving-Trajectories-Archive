## Proof

**Setup.** The Cauchy–Pompeiu formula for $f \in C^1(\bar\Omega)$ on a bounded d
omain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{C}$ with smooth boundary reads

$$f(z) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\partial\Omega}\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta
 - z}\,d\zeta}_{\text{first term}} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{\pi}\iint_\Omega \
frac{\partial f/\partial\bar\zeta\,(\zeta)}{\zeta - z}\,d\xi\,d\eta}_{\text{seco
nd term}}, \qquad z\in\Omega.$$

The second term has an integrable singularity at $\zeta = z$ (since $|\zeta-z|^{
-1}$ is locally integrable in $\mathbb{R}^2$: $\int_0^\varepsilon r^{-1}\cdot r\
,dr < \infty$). We explain why $\partial/\partial\bar z$ cannot be passed under
the integral here.

---

**Key computation: the difference quotient.** Fix $z\in\Omega$ and let $h\to 0$
in $\mathbb{C}$. The $\bar z$-difference quotient of the kernel is

$$\frac{1}{\bar h}\!\left(\frac{1}{\zeta-(z+h)} - \frac{1}{\zeta-z}\right) = \fr
ac{1}{\bar h}\cdot\frac{h}{(\zeta-z-h)(\zeta-z)} = \frac{h/\bar h}{(\zeta-z-h)(\
zeta-z)}.$$

Since $|h/\bar h|=1$, its modulus is

$$\left|\frac{h/\bar h}{(\zeta-z-h)(\zeta-z)}\right| = \frac{1}{|\zeta-z-h|\,|\z
eta-z|}.$$

---

**Pointwise limit.** For each fixed $\zeta\neq z$, as $h\to 0$,

$$\frac{1}{\bar h}\!\left(\frac{1}{\zeta-(z+h)}-\frac{1}{\zeta-z}\right) \;\long
rightarrow\; \frac{\partial}{\partial\bar z}\frac{1}{\zeta-z} = 0,$$

because $z\mapsto(\zeta-z)^{-1}$ is holomorphic (hence $\bar z$-independent) awa
y from $z=\zeta$.

---

**Why dominated convergence fails.** To interchange $\partial/\partial\bar z$ wi
th the integral, the standard Leibniz rule (via dominated convergence) requires
a **single $L^1$ function of $\zeta$** dominating the difference quotients for a
ll small $h$. The natural candidate is the pointwise limit of the moduli:

$$\frac{1}{|\zeta-z-h|\,|\zeta-z|} \;\xrightarrow{h\to 0}\; \frac{1}{|\zeta-z|^2
}.$$

But $\frac{1}{|\zeta-z|^2}$ is **not locally integrable** in two real dimensions
:

$$\int_{|\zeta-z|<\varepsilon}\frac{d\xi\,d\eta}{|\zeta-z|^2} = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\
!\int_0^\varepsilon \frac{r\,dr\,d\theta}{r^2} = 2\pi\int_0^\varepsilon\frac{dr}
{r} = +\infty.$$

Therefore **no $L^1$ dominator exists**: the singularity of the $\bar z$-differe
nce quotient is of order $|\zeta-z|^{-2}$, which is one order too singular for 2
D integration. The hypotheses of the dominated convergence theorem are violated,
 so

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar z}\iint_\Omega \frac{g(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\,d\xi\,d\
eta \;\neq\; \iint_\Omega g(\zeta)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar z}\!\left(\frac
{1}{\zeta-z}\right)d\xi\,d\eta \;(=0), \qquad g:=\partial f/\partial\bar\zeta.$$

---

**Distributional confirmation.** The failure is not a technicality — it reflects
 a genuine distributional identity. In the sense of distributions,

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar z}\frac{1}{\zeta - z} = \pi\,\delta(\zeta - z),$$

so the correct $\bar z$-derivative of the second term is

$$-\frac{1}{\pi}\iint_\Omega g(\zeta)\,\pi\,\delta(\zeta-z)\,d\xi\,d\eta = -g(z)
 = -\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar z}(z).$$

Combined with the first (Cauchy) term, which is holomorphic in $z$ and hence has
 zero $\bar z$-derivative, differentiating the full Cauchy–Pompeiu formula gives

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar z}(z) = 0 - \left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial\b
ar z}(z)\right) \;\;\text{(tautology)}.$$

Had we (incorrectly) differentiated under the integral, we would have obtained $
0$ for the second term and concluded $\partial f/\partial\bar z \equiv 0$, i.e.
that every $C^1$ function is holomorphic — an absurdity. This confirms that the
interchange is genuinely invalid.

---

**Conclusion.** We cannot differentiate under the integral in the second Cauchy–
Pompeiu term because the $\bar z$-difference quotient of the kernel $1/(\zeta-z)
$ is of order $|\zeta-z|^{-2}$ near the singularity, which is **non-integrable**
 in two dimensions ($\int r^{-1}dr$ diverges logarithmically). Hence no dominati
ng $L^1$ function exists, the dominated convergence theorem does not apply, and
the Leibniz rule fails. The "missing" contribution is precisely the Dirac mass $
\pi\delta(\zeta-z)$, which is what makes the Cauchy–Pompeiu representation consi
stent.
