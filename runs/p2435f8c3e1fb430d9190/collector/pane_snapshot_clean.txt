## Proof

We show such homomorphisms exist, taking **n = m = 2**.

### Setup

Let $\mathrm{UT}(2,\mathbb{R}) = \left\{\begin{pmatrix}1 & a \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatri
x} : a \in \mathbb{R}\right\} \cong (\mathbb{R}, +)$ and $\mathrm{T}^*(2,\mathbb
{R}) = \left\{\begin{pmatrix}d_1 & a \\ 0 & d_2\end{pmatrix} : d_1, d_2 \in \mat
hbb{R}^\times,\, a \in \mathbb{R}\right\}$. Then $\mathrm{UT}(2,\mathbb{R}) \tri
anglelefteq \mathrm{T}^*(2,\mathbb{R})$, and conjugation by $\mathrm{diag}(d_1,
d_2)$ sends $\begin{pmatrix}1 & a \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix}1
 & \frac{d_1}{d_2}a \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$.

### Step 1: Any extendable injective homomorphism $\varphi: \mathrm{UT}(2,\mathb
b{R}) \to \mathrm{UT}(2,\mathbb{R})$ must be $\mathbb{R}$-linear.

Suppose $\varphi$ extends to $\Phi: \mathrm{T}^*(2,\mathbb{R}) \to \mathrm{T}^*(
2,\mathbb{R})$. Identifying $\mathrm{UT}(2,\mathbb{R})$ with $(\mathbb{R}, +)$,
$\varphi$ is an injective additive map $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$.

For $g = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda, 1) \in \mathrm{T}^*(2,\mathbb{R})$ and $u_a = \b
egin{pmatrix}1 & a \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, the relation $\Phi(g u_a g^{-1}) = \P
hi(g)\,\Phi(u_a)\,\Phi(g)^{-1}$ yields:

$$\varphi(\lambda a) = \rho(\lambda)\cdot \varphi(a) \quad \text{for all } a \in
 \mathbb{R},\; \lambda \in \mathbb{R}^\times,$$

where $\rho(\lambda)$ is the ratio of diagonal entries of $\Phi(\mathrm{diag}(\l
ambda, 1))$. (A direct computation shows conjugation by $\begin{pmatrix}e_1 & b
\\ 0 & e_2\end{pmatrix}$ on $\mathrm{UT}(2,\mathbb{R})$ acts as multiplication b
y $e_1/e_2$, independent of $b$.)

Set $c = \varphi(1) \neq 0$ (by injectivity) and define $\psi(x) = \varphi(x)/c$
. Then:
- $\psi(1) = 1$,
- $\psi$ is additive,
- $\psi(\lambda a) = \psi(\lambda)\,\psi(a)$ for all $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}^\ti
mes$, $a \in \mathbb{R}$.

Since $\psi(0) = 0$, multiplicativity extends to all $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$: $\ps
i(ab) = \psi(a)\psi(b)$. Thus $\psi$ is a **unital ring endomorphism** of $\math
bb{R}$.

**Claim:** Every unital ring endomorphism $\sigma: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is
 the identity. Indeed, $\sigma$ fixes $\mathbb{Q}$; it preserves squares ($\sigm
a(x^2) = \sigma(x)^2 \geq 0$), hence sends non-negative elements to non-negative
 elements, hence preserves order; by density of $\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{R}$ and
 order-preservation, $\sigma = \mathrm{id}$.

Therefore $\psi = \mathrm{id}$, so $\varphi(a) = ca$ for some $c \in \mathbb{R}^
\times$—that is, **$\varphi$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear**.

### Step 2: There exist injective additive maps $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ that
 are not $\mathbb{R}$-linear.

View $\mathbb{R}$ as a $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space. By the Axiom of Choice, $\math
bb{R}$ admits a Hamel basis $\mathcal{B}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ with $1, \sqrt{2} \i
n \mathcal{B}$. Define a $\mathbb{Q}$-linear map $\varphi: \mathbb{R} \to \mathb
b{R}$ by permuting basis elements:

$$\varphi(1) = \sqrt{2}, \quad \varphi(\sqrt{2}) = 1, \quad \varphi(b) = b \text
{ for } b \in \mathcal{B}\setminus\{1, \sqrt{2}\}.$$

This is a $\mathbb{Q}$-linear bijection, hence an injective group homomorphism $
(\mathbb{R}, +) \to (\mathbb{R}, +)$. But it is **not** $\mathbb{R}$-linear:

$$\varphi(\sqrt{2}\cdot 1) = \varphi(\sqrt{2}) = 1, \qquad \sqrt{2}\cdot\varphi(
1) = \sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{2} = 2 \neq 1.$$

### Conclusion

The map $\varphi$ defines an injective group homomorphism $\mathrm{UT}(2,\mathbb
{R}) \to \mathrm{UT}(2,\mathbb{R})$ that, by Step 1, **cannot** be extended to a
ny homomorphism $\mathrm{T}^*(2,\mathbb{R}) \to \mathrm{T}^*(2,\mathbb{R})$. Hen
ce such homomorphisms exist. $\blacksquare$
