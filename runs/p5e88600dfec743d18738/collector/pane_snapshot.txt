The statement is **false**. Here is a counterexample.

**Counterexample.** Let $X = \mathbb{Q}$ equipped with the subspace topology inh
erited from $\mathbb{R}$, and let $Y = \mathbb{Q} \setminus \{0\} \subseteq X$.

We verify the three required properties:

1. **$Y$ is countable.** $Y$ is a subset of $\mathbb{Q}$, hence countable.

2. **$Y \subsetneq X$ (so $X \neq Y$).** Clearly $0 \in X \setminus Y$, so $Y$ i
s a proper subset of $X$.

3. **$Y$ is homeomorphic to $X$.** This follows from Sierpiński's theorem: *ever
y countable metrizable space with no isolated points is homeomorphic to $\mathbb
{Q}$ (with its usual topology).*

   - $X = \mathbb{Q}$ is countable, metrizable (as a subspace of $\mathbb{R}$),
and has no isolated points: every neighbourhood of a rational $q$ contains infin
itely many other rationals.
   - $Y = \mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}$ is likewise countable and metrizable. It als
o has no isolated points: for any $y \in Y$ and any $\varepsilon > 0$, the inter
val $(y-\varepsilon, y+\varepsilon)$ contains infinitely many rationals differen
t from $0$ and from $y$, so $y$ is not isolated in $Y$.

   By Sierpiński's theorem, both $X$ and $Y$ are homeomorphic to $\mathbb{Q}$, a
nd hence (by transitivity) $Y \cong X$.

For completeness, here is the idea behind Sierpiński's theorem (so the proof is
self-contained). The standard proof goes via a back-and-forth construction: enum
erate $X = \{x_1, x_2, \dots\}$ and $\mathbb{Q} = \{q_1, q_2, \dots\}$. Inductiv
ely build a bijection $f\colon X \to \mathbb{Q}$ preserving order (after fixing
a countable clopen partition of $X$ into copies of $\mathbb{Q}$, which exists be
cause $X$ is countable, metrizable, and zero-dimensional — having no isolated po
ints forces a base of clopen sets). The order-preserving bijection between two c
opies of $\mathbb{Q}$ is automatically a homeomorphism. The "no isolated points"
 hypothesis is exactly what guarantees that the inductive construction never get
s stuck (there is always room to insert the next point on either side).

Thus we have a topological space $X$, a proper subset $Y \subsetneq X$ with $Y \
cong X$ and $Y$ countable, yet $X \neq Y$. The statement is therefore **false**.
