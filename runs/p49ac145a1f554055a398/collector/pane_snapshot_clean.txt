## Proof

**Answer: No.**

We exhibit a counterexample: the complete graph $K_5$.

**Setup.** Let $G = K_5$, which has $5$ vertices and $\binom{5}{2} = 10$ edges.
Suppose, for contradiction, that $E(G)$ can be partitioned into two edge-disjoin
t subgraphs $F$ and $C$, where $F$ is a forest and $C$ is a collection of vertex
-disjoint cycles.

**Bounding $|E(F)|$.** Since $F$ is a forest (acyclic) on at most $5$ vertices,
we have
$$|E(F)| \le |V(F)| - 1 \le 5 - 1 = 4.$$

**Bounding $|E(C)|$.** The subgraph $C$ is a disjoint union of cycles, with each
 cycle of length $\ell \ge 3$ using exactly $\ell$ vertices and $\ell$ edges. Si
nce the cycles are vertex-disjoint and use at most $5$ vertices total, the numbe
r of edges equals the number of vertices used:
$$|E(C)| = (\text{vertices in } C) \le 5.$$
Moreover, equality $|E(C)| = 5$ requires all $5$ vertices to lie in cycles. Sinc
e every cycle has length $\ge 3$ and $5$ cannot be written as a sum of integers
each $\ge 3$ in more than one way ($5 = 5$ or $5 = 3 + 2$, but $2 < 3$ is invali
d), the only possibility is a single $5$-cycle.

**Deriving the contradiction.** Combining the bounds:
$$|E(G)| = |E(F)| + |E(C)| \le 4 + 5 = 9.$$
But $|E(G)| = |E(K_5)| = 10 > 9$, a contradiction.

**Checking the edge case.** One might ask whether $|E(C)| = 5$ and $|E(F)| = 5$
could both hold, but $|E(F)| = 5$ on at most $5$ vertices already violates the f
orest bound $|E(F)| \le 4$. Similarly, $|E(C)| = 5$ forces a $5$-cycle using all
 vertices, leaving $F$ with $5$ edges on $5$ vertices — necessarily containing a
 cycle, contradicting that $F$ is a forest. Every other split gives an even smal
ler total.

**Conclusion.** $K_5$ cannot be decomposed into a forest and a collection of ver
tex-disjoint cycles. Therefore it is **not** true that every undirected finite g
raph admits such a decomposition.
