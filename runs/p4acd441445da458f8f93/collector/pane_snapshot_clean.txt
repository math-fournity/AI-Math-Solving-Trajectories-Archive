## Analysis

The right-hand side is the sum-of-divisors function $\sigma(n)$ where $n = p_1^{
k_1}\cdots p_6^{k_6}$ (assuming distinct primes, as is standard in such factoriz
ations). The inequality is:

$$2n < \sigma(n) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \frac{\sigma(n)}{n} > 2$$

The abundancy index is:

$$\frac{\sigma(n)}{n} = \prod_{i=1}^{6} \frac{1 - p_i^{-(k_i+1)}}{1 - p_i^{-1}}$
$

Each factor is **increasing** in $k_i$ and bounded above by $\frac{p_i}{p_i - 1}
$. The minimum over positive exponents occurs at $k_i = 1$, giving factor $\frac
{p_i+1}{p_i}$.

## Proof that the inequality does NOT hold for all choices

**Counterexample.** Take the six odd primes $p_1=5,\; p_2=7,\; p_3=11,\; p_4=13,
\; p_5=17,\; p_6=19$ with **any** positive exponents $k_1,\dots,k_6 \geq 1$.

For any positive exponent $k_i$, each factor satisfies:

$$\frac{1 - p_i^{-(k_i+1)}}{1 - p_i^{-1}} < \frac{1}{1 - p_i^{-1}} = \frac{p_i}{
p_i - 1}$$

Therefore:

$$\frac{\sigma(n)}{n} < \prod_{i=1}^{6} \frac{p_i}{p_i - 1} = \frac{5}{4}\cdot\f
rac{7}{6}\cdot\frac{11}{10}\cdot\frac{13}{12}\cdot\frac{17}{16}\cdot\frac{19}{18
}$$

Computing the numerator and denominator:

**Numerator:** $5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13 \cdot 17 \cdot 19 = 1{,}616{,}615$

**Denominator:** $4 \cdot 6 \cdot 10 \cdot 12 \cdot 16 \cdot 18 = 829{,}440$

Since $1{,}616{,}615 < 2 \times 829{,}440 = 1{,}658{,}880$, we have:

$$\prod_{i=1}^{6} \frac{p_i}{p_i - 1} = \frac{1{,}616{,}615}{829{,}440} < 2$$

Therefore, for **any** choice of positive exponents:

$$\frac{\sigma(n)}{n} < 2 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \sigma(n) < 2n \quad \Long
rightarrow \quad 2n > \sigma(n)$$

The inequality $2n < \sigma(n)$ **fails** for these primes regardless of the exp
onents chosen. $\square$

## The inequality does hold for some choices

For completeness, with primes $p_1=3,\; p_2=5,\; p_3=7,\; p_4=11,\; p_5=13,\; p_
6=17$ and $k_i=1$ for all $i$:

$$\frac{\sigma(n)}{n} = \frac{4}{3}\cdot\frac{6}{5}\cdot\frac{8}{7}\cdot\frac{12
}{11}\cdot\frac{14}{13}\cdot\frac{18}{17} = \frac{580{,}608}{255{,}255} \approx
2.274 > 2$$

since $580{,}608 > 2 \times 255{,}255 = 510{,}510$. So the inequality holds here
.

## Conclusion

**The inequality does not hold for all choices of six odd primes and their expon
ents.** Specifically, for the primes $\{5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19\}$, the abundancy i
ndex $\sigma(n)/n$ is strictly less than $2$ for **every** choice of positive ex
ponents, because even the limiting value $\prod \frac{p_i}{p_i-1} = \frac{1{,}61
6{,}615}{829{,}440} < 2$.

The key insight is that the abundancy index of a number with 6 odd prime factors
 can be made arbitrarily close to 1 by choosing sufficiently large primes, so it
 certainly can fall below 2. The inequality holds only when the primes are small
 enough (e.g., including 3 as a factor) that the product $\prod \frac{p_i}{p_i-1
}$ exceeds 2.
