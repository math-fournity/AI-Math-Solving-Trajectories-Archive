**Interpretation.** "Integrable" here means the improper Riemann integral conver
ges (conditional convergence allowed). This is the interpretation that makes the
 oscillatory factors $(-1)^{[x]}$, $\sin x$, $(-1)^{[1/x]}$ meaningful; for the
positive functions in (d),(e) it coincides with Lebesgue integrability. I use $[
x]=\lfloor x\rfloor$.

A key building block (used in (a) and (c)):

> **Lemma.** $\displaystyle I(q):=\int_1^{\infty} x^{q}\,(-1)^{[x]}\,dx$ converg
es $\iff q<0$.
>
> *Proof.* On $[n,n+1)$, $(-1)^{[x]}=(-1)^n$, so $I(q)=\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^n a_n$ w
ith $a_n=\int_n^{n+1}x^q dx$. If $q<0$, $x^q$ is decreasing, hence $a_n$ is decr
easing, and $0<a_n\le n^q\to0$; by Leibniz the series converges. If $q\ge0$, the
n $a_n\ge n^q\ge1$ (for $q\ge0$, $\int_n^{n+1}x^q dx\ge n^q\ge1$ when $q=0$, and
 $\to\infty$ when $q>0$), so $(-1)^n a_n\not\to0$ and the series diverges. $\squ
are$

### a) $f(x)=x^{-p}(-1)^{[x]}$ on $(1,+\\infty)$

This is $I(q)$ with $q=-p$. By the Lemma, convergence $\iff -p<0\iff p>0$.

**Answer: $p>0$.**

---

### b) $f(x)=x^{-p}\sin x$ on $(0,+\\infty)$

Split at $1$.

*Near $0$:* $\sin x\sim x$, so $x^{-p}\sin x\sim x^{1-p}$; $\int_0^1 x^{1-p}dx$
converges $\iff 1-p>-1\iff p<2$.

*Near $+\infty$:* Dirichlet's test. For $p>0$, $g(x)=x^{-p}\downarrow0$ and $\in
t_0^A\sin x\,dx=1-\cos A$ is bounded, so $\int_1^\infty x^{-p}\sin x\,dx$ conver
ges. For $p=0$, $\int_0^A\sin x\,dx=1-\cos A$ has no limit. For $p<0$ ($q=-p>0$)
, integration by parts gives $\int_1^A x^q\sin x\,dx=-x^q\cos x\big|_1^A+q\int_1
^A x^{q-1}\cos x\,dx$; the boundary term $-A^q\cos A$ has no limit, so it diverg
es.

Combining: $0<p<2$.

**Answer: $0<p<2$.**

### c) $f(x)=x^{-p}(-1)^{[1/x]}$ on $(0,1)$

(For $x\in(0,1)$, $[x]=0$ would make $1/[x]$ undefined; the intended reading is
$[1/x]$.) Substitute $u=1/x$, $dx=-du/u^2$:

$$\int_0^1 x^{-p}(-1)^{[1/x]}dx=\int_1^\infty u^{p}\,(-1)^{[u]}\,\frac{du}{u^2}=
\int_1^\infty u^{p-2}(-1)^{[u]}du=I(p-2).$$

By the Lemma this converges $\iff p-2<0\iff p<2$.

**Answer: $p<2$.**

### d) $f(x)=e^{-x^p\sin^2 x}$ on $(0,+\\infty)$

$f\ge0$, so this is absolute integrability. Since $x^p\sin^2 x\ge0$ for $x>0$, w
e have $0<f\le1$; thus $\int_0^1 f\,dx\le1<\infty$ — no constraint near $0$.

At $+\infty$: consider $p>0$ first. Partition $(1,\infty)$ into $I_k=[k\pi-\pi/2
,\,k\pi+\pi/2]$. On $I_k$, $x\asymp k$ so $x^p\asymp k^p$, and $\sin^2 x=\sin^2(
x-k\pi)$. The contribution is

$$J_k=\int_{I_k}e^{-x^p\sin^2 x}dx\asymp\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}e^{-c\,k^p\sin^2 t}
dt.$$

Using Jordan's inequality $(2t/\pi)^2\le\sin^2 t$ for $|t|\le\pi/2$: upper bound
 $\le\int e^{-c'k^p t^2}dt=Ck^{-p/2}$. Lower bound: on $|t|\le k^{-p/2}$, $\sin^
2 t\le t^2\le k^{-p}$, so the integrand $\ge e^{-c}$ and $J_k\ge C'k^{-p/2}$. He
nce $J_k\asymp k^{-p/2}$.

Away from the $I_k$ (i.e. where $|\sin x|\ge c$), $e^{-x^p\sin^2 x}\le e^{-c x^p
}$, whose integral over $(1,\infty)$ converges for $p>0$. Thus the whole tail be
haves like $\sum_k k^{-p/2}$, convergent $\iff p/2>1\iff p>2$.

For $p=0$: $f=e^{-\sin^2 x}$ is periodic with positive mean $\to\infty$. For $p<
0$: $x^p\to0$, so $x^p\sin^2 x\to0$ and $f\to1$, giving $\int=\infty$.

**Answer: $p>2$.**

### e) $f(x)=\dfrac{x^p}{1+x^6\sin^2 x}$ on $(0,+\\infty)$

$f\ge0$.

*Near $0$:* $\sin^2 x\sim x^2$, so $x^6\sin^2 x\sim x^8\to0$ and $f\sim x^p$; $\
int_0^1 x^p dx$ converges $\iff p>-1$.

*At $+\infty$:* partition into $I_k=[k\pi-\pi/2,k\pi+\pi/2]$. On $I_k$, $x\asymp
 k$, so

$$J_k=\int_{I_k}\frac{x^p}{1+x^6\sin^2 x}dx\asymp k^p\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{
dt}{1+c\,k^6\sin^2 t}\asymp k^p\int\frac{dt}{1+k^6 t^2}=k^p\cdot\frac{C}{k^3}=C\
,k^{p-3}.$$

(Justify: $\sin t\asymp t$ near $0$ and the integrand is concentrated where $k^3
|t|\lesssim1$; substituting $u=k^3 t$ gives $\asymp k^{-3}\int\frac{du}{1+u^2}$.
) So $\sum_k J_k$ converges $\iff p-3<-1\iff p<2$.

Away from $I_k$: $|\sin x|\ge c$, so $f\le x^p/(cx^6)=C x^{p-6}$, integrable $\i
ff p-6<-1\iff p<5$ — weaker than $p<2$.

Combining near-$0$ ($p>-1$) and near-zeros ($p<2$): $-1<p<2$.

**Answer: $-1<p<2$.**
