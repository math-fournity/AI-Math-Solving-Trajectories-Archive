## Proof

**Claim.** Yes. There exists a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ and a sequence $\{x_n
\}\subset\mathcal{H}$ that is weakly convergent but not convergent in the Hilber
t space norm.

**Construction.** Let $\mathcal{H}=\ell^2(\mathbb{N})$, the space of square-summ
able sequences, which is an infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert space with in
ner product
$$\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}x_k\overline{y_k}.$$
Let $e_n=(0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots)$ be the standard orthonormal basis vector with $1
$ in the $n$-th coordinate. We take $x_n=e_n$.

**Step 1: $\{e_n\}$ is weakly convergent to $0$.**

By the Riesz representation theorem, every continuous linear functional $f\in(\e
ll^2)^*$ has the form $f(y)=\langle y,z\rangle$ for a unique $z\in\ell^2$. There
fore
$$f(e_n)=\langle e_n,z\rangle = z_n.$$
Since $z\in\ell^2$, we have $\sum_{k=1}^\infty |z_k|^2<\infty$, which implies $z
_n\to 0$ as $n\to\infty$ (the terms of a convergent series tend to zero). Hence
$f(e_n)\to 0=f(0)$ for every $f\in(\ell^2)^*$, which is exactly the definition o
f $e_n\rightharpoonup 0$ weakly.

**Step 2: $\{e_n\}$ is not convergent in norm.**

We have $\|e_n\|=\sqrt{\langle e_n,e_n\rangle}=1$ for every $n$. If $\{e_n\}$ co
nverged in norm to some $x\in\ell^2$, then necessarily $\|x\|=1$ (norm is contin
uous). But norm convergence implies weak convergence to the same limit, so $x$ w
ould have to equal the weak limit $0$. This gives $\|x\|=0\neq 1$, a contradicti
on.

More directly: for $n\neq m$,
$$\|e_n-e_m\|^2=\langle e_n-e_m,e_n-e_m\rangle=\|e_n\|^2+\|e_m\|^2=2,$$
so $\|e_n-e_m\|=\sqrt{2}$ for all $n\neq m$. Thus $\{e_n\}$ is not even a Cauchy
 sequence, hence cannot converge in norm (since $\ell^2$ is complete, norm-conve
rgent sequences are exactly the Cauchy sequences).

**Conclusion.** In the Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}=\ell^2(\mathbb{N})$, the seque
nce $\{e_n\}$ is weakly convergent (to $0$) but not convergent in norm. Therefor
e such a Hilbert space exists. $\blacksquare$
