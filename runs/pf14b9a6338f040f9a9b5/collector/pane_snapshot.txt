No, the condition $df_p(T_pM)=T_{f(p)}N$ does not necessarily hold. A smooth bij
ection between same-dimensional manifolds need not be a local diffeomorphism.

**Counterexample.** Take $M=N=\mathbb{R}$ (both one-dimensional smooth manifolds
 with the standard structure) and define
$$f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad f(x)=x^3.$$

**$f$ is smooth.** It is a polynomial, hence $C^\infty$.

**$f$ is a bijection.** It is injective because $x^3$ is strictly increasing (it
s derivative $3x^2\ge 0$ vanishes only at the origin, and the function is monoto
ne). It is surjective because every real number $y$ has a real cube root $y^{1/3
}$, with $f(y^{1/3})=y$.

**The dimensions agree.** Both $M$ and $N$ are $1$-dimensional.

**The differential condition fails at $p=0$.** The derivative is $f'(x)=3x^2$, s
o $f'(0)=0$. The differential at the origin is the linear map
$$df_0:T_0\mathbb{R}\to T_{f(0)}\mathbb{R}=T_0\mathbb{R},\qquad df_0(v)=f'(0)\,v
=0\cdot v=0.$$
Thus
$$df_0\!\left(T_0\mathbb{R}\right)=\{0\}\subsetneq T_0\mathbb{R}\cong\mathbb{R}.
$$

So at $p=0$ the image of the differential is a proper (zero-dimensional) subspac
e of the tangent space $T_{f(0)}N$, even though $f$ is a smooth bijection betwee
n manifolds of the same dimension.

**Conclusion.** Smooth bijectivity alone does not force the differential to be s
urjective. The missing hypothesis is that $f^{-1}$ be smooth (i.e., that $f$ be
a *diffeomorphism*); under that stronger assumption the inverse function theorem
 would guarantee $df_p$ is an isomorphism at every point. Without it, the answer
 to the question is **no**.
