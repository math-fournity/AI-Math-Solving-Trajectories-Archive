## Proof

Recall the power series representation of $\sin z$, valid for all $z \in \mathbb
{C}$:

$$\sin z = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n\, z^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!} = z - \frac{z^3
}{3!} + \frac{z^5}{5!} - \frac{z^7}{7!} + \cdots$$

Since the only point where the integrand $\dfrac{\sin z}{z}$ might be undefined
is $z = 0$, which lies inside the contour $|z| = 1$, we divide the series term-b
y-term by $z$ (legitimate for $z \neq 0$, and the resulting series gives the rem
ovable-extension value at $z = 0$):

$$\frac{\sin z}{z} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n\, z^{2n}}{(2n+1)!} = 1 - \
frac{z^2}{3!} + \frac{z^4}{5!} - \frac{z^6}{7!} + \cdots$$

This is a power series in **non-negative even powers** of $z$, converging absolu
tely and uniformly on the compact contour $|z| = 1$. Hence we may integrate term
 by term:

$$\oint_{|z|=1} \frac{\sin z}{z}\, dz = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{(2n+1)
!} \oint_{|z|=1} z^{2n}\, dz.$$

Now use the standard fact that for any integer $k$,

$$\oint_{|z|=1} z^k\, dz = \begin{cases} 2\pi i, & k = -1, \\ 0, & k \neq -1. \e
nd{cases}$$

(For $k \geq 0$ this follows because $z^k$ has an antiderivative $z^{k+1}/(k+1)$
 on all of $\mathbb{C}$, so the integral over any closed curve is $0$; for $k \l
eq -2$ one parametrizes $z = e^{i\theta}$ and finds the integral vanishes as wel
l.)

In our series every exponent is $2n$ with $n \geq 0$, i.e. an **even non-negativ
e integer**, so $2n \neq -1$ for every term. Therefore

$$\oint_{|z|=1} z^{2n}\, dz = 0 \quad \text{for all } n \geq 0.$$

Consequently every term in the sum vanishes:

$$\oint_{|z|=1} \frac{\sin z}{z}\, dz = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{(2n+1)
!} \cdot 0 = 0.$$

$$\boxed{\oint_{|z|=1} \frac{\sin z}{z}\, dz = 0}$$

This agrees with the residue-theoretic viewpoint: $\dfrac{\sin z}{z}$ has a remo
vable singularity at $z = 0$ (with limiting value $1$), so its residue there is
$0$, and the integral over any closed contour is $0$.
