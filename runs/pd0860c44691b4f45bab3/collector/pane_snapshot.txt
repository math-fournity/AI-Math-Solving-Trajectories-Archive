## Proof

**Claim:** No. A surface with fundamental group isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_2$ can
not be embedded in $\mathbb{R}^3$ without self-intersections.

We proceed in three steps: (1) classify which surfaces have $\pi_1 \cong \mathbb
{Z}_2$, (2) show the unique candidate is $\mathbb{RP}^2$, and (3) show $\mathbb{
RP}^2$ cannot embed in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

---

### Step 1: Surfaces with boundary or non-compact surfaces cannot have $\pi_1 \c
ong \mathbb{Z}_2$

**Compact surfaces with boundary.** Every compact surface with nonempty boundary
 deformation retracts onto a 1-dimensional CW-complex (a finite graph — specific
ally a wedge of circles). Hence $\pi_1$ is a free group $F_r$ for some $r \geq 0
$. Free groups are either trivial ($r=0$) or infinite ($r \geq 1$). In particula
r, no free group is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_2$.

**Non-compact surfaces.** Every non-compact (open) surface has the homotopy type
 of a (possibly infinite) graph, so $\pi_1$ is a free group (possibly of infinit
e rank). Again, a free group is either trivial or infinite — never $\mathbb{Z}_2
$.

Therefore, any surface with $\pi_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$ must be **closed** (compa
ct, without boundary).

---

### Step 2: The only closed surface with $\pi_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$ is $\mathbb{
RP}^2$

By the classification theorem for closed surfaces, every closed connected surfac
e is either:

- **Orientable** of genus $g \geq 0$: $S^2$ ($g=0$, $\pi_1 = 0$), torus ($g=1$,
$\pi_1 = \mathbb{Z}^2$), or genus $g \geq 2$ ($\pi_1$ is an infinite non-abelian
 surface group). None is $\mathbb{Z}_2$.

- **Non-orientable** of genus $k \geq 1$ (connected sum of $k$ copies of $\mathb
b{RP}^2$), with presentation
$$\pi_1 = \langle a_1, \dots, a_k \mid a_1^2 a_2^2 \cdots a_k^2 = 1 \rangle.$$

  - $k = 1$: $\pi_1 = \langle a_1 \mid a_1^2 = 1 \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$. ✓
  - $k = 2$: the Klein bottle group $\langle a,b \mid a^2 b^2 = 1\rangle$, which
 is infinite.
  - $k \geq 3$: infinite (contains a free subgroup, or equivalently surjects ont
o the Klein bottle group).

Thus the **unique** closed surface with $\pi_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$ is $\mathbb{R
P}^2$ (the real projective plane, $k=1$).

---

### Step 3: $\mathbb{RP}^2$ cannot be embedded in $\mathbb{R}^3$

We use two classical facts:

**Fact A (Jordan–Brouwer Separation Theorem).** If $M \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is a
 compact, connected, embedded $(n{-}1)$-dimensional submanifold without boundary
, then $\mathbb{R}^n \setminus M$ has exactly two connected components, and $M$
is the topological boundary of each.

**Fact B (Consequence).** Such an $M$ is **orientable**. Indeed, the two complem
entary components define a continuous unit normal field on $M$ (e.g., the normal
 pointing into one component), which orients $M$.

Now suppose for contradiction that $\mathbb{RP}^2$ embeds in $\mathbb{R}^3$. The
n:
- $\mathbb{RP}^2$ is compact, connected, and closed (no boundary), so the Jordan
–Brouwer theorem applies.
- By Fact B, $\mathbb{RP}^2$ would be orientable.

But $\mathbb{RP}^2$ is **non-orientable** (its first Stiefel–Whitney class is no
nzero; equivalently, it contains a Möbius band as a neighborhood of any projecti
ve line, and a surface containing a Möbius band is non-orientable). This is a co
ntradiction.

Therefore $\mathbb{RP}^2$ cannot be embedded in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

---

### Conclusion

The unique surface with fundamental group $\mathbb{Z}_2$ is $\mathbb{RP}^2$, and
 $\mathbb{RP}^2$ cannot be embedded in $\mathbb{R}^3$ because it is a closed non
-orientable surface, while every closed surface embedded in $\mathbb{R}^3$ must
be orientable (by Jordan–Brouwer). Hence:

**No, a surface with fundamental group isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_2$ cannot be em
bedded in $\mathbb{R}^3$ without self-intersections.**
