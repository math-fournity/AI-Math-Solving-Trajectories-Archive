## Proof

**Setup and Definitions.**

Let $P(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_n x^n$ be a polynomial of degree $n$ that i
s:
1. **Anti-palindromic:** $P(x) = -x^n P(1/x)$, equivalently $a_k = -a_{n-k}$ for
 all $0 \le k \le n$.
2. **With odd coefficients:** every $a_k$ is an odd integer.

We show that $P$ always has a root on the unit circle $\{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z|
= 1\}$.

---

**Step 1: $n$ must be odd.**

Suppose for contradiction that $n$ is even, say $n = 2m$. The anti-palindromic c
ondition gives $a_k = -a_{n-k}$ for all $k$. Setting $k = m$:

$$a_m = -a_{2m - m} = -a_m$$

so $a_m = 0$. But $0$ is not an odd integer, contradicting the hypothesis that a
ll coefficients are odd. Therefore $n$ is odd. $\square$

---

**Step 2: $P(1) = 0$, so $z = 1$ is a root.**

Since $n$ is odd, the involution $k \mapsto n-k$ on $\{0, 1, \ldots, n\}$ has no
 fixed point (a fixed point would require $k = n-k$, i.e., $k = n/2 \notin \math
bb{Z}$). Thus the indices pair up into $(n+1)/2$ disjoint pairs $\{k, n-k\}$.

Evaluating at $x = 1$:

$$P(1) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} a_k = \sum_{k=0}^{(n-1)/2} \bigl(a_k + a_{n-k}\bigr).$$

By the anti-palindromic condition, $a_k + a_{n-k} = a_k - a_k = 0$ for each pair
. Therefore:

$$P(1) = \sum_{k=0}^{(n-1)/2} 0 = 0.$$

Hence $z = 1$ is a root of $P$. Since $|1| = 1$, this root lies on the unit circ
le. $\square$

---

**Step 3 (Structural refinement): The quotient is palindromic.**

Since $P(1) = 0$, we can write $P(x) = (x - 1)\,Q(x)$ where $Q$ has degree $n -
1$ (which is even). Substituting into the anti-palindromic identity $P(x) = -x^n
 P(1/x)$:

$$(x-1)\,Q(x) = -x^n\!\left(\tfrac{1}{x} - 1\right) Q\!\left(\tfrac{1}{x}\right)
 = -x^n \cdot \frac{1-x}{x} \cdot Q\!\left(\tfrac{1}{x}\right) = x^{n-1}(x-1)\,Q
\!\left(\tfrac{1}{x}\right).$$

Dividing both sides by $(x-1)$:

$$Q(x) = x^{n-1}\,Q\!\left(\tfrac{1}{x}\right),$$

so $Q$ is a **palindromic** polynomial of even degree $n-1$. The remaining $n-1$
 roots of $P$ (i.e., the roots of $Q$) come in reciprocal pairs $(z,\, 1/z)$, an
d for real-coefficient $Q$, in conjugate pairs as well. Roots of $Q$ on the unit
 circle are self-paired under $z \mapsto 1/z = \bar{z}$, so they are precisely t
he roots invariant under this involution.

---

**Conclusion.**

Every anti-palindromic polynomial of degree $n$ with all odd coefficients must h
ave **odd** degree $n$, and **always** has the root $z = 1$ on the unit circle.
The answer is:

> **Yes — such a polynomial always has at least one root on the unit circle, nam
ely $z = 1$.**

The degree is necessarily odd, $P(1) = 0$ is forced by the anti-palindromic pair
ing of odd-degree coefficients, and $|1| = 1$.
