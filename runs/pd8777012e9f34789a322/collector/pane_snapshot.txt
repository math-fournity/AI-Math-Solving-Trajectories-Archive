Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether every nonperfect, non-nilpo
tent Lie algebra $L$ must have an ideal $M$ with $[L,L] \cap Z(M) \ne 0$.

**Answer: No.** Such an ideal need not exist. I provide a counterexample and ver
ify it completely.

---

## Counterexample

Let $L = \mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C}) \oplus \mathbb{C}x$, where $\mathbb{C}x$ is
 a one-dimensional abelian Lie algebra (with $x$ a central element).

### Step 1: $L$ is nonperfect

$$[L, L] = [\mathfrak{sl}_2 \oplus \mathbb{C}x,\; \mathfrak{sl}_2 \oplus \mathbb
{C}x] = [\mathfrak{sl}_2, \mathfrak{sl}_2] \oplus [\mathfrak{sl}_2, \mathbb{C}x]
 \oplus [\mathbb{C}x, \mathbb{C}x] = \mathfrak{sl}_2 \oplus 0 \oplus 0 = \mathfr
ak{sl}_2.$$

Since $\mathfrak{sl}_2 \subsetneq L$, we have $[L,L] \ne L$, so $L$ is nonperfec
t. ✓

### Step 2: $L$ is not nilpotent

The lower central series: $L^{(0)} = L$, $L^{(1)} = [L, L] = \mathfrak{sl}_2$, $
L^{(2)} = [L, \mathfrak{sl}_2] = [\mathfrak{sl}_2, \mathfrak{sl}_2] = \mathfrak{
sl}_2$, and inductively $L^{(n)} = \mathfrak{sl}_2$ for all $n \ge 1$. Since $\m
athfrak{sl}_2 \ne 0$, the lower central series never reaches $0$, so $L$ is not
nilpotent. ✓

### Step 3: Classification of ideals of $L$

Since $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ is simple (non-abelian) and $\mathbb{C}x$ is
 one-dimensional (simple as a Lie algebra), the ideals of the direct sum $L = \m
athfrak{sl}_2 \oplus \mathbb{C}x$ are precisely the direct sums of ideals of the
 summands:

$$\{0,\quad \mathfrak{sl}_2,\quad \mathbb{C}x,\quad L\}.$$

*Justification*: If $I \trianglelefteq L$, write $I = (I \cap \mathfrak{sl}_2) \
oplus (I \cap \mathbb{C}x)$. Each intersection is an ideal of the respective sum
mand (using that the summands are ideals of $L$). Since $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ is sim
ple, $I \cap \mathfrak{sl}_2 \in \{0, \mathfrak{sl}_2\}$. Since $\mathbb{C}x$ is
 1-dimensional, $I \cap \mathbb{C}x \in \{0, \mathbb{C}x\}$. This gives exactly
four ideals.

### Step 4: Check $[L,L] \cap Z(M) = 0$ for every ideal $M$

Recall $[L, L] = \mathfrak{sl}_2$.

- **$M = 0$**: $Z(M) = 0$, so $[L,L] \cap Z(M) = 0$.
- **$M = \mathfrak{sl}_2$**: Since $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ is simple and non-abelian,
$Z(\mathfrak{sl}_2) = 0$. Thus $[L,L] \cap Z(M) = \mathfrak{sl}_2 \cap 0 = 0$.
- **$M = \mathbb{C}x$**: $\mathbb{C}x$ is abelian, so $Z(\mathbb{C}x) = \mathbb{
C}x$. Thus $[L,L] \cap Z(M) = \mathfrak{sl}_2 \cap \mathbb{C}x = 0$ (direct sum
decomposition).
- **$M = L$**: $Z(L) = Z(\mathfrak{sl}_2) \oplus Z(\mathbb{C}x) = 0 \oplus \math
bb{C}x = \mathbb{C}x$. Thus $[L,L] \cap Z(M) = \mathfrak{sl}_2 \cap \mathbb{C}x
= 0$.

In every case, $[L, L] \cap Z(M) = 0$.

### Conclusion

The Lie algebra $L = \mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C}) \oplus \mathbb{C}$ is nonperfec
t and not nilpotent, yet **no** ideal $M$ of $L$ satisfies $[L, L] \cap Z(M) \ne
 0$. Therefore, such an ideal does **not** necessarily exist.

---

**Remark.** The obstruction is that $[L,L] = \mathfrak{sl}_2$ is perfect (so $Z(
[L,L]) = 0$), and the only central elements of $L$ lie in the abelian summand $\
mathbb{C}x$, which is disjoint from $[L,L]$. This contrasts with the solvable ca
se: if $L$ is solvable over an algebraically closed field of characteristic $0$
(and not nilpotent, hence $[L,L] \ne 0$), then by Lie's theorem $[L,L]$ is nilpo
tent, so $Z([L,L]) \ne 0$, and taking $M = [L,L]$ works. The counterexample expl
oits the non-solvable (semisimple) part of $L$.
