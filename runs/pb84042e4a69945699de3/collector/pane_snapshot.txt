**No.** $L^p$ convergence does not imply almost-everywhere convergence. Below is
 a counterexample (the classical "typewriter" / moving-bump sequence), followed
by a brief note on the partial positive statement that does hold.

---

### Counterexample

Let $f \equiv 0$ on $\mathbb{R}$, and define a sequence $(f_n)$ of characteristi
c functions supported on $[0,1]$ as follows. For each $n \ge 1$, write $n$ uniqu
ely as

$$
n = 2^k + j, \qquad k \ge 0,\; 0 \le j < 2^k,
$$

and set

$$
f_n = \mathbf{1}_{\left[\,j\,/\,2^k,\; (j+1)\,/\,2^k\,\right]}.
$$

So the sequence enumerates, level by level, the dyadic subintervals of $[0,1]$:
$$
\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]},\;\; \mathbf{1}_{[0,1/2]},\, \mathbf{1}_{[1/2,1]},\;\; \mathb
f{1}_{[0,1/4]},\, \mathbf{1}_{[1/4,1/2]},\, \mathbf{1}_{[1/2,3/4]},\, \mathbf{1}
_{[3/4,1]},\;\dots
$$

**$L^p$ convergence to $0$.** For $n = 2^k + j$ the support of $f_n$ has measure
 $2^{-k}$, so for any $1 \le p < \infty$,

$$
\|f_n - f\|_{L^p}^{\,p} = \|f_n\|_{L^p}^{\,p} = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbf{1}_{[j
/2^k,(j+1)/2^k]}\,dx = 2^{-k}.
$$

As $n \to \infty$ we have $k \to \infty$, hence $\|f_n - f\|_{L^p} = 2^{-k/p} \t
o 0$. Thus $f_n \to f$ in $L^p(\mathbb{R})$.

**No pointwise convergence anywhere on $[0,1]$.** Fix any $x \in [0,1]$. At each
 level $k$, exactly one of the $2^k$ dyadic intervals $[j/2^k,(j+1)/2^k]$ contai
ns $x$, so among the $2^k$ indices $n$ with that level, exactly one gives $f_n(x
)=1$ and the rest give $f_n(x)=0$. Therefore:

- There are infinitely many $n$ with $f_n(x) = 1$ (one per level),
- There are infinitely many $n$ with $f_n(x) = 0$.

Hence $\lim_{n\to\infty} f_n(x)$ does not exist for **every** $x \in [0,1]$. Sin
ce $[0,1]$ has positive (indeed full, relative to the support) measure, $f_n(x)
\to f(x)$ fails on a set of positive measure — in fact it fails at *every* point
 of $[0,1]$.

This gives a sequence converging to $f$ in $L^p(\mathbb{R})$ that converges to $
f$ pointwise **nowhere** on $[0,1]$.

---

### What *does* hold

The implication fails in general, but two weaker statements are true:

1. **Convergence in measure.** $L^p$ convergence implies convergence in measure:
 for every $\varepsilon > 0$,
$$
m\big(\{x : |f_n(x)-f(x)| > \varepsilon\}\big) \le \varepsilon^{-p}\|f_n-f\|_{L^
p}^{\,p} \to 0.
$$

2. **A.e.-convergent subsequence.** Convergence in measure implies there exists
a subsequence $(f_{n_k})$ with $f_{n_k}(x) \to f(x)$ for a.e. $x$ (Riesz–Weyl th
eorem). In the counterexample above, picking one representative per level, e.g.
$n_k = 2^k$, gives $f_{n_k} = \mathbf{1}_{[0,2^{-k}]} \to 0$ a.e.

So $L^p$ convergence guarantees a.e. convergence only along a *subsequence*, not
 for the full sequence.

---

### Conclusion

$L^p$ convergence does **not** imply almost-everywhere convergence. The typewrit
er sequence above converges to $0$ in every $L^p(\mathbb{R})$ ($1 \le p < \infty
$) yet fails to converge pointwise at *any* point of $[0,1]$.
