## Proof

**Setup.** The numbers are $\alpha_k = k + \sqrt{k+1}$ for $k = 1, 2, \ldots, 10
00$. The sum of coefficients of $p(x)$ equals $p(1)$, so we need $p(1)$ to be a
positive integer.

**Step 1: Classify the roots.**

$\sqrt{k+1}$ is rational iff $k+1$ is a perfect square. For $k+1 \in \{2, \ldots
, 1001\}$, the perfect squares are $m^2$ for $m = 2, 3, \ldots, 31$ (since $31^2
 = 961 \leq 1001 < 32^2 = 1024$). This gives **30 rational roots**: $\alpha_k =
k + m = m^2 - 1 + m = m^2 + m - 1$ (integers).

The remaining **970 roots** are irrational: $\alpha_k = k + \sqrt{k+1}$ where $k
+1$ is not a perfect square.

**Step 2: Minimal polynomials.**

- **Rational roots** ($m = 2, \ldots, 31$): minimal polynomial is $x - (m^2 + m
- 1)$, degree 1. These 30 linear polynomials are distinct (since $m^2+m-1$ is st
rictly increasing).

- **Irrational roots** ($k$ with $k+1$ not a perfect square): since $p$ has rati
onal coefficients, $k - \sqrt{k+1}$ must also be a root. The minimal polynomial
is $(x-k)^2 - (k+1) = x^2 - 2kx + (k^2 - k - 1)$, degree 2, irreducible over $\m
athbb{Q}$.

**Step 3: Distinctness of minimal polynomials.**

- Two quadratics $x^2 - 2k_1 x + c_1$ and $x^2 - 2k_2 x + c_2$ with $k_1 \neq k_
2$ are distinct (different linear coefficients).
- Two distinct irrational roots cannot coincide: if $k_1 + \sqrt{k_1+1} = k_2 +
\sqrt{k_2+1}$ with $k_1 \neq k_2$, then $\sqrt{k_1+1} - \sqrt{k_2+1} = k_2 - k_1
 \in \mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}$, which forces $\sqrt{k_2+1} \in \mathbb{Q}$ (cont
radiction).
- A rational root cannot equal an irrational root.

So all 1000 minimal polynomials are distinct irreducible polynomials over $\math
bb{Q}$.

**Step 4: Minimal degree.**

Any polynomial with rational coefficients having all $\alpha_k$ as roots must be
 divisible by each minimal polynomial. Since these are distinct irreducibles, th
e LCM is their product. The minimal degree is:

$$30 \cdot 1 + 970 \cdot 2 = 30 + 1940 = \boxed{1970}$$

**Step 5: The sum-of-coefficients condition.**

Let $p(x)$ be the product of all 1000 minimal polynomials (degree 1970). Compute
 $p(1)$:

- **Linear factors:** $1 - (m^2 + m - 1) = 2 - m^2 - m$. For $m \geq 2$, $m^2 +
m \geq 6 > 2$, so each factor is a nonzero integer.

- **Quadratic factors:** $1 - 2k + (k^2 - k - 1) = k^2 - 3k = k(k-3)$. For irrat
ional $k$: $k \geq 1$ so $k \neq 0$; and $k = 3$ gives $k+1 = 4 = 2^2$ (a perfec
t square), so $k = 3$ is **not** in the irrational set. Thus $k(k-3) \neq 0$ for
 all irrational $k$.

Therefore $p(1)$ is a **nonzero integer** (product of nonzero integers).

- If $p(1) > 0$: $p(x)$ already satisfies all conditions.
- If $p(1) < 0$: take $-p(x)$, same roots, same degree 1970, rational coefficien
ts, and $(-p)(1) = -p(1) > 0$ is a positive integer.

In either case, a degree-1970 polynomial satisfying all conditions exists.

**Step 6: No smaller degree is possible.**

Any polynomial with rational coefficients having all 1000 numbers as roots must
be divisible by the LCM of their minimal polynomials, which has degree 1970. The
 sum-of-coefficients condition only affects the sign/scaling, not the degree. He
nce 1970 is both achievable and minimal.
