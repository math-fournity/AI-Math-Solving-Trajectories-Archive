## The John–Nirenberg Inequality

### Setup: the space BMO

A locally integrable function $f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbf{R}^{d})$ belong
s to $\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbf{R}^{d})$ (bounded mean oscillation) if

$$
\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}} \;:=\; \sup_{Q}\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}\bigl|f(x)-f_{Q}\bigr|\
,dx \;<\;\infty,
$$

where the supremum runs over all cubes $Q\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}$ with sides paral
lel to the axes, $|Q|$ is the Lebesgue measure of $Q$, and

$$
f_{Q} \;:=\; \frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q} f(x)\,dx
$$

is the average of $f$ on $Q$. The quantity $\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}$ is a seminorm
(it vanishes on constants); it becomes a norm after quotienting out constants.

### Statement of the John–Nirenberg inequality

**Theorem (John–Nirenberg, 1961).** There exist absolute constants $C_{1},C_{2}>
0$, depending only on the dimension $d$, such that for every $f\in\mathrm{BMO}(\
mathbf{R}^{d})$, every cube $Q\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}$, and every $\lambda>0$,

$$
\bigl|\bigl\{x\in Q : |f(x)-f_{Q}|>\lambda\bigr\}\bigr| \;\le\; C_{1}\,|Q|\,\exp
\!\Bigl(-\frac{C_{2}\,\lambda}{\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}}\Bigr).
$$

Equivalently, writing $E_{\lambda}(Q)=\{x\in Q:|f(x)-f_{Q}|>\lambda\}$,

$$
\frac{|E_{\lambda}(Q)|}{|Q|} \;\le\; C_{1}\,e^{-C_{2}\lambda/\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}
}}.
$$

### What the inequality says

The BMO condition by itself only controls the **first moment** of the oscillatio
n $|f-f_{Q}|$: it says the *average* deviation is bounded. A priori, this gives
no control on higher moments — a function could have a very large average deviat
ion yet still be in $L^{1}$, and one might fear that $f-f_{Q}$ has a heavy, poly
nomial tail.

The John–Nirenberg inequality upgrades this dramatically: **the deviation $|f-f_
{Q}|$ has an exponentially decaying distribution.** Concretely, on every cube $Q
$, the measure of the set where $f$ deviates from its mean by more than $\lambda
$ shrinks *exponentially* in $\lambda$. This is far stronger than what the Marko
v inequality (which the BMO definition alone permits) would give — Markov only y
ields the polynomial bound $|E_{\lambda}|\le \|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}|Q|/\lambda$.

### Immediate corollaries

**1. Exponential integrability.** From the distributional bound one obtains, for
 a smaller constant $C_{2}$ (or equivalently after adjusting constants),

$$
\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{c\,|f(x)-f_{Q}|}{\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}}\Bi
gr)\,dx \;\le\; C
$$

for absolute constants $c,C>0$. In particular $f$ is **exponentially integrable*
* on every cube. This is the dual form of the distributional inequality and is o
ften quoted as the John–Nirenberg theorem itself.

**2. $L^{p}$ bounds for all $1\le p<\infty$.** Integrating the distributional ta
il gives

$$
\Bigl(\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|f-f_{Q}|^{p}\,dx\Bigr)^{1/p} \;\le\; C_{p}\,\|f\|_{\
mathrm{BMO}}, \qquad 1\le p<\infty,
$$

with $C_{p}=O(p)$ as $p\to\infty$ (growing linearly, not blowing up). Thus all t
he $L^{p}$-means of the oscillation are comparable to the BMO seminorm. The case
 $p\to\infty$ fails — BMO is strictly larger than $L^{\infty}$ — and the linear
growth of $C_{p}$ reflects exactly the exponential (not Gaussian) tail.

**3. BMO is the sharp substitute for $L^{\infty}$.** The John–Nirenberg theorem
is the foundational result showing that BMO behaves like a "weakened $L^{\infty}
$": although BMO functions need not be bounded, their deviations from local mean
s are sub-Gaussian-tail-controlled, making BMO the natural endpoint of the $L^{p
}$ scale in many analytic contexts (most notably in the $H^{1}\!-\!\mathrm{BMO}$
 duality of C. Fefferman).

### Idea of the proof (Calderón–Zygmund decomposition)

The standard argument proceeds by induction on the level $\lambda$. Fix a cube $
Q$ and a level $\lambda$. Apply the Calderón–Zygmund decomposition to $|f-f_{Q}|
$ on $Q$ at height $\lambda$: subdivide $Q$ dyadically and stop at subcubes $\{Q
_{j}\}$ where the mean oscillation first exceeds $\lambda$. The BMO condition co
ntrols the total volume of these "bad" subcubes:

$$
\sum_{j}|Q_{j}| \;\le\; \frac{\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}}{\lambda}\,|Q|.
$$

The key observation is that on each bad subcube $Q_{j}$, the oscillation $f-f_{Q
_{j}}$ is *again* a BMO function with the same seminorm, so the decomposition ca
n be re-applied at level $2\lambda$, then $3\lambda$, and so on. Iterating $n$ t
imes yields

$$
\bigl|\{x\in Q:|f-f_{Q}|>n\lambda\}\bigr| \;\le\; \Bigl(\frac{\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO
}}}{\lambda}\Bigr)^{\!n}|Q|.
$$

Choosing $\lambda = 2\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}$ makes the ratio $\le 1/2$, giving a g
eometric decay $2^{-n}$ in the level $n\cdot 2\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}$. Reparametri
zing $n\lambda\mapsto\lambda$ converts this discrete geometric decay into the co
ntinuous exponential bound of the theorem. The sharpness (one cannot replace the
 exponential by a Gaussian $e^{-c\lambda^{2}}$) is witnessed by $\log|x|$ near t
he origin.

### Summary of the distributional implication

> On **any** ball (or cube) $Q$, the set where a BMO function $f$ deviates from
its local average $f_{Q}$ by more than $\lambda$ has measure at most a constant
multiple of $|Q|$ times $\exp(-c\lambda/\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}})$. In words: **a BM
O function's deviation from its mean is exponentially concentrated** — the tail
of the distribution of $f-f_{Q}$ decays exponentially, uniformly over all balls,
 with rate governed by the BMO seminorm.
