**Proof.**

Let $k$ be a positive integer. We show there exists a prime $p$ and distinct ele
ments $x_1, \dots, x_{k+3} \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times$ such that $x_i x_{i+1} x_{i+
2} x_{i+3} = i$ for all $1 \le i \le k$.

**Step 1: Deriving the recurrence.**

Suppose $x_1, \dots, x_{k+3}$ satisfy the conditions. For $1 \le i \le k-1$, div
iding the equation $x_{i+1}x_{i+2}x_{i+3}x_{i+4} = i+1$ by $x_i x_{i+1} x_{i+2}
x_{i+3} = i$ yields
$$\frac{x_{i+4}}{x_i} = \frac{i+1}{i}, \quad \text{i.e.,} \quad x_{i+4} = \frac{
i+1}{i}\, x_i. \tag{$\star$}$$
Together with the initial constraint $x_1 x_2 x_3 x_4 = 1$, the entire sequence
is determined by the three free parameters $x_1, x_2, x_3 \in \mathbb{F}_p^\time
s$ (with $x_4 = (x_1 x_2 x_3)^{-1}$).

**Step 2: Four-class structure.**

Define multiplier sequences ($m \ge 0$):
$$A_m = \prod_{j=0}^{m-1} \frac{4j+2}{4j+1}, \quad B_m = \prod_{j=0}^{m-1} \frac
{4j+3}{4j+2}, \quad C_m = \prod_{j=0}^{m-1} \frac{4j+4}{4j+3}, \quad D_m = \prod
_{j=0}^{m-1} \frac{4j+5}{4j+4},$$
with $A_0 = B_0 = C_0 = D_0 = 1$. By iterating $(\star)$:
$$x_{1+4m} = A_m\, x_1, \quad x_{2+4m} = B_m\, x_2, \quad x_{3+4m} = C_m\, x_3,
\quad x_{4+4m} = D_m\, x_4.$$
Let $N_1, N_2, N_3, N_4$ be the number of valid indices in each class; then $N_1
 + N_2 + N_3 + N_4 = k+3$.

**Step 3: Choosing the prime $p$.**

Each factor in the products above has numerator and denominator at most $k+4$, s
o we need $p > k+4$ for all multipliers to be well-defined and nonzero in $\math
bb{F}_p$.

Since every factor $\frac{4j+r+1}{4j+r} > 1$ as a rational number, the sequences
 $A_m, B_m, C_m, D_m$ are **strictly increasing** as real numbers, hence all val
ues within each class are distinct rationals. For any pair $m \ne m'$ within a c
lass, the difference $A_m - A_{m'}$ (similarly $B, C, D$) is a nonzero rational
$u/v$ in lowest terms; only finitely many primes divide $u$. Let $\mathcal{S}$ b
e the finite set of all such "bad" primes across all classes and all pairs. Choo
se a prime $p$ satisfying:
$$p > \max\!\left(k+4,\; 1 + \frac{(k+3)^2}{2}\right), \qquad p \notin \mathcal{
S}.$$
Such a prime exists because there are infinitely many primes while $\mathcal{S}$
 is finite. With this choice, within-class distinctness is automatic (all $A_m$
are distinct mod $p$, etc.).

**Step 4: Counting argument.**

We count triples $(x_1, x_2, x_3) \in (\mathbb{F}_p^\times)^3$ for which all $k+
3$ values are distinct and nonzero.

- **Nonzero:** Automatic, since $x_4 = (x_1 x_2 x_3)^{-1} \ne 0$ and all multipl
iers are nonzero mod $p$.
- **Within-class distinctness:** Guaranteed by $p \notin \mathcal{S}$.
- **Cross-class distinctness:** For each pair of indices $(i, j)$ lying in diffe
rent classes, the condition $x_i = x_j$ becomes an equation in $(x_1, x_2, x_3)$
. We claim each such equation has **at most $(p-1)^2$ solutions** in $(\mathbb{F
}_p^\times)^3$:
  - *Classes $\{1,2,3\}$ pairwise:* e.g., $A_m x_1 = B_{m'} x_2$ gives $x_1 = \f
rac{B_{m'}}{A_m} x_2$; fixing $x_2$ determines $x_1$, and $x_3$ is free: $(p-1)^
2$ solutions.
  - *Class 4 vs class $a \in \{1,2,3\}$:* e.g., $A_m x_1 = D_{m'} x_4 = \frac{D_
{m'}}{x_1 x_2 x_3}$ gives $x_1^2 x_2 x_3 = \frac{D_{m'}}{A_m}$; fixing $(x_1, x_
2)$ uniquely determines $x_3 = \frac{D_{m'}/A_m}{x_1^2 x_2} \in \mathbb{F}_p^\ti
mes$: $(p-1)^2$ solutions. The other cases ($a=2$: fix $(x_1,x_2)$, solve for $x
_3$; $a=3$: fix $(x_1, x_3)$, solve for $x_2$) are identical.

The total number of cross-class conditions is $\sum_{a < b} N_a N_b \le \frac{(k
+3)^2}{2}$. By the union bound, the number of **bad** triples is at most
$$\frac{(k+3)^2}{2} \cdot (p-1)^2.$$
Hence the number of **good** triples is at least
$$(p-1)^3 - \frac{(k+3)^2}{2}(p-1)^2 = (p-1)^2\!\left(p - 1 - \frac{(k+3)^2}{2}\
right) > 0,$$
by our choice of $p$. Therefore a good triple $(x_1, x_2, x_3)$ exists, and the
resulting $x_1, \dots, x_{k+3}$ are distinct, nonzero, and satisfy $x_i x_{i+1}
x_{i+2} x_{i+3} = i$ for all $1 \le i \le k$. $\blacksquare$
