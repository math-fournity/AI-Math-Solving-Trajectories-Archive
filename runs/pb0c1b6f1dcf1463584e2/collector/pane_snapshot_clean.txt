Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether $C_f$ is dense in $C$. The
problem references $f$ and $P$ which are the standard Pythagorean parametrizatio
n: $P$ is the set of primitive Pythagorean triple parameters and $f(a,b) = \left
(\frac{a^2-b^2}{a^2+b^2}, \frac{2ab}{a^2+b^2}\right)$. This is the classic quest
ion of whether rational points on the unit circle are dense.

## Answer: Yes, $C_f$ is dense in $C$.

**Interpretation.** We take $P = \{(a,b) \in \mathbb{N}^2 : \gcd(a,b)=1,\; a > b
 > 0,\; a \not\equiv b \pmod{2}\}$ (primitive Pythagorean parameters) and $f(a,b
) = \left(\frac{a^2-b^2}{a^2+b^2},\; \frac{2ab}{a^2+b^2}\right)$.

---

### Step 1: $f$ maps $P$ into $C$

For $(a,b) \in P$ with $a > b > 0$:
- $x = \frac{a^2-b^2}{a^2+b^2} > 0$ since $a > b$.
- $y = \frac{2ab}{a^2+b^2} > 0$ since $a,b > 0$.
- $x^2 + y^2 = \frac{(a^2-b^2)^2 + 4a^2b^2}{(a^2+b^2)^2} = \frac{a^4+2a^2b^2+b^4
}{(a^2+b^2)^2} = 1$.

So $f(a,b) \in C$.

### Step 2: Reformulation via slope parameter

Define $g:(0,1) \to C$ by $g(t) = \left(\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2},\; \frac{2t}{1+t^2}\
right)$. Then $f(a,b) = g(b/a)$, so $C_f = g(T)$ where $T = \{b/a : (a,b) \in P\
}$.

### Step 3: $g$ is a homeomorphism from $(0,1)$ onto $C$

**Continuity:** $g$ is a rational function with denominator $1+t^2 > 0$, so cont
inuous.

**Injectivity:** If $g(t_1) = g(t_2)$, then from the second coordinate: $t_1(1+t
_2^2) = t_2(1+t_1^2)$, giving $(t_1 - t_2)(1 - t_1 t_2) = 0$. Since $t_1, t_2 \i
n (0,1)$, we have $t_1 t_2 < 1$, so $t_1 = t_2$.

**Surjectivity onto $C$:** For any $(x,y) \in C$, set $t = \frac{y}{1+x}$. Since
 $x > 0, y > 0$, we get $t > 0$; since $y < 1$ and $1+x > 1$, we get $t < 1$. On
e verifies: $\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2} = \frac{(1+x)^2 - y^2}{(1+x)^2 + y^2} = \frac{1
+2x+x^2-y^2}{1+2x+x^2+y^2} = \frac{1+2x+x^2-(1-x^2)}{1+2x+1} = \frac{2x^2+2x}{2+
2x} = x$, and similarly $\frac{2t}{1+t^2} = y$. So $g(t) = (x,y)$.

**Continuous inverse:** $g^{-1}(x,y) = \frac{y}{1+x}$ is continuous. Thus $g$ is
 a homeomorphism.

### Step 4: $T$ is dense in $(0,1)$

$T = \{p/q \in (0,1) : \gcd(p,q)=1,\; p,q \text{ opposite parity}\}$.

**Claim:** $T$ is dense in $(0,1)$.

*Proof.* Let $r \in (0,1)$ and $\epsilon > 0$. Choose an odd prime $q > \max\!\l
eft(\frac{2}{\epsilon},\, \frac{1}{r},\, \frac{1}{1-r}\right)$. Set $n = \lfloor
 rq \rfloor$. Then:
- $0 < n < q$ (since $q > 1/r$ gives $n \geq 1$, and $q > 1/(1-r)$ gives $rq < q
-1$ so $n < q-1$).
- $|n/q - r| < 1/q < \epsilon/2$.
- $\gcd(n, q) = 1$ (since $q$ is prime and $0 < n < q$).

**Case 1:** $n$ is even. Then $n$ (even) and $q$ (odd) have opposite parity, so
$n/q \in T$ and $|n/q - r| < \epsilon$. ✓

**Case 2:** $n$ is odd. Then $n+1$ is even, and $n+1 < q$ (since $n < q-1$). So
$\gcd(n+1, q) = 1$ (as $q$ is prime and $0 < n+1 < q$), $n+1$ and $q$ have oppos
ite parity, and:
$$\left|\frac{n+1}{q} - r\right| \leq \left|\frac{n}{q} - r\right| + \frac{1}{q}
 < \frac{\epsilon}{2} + \frac{\epsilon}{2} = \epsilon.$$

So $(n+1)/q \in T$ and is within $\epsilon$ of $r$. ✓

In both cases we found an element of $T$ within $\epsilon$ of $r$. Since $r$ and
 $\epsilon$ were arbitrary, $T$ is dense in $(0,1)$.

### Step 5: Conclusion

Since $g:(0,1) \to C$ is continuous (indeed a homeomorphism) and $T$ is dense in
 $(0,1)$, the image $g(T) = C_f$ is dense in $g((0,1)) = C$.

*Explicitly:* for any $(x_0, y_0) \in C$ and $\epsilon > 0$, let $t_0 = g^{-1}(x
_0, y_0) \in (0,1)$. By density of $T$, there exists $t \in T$ with $|t - t_0| <
 \delta$ for any chosen $\delta > 0$. By uniform continuity of $g$ on compact su
bintervals (or just continuity at $t_0$), choose $\delta$ small enough that $\|g
(t) - g(t_0)\| < \epsilon$. Then $g(t) \in C_f$ and $\|g(t) - (x_0, y_0)\| < \ep
silon$.

Therefore, **$C_f$ is dense in $C$**. $\blacksquare$
