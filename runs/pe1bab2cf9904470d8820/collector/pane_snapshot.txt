## Solution

**Answer: No.**

The condition that $A^2$ is positive definite is actually *automatic* for any no
n-singular symmetric matrix $A$, so it provides no constraint forcing $A$ itself
 to be positive definite.

### Why $A^2$ is automatically positive definite

Since $A$ is real symmetric, it is orthogonally diagonalizable: $A = Q\Lambda Q^
T$ with real eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$. Since $A$ is non-singula
r, every $\lambda_i \neq 0$. Then
$$A^2 = Q\Lambda^2 Q^T,$$
with eigenvalues $\lambda_1^2, \dots, \lambda_n^2$, all strictly positive (since
 each $\lambda_i \neq 0$). Hence $A^2$ is positive definite **for every** non-si
ngular symmetric $A$. The hypothesis "$A^2$ is positive definite" is therefore r
edundant — it holds automatically and imposes no extra restriction on the signs
of the $\lambda_i$.

### Counterexample

Take
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

- **Entries in $(0,\infty)$**: yes, all entries are positive. ✓
- **Symmetric**: yes. ✓
- **Non-singular**: $\det A = 1\cdot 1 - 2\cdot 2 = -3 \neq 0$. ✓
- **$A^2$ positive definite**:
$$A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 &
 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix},$$
with eigenvalues $5+4=9$ and $5-4=1$, both positive, so $A^2$ is positive defini
te. ✓
- **$A$ is NOT positive definite**: the eigenvalues of $A$ are $1+2=3$ and $1-2=
-1$. The eigenvalue $-1 < 0$, so $A$ is not positive definite. ✓

### Conclusion

All hypotheses are satisfied, yet $A$ has a negative eigenvalue and is therefore
 not positive definite. The answer is **No** — $A$ need not be positive definite
.
