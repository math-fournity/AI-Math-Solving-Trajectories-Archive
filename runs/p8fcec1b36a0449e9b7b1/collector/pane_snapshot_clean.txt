## Proof

Since $A$ has positive entries, by the Perron–Frobenius theorem $A$ is primitive
. Its dominant eigenvalue is $\hat\lambda = 1$, which is simple, and every other
 eigenvalue $\mu$ of $A$ satisfies $|\mu| < 1$. In particular $|\lambda| < 1$ an
d $\lambda \neq 1$.

### Step 1: The key identity $(A^k - \lambda^k I)^r v = 0$

Write
$$A^k - \lambda^k I = (A - \lambda I)\,Q_k, \qquad Q_k = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} \lambd
a^i A^{k-1-i}.$$
Since $Q_k$ is a polynomial in $A$, it commutes with $A$ and hence with $(A-\lam
bda I)$. Therefore
$$(A^k - \lambda^k I)^r = (A-\lambda I)^r\, Q_k^r.$$
Applying to $v$ and using $(A-\lambda I)^r v = 0$:
$$(A^k - \lambda^k I)^r v = Q_k^r\,(A-\lambda I)^r v = 0.$$

### Step 2: The eigenvector $v' = (A-\lambda I)^{r-1}v$

Set $v' = (A-\lambda I)^{r-1} v$. Then
$$(A-\lambda I)v' = (A-\lambda I)^r v = 0,$$
so $v'$ is an ordinary eigenvector: $Av' = \lambda v'$. Consequently
$$A^k v' = \lambda^k v' \xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{} 0 \qquad (\text{since } |\lam
bda|<1).$$

### Step 3: Induction on the grade $r$

We prove by induction on $r \geq 1$: if $(A-\lambda I)^r v = 0$ with $|\lambda|<
1$ and $\lambda \neq \hat\lambda = 1$, then $A^k v \to 0$.

**Base case $r=1$.** $(A-\lambda I)v = 0$ gives $Av = \lambda v$, hence $A^k v =
 \lambda^k v \to 0$.

**Inductive step ($r \geq 2$).** Assume the result for grade $r-1$. Let $v$ sati
sfy $(A-\lambda I)^r v = 0$ and define
$$u := (A-\lambda I)v.$$
Then $(A-\lambda I)^{r-1} u = (A-\lambda I)^r v = 0$, so by the inductive hypoth
esis $A^k u \to 0$. Denote $\epsilon_k := A^k u$; then $\epsilon_k \to 0$. Now
$$\epsilon_k = A^k(A-\lambda I)v = A^{k+1}v - \lambda\, A^k v,$$
i.e. $A^{k+1}v = \lambda\, A^k v + \epsilon_k$. Telescoping from $A^0 v = v$:
$$A^k v = \lambda^k v + \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} \lambda^{k-1-i}\,\epsilon_i = \lambda^k
 v + \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} \lambda^j\,\epsilon_{k-1-j}.$$

- The first term $\lambda^k v \to 0$ since $|\lambda|<1$.
- For the sum: $(\epsilon_i)$ is bounded (it converges), say $|\epsilon_i|\le M$
. Given $\delta>0$, pick $N$ with $|\epsilon_i|<\delta$ for $i\ge N$. For $k>N$,
$$\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1} \lambda^j \epsilon_{k-1-j}\right\| \le \sum_{j=0}^{k-1
-N} |\lambda|^j \delta + \sum_{j=k-N}^{k-1} |\lambda|^j M \le \frac{\delta}{1-|\
lambda|} + \frac{M\,|\lambda|^{k-N}}{1-|\lambda|} \xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{} \fr
ac{\delta}{1-|\lambda|}.$$
Since $\delta>0$ is arbitrary, the sum tends to $0$.

Therefore $A^k v \to 0$, completing the induction.

### Role of the identity $(A^k - \lambda^k I)^r v = 0$

The identity from Step 1 is the structural fact underpinning the induction: it a
sserts that $v$ remains a generalized eigenvector of $A^k$ (grade $\le r$) with
eigenvalue $\lambda^k$, so the Jordan-chain structure is preserved under powers.
 Expanding it via $A^k = \lambda^k I + (A^k - \lambda^k I)$ together with the ni
lpotent shift $(A-\lambda I)^j v =: v_j$ (where $v_j = 0$ for $j \ge r$) yields
the explicit closed form
$$A^k v = \sum_{j=0}^{r-1} \binom{k}{j}\,\lambda^{k-j}\,v_j,$$
in which every summand tends to $0$ (polynomial growth $\binom{k}{j}\sim k^j/j!$
 is dominated by the exponential decay $|\lambda|^{k-j}$). The inductive argumen
t above is precisely the grade-by-grade unpacking of this identity, with the bas
e case supplied by the eigenvector $v' = (A-\lambda I)^{r-1}v$ for which $A^k v'
 = \lambda^k v' \to 0$.

Since $v$ lies entirely in the generalized eigenspace of the non-dominant eigenv
alue $\lambda$ (with $|\lambda|<1$), and the dominant eigenvalue $\hat\lambda =
1$ contributes nothing to $A^k v$, we conclude

$$\boxed{\lim_{k\to\infty} A^k v = 0.} \qquad \blacksquare$$
