## Solution

We determine the values of $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ for which
$$I(\alpha) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^2} \frac{1}{(1+x^2+xy+y^2)^\alpha}\,dx\,dy$$
converges.

### Step 1. The quadratic form is positive definite

Let $Q(x,y) = x^2+xy+y^2$. Its associated matrix is
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 1 \end{pmatrix},$$
with eigenvalues $\frac{1}{2}$ and $\frac{3}{2}$, both positive. Hence $Q$ is **
positive definite**, so $1+Q(x,y) \ge 1 > 0$ everywhere — there is no singularit
y in the integrand at finite points. The only issue is behavior at infinity.

### Step 2. Diagonalize by a linear change of variables

Complete the square:
$$x^2+xy+y^2 = \left(x+\tfrac{y}{2}\right)^2 + \tfrac{3}{4}y^2.$$
Set $u = x + \frac{y}{2}$, $v = y$. The Jacobian is
$$\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)} = \det\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 1\end{
pmatrix} = 1,$$
so $dx\,dy = du\,dv$, and
$$I(\alpha) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^2} \frac{1}{\left(1+u^2+\frac{3}{4}v^2\right)^\al
pha}\,du\,dv.$$

### Step 3. Rescale to isotropic form

Let $w = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}v$, so $dv = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\,dw$. Then
$$I(\alpha) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}\frac{1}{(1+u^2+w^2)^\alpha}\
,du\,dw.$$
The constant prefactor is irrelevant for convergence, so convergence of $I(\alph
a)$ is equivalent to convergence of
$$J(\alpha) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^2}\frac{1}{(1+u^2+w^2)^\alpha}\,du\,dw.$$

### Step 4. Polar coordinates

Set $u = r\cos\theta$, $w = r\sin\theta$, with $du\,dw = r\,dr\,d\theta$:
$$J(\alpha) = 2\pi\int_0^\infty \frac{r}{(1+r^2)^\alpha}\,dr.$$

**Near $r=0$:** the integrand is $\frac{r}{(1+r^2)^\alpha} \sim r$, which is int
egrable on $[0,1]$ for every $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ (consistent with the absenc
e of finite singularities noted in Step 1).

**Near $r=\infty$:** for $r \ge 1$,
$$\frac{r}{(1+r^2)^\alpha} \sim \frac{r}{r^{2\alpha}} = r^{1-2\alpha}.$$
The integral $\int_1^\infty r^{1-2\alpha}\,dr$ converges if and only if the expo
nent satisfies $1-2\alpha < -1$, i.e.
$$2\alpha > 2 \iff \alpha > 1.$$

### Step 3 (verification of the boundary)

At $\alpha = 1$: $\int_1^\infty r^{-1}\,dr = \infty$ — diverges (logarithmic).
For $\alpha < 1$: $1-2\alpha > -1$, exponent $\ge -1$, diverges.
For $\alpha > 1$: $1-2\alpha < -1$, converges.

All the changes of variables used (translation, linear shear, anisotropic scalin
g) are invertible linear maps with nonzero constant Jacobian, so they preserve c
onvergence. Hence the convergence behavior of $I(\alpha)$ is exactly that of $J(
\alpha)$.

### Conclusion

The integral converges if and only if $\alpha > 1$.

$$\boxed{\alpha > 1}$$
