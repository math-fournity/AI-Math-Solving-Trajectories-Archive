Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the given "extension proper
ty" correctly characterizes compactifications.

**Answer: The statement is FALSE.**

## Proof

The "extension property" stated in the problem is:

> (Y, f) is a compactification of X **if and only if** for every compact space X
' and continuous map g: X → X', there exists a continuous map g': Y → X' with g'
(f(x)) = g(x) for all x ∈ X.

The standard definition of a compactification is: (Y, f) is a compactification o
f X if Y is compact (Hausdorff), f: X → Y is a topological embedding, and f(X) i
s dense in Y.

I will show the equivalence fails by exhibiting a compactification (in the stand
ard sense) that does **not** have the extension property.

### Counterexample

Let **X = ℕ** with the discrete topology. Let **Y = ℕ ∪ {∞}** be the one-point (
Alexandroff) compactification, and let **f: ℕ → Y** be the inclusion.

**(Y, f) is a compactification in the standard sense:**
- Y is compact Hausdorff (the one-point compactification of a locally compact, n
on-compact Hausdorff space is compact Hausdorff).
- f is an embedding: ℕ is discrete, and the subspace topology on ℕ ⊂ Y is discre
te (every singleton {n} is open in Y since {∞} is closed and {n} is open in the
discrete subspace).
- f(ℕ) = ℕ is dense in Y: every neighborhood of ∞ has the form Y \ F for finite
F ⊂ ℕ, which intersects ℕ.

**(Y, f) does NOT have the extension property:**

Take the compact space **X' = {0, 1}** with the discrete topology (finite, hence
 compact). Define **g: ℕ → {0, 1}** by g(n) = n mod 2. This is continuous since
ℕ is discrete.

Suppose, for contradiction, that a continuous extension **g': Y → {0, 1}** exist
s with g'(n) = n mod 2 for all n ∈ ℕ.

**Case 1: g'(∞) = 0.** Then g'⁻¹(0) is an open neighborhood of ∞. In the one-poi
nt compactification, every open set containing ∞ has the form Y \ F for some fin
ite F ⊂ ℕ. So g'⁻¹(0) ⊇ Y \ F, meaning g'(n) = 0 for all but finitely many n. Bu
t g'(n) = 1 for every odd n, and there are infinitely many odd numbers. **Contra
diction.**

**Case 2: g'(∞) = 1.** Then g'⁻¹(1) is an open neighborhood of ∞, so g'(n) = 1 f
or all but finitely many n. But g'(n) = 0 for every even n, and there are infini
tely many even numbers. **Contradiction.**

Both cases lead to contradictions, so no continuous extension g' exists. Therefo
re (Y, f) does not have the extension property.

### Conclusion

The one-point compactification of ℕ is a compactification (in the standard sense
) but fails the extension property. Therefore, it is **not true** that every com
pactification has the extension property, and the proposed equivalence is **fals
e**.

**Remark.** The extension property as stated is the universal property of the **
Stone–Čech compactification** βX (when X' ranges over compact Hausdorff spaces).
 The Stone–Čech compactification is the *maximal* compactification; other compac
tifications (such as the one-point compactification) do not satisfy this univers
al property. This is precisely why the equivalence fails: the extension property
 singles out one special compactification, not all of them.
