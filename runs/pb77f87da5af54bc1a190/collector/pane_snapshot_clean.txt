## Proof

0) + f'(0)$ has no value.

**Step 1: $f$ is strictly increasing.**

Since $f$ maps into $(0,\infty)$, we have $f(f(x)) > 0$ for every $x \in \mathbb
{R}$. The differential equation gives $f'(x) = f(f(x)) > 0$ for all $x$, so $f$
is strictly increasing.

**Step 2: The left-tail limit exists and is nonnegative.**

Since $f$ is strictly increasing and bounded below by $0$, the limit
$$\alpha := \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = \inf_{x \in \mathbb{R}} f(x)$$
exists with $\alpha \geq 0$.

**Step 3: Case $\alpha > 0$ leads to contradiction.**

If $\alpha > 0$, then as $x \to -\infty$, $f(x) \to \alpha$. By continuity of $f
$,
$$f'(x) = f(f(x)) \longrightarrow f(\alpha) > 0.$$
Choose $c = f(\alpha)/2 > 0$. There exists $M \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $f'(t) \
geq c$ for all $t \leq M$. Then for any $x < M$:
$$f(x) = f(M) - \int_x^M f'(t)\,dt \leq f(M) - c(M - x).$$
As $x \to -\infty$, the right-hand side $\to -\infty$, so $f(x) \to -\infty$, co
ntradicting $f(x) > 0$.

**Step 4: Case $\alpha = 0$ also leads to contradiction.**

If $\alpha = 0$, then as $x \to -\infty$, $f(x) \to 0$. By continuity of $f$,
$$f'(x) = f(f(x)) \longrightarrow f(0) > 0.$$
Choose $c = f(0)/2 > 0$. There exists $M \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $f'(t) \geq c
$ for all $t \leq M$. The identical integral estimate gives
$$f(x) \leq f(M) - c(M - x) \to -\infty \quad \text{as } x \to -\infty,$$
again contradicting $f(x) > 0$.

**Conclusion.**

Both possible values of $\alpha$ ($\alpha > 0$ and $\alpha = 0$) yield contradic
tions. Therefore **no continuously differentiable function $f: \mathbb{R} \to (0
,\infty)$ satisfying $f'(x) = f(f(x))$ exists**. The premise of the problem is f
alse, so $f(0) + f'(0)$ has no value.
