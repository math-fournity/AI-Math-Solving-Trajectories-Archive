## Solution

**The statement is FALSE.**

### Substitution

Let $u = x + b$, so $x = u - b$ and $dx = du$. When $x \to -\infty$, $u \to -\in
fty$; when $x = 0$, $u = b$. Therefore:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{0} f(x+b)\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{b} f(u)\,du.$$

The claimed identity becomes:

$$\lim_{b \to -\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{b} f(u)\,du \stackrel{?}{=} \int_{-\infty
}^{0} f(x)\,dx. \tag{$\ast$}$$

### The left-hand side is always $0$

Since $f$ is Lebesgue integrable, $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f|<\infty$. For any $
b$,

$$\left|\int_{-\infty}^{b} f(u)\,du\right| \le \int_{-\infty}^{b}|f(u)|\,du.$$

By absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral (or dominated convergence applie
d to $|f|\cdot\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,b]}$), $\int_{-\infty}^{b}|f(u)|\,du \to 0$ a
s $b\to -\infty$. Hence

$$\lim_{b\to -\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{b} f(u)\,du = 0.$$

So ($\ast$) reduces to the claim $0 = \int_{-\infty}^{0} f(x)\,dx$ for **every**
 integrable $f$, which is plainly false.

### Explicit counterexample

Define

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} e^{x}, & x \le 0,\\ 0, & x > 0. \end{cases}$$

Then $f\ge 0$ and

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{0} e^{x}\,dx = 1 < \infty,$
$

so $f$ is Lebesgue integrable.

**Right-hand side:**

$$\int_{-\infty}^{0} f(x)\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{0} e^{x}\,dx = 1.$$

**Left-hand side:** For $b \le 0$,

$$\int_{-\infty}^{b} f(u)\,du = \int_{-\infty}^{b} e^{u}\,du = e^{b} \xrightarro
w{b\to -\infty} 0.$$

(For $b>0$ the integral equals $1$, but this is irrelevant to the limit $b\to -\
infty$.)

Thus

$$\lim_{b\to -\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{0} f(x+b)\,dx = 0 \ne 1 = \int_{-\infty}^{0
} f(x)\,dx.$$

### Conclusion

The statement is **false**. The translation $f(x)\mapsto f(x+b)$ with $b\to -\in
fty$ slides the mass of $f$ toward $-\infty$, so the integral over the fixed win
dow $(-\infty,0]$ captures a vanishing fraction of the total mass—regardless of
where that mass originally sat. The equality can hold only in the special case $
\int_{-\infty}^{0}f=0$, not for every integrable $f$.
