## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. For any polynomial $p(z)$ of degree $n \ge 2$, there exists $R >
 0$ such that $\displaystyle\int_{|z|=R} \frac{1}{p(z)}\,dz = 0$. In fact, every
 $R > \max_k |a_k|$ works, where $a_1,\dots,a_n$ are the roots of $p$.

---

Let $p(z) = a_n z^n + a_{n-1}z^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0$ with $a_n \neq 0$ and $n \g
e 2$. Let $a_1, \dots, a_n$ be the roots of $p$, listed with multiplicity. The f
unction $f(z) = 1/p(z)$ is meromorphic on $\mathbb{C}$ with poles exactly at the
 roots $a_k$.

**Step 1: The residue at infinity vanishes.**

Compute the residue of $f$ at $z = \infty$ via the substitution $w = 1/z$:

$$\operatorname{Res}_{z=\infty} f(z) = -\operatorname{Res}_{w=0}\left[\frac{1}{w
^2}\,f\!\left(\tfrac{1}{w}\right)\right].$$

Now $p(1/w) = w^{-n}\bigl(a_n + a_{n-1}w + \cdots + a_0 w^n\bigr)$, so

$$\frac{1}{w^2}\,f\!\left(\tfrac{1}{w}\right) = \frac{1}{w^2}\cdot\frac{w^n}{a_n
 + a_{n-1}w + \cdots + a_0 w^n} = \frac{w^{n-2}}{a_n + a_{n-1}w + \cdots + a_0 w
^n}.$$

Since $n \ge 2$, the exponent $n - 2 \ge 0$, so this expression is **analytic at
** $w = 0$ (the denominator equals $a_n \neq 0$ there). Hence

$$\operatorname{Res}_{w=0}\left[\frac{w^{n-2}}{a_n + \cdots}\right] = 0 \quad\Lo
ngrightarrow\quad \operatorname{Res}_{z=\infty} f(z) = 0.$$

**Step 2: The sum of all finite residues is zero.**

By the global residue theorem, the sum of all residues of a meromorphic function
 on the Riemann sphere (including the residue at infinity) is zero:

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n}\operatorname{Res}_{z=a_k} f(z) \;+\; \operatorname{Res}_{z=\inf
ty} f(z) = 0.$$

Since $\operatorname{Res}_{z=\infty} f(z) = 0$ by Step 1,

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n}\operatorname{Res}_{z=a_k} \frac{1}{p(z)} = 0. \tag{$\ast$}$$

**Step 3: Choose $R$ larger than all roots.**

Set $R_0 = \max_{1 \le k \le n} |a_k|$ and pick any $R > R_0$. Then the circle $
|z| = R$ (positively oriented) encloses every root $a_k$. By the residue theorem
,

$$\int_{|z|=R} \frac{1}{p(z)}\,dz = 2\pi i \sum_{k=1}^{n}\operatorname{Res}_{z=a
_k}\frac{1}{p(z)} = 2\pi i \cdot 0 = 0,$$

where the last equality uses $(\ast)$.

**Conclusion.** For every $R > \max_k |a_k|$, the integral $\displaystyle\int_{|
z|=R} \frac{1}{p(z)}\,dz = 0$. Such an $R$ always exists (e.g., $R = 1 + \max_k
|a_k|$). $\blacksquare$
