Looking at this problem, I need to find all natural numbers $k$ such that $p_1 p
_2 \cdots p_k - 1 = a^n$ for some $a > 1$, $n \geq 2$.

**Answer: There are no such natural numbers $k$.**

## Proof

Let $P_k = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdots p_k$ (the $k$-th primor
ial). We seek $k$ with $P_k - 1 = a^n$, $a > 1$, $n \geq 2$.

### Step 1: Reduction to prime exponent

If $n = pm$ for a prime $p$, then $a^n = (a^m)^p$, so we may assume $n$ is prime
: either $n = 2$ or $n = p$ (odd prime).

### Step 2: Ruling out $n = 2$

- $k = 1$: $P_1 - 1 = 1$, not a perfect power with $a > 1$.
- $k = 2$: $P_2 - 1 = 5$, not a perfect square.
- $k \geq 3$: $3 \mid P_k$, so $a^2 = P_k - 1 \equiv -1 \equiv 2 \pmod{3}$. But
squares modulo 3 are only $0$ and $1$. Contradiction.

So $n = 2$ yields no solutions.

### Step 3: Ruling out $n = p$ (odd prime) for $k \leq 2$

- $k = 1$: $P_1 - 1 = 1 = a^p$ gives $a = 1$, not $> 1$.
- $k = 2$: $P_2 - 1 = 5$, which is prime, hence not a perfect power.

### Step 4: Key lemma — the exponent exceeds $p_k$ (for $k \geq 3$)

Assume $k \geq 3$ and $P_k - 1 = a^p$ with $p$ an odd prime. Factor:
$$P_k = a^p + 1 = (a+1) \cdot \Phi, \quad \text{where } \Phi = \frac{a^p + 1}{a+
1} = a^{p-1} - a^{p-2} + \cdots - a + 1.$$

**Claim:** $p > p_k$.

Suppose for contradiction that $p \leq p_k$. Then $p$ is one of the primes $p_1,
 \ldots, p_k$, so $p \mid P_k$. By Fermat's little theorem, $a^p \equiv a \pmod{
p}$, so $P_k = a^p + 1 \equiv a + 1 \pmod{p}$. Since $p \mid P_k$, we get $p \mi
d (a+1)$.

Now evaluate $\Phi$ modulo $(a+1)$: substituting $a = -1$ gives $\Phi(-1) = \und
erbrace{1 + 1 + \cdots + 1}_{p} = p$ (since $p$ is odd, the alternating signs al
l become $+1$). So $\Phi \equiv p \pmod{a+1}$. Since $p \mid (a+1)$, we get $p \
mid \Phi$.

Thus $p \mid (a+1)$ and $p \mid \Phi$, so $p^2 \mid P_k$. But $P_k = 2 \cdot 3 \
cdot 5 \cdots p_k$ is **squarefree**. Contradiction. $\square$

### Step 5: Contradiction for $k \geq 3$

Now $p > p_k \geq 5$, so $p \geq 7$ (in particular $p \geq 3$). Since $P_k$ is e
ven, $a^p = P_k - 1$ is odd, so $a$ is odd, hence $2 \mid (a+1)$.

For each prime $q$ with $3 \leq q \leq p_k$: since $q \mid P_k = a^p + 1$, we ha
ve $a^p \equiv -1 \pmod{q}$. The multiplicative order $\operatorname{ord}_q(a)$
divides $2p$ but not $p$ (since $a^p \equiv -1 \not\equiv 1$), so $\operatorname
{ord}_q(a) \in \{2, 2p\}$. If $\operatorname{ord}_q(a) = 2p$, then $2p \mid (q-1
)$. But $q \leq p_k < p$, so $q - 1 < p < 2p$, making $2p \mid (q-1)$ impossible
. Therefore $\operatorname{ord}_q(a) = 2$, meaning $a \equiv -1 \pmod{q}$, i.e.,
 $q \mid (a+1)$.

Since $2, 3, 5, \ldots, p_k$ all divide $a + 1$ and are distinct primes:
$$P_k \mid (a+1), \quad \text{so } a + 1 \geq P_k, \quad \text{i.e., } a \geq P_
k - 1.$$

But $a^p = P_k - 1$ with $p \geq 3$, so:
$$a = (P_k - 1)^{1/p} \leq (P_k - 1)^{1/3}.$$

For $k \geq 3$, $P_k \geq 30$, so $P_k - 1 \geq 29 > 1$, and thus $(P_k - 1)^{1/
3} < P_k - 1$. This gives:
$$a \geq P_k - 1 > (P_k - 1)^{1/3} \geq (P_k - 1)^{1/p} = a,$$
a contradiction.

### Conclusion

- $k = 1$: $P_1 - 1 = 1$, no solution ($a > 1$ required).
- $k = 2$: $P_2 - 1 = 5$, prime, no solution.
- $k \geq 3$: The exponent $p$ must exceed $p_k$ (Step 4), which forces $P_k \mi
d (a+1)$, giving $a \geq P_k - 1$ while $a = (P_k-1)^{1/p} \leq (P_k-1)^{1/3} <
P_k - 1$ — contradiction.

**There are no natural numbers $k$ satisfying the given condition.**
