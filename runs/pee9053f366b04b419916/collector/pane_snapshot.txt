## Answer

**Yes.** The induced measure $\mu_X$ can be complete even when $\mathbb{P}$ is n
ot.

### Setup and definitions

Let $X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^n$ be $(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n))$-measu
rable. The pushforward (induced) measure is defined on the σ-algebra
$$\sigma_X := \{A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n : X^{-1}(A)\in\mathcal{F}\}$$
by $\mu_X(A)=\mathbb{P}(X^{-1}(A))$. This is the largest σ-algebra on $\mathbb{R
}^n$ for which $X$ is measurable, and it contains $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)$.

**Recall** $\mu_X$ is *complete* on $\sigma_X$ if whenever $A\in\sigma_X$ with $
\mu_X(A)=0$ and $B\subseteq A$, then $B\in\sigma_X$ (and $\mu_X(B)=0$).

### The standard implication (complete $\mathbb{P}$ $\Rightarrow$ complete $\mu_
X$)

If $\mathbb{P}$ is complete: take $A\in\sigma_X$ with $\mu_X(A)=0$ and $B\subset
eq A$. Then $X^{-1}(A)\in\mathcal{F}$ with $\mathbb{P}(X^{-1}(A))=0$, and $X^{-1
}(B)\subseteq X^{-1}(A)$. Completeness of $\mathbb{P}$ gives $X^{-1}(B)\in\mathc
al{F}$, hence $B\in\sigma_X$. So $\mu_X$ is complete.

### The converse fails: a counterexample

Take $\Omega=\{1,2,3,4\}$, $\mathcal{F}=\{\varnothing,\{1,2\},\{3,4\},\Omega\}$,
 and $\mathbb{P}(\{1,2\})=0$, $\mathbb{P}(\{3,4\})=1$. Then $\{1,2\}$ is a $\mat
hbb{P}$-null set, but its subset $\{1\}\notin\mathcal{F}$, so **$\mathbb{P}$ is
not complete**.

Now let $X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$ be the **constant** map $X(\omega)=c$ for all $\
omega$. For any $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}$:
$$X^{-1}(A)=\begin{cases}\Omega & c\in A\\\varnothing & c\notin A\end{cases}$$
Both $\Omega$ and $\varnothing$ lie in $\mathcal{F}$, so $\sigma_X=\mathcal{P}(\
mathbb{R})$ (the full power set), and
$$\mu_X(A)=\begin{cases}1 & c\in A\\0 & c\notin A\end{cases}$$
This is the Dirac measure $\delta_c$ on $\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})$.

**$\mu_X$ is complete:** if $\mu_X(A)=0$ then $c\notin A$; any $B\subseteq A$ al
so satisfies $c\notin B$, so $B\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})=\sigma_X$ and $\mu_X(B
)=0$.

Thus $\mu_X$ is complete while $\mathbb{P}$ is not. $\square$

### Why this happens

Completeness of $\mu_X$ on $\sigma_X$ requires: for every $A\in\sigma_X$ with $\
mu_X(A)=0$ and every $B\subseteq A$, $X^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal{F}$. Completeness of
$\mathbb{P}$ is *sufficient* (via $X^{-1}(B)\subseteq X^{-1}(A)$), but not *nece
ssary*: the preimage $X^{-1}(B)$ may land in $\mathcal{F}$ for structural reason
s independent of $\mathbb{P}$'s completeness. A constant $X$ forces every preima
ge to be $\varnothing$ or $\Omega$, both always measurable, so the incompletenes
s of $\mathbb{P}$ is invisible to the pushforward. More generally, any $X$ that
"collapses" the non-measurable null subsets (so their preimages remain in $\math
cal{F}$) yields a complete $\mu_X$ from a non-complete $\mathbb{P}$.
