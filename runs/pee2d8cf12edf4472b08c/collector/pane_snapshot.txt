## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, $\hat{A} = A + E$ is diagonalizable (over $\mathbb{C}$) with pro
bability 1, for any $\epsilon > 0$.

**Key idea.** A matrix with $n$ distinct eigenvalues is diagonalizable. We show
$\hat{A}$ has distinct eigenvalues almost surely by proving the set of "bad" dia
gonal perturbations (those producing a repeated eigenvalue) is a proper algebrai
c variety of Lebesgue measure zero, which the Gaussian measure does not charge.

---

**Step 1: The discriminant is a polynomial.**

Let $E = \operatorname{diag}(e_1, \dots, e_n)$. The characteristic polynomial of
 $\hat{A}$ is

$$p(\lambda; e_1, \dots, e_n) = \det(\lambda I - A - \operatorname{diag}(e_1, \d
ots, e_n)),$$

which is a polynomial in $\lambda$ whose coefficients are polynomials in $e_1, \
dots, e_n$. The **discriminant**

$$D(e_1, \dots, e_n) := \operatorname{disc}_\lambda\bigl(p(\lambda;\, e_1, \dots
, e_n)\bigr)$$

is therefore a polynomial in $e_1, \dots, e_n$. By definition, $D = 0$ if and on
ly if $p$ has a repeated root, i.e., $\hat{A}$ has a repeated eigenvalue.

**Step 2: $D$ is not the zero polynomial.**

We exhibit a specific diagonal $E_0$ for which $A + E_0$ has $n$ distinct eigenv
alues. Set

$$E_0 = t \cdot \operatorname{diag}(1, 2, \dots, n), \quad t > 0,$$

and consider $A_t = A + E_0$. The diagonal entries of $A_t$ are $a_{ii} + i\,t$;
 the off-diagonal entries are those of $A$.

By **Gershgorin's circle theorem**, every eigenvalue of $A_t$ lies in $\bigcup_{
i=1}^n \overline{D}(a_{ii} + it,\, R_i)$, where $R_i = \sum_{j \neq i} |a_{ij}|$
 is a fixed constant (independent of $t$).

For $i \neq j$, the distance between centers satisfies

$$|a_{ii} + it - a_{jj} - jt| \geq |i - j|\,t - |a_{ii} - a_{jj}| \geq t - \max_
{i \neq j}|a_{ii} - a_{jj}|.$$

Choosing $t > 2\max_k R_k + \max_{i\neq j}|a_{ii} - a_{jj}|$ ensures every pair
of discs is disjoint. When all Gershgorin discs are pairwise disjoint, each disc
 contains **exactly one** eigenvalue (by continuity of eigenvalues and the fact
that a diagonal matrix has its eigenvalues at the disc centers). Hence $A_t$ has
 $n$ distinct eigenvalues.

Therefore $D(t, 2t, \dots, nt) \neq 0$ for all sufficiently large $t$, proving $
D$ is **not** the zero polynomial.

**Step 3: The bad set has measure zero.**

Since $D$ is a nonzero polynomial, its zero set

$$Z = \{(e_1, \dots, e_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n : D(e_1, \dots, e_n) = 0\}$$

is a proper real algebraic variety, which has **Lebesgue measure zero** in $\mat
hbb{R}^n$.

**Step 4: Probability is zero.**

The random vector $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ has distribution $\prod_{i=1}^n N(0, \epsi
lon^2)$, which is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure for eve
ry $\epsilon > 0$. Therefore

$$\Pr\bigl(\hat{A} \text{ has a repeated eigenvalue}\bigr) = \Pr\bigl((e_1, \dot
s, e_n) \in Z\bigr) = 0.$$

**Step 5: Conclusion.**

With probability 1, $\hat{A}$ has $n$ distinct eigenvalues. A matrix with $n$ di
stinct eigenvalues has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors and is therefore di
agonalizable (over $\mathbb{C}$).

**Remark on $\epsilon$.** The argument is independent of $\epsilon$: for any $\e
psilon > 0$, however small, the Gaussian product measure remains absolutely cont
inuous, so the probability of landing on $Z$ is zero. The result also does not d
epend on $A$ being nondiagonalizable — it holds for **any** fixed $A \in \mathbb
{R}^{n \times n}$. The role of $A$ being nondiagonalizable is only to make the q
uestion nontrivial; the random perturbation almost surely "breaks" any Jordan st
ructure.

**Remark on the field.** The conclusion is for diagonalizability over $\mathbb{C
}$. If "diagonalizable" were interpreted as over $\mathbb{R}$ (requiring all eig
envalues real), the answer would be **no** in general: a nondiagonalizable real
matrix can have complex eigenvalues (e.g., a Jordan block for a complex eigenval
ue), and small real perturbations preserve complex eigenvalues with positive pro
bability. Under the standard interpretation—diagonalizable over $\mathbb{C}$, eq
uivalently possessing a full set of eigenvectors—the answer is **yes**. $\blacks
quare$
