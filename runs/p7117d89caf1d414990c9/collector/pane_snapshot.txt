## Proof

**Answer: No.** Not every quasi-Euclidean field of characteristic zero is a Eucl
idean ordered field. We construct an explicit counterexample.

### Reformulation

The "quasi-Euclidean" condition — every nonzero $a$ satisfies exactly one of $a
\in (F^*)^2$ or $-a \in (F^*)^2$ — is equivalent to:
- $-1 \notin (F^*)^2$ (the "not both" clause, since if both $a$ and $-a$ are squ
ares then $-1 = (-a)/a$ is a square), and
- $F^* = (F^*)^2 \sqcup (-1)(F^*)^2$ (the "either" clause),

i.e., $|F^*/(F^*)^2| = 2$ with the nontrivial class represented by $-1$.

A Euclidean ordered field is an ordered field in which every positive element is
 a square. Any ordered field is formally real ($-1$ is not a sum of squares). So
 a necessary condition for being Euclidean ordered is **formal reality**.

We construct a characteristic-zero field $F$ with $|F^*/(F^*)^2| = 2$ that is **
not** formally real.

### The Base Field

Let $F_0 = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-2})$.

**$F_0$ has characteristic 0:** Clear, as it extends $\mathbb{Q}$.

**$-1$ is not a square in $F_0$:** Suppose $(a + b\sqrt{-2})^2 = -1$ for $a, b \
in \mathbb{Q}$. Then $a^2 - 2b^2 = -1$ and $2ab = 0$. If $a = 0$: $b^2 = 1/2$, n
o rational solution. If $b = 0$: $a^2 = -1$, no rational solution. So $-1 \notin
 (F_0^*)^2$.

**$-1$ is a sum of squares in $F_0$:** $-1 = (\sqrt{-2})^2 + 1^2 = -2 + 1$. Henc
e $F_0$ is **not formally real**.

### Zorn's Lemma Construction

Fix an algebraic closure $\overline{F_0}$ of $F_0$. Consider the poset $\mathcal
{P}$ of intermediate fields $F_0 \subseteq F \subseteq \overline{F_0}$ satisfyin
g $-1 \notin (F^*)^2$, ordered by inclusion.

**Every chain has an upper bound:** Given a chain $\{F_\alpha\}$ in $\mathcal{P}
$, let $F = \bigcup_\alpha F_\alpha$. Then $F$ is a field with $F_0 \subseteq F
\subseteq \overline{F_0}$. If $-1 = c^2$ for some $c \in F$, then $c \in F_\alph
a$ for some $\alpha$, giving $-1 \in (F_\alpha^*)^2$, contradiction. So $-1 \not
in (F^*)^2$ and $F \in \mathcal{P}$.

By **Zorn's Lemma**, $\mathcal{P}$ has a maximal element $F$.

### The Maximal Field is Quasi-Euclidean

**Claim:** $|F^*/(F^*)^2| = 2$.

Suppose for contradiction there exists $a \in F^*$ with $[a] \neq [1]$ and $[a]
\neq [-1]$ in $F^*/(F^*)^2$. Consider the extension $F(\sqrt{a}) \subseteq \over
line{F_0}$.

We check that $-1 \notin (F(\sqrt{a})^*)^2$: $-1$ becomes a square in $F(\sqrt{a
})$ if and only if $-1 \in \{1, a\} \cdot (F^*)^2$ (by the standard characteriza
tion of squares in a quadratic extension). This means either $-1 \in (F^*)^2$ (f
alse by hypothesis) or $-a \in (F^*)^2$, i.e., $[a] = [-1]$ (false by assumption
). So $-1 \notin (F(\sqrt{a})^*)^2$, meaning $F(\sqrt{a}) \in \mathcal{P}$, cont
radicting the maximality of $F$.

Therefore every element of $F^*$ is in the class $[1]$ or $[-1]$, giving $|F^*/(
F^*)^2| = 2$. Since $-1 \notin (F^*)^2$, the field $F$ is **quasi-Euclidean**.

### The Maximal Field is Not Formally Real

The relation $-1 = (\sqrt{-2})^2 + 1^2$ holds in $F_0$ and hence in $F$ (as $F_0
 \subseteq F$). So $-1$ is a sum of squares in $F$, meaning $F$ is **not formall
y real**.

### Conclusion

The field $F$ has:
- **Characteristic 0** (extends $\mathbb{Q}$),
- **$|F^*/(F^*)^2| = 2$ with $-1 \notin (F^*)^2$** (quasi-Euclidean), and
- **$-1$ is a sum of squares** (not formally real).

Since $F$ is not formally real, it admits **no ordering** (Artin–Schreier: a fie
ld is orderable iff it is formally real). Therefore $F$ cannot be a Euclidean or
dered field.

Hence **not every quasi-Euclidean field of characteristic zero is a Euclidean or
dered field**. The implication fails because the quasi-Euclidean condition $|F^*
/(F^*)^2| = 2$ does not force formal reality — one can have $-1 \notin (F^*)^2$
while $-1$ is nonetheless a sum of squares, and a maximal such extension yields
a quasi-Euclidean non-real field.
