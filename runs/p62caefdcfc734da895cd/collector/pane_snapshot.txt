## Proof

Let us work in ZF. Recall the Axiom of Infinity asserts the existence of an *ind
uctive* set: a set $x$ with $\varnothing \in x$ and $\forall y \in x,\; y \cup \
{y\} \in x$.

For an inductive set $x$, define
$$I(x) \;=\; \bigcap\{\, z \in \mathcal{P}(x) : z \text{ is inductive}\,\}.$$

We prove that $I(x)$ is independent of the choice of inductive $x$, hence the de
finition of $\mathbb{N}$ is well-defined.

---

**Step 1. The family is nonempty and $I(x)$ is inductive.**

Since $x$ is inductive and $x \subseteq x$, the set $x$ itself belongs to $\{\,z
 \in \mathcal{P}(x) : z \text{ inductive}\,\}$, so the family is nonempty and th
e intersection is well-defined. Clearly $I(x) \subseteq x$.

The intersection of inductive sets is inductive:
- $\varnothing$ belongs to every inductive set, so $\varnothing \in I(x)$.
- If $y \in I(x)$, then $y$ belongs to every inductive $z \subseteq x$, so $y \c
up \{y\}$ belongs to every such $z$, hence $y \cup \{y\} \in I(x)$.

Thus $I(x)$ is inductive.

---

**Step 2. $I(x)$ is an inductive subset of $x$, hence is among the sets being in
tersected.**

From Step 1, $I(x) \subseteq x$ and $I(x)$ is inductive, so $I(x) \in \{\,z \in
\mathcal{P}(x) : z \text{ inductive}\,\}$. Therefore
$$I(x) \subseteq z \quad\text{for every inductive } z \subseteq x.$$

---

**Step 3. $I(x)$ is contained in *every* inductive set (not merely subsets of $x
$).**

Let $w$ be any inductive set. Then $w \cap x$ is inductive (intersection of two
inductive sets) and $w \cap x \subseteq x$, so $w \cap x$ is an inductive subset
 of $x$. By Step 2,
$$I(x) \subseteq w \cap x \subseteq w.$$

Since $w$ was arbitrary, $I(x)$ is contained in every inductive set.

---

**Step 4. Independence of the choice of $x$.**

Let $x'$ be any other inductive set, and let $I(x')$ be defined analogously. By
Step 3 applied to $x$:
- $I(x)$ is contained in every inductive set. Since $I(x')$ is inductive (Step 1
), we get $I(x) \subseteq I(x')$.

By Step 3 applied to $x'$:
- $I(x')$ is contained in every inductive set. Since $I(x)$ is inductive, we get
 $I(x') \subseteq I(x)$.

Combining, $I(x) = I(x')$.

---

**Conclusion.** The set $I(x) = \bigcap\{z \in \mathcal{P}(x) : z \text{ inducti
ve}\}$ is the same for every inductive set $x$. Indeed it is characterized uniqu
ely as *the inductive set contained in every inductive set* (equivalently, the s
mallest inductive set). Therefore the definition of $\mathbb{N}$ as this interse
ction is **well-defined**: it yields the same set regardless of the choice of $x
$ satisfying the Axiom of Infinity.
