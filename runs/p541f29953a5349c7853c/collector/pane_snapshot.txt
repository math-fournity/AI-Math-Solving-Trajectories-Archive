## Proof

**Claim:** $R$ is **not** necessarily Noetherian. We construct an explicit count
erexample.

### Construction

Let $p_1, p_2, p_3, \ldots$ denote the primes in increasing order. Set
$$S = \{p_{2k} : k \geq 1\} = \{p_2, p_4, p_6, \ldots\}, \qquad T = \{p_{2k-1} :
 k \geq 1\} = \{p_1, p_3, p_5, \ldots\}.$$
Both $S$ and $T$ are infinite. Define
$$R = \mathbb{Z}\!\left[\tfrac{1}{p} : p \in S\right],$$
the localization of $\mathbb{Z}$ obtained by inverting all primes in $S$. This i
s a subring of $\mathbb{Q}$, hence an integral domain.

### $R$ is Hopfian (every surjective endomorphism is injective)

Let $f: R \to R$ be a ring homomorphism with $f(1)=1$.

**Step 1: $f$ fixes $\mathbb{Z}$ pointwise.** Since $f(1)=1$, we have $f(n)=n\cd
ot f(1)=n$ for all $n\geq 0$, and $f(-n)=-f(n)=-n$.

**Step 2: $f$ fixes $1/p$ for every $p\in S$.** Since $p\in\mathbb{Z}$, Step 1 g
ives $f(p)=p$. Since $p$ is a unit in $R$ (it was inverted), $f(p)=p$ is a unit
in $R$. Then
$$f(1/p)\cdot f(p) = f(1/p \cdot p) = f(1) = 1,$$
so $f(1/p) = 1/f(p) = 1/p$.

**Step 3: $f = \mathrm{id}$.** The ring $R$ is generated by $\mathbb{Z} \cup \{1
/p : p \in S\}$, and $f$ fixes every generator, so $f$ is the identity.

If we work in the convention where ring homomorphisms need not preserve $1$: sin
ce $R$ is an integral domain, its only idempotents are $0$ and $1$. If $f(1)=0$
then $f\equiv 0$, which is not surjective. If $f(1)=1$, we are in the case above
. Hence the only surjective endomorphism is the identity, which is injective. $\
checkmark$

### $R$ is not Noetherian

The ideals of $R$ are in bijection with ideals $(n)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}$ such tha
t $\gcd(n, \prod_{p\in S}p^{k_p})=1$ for all choices of exponents, i.e., all pri
me factors of $n$ lie in $T$. For such $n$, the ideal $nR$ satisfies $nR \subset
eq mR$ iff $m\mid n$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ (since $\gcd(m, \text{any } S\text{-product
})=1$ when all prime factors of $m$ are in $T$).

Let $q_1, q_2, q_3, \ldots$ be the elements of $T$ in order. Consider the chain
of ideals:
$$q_1 R \;\subsetneq\; q_1 q_2 R \;\subsetneq\; q_1 q_2 q_3 R \;\subsetneq\; \cd
ots$$

Each inclusion is strict: $q_1\cdots q_{i+1}R \subseteq q_1\cdots q_i R$ since $
q_1\cdots q_i \mid q_1\cdots q_{i+1}$, but the reverse inclusion would require $
q_1\cdots q_i R \subseteq q_1\cdots q_{i+1} R$, i.e., $q_{i+1} \mid 1$ in $R$, i
.e., $1/q_{i+1}\in R$. But $q_{i+1}\in T$ was **not** inverted, so $1/q_{i+1}\no
tin R$. Hence each inclusion is proper.

This is a strictly ascending infinite chain of ideals, so $R$ is **not Noetheria
n**. $\checkmark$

### Conclusion

The ring $R = \mathbb{Z}[1/p : p \in S]$, where $S$ is an infinite set of primes
 with infinite complement, is a commutative ring with unity that is Hopfian (eve
ry surjective endomorphism is injective—in fact, the only endomorphism is the id
entity) but is **not** Noetherian. Therefore, $R$ is not necessarily Noetherian.
