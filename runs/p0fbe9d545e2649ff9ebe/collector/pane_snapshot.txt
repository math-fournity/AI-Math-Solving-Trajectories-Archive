**Theorem.** Let $X$ be a separable Banach space and let $X^*$ denote its (conti
nuous) dual equipped with the weak-* topology $\sigma(X^*,X)$. Then $(X^*,\sigma
(X^*,X))$ is a completely regular (Tychonoff) space.

**Proof.**

The argument proceeds in four steps. In fact, separability of $X$ is not needed;
 the result holds for the weak-* dual of any normed space.

---

**Step 1. The weak-* topology embeds $X^*$ into a product of lines.**

For each $x\in X$, define the evaluation map
$$e_x : X^* \to \mathbb{R}, \qquad e_x(f)=f(x).$$
The weak-* topology $\sigma(X^*,X)$ is, by definition, the initial (coarsest) to
pology on $X^*$ making every $e_x$ continuous.

Define the evaluation embedding
$$E : X^* \to \mathbb{R}^{X}, \qquad E(f) = \bigl(f(x)\bigr)_{x\in X}.$$
This map is injective: if $E(f)=E(g)$, then $f(x)=g(x)$ for all $x\in X$, so $f=
g$.

Let $\pi_x : \mathbb{R}^X \to \mathbb{R}$ denote the $x$-coordinate projection.
The product topology on $\mathbb{R}^X$ is the initial topology for the family $\
{\pi_x\}_{x\in X}$. Since $\pi_x \circ E = e_x$ for every $x$, the initial topol
ogy on $X^*$ induced by $E$ coincides with the initial topology induced by $\{e_
x\}$, which is $\sigma(X^*,X)$. Because $E$ is injective, this initial topology
is exactly the subspace topology inherited from $\mathbb{R}^X$.

Hence $(X^*,\sigma(X^*,X))$ is homeomorphic to a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^X$ (wit
h the product topology).

---

**Step 2. $\mathbb{R}$ is completely regular.**

$\mathbb{R}$ is a metric space, hence normal, hence completely regular. In parti
cular $\mathbb{R}$ is $T_1$.

---

**Step 3. Arbitrary products of completely regular spaces are completely regular
.**

Let $\{Y_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in A}$ be a family of completely regular spaces and se
t $Y=\prod_\alpha Y_\alpha$ with the product topology.

*$Y$ is $T_1$:* Each factor is $T_1$, so each singleton $\{y_\alpha\}$ is closed
 in $Y_\alpha$; a point $p\in Y$ is $\prod_\alpha \{p_\alpha\}$, an intersection
 of closed sets $\pi_\alpha^{-1}(\{p_\alpha\})$, hence closed.

*Separation:* Let $p\in Y$ and let $F\subseteq Y$ be closed with $p\notin F$. Th
en $U:=Y\setminus F$ is an open neighborhood of $p$, so there exists a basic ope
n set $V=\prod_\alpha V_\alpha$ (with $V_\alpha=Y_\alpha$ for all but finitely m
any $\alpha$, and each $V_\alpha$ open in $Y_\alpha$) such that
$$p\in V\subseteq U.$$
Let $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n$ be the finitely many indices with $V_{\alpha_i}\ne
q Y_{\alpha_i}$. For each $i$, since $Y_{\alpha_i}$ is completely regular and $p
_{\alpha_i}\in V_{\alpha_i}$ (open), the closed set $Y_{\alpha_i}\setminus V_{\a
lpha_i}$ does not contain $p_{\alpha_i}$, so there exists a continuous function
$g_i:Y_{\alpha_i}\to[0,1]$ with
$$g_i(p_{\alpha_i})=0, \qquad g_i\bigl(Y_{\alpha_i}\setminus V_{\alpha_i}\bigr)=
\{1\}.$$
Define $g:Y\to[0,1]$ by
$$g(y)=\max_{1\le i\le n}\, g_i\bigl(\pi_{\alpha_i}(y)\bigr).$$
Each $g_i\circ\pi_{\alpha_i}$ is continuous, and the maximum of finitely many co
ntinuous functions is continuous, so $g$ is continuous. Moreover:
- $g(p)=\max_i g_i(p_{\alpha_i})=0$.
- If $y\in Y\setminus V$, then $y\notin V=\prod V_\alpha$, so for some $i$, $y_{
\alpha_i}\notin V_{\alpha_i}$, giving $g_i(y_{\alpha_i})=1$ and hence $g(y)=1$.

Since $F\subseteq Y\setminus V$, we have $g|_F\equiv 1$. Thus $Y$ is completely
regular.

Applying this with $Y_\alpha=\mathbb{R}$ for all $\alpha\in X$, the product $\ma
thbb{R}^X$ is completely regular.

---

**Step 4. Subspaces of completely regular spaces are completely regular.**

Let $Z$ be a completely regular space and $S\subseteq Z$ a subspace.

*$S$ is $T_1$:* For $s\in S$, the singleton $\{s\}$ is closed in $Z$, hence $\{s
\}=S\cap\{s\}$ is closed in $S$.

*Separation:* Let $s\in S$ and let $F$ be closed in $S$ with $s\notin F$. Write
$F=S\cap G$ for some $G$ closed in $Z$. Since $s\in S$ and $s\notin F=S\cap G$,
we have $s\notin G$. By complete regularity of $Z$, there exists continuous $h:Z
\to[0,1]$ with $h(s)=0$ and $h(G)=\{1\}$. The restriction $h|_S:S\to[0,1]$ is co
ntinuous, satisfies $h|_S(s)=0$, and $h|_S(F)\subseteq h(G)=\{1\}$. Hence $S$ is
 completely regular.

---

**Conclusion.**

By Step 1, $(X^*,\sigma(X^*,X))$ is homeomorphic to a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^X$
. By Steps 2 and 3, $\mathbb{R}^X$ is completely regular. By Step 4, every subsp
ace of $\mathbb{R}^X$ is completely regular. Therefore

$$\boxed{(X^*,\,\sigma(X^*,X)) \text{ is completely regular.}}$$

**Remark.** The separability hypothesis on $X$ is not used in the proof; the wea
k-* dual of *any* normed (or topological vector) space is completely regular, si
nce it is always a subspace of a product of completely regular spaces. Separabil
ity of $X$ does yield a stronger property—by Banach–Alaoglu together with separa
bility, the closed unit ball of $X^*$ is weak-* compact and metrizable—but compl
ete regularity of the full space $X^*$ holds without it.
