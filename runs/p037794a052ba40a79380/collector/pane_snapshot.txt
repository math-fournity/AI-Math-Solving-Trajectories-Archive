Looking at this problem, I need to find the natural number $n$ such that for **a
ny** two sets of $n$ distinct boundary points, there exists a **unique** automor
phism of $\mathbb{D}$ mapping one set to the other.

**Answer: $n = 3$.**

## Proof

### Setup: The Automorphism Group of $\mathbb{D}$

Via the Cayley transform $C: \mathbb{D} \to \mathbb{H}$ (upper half-plane), the
boundary $\mathbb{S}^1$ maps to $\widehat{\mathbb{R}} = \mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty
\} \cong \mathbb{RP}^1$. The automorphism group of $\mathbb{D}$ (the isometry gr
oup of the Poincaré disk, i.e., all Möbius and anti-Möbius transformations prese
rving $\mathbb{D}$) is conjugate to $\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ acting on $\wid
ehat{\mathbb{R}}$ by:
- **Orientation-preserving** ($\det > 0$): $z \mapsto \frac{az+b}{cz+d}$, and
- **Orientation-reversing** ($\det < 0$): $z \mapsto \frac{a\bar{z}+b}{c\bar{z}+
d}$,

with $a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{R}$. This is a **3-dimensional** real Lie group (3 rea
l parameters up to scalar).

### Step 1: $\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ is sharply 3-transitive on $\mathbb{RP}
^1$

**Existence.** Given three distinct points $x_1, x_2, x_3 \in \widehat{\mathbb{R
}}$ and three distinct points $y_1, y_2, y_3 \in \widehat{\mathbb{R}}$, define t
he cross-ratio maps:
$$\phi(z) = \frac{(z - x_1)(x_2 - x_3)}{(z - x_3)(x_2 - x_1)}, \qquad \psi(z) =
\frac{(z - y_1)(y_2 - y_3)}{(z - y_3)(y_2 - y_1)}.$$
Both $\phi$ and $\psi$ are Möbius transformations with **real coefficients** (si
nce all $x_i, y_i \in \mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty\}$), so $\phi, \psi \in \mathrm{P
GL}(2,\mathbb{R})$. We have $\phi(x_1) = 0,\ \phi(x_2) = 1,\ \phi(x_3) = \infty$
 and similarly $\psi(y_1) = 0,\ \psi(y_2) = 1,\ \psi(y_3) = \infty$. Then $T = \
psi^{-1} \circ \phi \in \mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ satisfies $T(x_i) = y_i$ for
 all $i$.

**Uniqueness.** If two elements of $\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ agree on three d
istinct points of $\widehat{\mathbb{R}}$, their ratio is a Möbius transformation
 vanishing at three distinct points, hence is identically zero — so they are equ
al. (Equivalently, $\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ has exactly 3 real degrees of fr
eedom, and 3 point constraints exhaust them.)

### Step 2: Sharp 3-transitivity transfers to $\mathbb{S}^1$

Under the Cayley transform $C$, the automorphism group of $\mathbb{D}$ is $C^{-1
}\,\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{R})\,C$, and $\mathbb{S}^1 = C^{-1}(\widehat{\mathbb{R
}})$. Since $\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ is sharply 3-transitive on $\widehat{\m
athbb{R}}$, the automorphism group of $\mathbb{D}$ is **sharply 3-transitive on
$\mathbb{S}^1$**: for any three distinct source points and any three distinct ta
rget points on $\mathbb{S}^1$, there exists a **unique** automorphism of $\mathb
b{D}$ mapping source to target.

### Step 3: $n = 3$ works

By Step 2, for **any** two sets $\{x_1, x_2, x_3\}$ and $\{y_1, y_2, y_3\}$ of t
hree distinct points on $\mathbb{S}^1$, there exists a unique automorphism $f$ w
ith $f(x_i) = y_i$. ✓

### Step 4: $n \leq 2$ fails (uniqueness)

The automorphism group is 3-dimensional. Specifying $f(x_1) = y_1$ imposes 1 rea
l constraint (both points lie on the 1-dimensional $\mathbb{S}^1$), leaving a 2-
parameter family. Specifying two correspondences imposes 2 constraints, leaving
a **1-parameter family** — so uniqueness fails for $n = 2$ (and worse for $n = 1
$).

### Step 5: $n \geq 4$ fails (existence)

For $n = 4$: the first three point correspondences uniquely determine an automor
phism $T$ (by sharp 3-transitivity). The fourth image $T(x_4)$ is then **forced*
*. For a generic choice of $y_4 \neq T(x_4)$, no automorphism satisfies all four
 conditions. Hence existence fails for some choices when $n = 4$, and similarly
for all $n \geq 4$.

### Conclusion

- $n \leq 2$: uniqueness fails.
- $n = 3$: existence and uniqueness hold for **all** choices. ✓
- $n \geq 4$: existence fails for some choices.

$$\boxed{n = 3}$$
