## Proof

**Claim:** The statement is **true**: $\displaystyle I(t) = \int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0
}^{1} n(x,y,t)\,dy\,dx = 0$ for every $t \ge 0$.

The strategy is a **symmetry (odd-in-$x$) argument** combined with uniqueness of
 the Cauchy–Neumann problem.

---

### Step 1. The initial data is odd in $x$.

The initial condition is $n(x,y,0) = x$, so
$$n(-x,y,0) = -x = -n(x,y,0),$$
i.e., $n(\cdot,y,0)$ is an **odd function** of $x$ for every $y \in [0,1]$.

---

### Step 2. The PDE and boundary conditions preserve odd-in-$x$ symmetry.

Suppose $n(x,y,t)$ is a solution. Define the reflected function
$$m(x,y,t) := -\,n(-x,\,y,\,t).$$

We verify that $m$ satisfies the same PDE, the same boundary conditions, and the
 same initial condition.

**PDE.** Compute each term of $\partial_t m + 2xt\,\partial_x m$:

- $\partial_t m(x,y,t) = -\,\partial_t n(-x,y,t)$,
- $\partial_x m(x,y,t) = -\,\partial_x n(-x,y,t)\cdot(-1) = \partial_x n(-x,y,t)
$,
- $\partial^2_{yy} m(x,y,t) = -\,\partial^2_{yy} n(-x,y,t)$.

Hence the left-hand side of the PDE for $m$ is:
$$\partial_t m + 2xt\,\partial_x m = -\,\partial_t n(-x,y,t) + 2xt\,\partial_x n
(-x,y,t). \tag{$\ast$}$$

Now evaluate the original PDE at the point $(-x, y, t)$:
$$\partial_t n(-x,y,t) + 2(-x)t\,\partial_x n(-x,y,t) = n(-x,y,t) + \frac{1}{1+t
^\alpha}\,\partial^2_{yy} n(-x,y,t),$$
i.e.,
$$\partial_t n(-x,y,t) - 2xt\,\partial_x n(-x,y,t) = n(-x,y,t) + \frac{1}{1+t^\a
lpha}\,\partial^2_{yy} n(-x,y,t).$$

Negating both sides:
$$-\,\partial_t n(-x,y,t) + 2xt\,\partial_x n(-x,y,t) = -\,n(-x,y,t) - \frac{1}{
1+t^\alpha}\,\partial^2_{yy} n(-x,y,t).$$

The left-hand side is exactly $(\ast)$. The right-hand side is:
$$m(x,y,t) + \frac{1}{1+t^\alpha}\,\partial^2_{yy} m(x,y,t).$$

Therefore $m$ satisfies the same PDE. ✓

**Boundary conditions.** Since $\partial_y m(x,y,t) = -\,\partial_y n(-x,y,t)$:
$$\partial_y m(x,0,t) = -\,\partial_y n(-x,0,t) = 0, \qquad \partial_y m(x,1,t)
= -\,\partial_y n(-x,1,t) = 0. \quad\checkmark$$

**Initial condition.**
$$m(x,y,0) = -\,n(-x,y,0) = -(-x) = x = n(x,y,0). \quad\checkmark$$

---

### Step 3. Uniqueness gives $m = n$.

The PDE
$$\partial_t n + 2xt\,\partial_x n = n + \frac{1}{1+t^\alpha}\,\partial^2_{yy} n
$$
is a **linear** equation with smooth coefficients (the transport field $b(x,t) =
 2xt$ is smooth, the diffusion coefficient $a(t) = \frac{1}{1+t^\alpha} > 0$ is
smooth for $t > 0$ and continuous at $t = 0$). With the Neumann conditions in $y
$ and the given initial data, the Cauchy–Neumann problem admits a **unique** sol
ution (in the class of, say, bounded or polynomially growing classical solutions
; standard parabolic theory / energy methods apply since the $y$-diffusion provi
des the dissipative mechanism while the $x$-transport is a smooth drift).

Since both $n$ and $m$ solve the same Cauchy–Neumann problem with the same data,
 uniqueness yields
$$m(x,y,t) = n(x,y,t) \quad \text{for all } (x,y,t).$$

By definition of $m$, this means:
$$\boxed{n(-x,\,y,\,t) = -\,n(x,\,y,\,t) \quad \text{for all } (x,y,t),}$$
i.e., **$n$ is odd in $x$ for every $t \ge 0$.**

---

### Step 4. The integral vanishes.

Since $n(\cdot, y, t)$ is odd in $x$ and the domain $[-1,1]$ is symmetric about
the origin:
$$\int_{-1}^{1} n(x,y,t)\,dx = 0 \quad \text{for every } y \in [0,1],\; t \ge 0.
$$

Integrating over $y \in [0,1]$:
$$I(t) = \int_{0}^{1}\!\left(\int_{-1}^{1} n(x,y,t)\,dx\right)dy = \int_{0}^{1}
0\,dy = 0 \quad \text{for every } t \ge 0.$$

---

### Conclusion

The statement is **true**: $\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{1} n(x,y,t)\,dy\
,dx = 0$ for each $t \ge 0$.

The key mechanism is that the initial datum $n(x,y,0)=x$ is odd in $x$, the tran
sport coefficient $2xt$ is odd in $x$, and all other terms (the reaction term $n
$, the $y$-diffusion $\frac{1}{1+t^\alpha}\partial^2_{yy}n$, and the Neumann bou
ndary conditions) are even in $x$ (or $x$-independent). This structural symmetry
 is preserved by the evolution, and uniqueness forces the solution to remain odd
 in $x$ for all time, making the integral over the symmetric $x$-interval $[-1,1
]$ vanish identically.
