## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, it is always possible to find a non-zero $R$-homomorphism $M \to
 E(N)$.

**Setup.** We have $N \subseteq M$ with $N \neq 0$, and $E(N)$ is the injective
envelope of $N$. By definition, $N$ embeds into $E(N)$ as an essential submodule
; let $\iota \colon N \hookrightarrow E(N)$ denote this inclusion. Since $N \neq
 0$, the map $\iota$ is non-zero (in fact, injective).

**Key step.** The inclusion $j \colon N \hookrightarrow M$ is a monomorphism of
$R$-modules. Since $E(N)$ is an injective $R$-module, the defining property of i
njectivity states that every $R$-homomorphism $N \to E(N)$ extends along the mon
omorphism $j$ to an $R$-homomorphism $M \to E(N)$.

Applying this to $\iota \colon N \to E(N)$, there exists an $R$-homomorphism $f
\colon M \to E(N)$ such that the diagram

$$
N \xrightarrow{\;j\;} M
$$
$$\iota \searrow \quad \downarrow f$$
$$E(N)$$

commutes, i.e., $f \circ j = \iota$, or equivalently $f|_N = \iota$.

**Non-vanishing.** Since $N \neq 0$ and $\iota$ is injective, $\iota$ is a non-z
ero map. Therefore $f|_N = \iota \neq 0$, which forces $f \neq 0$.

**Remark.** The hypothesis that $M$ is finitely generated is not needed; the arg
ument works for any $R$-module $M$ containing a non-zero submodule $N$. The only
 ingredients are the injectivity of $E(N)$ and the non-vanishing of the inclusio
n $N \hookrightarrow E(N)$.
