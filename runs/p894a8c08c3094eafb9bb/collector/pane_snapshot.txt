## Proof

**Answer-leak check:** The problem statement contains no solutions, sketches, or
 hints. Proceeding.

**Goal.** Show that under the hypotheses
- $f\in C^1[0,\infty)$,
- $f\in L^1[0,\infty)$ and $f'\in L^1[0,\infty)$ (both Lebesgue integrable),

we have $\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x)=0$.

---

### Step 1. The limit of $f$ at infinity exists (and is finite).

Since $f$ is continuously differentiable, the (Lebesgue) fundamental theorem of
calculus gives, for every $x\ge 0$,

$$f(x)=f(0)+\int_0^x f'(t)\,dt.$$

Because $f'\in L^1[0,\infty)$, the improper integral $\int_0^x f'(t)\,dt$ conver
ges as $x\to\infty$ to the finite value $\int_0^\infty f'(t)\,dt$ (absolute inte
grability implies the tail integrals vanish: $\bigl|\int_x^\infty f'(t)\,dt\bigr
|\le \int_x^\infty |f'(t)|\,dt\to 0$). Hence

$$\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) \;=\; f(0)+\int_0^\infty f'(t)\,dt \;=:\; L,$$

where $L\in\mathbb{R}$ is a well-defined finite real number.

### Step 2. The limit must be $0$.

We now use the integrability of $f$ itself. Suppose, for contradiction, that $L\
neq 0$; without loss assume $L>0$ (the case $L<0$ is symmetric, applied to $-f$)
.

Pick $\varepsilon = L/2>0$. Since $f(x)\to L$, there exists $N\ge 0$ such that f
or all $x\ge N$,

$$f(x)\ge L-\varepsilon = \frac{L}{2}>0.$$

In particular $f$ is nonnegative on $[N,\infty)$, so its Lebesgue integral there
 equals the improper Riemann integral, and

$$\int_N^\infty |f(x)|\,dx \;=\; \int_N^\infty f(x)\,dx \;\ge\; \int_N^\infty \f
rac{L}{2}\,dx \;=\; +\infty.$$

This contradicts $f\in L^1[0,\infty)$, which requires $\int_0^\infty |f(x)|\,dx<
\infty$ (and hence $\int_N^\infty |f(x)|\,dx<\infty$).

The case $L<0$ is identical with $-f$, giving $\int_N^\infty |f(x)|\,dx\ge \int_
N^\infty |L|/2\,dx=+\infty$, again a contradiction.

Therefore $L=0$.

### Conclusion.

Combining Steps 1 and 2,

$$\boxed{\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x)=0.}$$

**Remark on the role of each hypothesis.** The integrability of $f'$ is what gua
rantees the *existence* of the limit $L$: it lets us write $f$ as a convergent i
ntegral of an $L^1$ function. The integrability of $f$ then forces that limit to
 be $0$: a continuous function tending to a nonzero constant cannot be integrabl
e on a half-line. Neither hypothesis alone suffices — e.g. $f(x)=\sin(x^2)$ has
$f\in L^1$ but no limit at infinity (and $f'\notin L^1$), while a nonzero consta
nt has $f'\in L^1$ but $f\notin L^1$.
