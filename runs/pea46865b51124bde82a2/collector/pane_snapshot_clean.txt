## Solution

**Function.** $f(z) = \dfrac{e^z - \sin z - 1}{z^5 + z^3}$, with denominator $z^
5+z^3 = z^3(z^2+1)$.

### Step 1. Expand the numerator about $z=0$

Using the Taylor series
$$e^z = 1 + z + \tfrac{z^2}{2} + \tfrac{z^3}{6} + \tfrac{z^4}{24} + \tfrac{z^5}{
120} + \tfrac{z^6}{720} + \cdots,$$
$$\sin z = z - \tfrac{z^3}{6} + \tfrac{z^5}{120} - \cdots,$$

we subtract term by term:
$$e^z - \sin z - 1 = \tfrac{z^2}{2} + \left(\tfrac{1}{6}+\tfrac{1}{6}\right)z^3
+ \tfrac{z^4}{24} + \left(\tfrac{1}{120}-\tfrac{1}{120}\right)z^5 + \tfrac{z^6}{
720} + \cdots$$
$$= \tfrac{z^2}{2} + \tfrac{z^3}{3} + \tfrac{z^4}{24} + 0\cdot z^5 + \tfrac{z^6}
{720} + \cdots$$

The lowest-order term is $\tfrac{z^2}{2}$, so the numerator vanishes to **order
2** at $z=0$ (the $z^5$ coefficient conveniently cancels, but that does not affe
ct the leading behavior).

### Step 2. Nature of the singularity at $z=0$

Factor the denominator: $z^5+z^3 = z^3(z^2+1)$, which vanishes to **order 3** at
 $z=0$. Since the numerator vanishes to order 2 and the denominator to order 3,

$$f(z) = \frac{\tfrac{z^2}{2}\bigl(1 + \tfrac{2}{3}z + \tfrac{1}{12}z^2 + \cdots
\bigr)}{z^3(z^2+1)} = \frac{1}{z}\cdot \underbrace{\frac{\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1 + \
tfrac{2}{3}z + \tfrac{1}{12}z^2 + \cdots\bigr)}{z^2+1}}_{=:g(z)}.$$

The function $g(z)$ is analytic in a neighborhood of $z=0$ (its denominator $z^2
+1=1\neq 0$ there) and
$$g(0) = \frac{1/2}{1} = \frac{1}{2} \neq 0.$$

Therefore $z=0$ is a **simple pole** (pole of order 1) of $f$.

### Step 3. Residue at $z=0$

For a simple pole written as $f(z)=\dfrac{g(z)}{z}$ with $g$ analytic and $g(0)\
neq 0$, the residue is $g(0)$. Hence
$$\operatorname{Res}_{z=0} f(z) = g(0) = \frac{1}{2}.$$

*Cross-check via the limit formula.* For a simple pole,
$$\operatorname{Res}_{z=0} f(z) = \lim_{z\to 0} z\,f(z) = \lim_{z\to 0}\frac{e^z
-\sin z - 1}{z^2(z^2+1)} = \frac{\tfrac{1}{2}}{1} = \frac{1}{2},$$
since $\frac{e^z-\sin z -1}{z^2}\to \frac{1}{2}$ and $z^2+1\to 1$. Confirmed.

### Step 4. Other singularities (for completeness of "nature of singularities")

The denominator $z^3(z^2+1)$ also vanishes at $z=\pm i$, where $z^2+1$ has **sim
ple zeros** (derivative $2z\neq 0$ at $z=\pm i$) and the factor $z^3$ is nonzero
. The numerator at these points is
$$e^{i}-\sin i - 1 = (\cos 1 - 1) + i(\sin 1 - \sinh 1) \approx -0.4597 - 0.3337
\,i \neq 0,$$
and similarly $e^{-i}-\sin(-i)-1 = (\cos 1-1)+i(\sinh 1-\sin 1)\neq 0$. Hence $z
=i$ and $z=-i$ are **simple poles** of $f$.

### Conclusion

- $z=0$: **simple pole**, with $\operatorname{Res}_{z=0} f(z) = \boxed{\dfrac{1}
{2}}$.
- $z=\pm i$: **simple poles** (residues not requested, but they are finite and n
onzero).
