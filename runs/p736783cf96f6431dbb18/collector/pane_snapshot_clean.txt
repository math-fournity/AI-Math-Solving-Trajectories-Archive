## Solution

We consider the infinite product
$$P = \frac{1}{2}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_n-1}{p_n} = \frac{1}{2}\prod_{p\tex
t{ prime}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right),$$
where $p_n$ denotes the $n$-th prime.

### Step 1. The partial products have a limit.

Define $P_N = \frac{1}{2}\prod_{n=1}^{N}\frac{p_n-1}{p_n}$. Each factor satisfie
s $0 < \frac{p_n-1}{p_n} < 1$, so the sequence $(P_N)$ is positive and strictly
decreasing. Being bounded below by $0$, it converges to some limit $L \ge 0$. It
 remains to determine whether $L = 0$ or $L > 0$.

### Step 2. Relate the product to a partial harmonic sum (Euler's argument).

For any $x \ge 2$, expand each geometric series:
$$\prod_{p \le x}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{-1} = \prod_{p \le x}\left(1+\frac{
1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^2}+\cdots\right).$$
Multiplying out (and using unique prime factorization, which guarantees each int
eger appears exactly once), the right-hand side equals
$$\sum_{\substack{n \ge 1 \\ \text{every prime factor of } n \text{ is } \le x}}
 \frac{1}{n}.$$
Every integer $n \le x$ has all its prime factors $\le x$, so this sum contains
the partial harmonic sum:
$$\prod_{p \le x}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{-1} \;\ge\; \sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor x \
rfloor}\frac{1}{n} \;\ge\; \int_1^{\lfloor x\rfloor+1}\frac{dt}{t} \;>\; \log x.
$$

### Step 3. Conclude the product tends to $0$.

Taking reciprocals (all quantities are positive):
$$\prod_{p \le x}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right) \;<\; \frac{1}{\log x}.$$
As $x \to \infty$, the right-hand side tends to $0$. Since the partial product o
n the left is nonnegative and decreasing in $x$, it must tend to $0$:
$$\prod_{p\text{ prime}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right) = \lim_{x\to\infty}\prod_{p\l
e x}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right) = 0.$$

### Step 4. Final value.

Therefore
$$P = \frac{1}{2}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_n-1}{p_n} = \frac{1}{2}\cdot 0 = 0.
$$

### Remark on terminology.

The partial products form a monotone decreasing sequence converging to $0$. Unde
r the convention that an infinite product "converges" when its partial products
tend to a finite limit (allowing $0$), the product **converges to $0$**. Under t
he stricter convention (used in some analysis texts) where "convergence" of $\pr
od(1+a_n)$ requires a *nonzero* limit, the product is said to "diverge to $0$";
in either convention the limiting value of the partial products is $0$.

### Answer.

The infinite product converges (its partial products tend to a finite limit), an
d its value is
$$\boxed{0}.$$
