## Proof

**Yes, such a function exists.** We construct one explicitly.

### Construction

Enumerate the rationals in $[0,1]$ as $\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]=\{q_1,q_2,q_3,\dots\}
$. Define
$$
f(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\,\mathbf{1}_{[q_n,1]}(x),\qquad x\in[0,1].
$$
The series converges absolutely and uniformly by the Weierstrass $M$-test ($M_n=
2^{-n}$, $\sum M_n=1$), so $f$ is a well-defined bounded function with $0\le f(x
)\le 1$.

### $f$ is monotone non-decreasing

Each summand $\mathbf{1}_{[q_n,1]}$ is non-decreasing, so any partial sum is non
-decreasing, and the uniform limit $f$ is non-decreasing.

**Consequence (key fact):** A monotone function on an interval has finite one-si
ded limits at *every* point. Hence *every* discontinuity of $f$ is automatically
 a jump discontinuity (both one-sided limits exist and are finite; a discontinui
ty then means they differ). So $f$ is "entirely composed of jump discontinuities
" — it has no discontinuities of any other type.

### Locating the discontinuities

We compute the one-sided limits using monotonicity. For a non-decreasing functio
n,
$$
f(a^-)=\sup_{x<a}f(x),\qquad f(a^+)=\inf_{x>a}f(x).
$$

**At a rational $q_k$.** For $x<q_k$,
$$
f(x)=\sum_{n:\,q_n\le x}2^{-n}\le\sum_{n:\,q_n<q_k}2^{-n}.
$$
Conversely, for any finite $S\subseteq\{n:q_n<q_k\}$, setting $x_S=\max_{n\in S}
q_n<q_k$ gives $f(x_S)\ge\sum_{n\in S}2^{-n}$; taking the supremum over finite $
S$ yields
$$
f(q_k^-)=\sup_{x<q_k}f(x)=\sum_{n:\,q_n<q_k}2^{-n}.
$$
Since $q_k$ itself contributes the term $2^{-k}$,
$$
f(q_k)=\sum_{n:\,q_n\le q_k}2^{-n}=\sum_{n:\,q_n<q_k}2^{-n}+2^{-k}=f(q_k^-)+2^{-
k}.
$$
A symmetric argument gives $f(q_k^+)=\sum_{n:\,q_n\le q_k}2^{-n}=f(q_k)$. Theref
ore
$$
f(q_k^-)=f(q_k)-2^{-k}\neq f(q_k)=f(q_k^+),
$$
so $f$ has a **jump discontinuity** of size $2^{-k}>0$ at every $q_k$.

**At an irrational $\alpha\in[0,1]$.** No $q_n$ equals $\alpha$, so
$$
f(\alpha)=\sum_{n:\,q_n\le\alpha}2^{-n}=\sum_{n:\,q_n<\alpha}2^{-n}.
$$
The same supremum/infimum computation gives
$$
f(\alpha^-)=\sum_{n:\,q_n<\alpha}2^{-n}=f(\alpha),\qquad f(\alpha^+)=\sum_{n:\,q
_n\le\alpha}2^{-n}=f(\alpha).
$$
Thus $f$ is **continuous** at every irrational.

### $f$ is not piecewise continuous

The discontinuity set of $f$ is exactly $\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$, which is infinite
. The interval $[0,1]$ is itself a finite subinterval of $[0,1]$, and it contain
s infinitely many jump discontinuities. By the given definition ("piecewise cont
inuous = every finite subinterval contains only finitely many jump discontinuiti
es"), $f$ is **not** piecewise continuous.

### Conclusion

The function $f$ defined above has the property that

1. every discontinuity of $f$ is a jump discontinuity (both one-sided limits exi
st and are finite, and they differ at each discontinuity), and
2. $f$ is not piecewise continuous, since $[0,1]$ contains infinitely many such
jumps.

Hence such a function **does exist**. $\blacksquare$
