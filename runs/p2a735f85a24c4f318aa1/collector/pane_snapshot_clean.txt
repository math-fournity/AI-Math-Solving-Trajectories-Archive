The answer is **No**.

**Counterexample.**

Let $X = [0,1]$ with Lebesgue measure $\mu$. For each $n \geq 1$, define

$$
f_n(x) = \begin{cases}
+n, & 0 < x \le \tfrac{1}{2n},\\[4pt]
-n, & \tfrac{1}{2n} < x \le \tfrac{1}{n},\\[4pt]
0, & x = 0 \text{ or } x > \tfrac{1}{n}.
\end{cases}
$$

Each $f_n$ is measurable (a simple function).

**Pointwise limit.** At $x = 0$, $f_n(0) = 0$ for all $n$. For any $x > 0$, once
 $n > 1/x$ we have $x > 1/n$, hence $f_n(x) = 0$. Thus $f_n(x) \to 0$ for every
$x \in [0,1]$, i.e. $f \equiv 0$.

**Integrals of $f_n$.** By symmetry of the two halves of $[0, 1/n]$,

$$
\int_0^1 f_n\,d\mu = n\cdot\tfrac{1}{2n} + (-n)\cdot\tfrac{1}{2n} = \tfrac{1}{2}
 - \tfrac{1}{2} = 0.
$$

So $\lim_{n\to\infty}\int f_n\,d\mu = 0 = \int f\,d\mu$. The hypothesis is satis
fied.

**Integrals of $|f_n|$.** Since $|f_n| = n$ on $(0, 1/n]$ and $0$ elsewhere,

$$
\int_0^1 |f_n|\,d\mu = n \cdot \tfrac{1}{n} = 1 \quad\text{for all } n.
$$

Hence $\lim_{n\to\infty}\int |f_n|\,d\mu = 1$, whereas $\int |f|\,d\mu = \int 0\
,d\mu = 0$.

**Conclusion.** We have $\lim_n\int f_n\,d\mu = \int f\,d\mu$ but $\lim_n\int|f_
n|\,d\mu = 1 \neq 0 = \int|f|\,d\mu$. The implication fails.

The underlying reason is that convergence of the signed integrals only controls
cancellation between positive and negative parts, not the total mass. Here the p
ositive and negative parts of $f_n$ each carry mass $1/2$ that cancels in $\int
f_n$ but adds in $\int|f_n|$, and this mass does not vanish as $n\to\infty$.
