Looking at this problem, I need to find the maximum number of pairs from towers
{1,...,10} such that for any two distinct pairs {a,b} and {x,y}, the value (ax+b
y)(ay+bx) is not divisible by 11.

## Proof

**Setup.** Since 11 is prime and towers are numbered 1–10 (all nonzero residues
mod 11), we work in $\mathbb{F}_{11}^*$. The product $(ax+by)(ay+bx) \equiv 0 \p
mod{11}$ iff at least one factor vanishes mod 11.

**Ratio classes.** For an unordered pair $\{a,b\}$ with $a \neq b$, define the r
atio $r = ab^{-1} \pmod{11}$. Since the pair is unordered, $\{a,b\}$ determines
the class $\{r, r^{-1}\}$. As $r \neq 1$ (since $a \neq b$) and $r \neq 0$, the
possible ratios are $\{2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9,10\}$.

Computing inverses mod 11 ($2 \cdot 6 \equiv 1$, $3 \cdot 4 \equiv 1$, $5 \cdot
9 \equiv 1$, $7 \cdot 8 \equiv 1$, $10 \cdot 10 \equiv 1$), the five ratio class
es are:
$$\{2,6\},\quad \{3,4\},\quad \{5,9\},\quad \{7,8\},\quad \{10\}.$$

**Incompatibility condition.** Fix orderings with $r = a/b$ and $s = x/y$. Then:
- $ax + by = by(rs + 1)$, so $ax+by \equiv 0 \iff rs \equiv -1$.
- $ay + bx = by(r + s)$, so $ay+bx \equiv 0 \iff r \equiv -s$.

Swapping $a \leftrightarrow b$ (replacing $r$ by $r^{-1}$) yields conditions $s
\equiv -r$ or $s \equiv -r^{-1}$ — the same set. Thus two pairs with ratio class
es $C_1 = \{r, r^{-1}\}$ and $C_2 = \{s, s^{-1}\}$ are **incompatible** iff:
$$C_2 \cap \{-r,\, -r^{-1}\} \neq \emptyset,$$
i.e., $C_2$ is the "negation class" $-C_1 = \{-r, -r^{-1}\}$.

**Negation classes:**
- $-\{2,6\} = \{9,5\} = \{5,9\}$
- $-\{3,4\} = \{8,7\} = \{7,8\}$
- $-\{5,9\} = \{6,2\} = \{2,6\}$ (symmetric to above)
- $-\{7,8\} = \{4,3\} = \{3,4\}$ (symmetric to above)
- $-\{10\} = \{1\}$, but $1$ is not a valid ratio ($r=1$ means $a=b$), so $\{10\
}$ has **no** incompatible class.

**Within-class compatibility.** Two pairs in the same class with ratio $r$ are i
ncompatible iff $r \equiv -r$ (i.e., $2r \equiv 0$, impossible) or $r \equiv -r^
{-1}$ (i.e., $r^2 \equiv -1 \equiv 10$). Since $-1$ is not a quadratic residue m
od 11 (the QRs are $\{1,3,4,5,9\}$), this has no solution. So **all pairs within
 a class are mutually compatible**.

**Incompatibility graph.** The class-level incompatibility graph has two edges:
$\{2,6\}$—$\{5,9\}$ and $\{3,4\}$—$\{7,8\}$, with $\{10\}$ isolated. Every pair
from one class is incompatible with every pair from its negation class, and comp
atible with all pairs from all other classes.

**Counting pairs per class.** The total number of unordered pairs from 10 towers
 is $\binom{10}{2} = 45$.

- For classes with $r \neq r^{-1}$ (i.e., $\{2,6\}, \{3,4\}, \{5,9\}, \{7,8\}$):
 each has 20 ordered pairs (10 for ratio $r$, 10 for $r^{-1}$), giving $20/2 = 1
0$ unordered pairs. Verification: $4 \times 10 = 40$.
- For $\{10\}$ (where $r = r^{-1} = 10$): 10 ordered pairs, giving $10/2 = 5$ un
ordered pairs. These are $\{1,10\}, \{2,9\}, \{3,8\}, \{4,7\}, \{5,6\}$ (pairs s
umming to 11).

Check: $40 + 5 = 45 = \binom{10}{2}$. ✓

**Maximizing.** To build the largest compatible set:
- Take all 5 pairs from class $\{10\}$ (compatible with everything).
- From the conflicting pair $(\{2,6\}, \{5,9\})$: take all 10 from one class (bo
th have 10).
- From the conflicting pair $(\{3,4\}, \{7,8\})$: take all 10 from one class (bo
th have 10).

Total: $5 + 10 + 10 = \boxed{25}$.

This is optimal because:
- No pair from $\{2,6\}$ can coexist with any pair from $\{5,9\}$, so at most 10
 from this conflict group.
- No pair from $\{3,4\}$ can coexist with any pair from $\{7,8\}$, so at most 10
 from this conflict group.
- At most 5 from class $\{10\}$.
- These three groups partition all 45 pairs, so the maximum is $10 + 10 + 5 = 25
$.
