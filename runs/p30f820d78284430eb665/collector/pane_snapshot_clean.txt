## Proof

**Claim.** Let $A = C[0,1]$ be the space of real-valued (or complex-valued) cont
inuous functions on the closed interval $[0,1]$, equipped with the supremum norm
$$\|f\|_{\infty} = \sup_{x\in[0,1]} |f(x)|.$$
Then $A$ is a Banach space, i.e. a complete normed vector space.

We verify the three required properties: (1) $A$ is a vector space, (2) $\|\cdot
\|_{\infty}$ is a norm, (3) $A$ is complete with respect to this norm.

---

### (1) $A$ is a vector space

The sum of two continuous functions is continuous, and a scalar multiple of a co
ntinuous function is continuous. The zero function is continuous and serves as t
he additive identity. All other vector-space axioms are inherited pointwise from
 $\mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{C}$). Hence $A$ is a vector space.

---

### (2) $\|\cdot\|_{\infty}$ is a norm on $A$

For $f,g\in A$ and scalar $\lambda$:

- **Non-negativity.** $\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup|f|\ge 0$.
- **Definiteness.** If $\|f\|_{\infty}=0$, then $|f(x)|\le 0$ for all $x$, so $f
\equiv 0$. Conversely $\|0\|_{\infty}=0$.
- **Absolute homogeneity.** $\|\lambda f\|_{\infty}=\sup|\lambda f(x)|=|\lambda|
\sup|f(x)|=|\lambda|\,\|f\|_{\infty}$.
- **Triangle inequality.** For every $x$, $|f(x)+g(x)|\le|f(x)|+|g(x)|\le\|f\|_{
\infty}+\|g\|_{\infty}$. Taking the supremum over $x$ gives $\|f+g\|_{\infty}\le
\|f\|_{\infty}+\|g\|_{\infty}$.

Thus $\|\cdot\|_{\infty}$ is a norm. (Finiteness of $\|f\|_{\infty}$ follows bec
ause a continuous function on the compact set $[0,1]$ is bounded and attains its
 maximum, so the supremum is in fact a maximum.)

---

### (3) Completeness

Let $(f_n)_{n=1}^{\infty}$ be a Cauchy sequence in $(A,\|\cdot\|_{\infty})$. We
must show it converges to some $f\in A$ in the sup norm.

**Step 3a — Pointwise limit exists.** For each fixed $x\in[0,1]$,
$$|f_n(x)-f_m(x)|\le\|f_n-f_m\|_{\infty}.$$
Since $(f_n)$ is Cauchy in sup norm, $(f_n(x))$ is a Cauchy sequence in $\mathbb
{R}$ (or $\mathbb{C}$), which is complete. Hence there exists a value, call it $
f(x)$, such that
$$f(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty} f_n(x).$$
This defines a function $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ pointwise.

**Step 3b — Convergence is uniform.** Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ so that $\
|f_n-f_m\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon/2$ for all $n,m\ge N$. Fix $n\ge N$ and $x\in[0,
1]$. For any $m\ge N$,
$$|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le|f_n(x)-f_m(x)|+|f_m(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon/2+|f_m(x)-f(x)|.$$
Letting $m\to\infty$, the second term vanishes, giving
$$|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le\varepsilon/2<\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }x\in[0,1],\;n\ge
N.$$
Therefore $\|f_n-f\|_{\infty}\le\varepsilon/2<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$, i.e
. $f_n\to f$ uniformly.

**Step 3c — The limit $f$ is continuous.** A uniform limit of continuous functio
ns is continuous. Concretely: fix $x_0\in[0,1]$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$
with $\|f_N-f\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon/3$. Since $f_N$ is continuous at $x_0$, the
re exists $\delta>0$ such that $|x-x_0|<\delta$ implies $|f_N(x)-f_N(x_0)|<\vare
psilon/3$. For such $x$,
$$|f(x)-f(x_0)|\le|f(x)-f_N(x)|+|f_N(x)-f_N(x_0)|+|f_N(x_0)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon/
3+\varepsilon/3+\varepsilon/3=\varepsilon.$$
Hence $f$ is continuous at $x_0$. Since $x_0$ was arbitrary, $f\in C[0,1]=A$.

**Step 3d — Conclusion.** We have produced $f\in A$ with $\|f_n-f\|_{\infty}\to
0$. Since the Cauchy sequence was arbitrary, every Cauchy sequence in $A$ conver
ges in $A$. Therefore $A$ is complete.

---

### Final answer

Combining (1), (2), and (3): $A=C[0,1]$ equipped with the supremum norm is a nor
med vector space that is complete. **Yes, $A$ is a Banach space with respect to
the supremum norm.**
