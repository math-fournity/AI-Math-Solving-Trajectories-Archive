Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the antipode of a free, fin
ite-rank Hopf algebra over an arbitrary commutative ring is always a linear isom
orphism.

**Answer: Yes, the antipode is always a linear isomorphism.**

## Proof

**Setup.** Let $H$ be a Hopf algebra over a commutative ring $R$, with antipode
$S: H \to H$. Assume $H$ is free of finite rank $n$ as an $R$-module. We prove $
S$ is an $R$-linear isomorphism.

**Step 1 (Matrix criterion).** Since $H$ is free of rank $n$, choose an $R$-basi
s. The $R$-linear map $S$ is then represented by an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ with
 entries in $R$. The map $S$ is an $R$-linear isomorphism if and only if $\det(A
)$ is a unit in $R$.

**Step 2 (Unit criterion via maximal ideals).** An element $a \in R$ is a unit i
f and only if $a \notin \mathfrak{m}$ for every maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ of
$R$. This is standard: $a$ is a non-unit iff $(a) \subsetneq R$, iff $(a)$ is co
ntained in some maximal ideal.

**Step 3 (Base change to residue fields).** For each maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}
$, let $k(\mathfrak{m}) = R_{\mathfrak{m}}/\mathfrak{m}R_{\mathfrak{m}}$ be the
residue field. Form the base change:
$$H_{(\mathfrak{m})} := H \otimes_R k(\mathfrak{m}).$$
Since $H$ is free of rank $n$ over $R$, the space $H_{(\mathfrak{m})}$ is an $n$
-dimensional vector space over $k(\mathfrak{m})$.

All Hopf algebra structure maps (multiplication $\mu$, comultiplication $\Delta$
, unit $\eta$, counit $\varepsilon$, antipode $S$) are $R$-linear maps satisfyin
g the Hopf algebra axioms expressed as commutative diagrams. Tensor product with
 $k(\mathfrak{m})$ preserves commutative diagrams, so $H_{(\mathfrak{m})}$ inher
its the structure of a Hopf algebra over $k(\mathfrak{m})$, with antipode:
$$S_{(\mathfrak{m})} = S \otimes \mathrm{id}_{k(\mathfrak{m})} : H_{(\mathfrak{m
})} \to H_{(\mathfrak{m})}.$$

Since $H$ has a unit $1_H \neq 0$, we have $n \geq 1$, so $H_{(\mathfrak{m})}$ i
s a **nonzero** finite-dimensional Hopf algebra over the field $k(\mathfrak{m})$
.

**Step 4 (Larson–Sweedler theorem).** The Larson–Sweedler theorem (1969) states:

> *If $K$ is a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra over a field, then its antipode i
s bijective.*

The proof uses the theory of integrals: a nonzero left integral $\Lambda \in K$
exists (by finite-dimensionality and duality with $K^*$), and the interaction of
 $S$ with the integral structure forces $S$ to be injective; finite-dimensionali
ty over a field then yields surjectivity.

Applying this to $K = H_{(\mathfrak{m})}$, we conclude that $S_{(\mathfrak{m})}$
 is a bijective $k(\mathfrak{m})$-linear map, hence an isomorphism of $n$-dimens
ional $k(\mathfrak{m})$-vector spaces.

**Step 5 (Fiberwise invertibility).** The map $S_{(\mathfrak{m})}$ is represente
d by the matrix $A$ with entries reduced modulo $\mathfrak{m}$ (i.e., projected
to $k(\mathfrak{m})$). This reduced matrix is invertible over $k(\mathfrak{m})$
if and only if $\det(A) \bmod \mathfrak{m} \neq 0$ in $k(\mathfrak{m})$, if and
only if $\det(A) \notin \mathfrak{m}$.

By Step 4, $S_{(\mathfrak{m})}$ is an isomorphism for every maximal ideal $\math
frak{m}$, so $\det(A) \notin \mathfrak{m}$ for every maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}
$.

**Step 6 (Conclusion).** By Step 2, $\det(A) \notin \mathfrak{m}$ for all maxima
l ideals $\mathfrak{m}$ implies $\det(A)$ is a unit in $R$. By Step 1, this mean
s $S$ is an $R$-linear isomorphism. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The argument extends beyond the free case: the same fiberwise reason
ing works for any Hopf algebra that is finitely generated and projective as an $
R$-module, since such modules also localize to finite-dimensional vector spaces
over residue fields. The key ingredients are (1) the reduction of invertibility
of an endomorphism of a finite free module to fiberwise invertibility, and (2) t
he Larson–Sweedler theorem providing bijectivity of the antipode over each resid
ue field.
