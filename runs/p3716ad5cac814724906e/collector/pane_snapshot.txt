## Proof

Let $X = \{*\}$ be the one-point space, and let $Y = \mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$.

**Step 1: Maps from a one-point space are determined by a single point.**

A continuous map $f\colon X \to Y$ is completely determined by the image $f(*)\i
n Y$, since $X$ has only one point and every such choice is continuous (any map
from a one-point space is continuous). Thus maps $X\to Y$ are in bijection with
points of $Y$.

**Step 2: Homotopy of such maps corresponds to path-connectedness in $Y$.**

Two maps $f,g\colon X\to Y$ are homotopic iff there exists a continuous map
$$H\colon X\times[0,1]\to Y$$
with $H(*,0)=f(*)$ and $H(*,1)=g(*)$. Since $X\times[0,1]\cong[0,1]$ (a one-poin
t space times an interval is an interval), such an $H$ is exactly a continuous p
ath in $Y$ from $f(*)$ to $g(*)$. Therefore:

$$f\simeq g \iff f(*) \text{ and } g(*) \text{ lie in the same path component of
 } Y.$$

**Step 3: The two maps.**

Define
$$f(*) = 1,\qquad g(*) = -1.$$

Equivalently, $f\colon X\to Y$ is the constant map sending $*$ to $1$, and $g\co
lon X\to Y$ is the constant map sending $*$ to $-1$. Both are continuous (consta
nt maps are always continuous), so they are valid maps $X\to Y$.

**Step 4: Why they are not homotopic.**

The space $Y=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}=(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)$ has exactly two
 path components:
$$Y_+ = (0,\infty),\qquad Y_- = (-\infty,0).$$

Indeed, $Y_+$ and $Y_-$ are each path-connected (being intervals), and no contin
uous path in $Y$ can cross from one to the other: if $\gamma\colon[0,1]\to Y$ we
re continuous with $\gamma(0)\in Y_+$ and $\gamma(1)\in Y_-$, then by the Interm
ediate Value Theorem applied to the continuous real-valued function $\gamma$, th
ere would exist $t_0\in[0,1]$ with $\gamma(t_0)=0$, contradicting $\gamma(t_0)\i
n Y=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$.

Now $f(*)=1\in Y_+$ and $g(*)=-1\in Y_-$ lie in **different** path components of
 $Y$. By Step 2, a homotopy between $f$ and $g$ would be a path in $Y$ joining $
1$ to $-1$, which is impossible since they are in distinct path components.

**Conclusion.** The two constant maps $f(*)=1$ and $g(*)=-1$ from the one-point
space to $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ are not homotopic, because a homotopy betwee
n them would yield a continuous path in $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ from $1$ to $
-1$, but $1$ and $-1$ lie in different path components of $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{
0\}$, and no such path exists (by the Intermediate Value Theorem).
