**Problem.** Does there exist a holomorphic function, not identically zero, with
 an uncountable number of zeros?

**Answer: No.**

---

**Proof.**

Let $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{C}$ be a nonempty open set and let $f : \Omega \to
 \mathbb{C}$ be holomorphic. We show that if $f$ is not identically zero, then i
ts zero set
$$Z(f) = \{z \in \Omega : f(z) = 0\}$$
is at most countable, hence certainly not uncountable.

**Step 1 (Zeros are isolated).** This is the classical *identity theorem* (princ
iple of isolated zeros). We recall the argument.

Suppose $f$ is holomorphic on a connected open set $U \subseteq \Omega$ and $f$
is not identically zero on $U$. Let $a \in U$ with $f(a) = 0$. Since $f$ is holo
morphic, it admits a power series expansion centered at $a$ with positive radius
 of convergence:
$$f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n (z-a)^n.$$
Since $f \not\equiv 0$ on $U$, not all $c_n$ are zero. Let $m \geq 1$ be the sma
llest index with $c_m \neq 0$ (note $c_0 = f(a) = 0$, so $m \geq 1$). Then
$$f(z) = (z-a)^m g(z), \quad \text{where } g(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_{n+m}(z-
a)^n,$$
and $g$ is holomorphic near $a$ with $g(a) = c_m \neq 0$. By continuity of $g$,
there exists $r > 0$ such that $g(z) \neq 0$ for all $z$ with $|z - a| < r$. Hen
ce within the punctured disk $0 < |z-a| < r$, the only zero of $f$ is at $z = a$
 itself. This shows **every zero of $f$ is isolated**: each zero has a neighborh
ood containing no other zero.

**Step 2 (Isolated subsets of $\mathbb{C}$ are countable).** The plane $\mathbb{
C}$ is *second-countable*: it has a countable base, for instance the collection
of open disks
$$\mathcal{B} = \{B(q, r) : q \in \mathbb{Q} + i\mathbb{Q},\; r \in \mathbb{Q}_{
>0}\},$$
which is countable. Now let $S \subseteq \mathbb{C}$ be a set of isolated points
 (every point of $S$ has a neighborhood meeting $S$ only at that point). For eac
h $s \in S$, by isolation there exists a basic open set $B_s \in \mathcal{B}$ wi
th $B_s \cap S = \{s\}$. The map $s \mapsto B_s$ is injective (distinct points o
f $S$ map to distinct basic open sets, since each $B_s$ "picks out" exactly $s$)
. Thus $S$ injects into the countable set $\mathcal{B}$, so $S$ is countable.

**Step 3 (Reduction to the connected case).** An open set $\Omega \subseteq \mat
hbb{C}$ decomposes into connected components, each of which is open. Since $\mat
hbb{C}$ is second-countable, $\Omega$ has at most countably many connected compo
nents $\{U_j\}_{j \in J}$ (each component contains a point with rational coordin
ates, giving an injection from components into $\mathbb{Q}+i\mathbb{Q}$). On eac
h component $U_j$, either $f|_{U_j} \equiv 0$ or $f|_{U_j} \not\equiv 0$.

- If $f|_{U_j} \not\equiv 0$, then by Steps 1–2, $Z(f) \cap U_j$ is a countable
set of isolated points.
- If $f|_{U_j} \equiv 0$, then $Z(f) \cap U_j = U_j$, which is uncountable.

**Step 4 (Conclusion).** The crucial observation: if $f|_{U_j} \equiv 0$ on even
 a single component $U_j$, then $Z(f) \supseteq U_j$ is uncountable — *but this
is exactly the case where $f$ vanishes identically on a nontrivial open set*. Th
e problem requires $f$ **not identically zero**. The precise meaning matters:

- If "$f$ not identically zero" means $f \not\equiv 0$ on **each** connected com
ponent of $\Omega$ (i.e., $f$ is not the zero function on any component), then o
n every component the zeros are isolated and countable. A countable union of cou
ntable sets is countable, so $Z(f) = \bigcup_j (Z(f) \cap U_j)$ is countable.

- Even under the weaker reading where $f \not\equiv 0$ on $\Omega$ as a whole (s
o $f$ might vanish identically on some components but not all): the zeros coming
 from components where $f \not\equiv 0$ are countable, and the components where
$f \equiv 0$ contribute entire uncountable open sets. In this case $Z(f)$ *can*
be uncountable — but only because $f$ is identically zero on an open set, which
is a degenerate case that the spirit of the question excludes.

Under the standard and intended interpretation — **$f$ is holomorphic on a conne
cted domain $\Omega$ and $f \not\equiv 0$** — Steps 1 and 2 apply directly: the
zero set consists of isolated points in a second-countable space, hence is at mo
st countable.

Therefore, **no holomorphic function that is not identically zero can have an un
countable number of zeros.** $\blacksquare$

---

**Remark.** The essential ingredients are: (1) the identity theorem, which force
s zeros of a nonzero holomorphic function to be isolated, and (2) second-countab
ility of $\mathbb{C}$, which forces any discrete subset to be countable. The sam
e conclusion holds for holomorphic functions on any connected complex manifold (
more generally, any second-countable space where the identity theorem applies).
