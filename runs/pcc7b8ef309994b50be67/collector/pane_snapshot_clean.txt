Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the Fourier transform of a
compactly supported function can have isolated zeros.

## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, it is possible. In fact, something stronger is true: for *every*
 nonzero compactly supported $L^1$ function, all zeros of its Fourier transform
are necessarily isolated.

**Setup.** Let $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ be a nonzero function with compact suppor
t, say $\operatorname{supp}(f) \subseteq [-A, A]$. Define the Fourier transform
$$\hat{f}(\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\, e^{-2\pi i x \xi}\, dx = \int_{-
A}^{A} f(x)\, e^{-2\pi i x \xi}\, dx.$$

**Step 1: $\hat{f}$ extends to an entire function.**

Since the support of $f$ is contained in the compact interval $[-A, A]$, the int
egrand $f(x) e^{-2\pi i x \xi}$ is an entire function of $\xi$ for each fixed $x
$, and $|f(x) e^{-2\pi i x \xi}| = |f(x)| \cdot e^{2\pi x \,\operatorname{Im}(\x
i)} \leq |f(x)| \cdot e^{2\pi A |\operatorname{Im}(\xi)|}$, which is integrable
over $[-A, A]$. By the theorem on differentiation under the integral sign (with
a locally uniform dominating function), $\hat{f}(\xi)$ is defined and holomorphi
c on all of $\mathbb{C}$; that is, $\hat{f}$ is an **entire function**. (This is
 the classical Paley–Wiener theorem in its basic form.)

**Step 2: $\hat{f}$ is not identically zero.**

Since $f \in L^1$ and $f \not\equiv 0$, the Fourier transform $\hat{f}$ is not i
dentically zero. This follows from the injectivity of the Fourier transform on $
L^1$: if $\hat{f} \equiv 0$, then by the Fourier inversion theorem (applicable s
ince $f \in L^1$ with $\hat{f} \in L^1$, or more directly by the uniqueness theo
rem for Fourier transforms of $L^1$ functions), we would have $f = 0$ a.e., cont
radicting $f \not\equiv 0$.

**Step 3: The zeros of a nonzero entire function are isolated.**

This is a fundamental theorem of complex analysis (a consequence of the identity
 theorem / the fact that zeros of holomorphic functions are isolated unless the
function is identically zero). Specifically: if $g$ is holomorphic on a domain $
\Omega$ and $g \not\equiv 0$, then for every $z_0 \in \Omega$ with $g(z_0) = 0$,
 there exists $r > 0$ such that $g(z) \neq 0$ for all $z$ with $0 < |z - z_0| <
r$.

Applying this to $g = \hat{f}$ on $\Omega = \mathbb{C}$: since $\hat{f}$ is enti
re and not identically zero, **every zero of $\hat{f}$ is isolated**.

**Step 4: Concrete example.**

Take $f = \mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}$, the indicator function of $[-1,1]$. This is comp
actly supported and in $L^1$. Its Fourier transform is
$$\hat{f}(\xi) = \int_{-1}^{1} e^{-2\pi i x \xi}\, dx = \frac{\sin(2\pi \xi)}{\p
i \xi},$$
which has isolated zeros at $\xi = \frac{n}{2}$ for every nonzero integer $n$. E
ach such zero is isolated (separated from neighboring zeros by distance $\frac{1
}{2}$), confirming the claim explicitly.

**Conclusion.** The Fourier transform of a nonzero compactly supported function
is a nonzero entire function, so by the identity theorem of complex analysis, al
l of its zeros are isolated. Not only is it *possible* for such a Fourier transf
orm to have isolated zeros — it is *inevitable* that every zero (if any exist) i
s isolated. A concrete example is $f = \mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}$, whose Fourier trans
form $\frac{\sin(2\pi\xi)}{\pi\xi}$ has isolated zeros at $\xi = n/2$, $n \in \m
athbb{Z} \setminus \{0\}$.
