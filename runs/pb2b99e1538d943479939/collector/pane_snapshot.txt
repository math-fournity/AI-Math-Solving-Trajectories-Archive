## Proof

**Key identity.** Using the triple-angle identity,
$$\sin^3 t = \frac{3\sin t - \sin 3t}{4},$$
so for real $t$,
$$\frac{\sin^3 t}{t} = \operatorname{Im}\!\left(\frac{3e^{it}-e^{3it}}{4t}\right
).$$

Define
$$g(z)=\frac{3e^{iz}-e^{3it}}{4z}.$$

Both $e^{iz}$ and $e^{3iz}$ decay exponentially in the upper half-plane ($\opera
torname{Im}z>0$), so $g$ is well-suited to a semicircular contour.

**Contour.** Let $C$ be the standard indented semicircular contour in the upper
half-plane, consisting of:
- the real segment $[-R,-\varepsilon]$,
- the small semicircle $\gamma_\varepsilon$: $z=\varepsilon e^{i\theta}$, $\thet
a$ from $\pi$ to $0$ (clockwise, indenting above the pole at $0$),
- the real segment $[\varepsilon,R]$,
- the large semicircle $\gamma_1$: $z=Re^{i\theta}$, $\theta$ from $0$ to $\pi$
(counterclockwise).

**Pole and residue.** $g$ has a simple pole at $z=0$ with
$$\operatorname{Res}_{z=0} g(z)=\frac{3-1}{4}=\frac{1}{2}.$$
Since the pole lies on the boundary (excluded by the indentation), $g$ is holomo
rphic inside $C$, and Cauchy's theorem gives
$$\int_{-R}^{-\varepsilon}g(x)\,dx+\int_{\gamma_\varepsilon}g(z)\,dz+\int_{\vare
psilon}^{R}g(x)\,dx+\int_{\gamma_1}g(z)\,dz=0.$$

**Large semicircle $\gamma_1$ vanishes (Jordan's lemma).** For $z=Re^{i\theta}$
with $\theta\in[0,\pi]$, $\operatorname{Im}z=R\sin\theta\ge 0$, so $|e^{iz}|=e^{
-\operatorname{Im}z}\le 1$ and $|e^{3iz}|=e^{-3\operatorname{Im}z}\le 1$. Hence
$$|g(z)|\le\frac{3+1}{4R}=\frac{1}{R},$$
and
$$\left|\int_{\gamma_1}g(z)\,dz\right|\le \frac{1}{R}\cdot \pi R=\pi\cdot\frac{1
}{R}\cdot R\to 0\quad\text{as }R\to\infty.$$
(More precisely, Jordan's lemma applied to each exponential gives decay faster t
han any $1/R$.) Thus $\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{\gamma_1}g(z)\,dz=0$.

**Small semicircle $\gamma_\varepsilon$.** Near $z=0$, $g(z)=\frac{1/2}{z}+\text
{holomorphic}$, so
$$\int_{\gamma_\varepsilon}g(z)\,dz\to -i\pi\cdot\operatorname{Res}_{z=0}g=-\fra
c{i\pi}{2}\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0,$$
the minus sign because $\gamma_\varepsilon$ is traversed clockwise (half a posit
ive loop in the negative direction).

**Combining the real-axis parts.** For real $x$,
$$g(-x)=\frac{3e^{-ix}-e^{-3ix}}{4(-x)}=-\frac{3e^{-ix}-e^{-3ix}}{4x},$$
so
$$\int_{-R}^{-\varepsilon}g(x)\,dx=\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}g(-x)\,dx,$$
and therefore
$$\int_{-R}^{-\varepsilon}g(x)\,dx+\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}g(x)\,dx=\int_{\varepsi
lon}^{R}\bigl(g(x)+g(-x)\bigr)\,dx.$$
Now
$$g(x)+g(-x)=\frac{3e^{ix}-e^{3ix}}{4x}-\frac{3e^{-ix}-e^{-3ix}}{4x}=\frac{3(e^{
ix}-e^{-ix})-(e^{3ix}-e^{-3ix})}{4x}=\frac{2i(3\sin x-\sin 3x)}{4x}=2i\,\frac{\s
in^3 x}{x}.$$

**Taking the limits.** Letting $R\to\infty$ and $\varepsilon\to 0$ in Cauchy's t
heorem:
$$2i\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^3 x}{x}\,dx-\frac{i\pi}{2}+0=0,$$
hence
$$2i\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^3 x}{x}\,dx=\frac{i\pi}{2},$$
and
$$\boxed{\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^3 t}{t}\,dt=\frac{\pi}{4}}.$$
