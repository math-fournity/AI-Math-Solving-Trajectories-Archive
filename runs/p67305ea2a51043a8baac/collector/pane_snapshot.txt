## Proof

**Answer: Yes, $g_n \to T^{-1}g$.**

**Setup.** Let $T$ be a bounded linear operator on a Hilbert space $H$, and supp
ose $T$ is invertible. By the standard meaning of "invertible" for bounded opera
tors on a Banach (in particular Hilbert) space, there exists a bounded operator
$S \in \mathcal{B}(H)$ with $ST = TS = I$; that is, $S = T^{-1}$ and $T^{-1}$ is
 itself bounded. (Equivalently: $T$ is bijective, and by the Bounded Inverse The
orem / Open Mapping Theorem, the algebraic inverse of a bounded bijection betwee
n Banach spaces is automatically bounded.)

**Key fact.** A bounded linear operator on a Hilbert (or Banach) space is contin
uous. Since $T^{-1}$ is bounded, $T^{-1}$ is continuous.

**Argument.** We are given that $Tg_n \to g$ in the norm of $H$. Apply the conti
nuous map $T^{-1}$ to this convergent sequence:

$$g_n = T^{-1}(Tg_n) \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} T^{-1}g.$$

Continuity of $T^{-1}$ guarantees that the image of a convergent sequence conver
ges to the image of the limit, so

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} g_n = T^{-1}g.$$

**Remark on the necessity of boundedness of $T^{-1}$.** The conclusion hinges on
 $T^{-1}$ being continuous. If "invertible" only meant algebraically invertible
(bijective) without guaranteeing a bounded inverse, the conclusion could fail in
 general Banach spaces — one can have a bounded bijection whose inverse is unbou
nded and discontinuous, and a discontinuous linear map need not preserve sequent
ial limits. However, on Hilbert spaces (and more generally Banach spaces), the B
ounded Inverse Theorem ensures that every bounded *bijection* has a bounded inve
rse, so the two notions of invertibility coincide for bounded operators, and the
 argument above is valid.

**Conclusion.** Since $T$ is an invertible bounded operator, $T^{-1}$ is bounded
 and hence continuous, and therefore $g_n = T^{-1}(Tg_n) \to T^{-1}g$.
