## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. If $S$ is a regular (compact) surface, then $S$ has finitely man
y connected components.

**Key facts used.**

1. **A regular surface is locally connected.** By definition, every point $p \in
 S$ has an open neighborhood $V \subset S$ that is the image of an open set $U \
subset \mathbb{R}^2$ under a map $\mathbf{x}: U \to V$ that is a homeomorphism.
Since $U \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ is locally connected and local connectedness is a
 topological invariant preserved by homeomorphism, $V$ is locally connected. Hen
ce $S$ is locally connected at every point, i.e. $S$ is locally connected.

2. **In a locally connected space, every connected component is open.** Let $C$
be a connected component of $S$ and $p \in C$. By local connectedness, $p$ has a
 connected neighborhood $W \subset S$. Since $C$ is the connected component of $
p$ (the largest connected set containing $p$), we have $W \subset C$. Thus $C$ c
ontains a neighborhood of each of its points, so $C$ is open in $S$.

3. **Connected components are always closed** (in any topological space), being
the complement of the union of all other components, each of which is open by (2
). So each component is clopen.

**The argument.**

Let $\{C_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}$ be the family of connected components of $S$.
By fact (2), each $C_\alpha$ is open in $S$. The family $\{C_\alpha\}_{\alpha \i
n A}$ is pairwise disjoint and $\bigcup_{\alpha \in A} C_\alpha = S$, so it is a
n open cover of $S$.

Since $S$ is compact, this open cover admits a finite subcover:
$$S = C_{\alpha_1} \cup C_{\alpha_2} \cup \cdots \cup C_{\alpha_n}.$$

Because the components are pairwise disjoint and each is nonempty, no component
other than $C_{\alpha_1}, \dots, C_{\alpha_n}$ can exist — any additional compon
ent $C_\beta$ would be a nonempty subset of $S$ disjoint from $C_{\alpha_1} \cup
 \cdots \cup C_{\alpha_n} = S$, which is impossible. Therefore $A = \{\alpha_1,
\dots, \alpha_n\}$, and $S$ has exactly $n$ connected components.

**Remark on the role of compactness.** The compactness hypothesis is essential.
Without it, a regular surface can have infinitely (even countably) many componen
ts: for instance, the disjoint union $\bigsqcup_{n=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{S}^2$ of
countably many unit spheres placed far apart in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is a regular surf
ace with countably many connected components, but it is not compact (it is unbou
nded, and even if bounded the components would accumulate, breaking either close
dness or the regular-surface property at accumulation points). The compactness f
orces the open cover by components to reduce to a finite one.

**Conclusion.** A regular and compact surface has a finite number of connected c
omponents. $\blacksquare$
