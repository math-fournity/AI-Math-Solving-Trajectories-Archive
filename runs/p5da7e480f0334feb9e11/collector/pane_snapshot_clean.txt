The closure of $C$ in $(C([0,1]), \|\cdot\|)$ is the entire space $C([0,1])$.

**Proof.** We show every $f\in C([0,1])$ lies in the closure of $C$.

Fix $f\in C([0,1])$. For each integer $n\ge 1$, define
$$g_n(x)=\min(nx,1),\qquad x\in[0,1],$$
and set $f_n(x)=f(x)\,g_n(x)$.

Each $g_n$ is continuous on $[0,1]$ with $0\le g_n\le 1$, and $g_n(0)=0$. Hence
$f_n$ is continuous and $f_n(0)=f(0)\,g_n(0)=0$, so $f_n\in C$.

For every $x>0$, once $n\ge 1/x$ we have $nx\ge 1$, so $g_n(x)=1$ and therefore
$f_n(x)=f(x)$. Thus $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ for all $x\in(0,1]$; at $x=0$, $f_n(0)=0$,
which may differ from $f(0)$, but $\{0\}$ has Lebesgue measure zero.

Moreover $|f_n(x)-f(x)|=|f(x)|\,|g_n(x)-1|\le |f(x)|\cdot 2$, and $|f|^2$ is int
egrable on $[0,1]$ since $f$ is continuous on a compact interval. By the dominat
ed convergence theorem,
$$\|f_n-f\|^2=\int_0^1|f_n(x)-f(x)|^2\,dx\longrightarrow 0.$$

Therefore $f_n\to f$ in the $L^2$ norm, with each $f_n\in C$. Since $f\in C([0,1
])$ was arbitrary, $C([0,1])\subseteq\overline{C}$. The reverse inclusion $\over
line{C}\subseteq C([0,1])$ holds because the closure is taken inside the ambient
 space $C([0,1])$.

Hence $\overline{C}=C([0,1])$.
