## Proof

We show that the inhomogeneous heat equation with zero initial data can be trans
formed, via the Fourier transform, into the stated integral equation.

**Setup.** Consider
$$u_t = u_{xx} + h(x,t), \qquad x\in\mathbb{R},\ t>0, \qquad u(x,0)=0.$$

Define the Fourier transform in the spatial variable:
$$\widehat{u}(\xi,t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} u(x,t)\,e^{-i\xi x}\,dx, \qquad \
widehat{h}(\xi,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x,t)\,e^{-i\xi x}\,dx.$$

Under the standard convention, $\mathcal{F}[u_{xx}](\xi,t) = -\xi^{2}\,\widehat{
u}(\xi,t)$ (using integration by parts twice, assuming sufficient decay so bound
ary terms vanish). Transforming the PDE in $x$ therefore yields, for each fixed
$\xi$, an ODE in $t$:
$$\widehat{u}_t(\xi,t) = -\xi^{2}\,\widehat{u}(\xi,t) + \widehat{h}(\xi,t), \qqu
ad \widehat{u}(\xi,0)=0.$$

**Solving the ODE.** This is a first-order linear inhomogeneous ODE. Multiplying
 by the integrating factor $e^{\xi^{2}t}$:
$$\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(e^{\xi^{2}t}\,\widehat{u}(\xi,t)\right) = e^{\xi^{2}t}\,\w
idehat{h}(\xi,t).$$

Integrating from $0$ to $t$ and using $\widehat{u}(\xi,0)=0$:
$$e^{\xi^{2}t}\,\widehat{u}(\xi,t) = \int_{0}^{t} e^{\xi^{2}s}\,\widehat{h}(\xi,
s)\,ds,$$
so
$$\widehat{u}(\xi,t) = \int_{0}^{t} e^{-\xi^{2}(t-s)}\,\widehat{h}(\xi,s)\,ds.$$

**Inverting the Fourier transform.** Recall the fundamental identity
$$\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left[e^{-\xi^{2}\tau}\right]\!(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi\tau
}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{4\tau}\right), \qquad \tau>0,$$
which is the heat kernel $G(x,\tau)$. Setting $\tau = t-s$, the factor $e^{-\xi^
{2}(t-s)}$ inverts to $G(x,t-s)$.

By linearity of the inverse Fourier transform and the convolution theorem — the
inverse transform of a product $\widehat{f}\cdot\widehat{g}$ is the convolution
$f*g$ — we obtain
$$u(x,t) = \int_{0}^{t} \left(G(\cdot,t-s) * h(\cdot,s)\right)(x)\,ds.$$

Writing the convolution explicitly:
$$u(x,t) = \int_{0}^{t}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi(t-s)}}\,\exp\
!\left(\frac{-(x-y)^{2}}{4(t-s)}\right)\,h(y,s)\,dy\,ds.$$

**Verification of the integral equation.** This is precisely the integral equati
on stated in the problem. Conversely, one may verify directly: differentiating u
nder the integral sign (Duhamel's principle) shows that this $u$ satisfies $u_t=
u_{xx}+h$ and $u(x,0)=0$, confirming the equivalence.

**Conclusion.** Yes — applying the Fourier transform in $x$ reduces the inhomoge
neous heat equation to a solvable first-order ODE in $t$; inverting the transfor
m via the convolution theorem with the heat kernel $G(x,t-s)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi
(t-s)}}e^{-(x-y)^2/4(t-s)}$ yields exactly the stated integral equation. The tra
nsformation is valid.
