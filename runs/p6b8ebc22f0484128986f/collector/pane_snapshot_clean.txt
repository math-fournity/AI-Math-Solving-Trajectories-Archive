# Covariant Derivative of a Covariant Vector

## Setup

In a general curvilinear coordinate system with coordinates $x^i$ ($i = 1, 2, \d
ots, n$), the basis vectors $\mathbf{e}_i = \partial\mathbf{r}/\partial x^i$ var
y from point to point. Consequently, the partial derivative of a vector field do
es **not** itself transform as a tensor. To obtain a derivative that *is* a tens
or, we must correct for the variation of the basis.

The Christoffel symbols of the second kind encode this basis variation:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathbf{e}_i}{\partial x^k} = \Gamma^{j}{}_{ik}\,\mathbf{e}_j,
$$

and are given in terms of the metric tensor $g_{ij}$ (with inverse $g^{ij}$) by

$$
\Gamma^{k}{}_{ij} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,g^{k\ell}\!\left(\frac{\partial g_{\ell i}}{\p
artial x^j} + \frac{\partial g_{\ell j}}{\partial x^i} - \frac{\partial g_{ij}}{
\partial x^{\ell}}\right).
$$

## Derivation

A covariant vector (covector) field $u_j$ is the component form of the one-form
$\boldsymbol{u} = u_j\,\mathbf{e}^j$, where $\mathbf{e}^j$ is the reciprocal bas
is satisfying $\mathbf{e}^j \cdot \mathbf{e}_i = \delta^j_i$. The reciprocal bas
is also varies with position:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathbf{e}^j}{\partial x^k} = -\Gamma^{j}{}_{\ell k}\,\mathbf{e}^
{\ell},
$$

(the minus sign follows from differentiating $\mathbf{e}^j\cdot\mathbf{e}_i = \d
elta^j_i$).

Taking the partial derivative of the one-form:

$$
\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{u}}{\partial x^k}
= \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x^k}\,\mathbf{e}^j + u_j\,\frac{\partial \mathbf{
e}^j}{\partial x^k}
= \left(\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x^k} - \Gamma^{\ell}{}_{jk}\,u_{\ell}\right
)\mathbf{e}^j.
$$

The quantity in parentheses is, by construction, the component of a genuine $(0,
2)$-tensor. We define it as the **covariant derivative** of $u_j$.

## Formula

$$
\boxed{\;u_{j;k} \;=\; \nabla_k u_j \;=\; \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x^k} - \G
amma^{\ell}{}_{jk}\,u_{\ell}\;}
$$

## Explanation of the Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| $u_{j;k} = \nabla_k u_j$ | The covariant derivative of the covariant vector co
mponent $u_j$ with respect to coordinate $x^k$. It is a rank-2 covariant tensor
(a $(0,2)$-tensor). |
| $\dfrac{\partial u_j}{\partial x^k}$ | The ordinary partial derivative of the
component $u_j$. This alone does **not** transform as a tensor because it ignore
s the variation of the basis. |
| $\Gamma^{\ell}{}_{jk}$ | The Christoffel symbol of the second kind, encoding h
ow the basis changes from point to point. It is symmetric in its lower indices:
$\Gamma^{\ell}{}_{jk} = \Gamma^{\ell}{}_{kj}$. |
| $-\Gamma^{\ell}{}_{jk}\,u_{\ell}$ | The **connection correction term**. It sub
tracts the contribution due to the twisting/expansion/contraction of the coordin
ate basis, exactly cancelling the non-tensorial part of the partial derivative.
The minus sign (vs. the $+$ sign for contravariant vectors) reflects that the *r
eciprocal* basis varies with the opposite sign to the tangent basis. |
| $u_{\ell}$ | The original covariant vector component, contracted with the Chri
stoffel symbol over the dummy index $\ell$. |

## Why the Minus Sign (vs. contravariant case)

For a contravariant vector $v^i$ one has $v^i{}_{;k} = \partial_k v^i + \Gamma^i
{}_{\ell k}v^{\ell}$ (a **plus** sign), because the tangent basis $\mathbf{e}_i$
 varies as $+\Gamma\,\mathbf{e}$. For the covariant case the reciprocal basis $\
mathbf{e}^j$ varies as $-\Gamma\,\mathbf{e}^{\ell}$, producing the **minus** sig
n. This sign difference is essential: it guarantees that the covariant derivativ
e of the scalar $u_j v^j$ reduces to the ordinary partial derivative,

$$
(u_j v^j)_{;k} = u_{j;k}v^j + u_j v^j{}_{;k} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x^k}(u_j
 v^j),
$$

i.e. the covariant derivative satisfies the Leibniz rule and agrees with the ord
inary derivative on scalars — the defining properties of a connection-compatible
 derivative.

## Tensor Character (Verification)

Under a coordinate change $x^i \to x^{i'}$, the component $u_j$ transforms as $u
_{j'} = \dfrac{\partial x^\ell}{\partial x^{j'}}\,u_\ell$, and the Christoffel s
ymbols transform inhomogeneously. A direct substitution shows

$$
u_{j';k'} = \frac{\partial x^m}{\partial x^{k'}}\frac{\partial x^\ell}{\partial
x^{j'}}\,u_{\ell;m},
$$

confirming that $u_{j;k}$ is a genuine $(0,2)$-tensor, whereas the bare partial
derivative $\partial u_j/\partial x^k$ is not. This tensorial character is preci
sely the reason the covariant derivative — rather than the partial derivative —
is used in formulating coordinate-independent physical laws (e.g. the covariant
form of Maxwell's equations, geodesic equations, conservation laws).
