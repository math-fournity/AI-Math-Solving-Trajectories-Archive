(D1) $K\bigl(g^{-1}(x,y),\big|,x,y\bigr)=1$ for $(\alpha\otimes Q)$-a.e.
$(x,y)$, i.e., $K$ is concentrated on ${x}\times f^{-1}(y)$.

(D2) For every $C\in\mathcal{B}(X\times X)$: $$ \mu(C)=\int_{X\times Y} K(C\mid
x,y),(\alpha\otimes Q)(dx,dy). $$

Step 4. Define $R$ and verify the two conditions

Define $$ R(A\mid x,y) ;:=; K(X\times A\mid x,y), \qquad A\in\mathcal{B}(X). $$

$R$ is a stochastic kernel $X\times Y\to\Delta(X)$: For each $(x,y)$,
$R(\cdot\mid x,y)$ is the marginal of the probability measure $K(\cdot\mid x,y)$
on the second coordinate, hence a probability measure. For each $A$, the map
$(x,y)\mapsto K(X\times A\mid x,y)$ is measurable because $K$ is a kernel.

Condition 1. Since $K(\cdot\mid x,y)$ is concentrated on ${x}\times f^{-1}(y)$
(by (D1)), we have $$ R(f^{-1}(y)\mid x,y)=K\bigl(X\times f^{-1}(y)\mid
x,y\bigr)=1 $$ for $(\alpha\otimes Q)$-a.e. $(x,y)$. $\checkmark$

Condition 2. Take $C=A\times B\in\mathcal{B}(X)\otimes\mathcal{B}(X)$ in (D2),
where $A,B\in\mathcal{B}(X)$. Then: $$ \mu(A\times B)=\int_A P(B\mid u),
\alpha(du). $$ On the other hand, using (D2) and the fact that $K$ is
concentrated on ${x}\times f^{-1}(y)$: $$ \mu(A\times B)=\int_{X\times Y}
K(A\times B\mid x,y),(\alpha\otimes Q)(dx,dy)=\int_{X\times Y}\mathbf{1}
_A(x),R(B\mid x,y),(\alpha\otimes Q)(dx,dy). $$ Expanding the iterated integral:
$$ \int_A\left[\int_Y R(B\mid x,y),Q(dy\mid x)\right]\alpha(dx). $$ Therefore,
for every $A\in\mathcal{B}(X)$: $$ \int_A P(B\mid x),
\alpha(dx)=\int_A\left[\int_Y R(B\mid x,y),Q(dy\mid x)\right]\alpha(dx). $$
Since this holds for all measurable $A$, the integrands coincide $\alpha$-a.e.:
$$ P(B\mid x)=\int_Y R(B\mid x,y),Q(dy\mid x) \quad\text{for $\alpha$-a.e. }x. $
$

This holds for each fixed $B$. To obtain the result for all $B$ simultaneously,
let ${B_n}_{n\ge1}$ be a countable $\pi$-system generating $\mathcal{B}(X)$
(which exists because $X$ is standard Borel, hence countably generated). For
each $n$, let $N_n$ be the $\alpha$-null set off which the equality holds for
$B_n$, and set $N=\bigcup_n N_n$. For $x\notin N$:

  • Both $P(\cdot\mid x)$ and $B\mapsto\int_Y R(B\mid x,y),Q(dy\mid x)$ are
  probability measures on $X$ (the latter is a mixture of probability measures).
  • They agree on the $\pi$-system ${B_n}$.

By Dynkin's $\pi$-$\lambda$ theorem, they agree on all of $\mathcal{B}(X)$. Thus
for all $A\in\mathcal{B}(X)$ and $\alpha$-a.e. $x$: $$ P(A\mid x)=\int_Y R(A\mid
x,y),Q(dy\mid x). \qquad\checkmark $$

Step 5. Role of conditional expectations

The entire construction rests on a single application of the disintegration
theorem: given a probability measure $\mu$ on a standard Borel space $Z=X\times
X$ and a Borel map $g:Z\to W=X\times Y$ to another standard Borel space, there
exists a regular conditional probability $K:W\to\Delta(Z)$ with $K(g^{-1}(w)\mid
w)=1$ for $(g_*\mu)$-a.e. $w$.

This theorem is precisely the existence of regular conditional probabilities
(equivalently, conditional expectations) on standard Borel spaces. The
assumption that conditional expectations exist for general (in particular,
standard Borel) measurable spaces is exactly what is needed. No additional
structure (topology, metric, completeness beyond standard Borel) is required
beyond what guarantees the existence of regular conditional probabilities.

Therefore, yes, the kernel $R$ exists, and yes, this can be established under
the assumption of the existence of conditional expectations for general
measurable spaces. $\blacksquare$
