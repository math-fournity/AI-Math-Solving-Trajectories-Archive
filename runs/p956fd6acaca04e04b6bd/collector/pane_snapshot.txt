## Solution

We evaluate
$$I = \int_0^{2\pi} e^{e^{i\theta}}\,d\theta.$$

**Step 1: Expand the integrand as a power series.**

The exponential function has the everywhere-convergent Taylor series $e^z = \sum
_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{z^n}{n!}$. Substituting $z = e^{i\theta}$ (which has $|z|=
1$) gives, for every $\theta \in [0,2\pi]$,

$$e^{e^{i\theta}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(e^{i\theta})^n}{n!} = \sum_{n=0}^
{\infty} \frac{e^{in\theta}}{n!}.$$

**Step 2: Justify term-by-term integration.**

The series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{in\theta}}{n!}$ converges absolutely an
d uniformly on $[0,2\pi]$, since
$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left|\frac{e^{in\theta}}{n!}\right| = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
 \frac{1}{n!} = e < \infty,$$
independent of $\theta$. By the Weierstrass M-test, uniform convergence permits
term-by-term integration:
$$I = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n!}\int_0^{2\pi} e^{in\theta}\,d\theta.$$

**Step 3: Evaluate each integral.**

- For $n = 0$: $\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi} e^{i\cdot 0\cdot\theta}\,d\theta = \i
nt_0^{2\pi} 1\,d\theta = 2\pi.$
- For $n \geq 1$: $\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi} e^{in\theta}\,d\theta = \left[\fra
c{e^{in\theta}}{in}\right]_0^{2\pi} = \frac{e^{2\pi i n}-1}{in} = \frac{1-1}{in}
 = 0,$
  since $e^{2\pi i n} = 1$ for every integer $n$.

**Step 4: Conclude.**

Every term with $n \geq 1$ vanishes, leaving only the $n=0$ term:
$$I = \frac{1}{0!}\cdot 2\pi + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\cdot 0 = 2\pi.$$

$$\boxed{I = 2\pi}$$

**Remark.** The same result follows immediately from the mean-value property of
the entire function $f(z)=e^z$: the average of $e^z$ over the unit circle $\{|z|
=1\}$ equals $f(0)=e^0=1$, so $\frac{1}{2\pi}I = 1$, giving $I = 2\pi$.
