## Urysohn's Lemma

**Statement.** Let $X$ be a normal topological space, and let $A, B \subseteq X$
 be two disjoint closed subsets. Then there exists a continuous function
$$f : X \to [0,1]$$
such that
$$f(x) = 0 \text{ for all } x \in A, \qquad f(x) = 1 \text{ for all } x \in B.$$

Equivalently (by rescaling), for any two disjoint closed sets $A, B$ in a normal
 space $X$ and any real numbers $a < b$, there is a continuous $f : X \to [a,b]$
 with $f|_A \equiv a$ and $f|_B \equiv b$.

### Proof sketch (construction)

The proof constructs $f$ by building a family of open sets indexed by the dyadic
 rationals in $[0,1]$.

Let $D = \{k/2^n : n \ge 1,\, 0 < k < 2^n\} \cup \{0,1\}$ be the dyadic rational
s in $[0,1]$, which are dense in $[0,1]$.

We construct open sets $\{U_r\}_{r \in D}$ with the nesting property
$$s < r \implies \overline{U_s} \subseteq U_r, \qquad A \subseteq U_0, \qquad \o
verline{U_0} \subseteq U_1, \qquad B \cap U_1 = \emptyset.$$

**Base.** Since $X$ is normal and $A, B$ are disjoint closed sets, normality giv
es an open $V$ with $A \subseteq V$ and $\overline{V} \cap B = \emptyset$. Set $
U_0 = V$ (so $\overline{U_0} \subseteq X \setminus B$). Set $U_1 = X \setminus B
$; then $\overline{U_0} \subseteq U_1$ and $B \cap U_1 = \emptyset$.

**Inductive step.** Suppose $U_{k/2^n}$ have been defined for all $k$ with the n
esting property. To insert $U_{(2k+1)/2^{n+1}}$ between $U_{k/2^n}$ and $U_{(k+1
)/2^n}$: since $\overline{U_{k/2^n}} \subseteq U_{(k+1)/2^n}$ and both sides are
 closed (left) / open (right) with the closure contained in the open set, normal
ity applied to the disjoint closed sets $\overline{U_{k/2^n}}$ and $X \setminus
U_{(k+1)/2^n}$ yields an open $W$ with
$$\overline{U_{k/2^n}} \subseteq W, \qquad \overline{W} \subseteq U_{(k+1)/2^n}.
$$
Set $U_{(2k+1)/2^{n+1}} = W$. This preserves the nesting property.

**Define $f$.** For $x \in X$ set
$$f(x) = \inf\{r \in D : x \in U_r\}, \qquad f(x) = 1 \text{ if } x \notin \bigc
up_{r<1} U_r.$$

- If $x \in A \subseteq U_0$, then $f(x) = 0$.
- If $x \in B$, then $x \notin U_1$ (and not in any $U_r$, $r<1$), so $f(x) = 1$
.
- **Continuity:** For any $a \in (0,1)$, one checks
$$f^{-1}((-\infty, a)) = \bigcup_{r < a} U_r, \qquad f^{-1}((a, +\infty)) = \big
cup_{r > a} (X \setminus \overline{U_r}),$$
both open. Since the rays $(-\infty,a)$ and $(a,+\infty)$ generate the topology
on $\mathbb{R}$, $f$ is continuous. $\blacksquare$

## Significance in the context of normal topological spaces

**1. It is the fundamental separation theorem for normal spaces.** Normality (th
e $T_4$ axiom: disjoint closed sets can be separated by disjoint open neighborho
ods) is a purely set-theoretic/topological condition — it only promises the exis
tence of *open sets* separating $A$ and $B$. Urysohn's Lemma upgrades this quali
tative separation into a *quantitative* one: a single continuous real-valued fun
ction that is exactly $0$ on $A$ and exactly $1$ on $B$, taking every intermedia
te value on the rest of the space. This is a dramatic strengthening: from "two d
isjoint open sets" to "a continuous function that interpolates between the two c
losed sets."

**2. It characterizes the gap between $T_3$ and $T_4$.** Regular ($T_3$) spaces
separate a point from a closed set by open sets, but in general *cannot* separat
e two closed sets by a continuous function. Urysohn's Lemma shows that the addit
ional power of normality — separating two closed sets — is *exactly* what is nee
ded to produce continuous real-valued separators. In this sense the lemma pinpoi
nts the precise topological content of normality: **normality = the ability to b
uild continuous functions that vanish on prescribed closed sets.**

**3. It is the engine behind the major theorems of general topology.** Urysohn's
 Lemma is the key ingredient in:
- **The Tietze Extension Theorem:** every continuous function from a closed subs
pace $A$ of a normal space $X$ into $[0,1]$ (or $\mathbb{R}$) extends continuous
ly to all of $X$. The proof repeatedly applies Urysohn's Lemma to approximate th
e function from above and below and takes a limit.
- **Urysohn's Metrization Theorem:** every second-countable normal $T_1$ space i
s metrizable, via an embedding into $[0,1]^\omega$ built from a countable family
 of Urysohn functions.
- **The embedding of compact Hausdorff spaces into cubes** $[0,1]^\kappa$, leadi
ng to the characterization of compact Hausdorff spaces as exactly the closed sub
spaces of products of $[0,1]$.

Without Urysohn's Lemma, none of these cornerstone results would go through, bec
ause they all require the ability to manufacture continuous real-valued function
s from purely topological (closed-set) data.

**4. It reveals that normal spaces have a rich supply of continuous functions.**
 A normal space need not be metrizable, yet Urysohn's Lemma guarantees that ther
e are *enough* continuous $[0,1]$-valued functions to separate any two closed se
ts. This is the topological analogue of the Hahn–Banach theorem in functional an
alysis: a geometric/separation axiom guarantees an abundance of continuous linea
r (here: scalar) functionals. Indeed, the proof of Urysohn's Lemma is structural
ly parallel to the proof of Hahn–Banach — both build an object by a transfinite/
density argument through a dense ordered set of intermediate stages.

**5. It is sharp — the converse direction matters.** The existence of such a sep
arating function for *every* pair of disjoint closed sets is not automatic: ther
e are completely regular ($T_{3\frac{1}{2}}$) spaces that are not normal, where
some pairs of closed sets cannot be separated by any continuous function. Thus U
rysohn's Lemma draws a genuine boundary: normality is sufficient, and the failur
e of normality is detectable as the failure of continuous-function separation.

**6. It bridges topology and analysis.** Many analytic constructions on topologi
cal spaces (partitions of unity, Riesz representation, measure theory on compact
 spaces via the Riesz–Markov–Kakutani theorem) require a rich algebra of continu
ous functions. Urysohn's Lemma is the theorem that first produces this richness
from a bare topological axiom, making it the foundational bridge from point-set
topology to analysis on general (non-metrizable) spaces.

In summary, Urysohn's Lemma is the central result connecting the order-theoretic
 separation axiom of normality to the existence of continuous real-valued functi
ons; it is the lemma on which the Tietze Extension Theorem, Urysohn Metrization
Theorem, and the entire theory of continuous functions on normal/compact-Hausdor
ff spaces depend.
