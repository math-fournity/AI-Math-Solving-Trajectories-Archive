## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, such integers exist. In fact, for *any* two integers $a, b$ with
 $\sqrt{a}, \sqrt{b} \notin \mathbb{Z}$ and $a \neq b$, the $\mathbb{Z}$-modules
 $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{a}]$ and $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{b}]$ are isomorphic.

**Concrete example:** Take $a = 2$ and $b = 3$. Then $\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3} \notin
\mathbb{Z}$ and $2 \neq 3$.

---

**Step 1: $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{a}]$ is a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module of rank 2 when $\
sqrt{a} \notin \mathbb{Z}$.**

By definition,
$$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{a}] = \{m + n\sqrt{a} : m, n \in \mathbb{Z}\}.$$

We show that $\{1, \sqrt{a}\}$ is a $\mathbb{Z}$-basis.

*Spanning:* Every element is $m \cdot 1 + n \cdot \sqrt{a}$ by construction, so
$\{1, \sqrt{a}\}$ spans.

*Linear independence:* Suppose $m + n\sqrt{a} = 0$ for some $m, n \in \mathbb{Z}
$. If $n \neq 0$, then $\sqrt{a} = -m/n \in \mathbb{Q}$. But $a$ is an integer t
hat is not a perfect square (since $\sqrt{a} \notin \mathbb{Z}$), so $\sqrt{a}$
is irrational — a contradiction. Hence $n = 0$, and then $m = 0$.

Thus $\{1, \sqrt{a}\}$ is a $\mathbb{Z}$-basis and $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{a}] \cong \
mathbb{Z}^2$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module.

**Step 2: Both modules are isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}^2$.**

By Step 1, $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{a}] \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$ and $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{b}] \
cong \mathbb{Z}^2$. By transitivity of isomorphism,
$$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{a}] \cong \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{b}] \quad \text{as } \mathbb{Z}\te
xt{-modules}.$$

An explicit isomorphism $\varphi: \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{a}] \to \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{b}]$
 is given by
$$\varphi(m + n\sqrt{a}) = m + n\sqrt{b}, \quad m, n \in \mathbb{Z}.$$
This is well-defined (since $\{1, \sqrt{a}\}$ is a basis, the representation $m
+ n\sqrt{a}$ is unique), $\mathbb{Z}$-linear, bijective (its inverse sends $m +
n\sqrt{b} \mapsto m + n\sqrt{a}$), and hence a $\mathbb{Z}$-module isomorphism.

**Step 3: Verification of the example $a=2, b=3$.**

- $a = 2 \neq 3 = b$: ✓
- $\sqrt{2} \notin \mathbb{Z}$ (2 is not a perfect square): ✓
- $\sqrt{3} \notin \mathbb{Z}$ (3 is not a perfect square): ✓
- $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}] \cong \mathbb{Z}^2 \cong \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{3}]$ as $\math
bb{Z}$-modules by the argument above: ✓

**Remark.** The key point is that the $\mathbb{Z}$-module structure of $\mathbb{
Z}[\sqrt{a}]$ depends only on its *rank* as a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module, not on t
he ring-theoretic properties of $\sqrt{a}$. As a $\mathbb{Z}$-module (i.e., as a
n abelian group), $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{a}]$ forgets the multiplicative structure en
tirely; it only remembers the additive group, which is always $\mathbb{Z}^2$ whe
never $\sqrt{a} \notin \mathbb{Z}$. The distinction between $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}
]$ and $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{3}]$ only appears at the level of *ring* isomorphisms (
or $\mathbb{Z}$-algebra isomorphisms), where they are indeed non-isomorphic — bu
t the question asks only about $\mathbb{Z}$-module isomorphism. $\blacksquare$
