The statement is **FALSE**. The forward implication holds, but the reverse does
not. Below is the complete proof.

---

## Proof

We analyze the two directions of the claimed equivalence separately.

### Direction 1: Hausdorff ⟹ unique limits of sequences (TRUE)

Let $X$ be a Hausdorff space, and let $(x_n)$ be a sequence in $X$ converging to
 both $x$ and $y$. Suppose for contradiction that $x \neq y$. By the Hausdorff p
roperty, there exist disjoint open neighborhoods $U \ni x$ and $V \ni y$ with $U
 \cap V = \varnothing$.

Since $x_n \to x$, there exists $N_1$ such that $x_n \in U$ for all $n \ge N_1$.
 Since $x_n \to y$, there exists $N_2$ such that $x_n \in V$ for all $n \ge N_2$
. For $n \ge \max(N_1, N_2)$, we have $x_n \in U \cap V$, contradicting $U \cap
V = \varnothing$. Hence $x = y$, and limits of sequences are unique. ∎

### Direction 2: Unique limits of sequences ⟹ Hausdorff (FALSE)

We exhibit a counterexample: a topological space in which every convergent seque
nce has a unique limit, yet the space is not Hausdorff.

**The space.** Let $X$ be an **uncountable** set equipped with the **cocountable
 topology**: a set $U \subseteq X$ is open if and only if $U = \varnothing$ or $
X \setminus U$ is countable.

**Claim A: $X$ is not Hausdorff.** Take any two nonempty open sets $U, V \subset
eq X$. Their complements $X \setminus U$ and $X \setminus V$ are each countable,
 so $X \setminus (U \cap V) = (X \setminus U) \cup (X \setminus V)$ is countable
. Since $X$ is uncountable, $U \cap V \neq \varnothing$. Thus no two nonempty op
en sets are disjoint, so $X$ is not Hausdorff (it is, however, $T_1$, since sing
letons are closed).

**Claim B: Every convergent sequence in $X$ is eventually constant.** Let $(x_n)
$ be a sequence with $x_n \to x$. Consider the set
$$S = \{x_n : n \in \mathbb{N},\ x_n \neq x\}.$$
This set is countable (it is the image of a subset of $\mathbb{N}$). Therefore $
U = X \setminus S$ is open, and $x \in U$ (since $x \notin S$ by construction).
By convergence, there exists $N$ such that $x_n \in U$ for all $n \ge N$. But $U
 \cap S = \varnothing$, so for $n \ge N$ we cannot have $x_n \neq x$; hence $x_n
 = x$ for all $n \ge N$. The sequence is eventually constant at $x$.

**Claim C: Limits of sequences are unique.** Suppose $x_n \to x$ and $x_n \to y$
. By Claim B, the sequence is eventually constant at $x$ and eventually constant
 at $y$. That is, there exist $N_1, N_2$ with $x_n = x$ for all $n \ge N_1$ and
$x_n = y$ for all $n \ge N_2$. For $n \ge \max(N_1, N_2)$, we get $x = x_n = y$.
 Hence $x = y$.

### Conclusion

We have shown:
- Hausdorff implies unique limits of sequences (Direction 1), but
- the cocountable topology on an uncountable set has unique limits of sequences
yet is **not** Hausdorff (Direction 2 fails).

Therefore the biconditional "a topological space has unique limits of sequences
if and only if it is a Hausdorff space" is **false**. The correct relationship i
s one-way: Hausdorff is a *sufficient* but not *necessary* condition for unique
sequential limits. (Spaces with unique sequential limits are sometimes called *U
S spaces*; they form a class strictly between $T_1$ and Hausdorff, and are in ge
neral incomparable with several other weak separation properties.)
