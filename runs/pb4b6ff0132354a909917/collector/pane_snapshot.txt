## Proof

**Setup.** Consider the integrand

$$f(z) = \frac{e^{\sin z^{2}}}{(z^{2}+1)(z-2i)^{3}}.$$

The denominator factors as

$$(z^{2}+1)(z-2i)^{3} = (z-i)(z+i)(z-2i)^{3},$$

so the only singularities of $f$ are at

$$z = i, \quad z = -i, \quad z = 2i.$$

The numerator $e^{\sin z^{2}}$ is entire, so it introduces no additional singula
rities.

**Contour.** The contour $\gamma$ is a circle centered at the origin whose radiu
s is chosen so that **all three** singular points $z = \pm i$ (at distance $1$ f
rom the origin) and $z = 2i$ (at distance $2$ from the origin) lie **outside** $
\gamma$.

**Applying the Cauchy Integral Formula.** The Cauchy Integral Formula in its gen
eral (Cauchy theorem) form states:

> *If $g$ is analytic on and inside a simple closed contour $\gamma$, then $\dis
playstyle\oint_{\gamma} g(z)\,dz = 0$.*

This is a direct consequence of the Cauchy Integral Formula: with no singularity
 enclosed, the formula $\oint_\gamma \frac{g(z)}{z-a}\,dz = 2\pi i\, g(a)$ has n
o contributing point $a$, giving zero.

Since the contour $\gamma$ excludes all singularities $z = i,\,-i,\,2i$, the fun
ction $f(z)$ is **analytic everywhere on and inside** $\gamma$. Therefore, by th
e Cauchy Integral Formula (zero-singularity case),

$$\boxed{\oint_{\gamma} \frac{e^{\sin z^{2}}}{(z^{2}+1)(z-2i)^{3}}\,dz = 0.}$$
