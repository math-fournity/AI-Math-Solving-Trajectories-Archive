< \epsilon$ for all $z \in S^1$.

**Step 1: Characterize $\Re(p(z))$ on the unit circle.**

Let $p(z) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} c_k z^k$ with $c_k \in \mathbb{C}$. On the unit circl

$$p(e^{i\theta}) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} c_k e^{ik\theta}.$$

Writing $c_k = \alpha_k + i\beta_k$ with $\alpha_k, \beta_k \in \mathbb{R}$:
$$\Re(p(e^{i\theta})) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \bigl(\alpha_k \cos k\theta - \beta_k \si
n k\theta\bigr).$$

Since $c_k$ ranges over all of $\mathbb{C}$, the pairs $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$ ran
ge over all of $\mathbb{R}^2$. Setting $a_k = \alpha_k$ and $b_k = -\beta_k$, we
 obtain:
$$\Re(p(e^{i\theta})) = a_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{n} \bigl(a_k \cos k\theta + b_k \sin k
\theta\bigr), \qquad a_k, b_k \in \mathbb{R} \text{ arbitrary.}$$

This is precisely the general form of a **real-valued trigonometric polynomial**
. Conversely, every real-valued trigonometric polynomial arises this way (choose

$$\{\Re(p(z))|_{S^1} : p \text{ analytic polynomial}\} = \{\text{real-valued tri
