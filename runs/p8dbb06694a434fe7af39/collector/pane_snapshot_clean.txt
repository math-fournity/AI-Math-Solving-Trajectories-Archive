## Solution

The answer is **no**: $f$ need not be injective.

### Construction

Let $C = \{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}$ be the Cantor space (homeomorphic to the Cantor s
et). Set $p = 0^\infty = (0,0,0,\dots)$ and $q = 1^\infty = (1,1,1,\dots)$, with
 $p \neq q$.

Define an equivalence relation $\sim$ on $C$ by: $x \sim y$ iff $x = y$ or $\{x,
y\} = \{p,q\}$. This identifies exactly the two points $p$ and $q$, and is trivi
al elsewhere. Let $\pi: C \to C/{\sim}$ be the quotient map.

**Claim.** $C/{\sim} \cong C$.

We verify the hypotheses of Brouwer's characterization theorem: a space is homeo
morphic to the Cantor set iff it is nonempty, compact, metrizable, perfect, and
totally disconnected.

**Nonempty, compact.** $C/{\sim}$ is the continuous image of the nonempty compac
t space $C$.

**Metrizable (and Hausdorff).** The relation $R = \{(x,x): x \in C\} \cup \{(p,q
),(q,p)\}$ is closed in $C \times C$ (the diagonal is closed since $C$ is Hausdo
rff, and we adjoin two points). A standard result: if $X$ is compact metrizable
and $R \subseteq X \times X$ is a closed equivalence relation, then $X/R$ is com
pact metrizable.

**Totally disconnected.** Since $\pi$ is a closed map (continuous from compact t
o Hausdorff), $U \subseteq C/{\sim}$ is clopen iff $\pi^{-1}(U)$ is clopen in $C
$. We separate any two distinct points $[a] \neq [b]$ by a saturated clopen set:

- *Case 1:* $[a],[b] \neq [p]$. Then $a, b \in C\setminus\{p,q\}$ are distinct.
Since $C$ is zero-dimensional, there is a clopen neighborhood $V$ of $a$ avoidin
g $b$, $p$, and $q$. Then $V$ is saturated and $\pi(V)$ is clopen, separating $[
a]$ from $[b]$.

- *Case 2:* $[a] = [p] = [q]$, $[b] \neq [p]$, so $b \notin \{p,q\}$. Take clope
n neighborhoods $V_p \ni p$, $V_q \ni q$ both avoiding $b$ (zero-dimensionality)
. Then $V = V_p \cup V_q$ is clopen, saturated, contains $p$ and $q$, excludes $
b$. So $\pi(V)$ is clopen, separating $[p]$ from $[b]$.

**Perfect.** For $[a]$ with $a \notin \{p,q\}$: since $C$ is perfect, there is a
 sequence $a_n \to a$ with $a_n \neq a$; for large $n$, $a_n \notin \{p,q\}$ (as
 $a \notin \{p,q\}$), so $[a_n] \neq [a]$ and $[a_n] \to [a]$. For $[p]$: take $
a_n \to p$ with $a_n \neq p$; since $a_n \to p \neq q$, we have $a_n \neq q$ for
 large $n$, so $[a_n] \neq [p]$ and $[a_n] \to [p]$.

By Brouwer's theorem, $C/{\sim} \cong C$. Let $h: C/{\sim} \to C$ be a homeomorp
hism and set $f = h \circ \pi: C \to C$.

### Verification

- **Continuous:** $f$ is a composition of continuous maps.
- **Not injective:** $f(p) = h([p]) = h([q]) = f(q)$, and $p \neq q$.
- **Injective on $C \setminus \{p\}$:** The only non-trivial identification unde
r $\sim$ is $p \sim q$. On $C \setminus \{p\}$, the point $q$ is the unique elem
ent in its fiber $[q]=[p]$, and every other equivalence class is a singleton. He
nce $\pi|_{C\setminus\{p\}}$ is injective, and therefore $f|_{C\setminus\{p\}}$
is injective.

Taking $X = Y = C$ and $x_0 = p$, this yields a continuous $f: X \to Y$ that is
injective on $X \setminus \{x_0\}$ but not injective on $X$.

### Conclusion

$f$ **need not** be injective. $\blacksquare$
