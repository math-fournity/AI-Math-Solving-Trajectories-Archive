## Proof

We compute
$$I(a) = \int_0^{\infty} \frac{\sin(ax)}{x}\,e^{-x}\,dx.$$

**Step 1. Differentiate under the integral sign.**

For any fixed $a$, consider a neighborhood $|a|\le A$. On this neighborhood:
- The integrand satisfies $\left|\frac{\sin(ax)}{x}e^{-x}\right|\le A\,e^{-x}$ (
using $|\sin u|\le |u|$), and $Ae^{-x}$ is integrable on $[0,\infty)$.
- Its $a$-partial derivative is $\cos(ax)\,e^{-x}$, with $|\cos(ax)e^{-x}|\le e^
{-x}$, also integrable.

Hence differentiation under the integral is justified, and
$$I'(a)=\int_0^{\infty}\cos(ax)\,e^{-x}\,dx.$$

**Step 2. Evaluate $I'(a)$ by integration by parts (no series used).**

Let
$$J=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-x}\cos(ax)\,dx,\qquad K=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-x}\sin(ax)\,dx
.$$

Integrating $J$ by parts with $u=\cos(ax)$, $dv=e^{-x}dx$:
$$J=\Big[-e^{-x}\cos(ax)\Big]_0^{\infty}-\int_0^{\infty}(-e^{-x})(-a\sin(ax))\,d
x=1-aK.$$

Integrating $K$ by parts with $u=\sin(ax)$, $dv=e^{-x}dx$:
$$K=\Big[-e^{-x}\sin(ax)\Big]_0^{\infty}-\int_0^{\infty}(-e^{-x})(a\cos(ax))\,dx
=0+aJ=aJ.$$

Substituting $K=aJ$ into $J=1-aK$:
$$J=1-a(aJ)=1-a^2J\implies J(1+a^2)=1\implies J=\frac{1}{1+a^2}.$$

Thus
$$I'(a)=\frac{1}{1+a^2}.$$

**Step 3. Integrate in $a$ and determine the constant.**

$$I(a)=\int\frac{da}{1+a^2}=\arctan(a)+C.$$

At $a=0$:
$$I(0)=\int_0^{\infty}\frac{\sin(0)}{x}e^{-x}\,dx=0=\arctan(0)+C=C,$$
so $C=0$.

**Conclusion.**

$$\boxed{\int_0^{\infty}\frac{\sin(ax)}{x\,e^x}\,dx=\arctan(a).}$$
