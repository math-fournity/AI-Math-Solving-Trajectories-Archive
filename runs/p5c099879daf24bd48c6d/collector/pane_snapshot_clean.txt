## Proof

**Yes**, such a function exists. The example is **Thomae's function** (the popco
rn function).

### Construction

Define $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ by

$$
f(x)=\begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{q} & \text{if } x=\dfrac{p}{q}\text{ with }p,q\text
{ coprime integers, }q>0,\\[6pt] 0 & \text{if } x\text{ is irrational.}\end{case
s}
$$

We prove that (i) $\sup f$ is finite, (ii) $f$ is discontinuous at every rationa
l, and (iii) $f$ is continuous at every irrational. Since the rationals $\mathbb
{Q}$ are countable and dense in $\mathbb{R}$, this gives the desired function.

---

### (i) The supremum is finite

At $x=0=\tfrac{0}{1}$ we have $f(0)=1$. For every rational $x=\tfrac{p}{q}$ in l
owest terms, $f(x)=\tfrac{1}{q}\le 1$, and for every irrational $x$, $f(x)=0$. H
ence

$$
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}} f(x)=1<\infty.
$$

---

### (ii) $f$ is discontinuous at every rational

Let $r=\tfrac{p}{q}\in\mathbb{Q}$ in lowest terms with $q>0$. Then $f(r)=\tfrac{
1}{q}>0$. Every neighborhood of $r$ contains irrational numbers, at which $f$ ta
kes the value $0$. Setting $\varepsilon=\tfrac{1}{2q}>0$, for every $\delta>0$ t
here exists an irrational $x$ with $|x-r|<\delta$ and

$$
|f(x)-f(r)|=\tfrac{1}{q}>\varepsilon.
$$

Thus $f$ is discontinuous at $r$.

---

### (iii) $f$ is continuous at every irrational

Let $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$, so $f(\alpha)=0$. We show: for eve
ry $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $\delta>0$ with $|x-\alpha|<\delta\Rightarrow |f
(x)|<\varepsilon$.

Given $\varepsilon>0$, pick $N\in\mathbb{N}$ with $\tfrac{1}{N}<\varepsilon$. Co
nsider the finite set

$$
S_N=\Bigl\{\tfrac{p}{q}\in\mathbb{Q}:\ \gcd(p,q)=1,\ 1\le q\le N,\ \tfrac{p}{q}\
in[\alpha-1,\alpha+1]\Bigr\}.
$$

This set is finite (bounded interval, finitely many denominators $\le N$). Since
 $\alpha$ is irrational, $\alpha\notin S_N$, so

$$
\delta:=\min\Bigl(\{1\}\cup\{|\alpha-s|:s\in S_N\}\Bigr)>0.
$$

Now suppose $|x-\alpha|<\delta$.

- **If $x$ is irrational**, then $f(x)=0<\varepsilon$. ✓
- **If $x=\tfrac{p}{q}$ (lowest terms) is rational**, then $|x-\alpha|<\delta\le
 1$, so $x\in(\alpha-1,\alpha+1)$. If $q\le N$ then $x\in S_N$, but $|x-\alpha|<
\delta\le|\alpha-s|$ for every $s\in S_N$—a contradiction. Therefore $q>N$, givi
ng

$$
f(x)=\tfrac{1}{q}<\tfrac{1}{N}<\varepsilon.\quad\checkmark
$$

Hence $f$ is continuous at $\alpha$.

---

### Conclusion

The function $f$ is continuous exactly on $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ and di
scontinuous exactly on $\mathbb{Q}$, which is **countable and dense** in $\mathb
b{R}$. Its supremum equals $1$, which is **finite**. Therefore such a function e
xists.
