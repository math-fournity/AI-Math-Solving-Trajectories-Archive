Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether $AB = \{ab : a \in A, b \in
 B\}$ (scalar multiplication, with $a \in \mathbb{R}$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}^2$)
is always Borel when $A$ and $B$ are Borel.

**Answer: No.** Here is the proof.

---

**Proof.** We construct a counterexample with $A = \mathbb{R}$ and a carefully c
hosen Borel set $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ such that $AB$ is not Borel.

**Step 1: A classical ingredient.** By a fundamental theorem of descriptive set
theory (Souslin, 1917), there exists a Borel set $F \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ whos
e projection $\pi_1(F) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : \exists\, y,\; (x,y) \in F\}$ is a
nalytic but **not** Borel.

Applying the homeomorphism $(x, y) \mapsto (x, e^y)$ from $\mathbb{R}^2$ to $\ma
thbb{R} \times (0,\infty)$, we obtain a Borel set $E \subseteq \mathbb{R} \times
 (0,\infty)$ with $\pi_1(E) = \pi_1(F)$, which is not Borel.

**Step 2: Constructing $B$.** Define the map $\psi : \mathbb{R} \times (0,\infty
) \to \mathbb{R} \times (0,\infty)$ by $\psi(x, y) = (xy,\, y)$. This is a homeo
morphism with inverse $\psi^{-1}(u, v) = (u/v,\, v)$ for $v > 0$.

Set $B = \psi(E) = \{(xy, y) : (x, y) \in E\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$. Since $E$
 is Borel in $\mathbb{R}^2$ (as a Borel subset of the open set $\mathbb{R} \time
s (0,\infty)$) and $\psi$ is a homeomorphism, $B$ is Borel in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Step 3: Set $A = \mathbb{R}$** (clearly Borel) and consider the line $L = \{(x
, 1) : x \in \mathbb{R}\}$.

**Step 4: Computing $AB \cap L$.** A point $(x, 1) \in AB$ iff there exist $a \i
n \mathbb{R}$ and $(u, v) \in B$ with $au = x$ and $av = 1$.

- From $av = 1$: we need $a \neq 0$ and $v = 1/a$. Since $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}
 \times (0,\infty)$, we have $v > 0$, so $a > 0$.
- Then $u = x/a$, giving $(u, v) = (x/a,\, 1/a)$.
- The condition $(u, v) \in B$ is equivalent to $\psi^{-1}(u, v) \in E$, i.e., $
(x,\, 1/a) \in E$.

Setting $w = 1/a > 0$, we conclude:
$$(x, 1) \in AB \iff \exists\, w > 0 \text{ such that } (x, w) \in E \iff x \in
\pi_1(E).$$

Therefore $AB \cap L = \{(x, 1) : x \in \pi_1(E)\}$.

**Step 5: Conclusion.** Suppose for contradiction that $AB$ is Borel. Then $AB \
cap L$ is Borel (intersection of Borel sets, since $L$ is closed). The map $(x,
1) \mapsto x$ is a homeomorphism from $L$ to $\mathbb{R}$, so $\pi_1(E) = \{x :
(x,1) \in AB \cap L\}$ would be Borel. This contradicts the fact that $\pi_1(E)$
 is not Borel.

Hence $AB$ is **not** Borel, even though $A = \mathbb{R}$ and $B$ are both Borel
. $\blacksquare$

---

**Remark.** The essential mechanism: $AB$ with $A = \mathbb{R}$ is the cone over
 $B$ (all scalar multiples of points in $B$). By encoding a Borel set with non-B
orel projection into $B$ via the homeomorphism $\psi(x,y) = (xy, y)$, the inters
ection of the cone $AB$ with the line $y = 1$ recovers exactly the non-Borel pro
jection $\pi_1(E)$. Since Borel sets are closed under intersection with Borel li
nes, $AB$ itself cannot be Borel.
