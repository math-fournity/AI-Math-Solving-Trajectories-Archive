## Solution

**The statement is TRUE: $f(x) \equiv 0$ on $[0,\pi]$.**

### Proof

The key fact is that the family $\{\sin(nx) : n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\}$ forms a **co
mplete orthogonal system** in $L^2([0,\pi], dx)$.

**Completeness of the sine system.** Let $h \in L^2[0,\pi]$. Define its **odd ex
tension** to $[-\pi,\pi]$ by
$$
g(x) = \begin{cases} h(x), & x \in [0,\pi], \\ -h(-x), & x \in [-\pi, 0). \end{c
ases}
$$
Then $g \in L^2[-\pi,\pi]$ and $g$ is odd. The classical trigonometric system $\
{1,\, \cos(nx),\, \sin(nx) : n \ge 1\}$ is complete in $L^2[-\pi,\pi]$ (this is
the standard Fourier-basis completeness theorem, following from the Stone–Weiers
trass theorem applied to continuous periodic functions, combined with the densit
y of $C[-\pi,\pi]$ in $L^2$). Since $g$ is odd, its Fourier coefficients against
 $1$ and against every $\cos(nx)$ vanish identically, so the Fourier expansion o
f $g$ reduces to a pure sine series:
$$
g = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n \sin(nx) \quad \text{in } L^2[-\pi,\pi].
$$
Restricting this identity to $[0,\pi]$ gives $h = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n \sin(n
x)$ in $L^2[0,\pi]$. Hence every $L^2[0,\pi]$ function is an $L^2$-limit of fini
te linear combinations of $\{\sin(nx)\}$, i.e., the sine system is **complete**
in $L^2[0,\pi]$.

**Applying the hypothesis.** Now let $f \in C[0,\pi]$ satisfy
$$
\int_0^\pi f(x)\sin(nx)\,dx = 0 \quad \text{for every integer } n \ge 1.
$$
Since $f$ is continuous on a compact interval, $f \in L^2[0,\pi]$. The hypothesi
s says $f$ is orthogonal (in the $L^2$ inner product) to every element of a comp
lete orthogonal system. By completeness, the only $L^2$ function orthogonal to a
ll $\sin(nx)$ is the zero function. Therefore
$$
\int_0^\pi |f(x)|^2\,dx = 0.
$$
Since $|f|^2$ is continuous and non-negative with zero integral, $|f(x)|^2 = 0$
for all $x \in [0,\pi]$, hence
$$
f(x) = 0 \quad \text{for all } x \in [0,\pi].
$$

### Conclusion

The condition $\int_0^\pi f(x)\sin(nx)\,dx = 0$ for all $n \ge 1$, together with
 the continuity of $f$, forces $f \equiv 0$ on $[0,\pi]$. The statement is **tru
e**.
