## Solution

**Yes, such integer functions exist.**

**Construction.** Let $p_n/q_n$ (with $q_n > 0$) denote the $n$-th convergent of
 the continued fraction expansion of $\pi$. Define
$$f(n) = q_n, \qquad g(n) = -p_n.$$
Both are integer-valued functions on the positive integers.

**The expression is never zero.** We have $f(n)\pi + g(n) = q_n \pi - p_n$. If t
his were zero, then $\pi = p_n/q_n$, a rational number, contradicting the irrati
onality of $\pi$. Hence $f(n)\pi + g(n) \neq 0$ for every positive integer $n$.

**The limit is zero.** A standard property of continued fraction convergents is
the error bound
$$\left|\pi - \frac{p_n}{q_n}\right| < \frac{1}{q_n\, q_{n+1}}.$$
Multiplying by $q_n$:
$$|q_n \pi - p_n| < \frac{1}{q_{n+1}}.$$
Since the denominators $q_n$ of the convergents form a strictly increasing seque
nce of positive integers (for $n \geq 1$, $q_{n+1} \geq q_n + q_{n-1} \geq q_n +
 1$), we have $q_{n+1} \to \infty$. Therefore
$$|f(n)\pi + g(n)| = |q_n \pi - p_n| < \frac{1}{q_{n+1}} \to 0 \quad \text{as }
n \to \infty.$$

**Conclusion.** The integer functions $f(n) = q_n$ and $g(n) = -p_n$, built from
 the continued fraction convergents of $\pi$, satisfy both $\lim_{n\to\infty} f(
n)\pi + g(n) = 0$ and $f(n)\pi + g(n) \neq 0$ for all positive integers $n$.
