## Solution

We evaluate the integral
$$I = \int_{0}^{2\pi} e^{-\sin\theta}\cos(\theta+\cos\theta)\,d\theta.$$

**Key observation.** We look for an antiderivative of the integrand. Consider th
e function
$$F(\theta) = e^{-\sin\theta}\cos(\cos\theta).$$

**Computing the derivative.** By the product rule and chain rule:
$$F'(\theta) = \frac{d}{d\theta}\!\left[e^{-\sin\theta}\right]\cdot\cos(\cos\the
ta) + e^{-\sin\theta}\cdot\frac{d}{d\theta}\!\left[\cos(\cos\theta)\right].$$

The two factors are:
$$\frac{d}{d\theta}\!\left[e^{-\sin\theta}\right] = -\cos\theta\cdot e^{-\sin\th
eta},$$
$$\frac{d}{d\theta}\!\left[\cos(\cos\theta)\right] = -\sin(\cos\theta)\cdot(-\si
n\theta) = \sin\theta\cdot\sin(\cos\theta).$$

Combining:
$$F'(\theta) = e^{-\sin\theta}\Big[-\cos\theta\cos(\cos\theta) + \sin\theta\sin(
\cos\theta)\Big].$$

Using the cosine addition formula $\cos(\alpha+\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\a
lpha\sin\beta$ with $\alpha=\theta$, $\beta=\cos\theta$:
$$\cos\theta\cos(\cos\theta) - \sin\theta\sin(\cos\theta) = \cos(\theta+\cos\the
ta).$$

Therefore:
$$F'(\theta) = -e^{-\sin\theta}\cos(\theta+\cos\theta).$$

**Applying the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.** Since $F'(\theta) = -e^{-\sin\
theta}\cos(\theta+\cos\theta)$, we have
$$I = \int_{0}^{2\pi} e^{-\sin\theta}\cos(\theta+\cos\theta)\,d\theta = -\Big[F(
\theta)\Big]_{0}^{2\pi} = -\Big[e^{-\sin\theta}\cos(\cos\theta)\Big]_{0}^{2\pi}.
$$

**Evaluating at the bounds.** Using $\sin(0)=\sin(2\pi)=0$ and $\cos(0)=\cos(2\p
i)=1$:
$$F(2\pi) = e^{-\sin(2\pi)}\cos(\cos(2\pi)) = e^{0}\cos(1) = \cos(1),$$
$$F(0) = e^{-\sin(0)}\cos(\cos(0)) = e^{0}\cos(1) = \cos(1).$$

Hence:
$$I = -\big[\cos(1) - \cos(1)\big] = -0 = 0.$$

**Conclusion.** The integrand is the derivative (up to sign) of the periodic fun
ction $F(\theta)=e^{-\sin\theta}\cos(\cos\theta)$, which is smooth and $2\pi$-pe
riodic. The integral over one full period therefore vanishes.

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi} e^{-\sin\theta}\cos(\theta+\cos\theta)\,d\
theta = 0.}$$
