## Proof

**Setup and the polar.** For any set $\mathcal{M}$, define
$$\mathcal{M}^* = \{(x^*,y^*) : xx^*+yy^* \le 1 \text{ for all } (x,y)\in\mathca
l{M}\}.$$
This is the **polar** $\mathcal{M}^\circ$: the intersection of all half-planes $
\langle p, p^*\rangle \le 1$ over $p\in\mathcal{M}$. Since $\mathcal{M}^*$ is an
 intersection of half-planes, it is always closed and convex. If $\mathcal{M}$ i
s a convex polygon, $\mathcal{M}^*$ is determined by the vertices of $\mathcal{M
}$ alone (a linear functional attains its maximum on a polygon at a vertex).

**The origin must lie in the interior of T.** If the origin is not in the interi
or of $\mathcal{T}$, then $\mathcal{T}^*$ is unbounded (it contains a ray or hal
f-plane), so it cannot equal $-\mathcal{T}$, which is a bounded triangle. Hence
the origin lies in the interior of $\mathcal{T}$.

**Vertices of the polar.** Let $\mathcal{T}$ have vertices $A,B,C$ (in countercl
ockwise order), with the origin in the interior. The polar $\mathcal{T}^\circ$ i
s the intersection of the three half-planes
$$\langle A, p^*\rangle \le 1,\quad \langle B, p^*\rangle \le 1,\quad \langle C,
 p^*\rangle \le 1.$$
Its vertices are the pairwise intersections of the boundary lines:
- $P_{BC}$: $\langle B,p^*\rangle=1,\ \langle C,p^*\rangle=1$ (opposite to the e
dge on $\langle A,\cdot\rangle=1$),
- $P_{CA}$: $\langle C,p^*\rangle=1,\ \langle A,p^*\rangle=1$,
- $P_{AB}$: $\langle A,p^*\rangle=1,\ \langle B,p^*\rangle=1$.

**Deriving the conditions.** We require $\mathcal{T}^\circ = -\mathcal{T}$, i.e.
, the vertex set $\{P_{BC},P_{CA},P_{AB}\}$ equals $\{-A,-B,-C\}$.

**Claim:** The only possible assignment is $P_{BC}=-A$, $P_{CA}=-B$, $P_{AB}=-C$
.

*Proof of claim.* The point $P_{BC}$ lies on the lines $\langle B,\cdot\rangle=1
$ and $\langle C,\cdot\rangle=1$. If $P_{BC}=-B$, then $\langle B,-B\rangle=1$,
giving $|B|^2=-1$, impossible. If $P_{BC}=-C$, then $|C|^2=-1$, impossible. Henc
e $P_{BC}=-A$. By the same argument, $P_{CA}=-B$ and $P_{AB}=-C$. $\square$

From $P_{BC}=-A$: the point $-A$ lies on $\langle B,\cdot\rangle=1$ and $\langle
 C,\cdot\rangle=1$, so
$$\langle B,-A\rangle = 1 \implies A\cdot B = -1, \qquad \langle C,-A\rangle=1 \
implies A\cdot C=-1.$$
From $P_{CA}=-B$: $\langle C,-B\rangle=1 \implies B\cdot C=-1$.

Thus the **necessary** conditions are:
$$\boxed{A\cdot B = B\cdot C = C\cdot A = -1.} \tag{$\star$}$$

**Sufficiency.** Conversely, suppose $A,B,C$ are non-collinear and satisfy $(\st
ar)$. Then $-A$ lies on $\langle B,\cdot\rangle=1$ and $\langle C,\cdot\rangle=1
$ (since $A\cdot B=A\cdot C=-1$), so $P_{BC}=-A$. Similarly $P_{CA}=-B$ and $P_{
AB}=-C$. Hence $\mathcal{T}^\circ$ has vertices $-A,-B,-C$, which is exactly $-\
mathcal{T}$. So $(\star)$ is **sufficient** (provided the origin is in the inter
ior, which we verify next).

**The origin is automatically in the interior.** We show that $(\star)$ forces t
he origin to be an interior point of $\triangle ABC$. Define
$$\alpha=\frac{1}{1+|A|^2},\quad \beta=\frac{1}{1+|B|^2},\quad \gamma=\frac{1}{1
+|C|^2}.$$
These are all positive. Taking the dot product of $\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C$ wi
th $A$:
$$\alpha|A|^2+\beta(A\cdot B)+\gamma(A\cdot C)=\alpha|A|^2-\beta-\gamma.$$
Using $\alpha|A|^2 = \frac{|A|^2}{1+|A|^2}=1-\alpha=1-\frac{1}{1+|A|^2}$ and $\b
eta+\gamma = 1-\alpha$ (once we know $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1$), this vanishes. Si
milarly the dot products with $B$ and $C$ vanish. Since $A,B,C$ span $\mathbb{R}
^2$ (non-collinear), we get $\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C=0$.

It remains to check $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1$. Let $a=|A|^2, b=|B|^2, c=|C|^2$. Th
e Gram matrix
$$G=\begin{pmatrix} a & -1 & -1\\ -1 & b & -1\\ -1&-1&c\end{pmatrix}$$
has $\det G = abc-a-b-c-2$. Since $A,B,C\in\mathbb{R}^2$, the three vectors are
linearly dependent, so $\det G=0$, giving $abc=a+b+c+2$. Now:
$$\alpha+\beta+\gamma = \frac{(1+b)(1+c)+(1+a)(1+c)+(1+a)(1+b)}{(1+a)(1+b)(1+c)}
.$$
The numerator is $3+2a+2b+2c+ab+bc+ca$. The denominator is $1+a+b+c+ab+bc+ca+abc
 = 1+a+b+c+ab+bc+ca+(a+b+c+2)=3+2a+2b+2c+ab+bc+ca$. These are equal, so $\alpha+
\beta+\gamma=1$. $\checkmark$

Thus the barycentric coordinates of the origin are $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$, all
positive, confirming the origin is in the **interior** of $\mathcal{T}$.

**Geometric characterization.** The condition $A\cdot B = A\cdot C$ gives $A\cdo
t(B-C)=0$, so $OA\perp BC$; the line $OA$ is the altitude from $A$. Similarly $O
B\perp AC$ and $OC\perp AB$. Hence the origin is the **orthocenter** of $\triang
le ABC$. The single scalar condition $A\cdot B=-1$ fixes the scale. So equivalen
tly:

> $\mathcal{T}^*=-\mathcal{T}$ if and only if the origin is the orthocenter of $
\mathcal{T}$ and the common value of the pairwise dot products of the vertex pos
ition vectors is $-1$.

**The family is non-trivial (not just equilateral).** For example, $A=(1,0),\ B=
(-1,2),\ C=(-1,-1)$ satisfies $(\star)$:
$$A\cdot B=-1,\quad A\cdot C=-1,\quad B\cdot C=1-2=-1.$$
The polar vertices are $P_{BC}=(-1,0)=-A$, $P_{CA}=(1,-2)=-B$, $P_{AB}=(1,1)=-C$
, confirming $\mathcal{T}^\circ=-\mathcal{T}$. This is a scalene triangle, so th
e family is a genuine 3-parameter family (up to rotation, a 1-parameter family o
f shapes at each scale—but scale is fixed by the condition $A\cdot B=-1$, so it
is a 1-parameter family of shapes up to rotation).

**Conclusion.** The triangles $\mathcal{T}$ such that $\mathcal{T}^*$ is the ref
lection of $\mathcal{T}$ at the origin are exactly the triangles with vertices $
A,B,C$ (non-collinear) satisfying
$$A\cdot B = B\cdot C = C\cdot A = -1,$$
i.e., the triangles whose orthocenter is the origin and whose vertices have pair
wise dot product $-1$.
