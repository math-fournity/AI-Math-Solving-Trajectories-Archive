## Proof

Let $x$ and $x'$ both satisfy the axiom-of-infinity property, i.e., each is **in
ductive**:

$$\emptyset \in x \;\land\; \forall y\,(y\in x \Rightarrow y\cup\{y\}\in x),$$

and likewise for $x'$. Define

$$\mathbb{N}_x := \bigcap\{z\in\mathcal{P}(x): \emptyset\in z \land \forall y\,(
y\in z \Rightarrow y\cup\{y\}\in z)\},$$

and define $\mathbb{N}_{x'}$ analogously with $x'$ in place of $x$. We prove $\m
athbb{N}_x = \mathbb{N}_{x'}$.

---

### Step 1. The defining family is non-empty, so the intersection is well-define
d.

Since $x$ itself belongs to $\mathcal{P}(x)$ and is inductive (by hypothesis), $
x$ is a member of the family $\{z\in\mathcal{P}(x): z \text{ inductive}\}$. Henc
e this family is non-empty, and $\mathbb{N}_x$ is a well-defined set with $\math
bb{N}_x \subseteq x$. The same holds for $\mathbb{N}_{x'}$.

---

### Step 2. $\mathbb{N}_x$ is inductive.

Each member $z$ of the family is inductive by definition. The intersection of in
ductive sets is inductive:

- $\emptyset \in z$ for every $z$ in the family, so $\emptyset \in \mathbb{N}_x$
.
- If $y \in \mathbb{N}_x$, then $y \in z$ for every $z$ in the family; since eac
h $z$ is inductive, $y\cup\{y\} \in z$ for every such $z$, hence $y\cup\{y\}\in\
mathbb{N}_x$.

Thus $\mathbb{N}_x$ is inductive. By the identical argument, $\mathbb{N}_{x'}$ i
s inductive.

---

### Step 3. $x \cap x'$ is an inductive subset of $x$ (and of $x'$).

Clearly $x\cap x' \subseteq x$, so $x\cap x' \in \mathcal{P}(x)$.

- $\emptyset \in x$ and $\emptyset \in x'$, so $\emptyset \in x\cap x'$.
- If $y \in x\cap x'$, then $y\in x$ and $y\in x'$. Since $x$ is inductive, $y\c
up\{y\}\in x$; since $x'$ is inductive, $y\cup\{y\}\in x'$. Hence $y\cup\{y\}\in
 x\cap x'$.

So $x\cap x'$ is inductive. By symmetry it is also an inductive subset of $x'$.

---

### Step 4. $\mathbb{N}_x \subseteq x'$ and $\mathbb{N}_{x'} \subseteq x$.

Since $x\cap x'$ is an inductive subset of $x$ (Step 3), it is one of the sets b
eing intersected in the definition of $\mathbb{N}_x$. Therefore

$$\mathbb{N}_x \subseteq x\cap x' \subseteq x'.$$

Combined with Step 2 ($\mathbb{N}_x$ is inductive), this shows $\mathbb{N}_x$ is
 an **inductive subset of $x'$**. By the symmetric argument, $\mathbb{N}_{x'}$ i
s an inductive subset of $x$.

---

### Step 5. $\mathbb{N}_{x'} \subseteq \mathbb{N}_x$ and $\mathbb{N}_x \subseteq
 \mathbb{N}_{x'}$.

By Step 4, $\mathbb{N}_x$ is an inductive subset of $x'$. Since $\mathbb{N}_{x'}
$ is the intersection of **all** inductive subsets of $x'$, it is contained in e
very one of them, in particular in $\mathbb{N}_x$:

$$\mathbb{N}_{x'} \subseteq \mathbb{N}_x.$$

By the symmetric argument (using that $\mathbb{N}_{x'}$ is an inductive subset o
f $x$):

$$\mathbb{N}_x \subseteq \mathbb{N}_{x'}.$$

---

### Conclusion

From the two inclusions of Step 5,

$$\mathbb{N}_x = \mathbb{N}_{x'}.$$

**Yes**, defining $\mathbb{N}$ with respect to any other set $x'$ satisfying the
 axiom-of-infinity property yields exactly the same set. The essential reason is
 that the intersection of all inductive subsets of an inductive set $x$ is force
d to lie inside $x\cap x'$ (since $x\cap x'$ is itself inductive), and hence is
an inductive subset of $x'$; being the *smallest* inductive subset of each, the
two constructions coincide. The definition is therefore **independent of the par
ticular choice of $x$**, and $\mathbb{N}$ is canonically determined.
