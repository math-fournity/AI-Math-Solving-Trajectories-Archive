Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether there exists an infinite-di
mensional Banach space $X$ and a function $f:X\to X$ that is weakly continuous a
t every point but has uncountably many points of strong (norm) discontinuity.

**Key observation.** For a *scalar-valued* function $g:X\to\mathbb{R}$, weak con
tinuity at a point implies norm continuity there (since every weakly open set is
 norm-open, a weak neighborhood contains a norm ball). So the question is only n
ontrivial for *vector-valued* functions $f:X\to X$, where weak-to-weak continuit
y does **not** imply norm-to-norm continuity, because norm-open sets in the codo
main need not be weakly open.

I will show the answer is **yes** by an explicit construction on $X=\ell^2$.

---

## Construction

Let $X = \ell^2$ with standard orthonormal basis $\{e_n\}_{n\geq 1}$.

### Step 1: Auxiliary function $G:\mathbb{R}\to\ell^2$

Define:
- $G(t) = 0$ for $t \le 0$.
- $G(t) = e_1$ for $t \ge 1$.
- For $t \in \bigl(\frac{1}{n+1},\, \frac{1}{n}\bigr]$ with $n \ge 1$:
$$G(t) = (1 - s_n(t))\,e_n + s_n(t)\,e_{n+1}, \qquad s_n(t) = (n+1)(1 - nt).$$

Note $s_n(1/n)=0$ and $s_n(1/(n+1))=1$, so $G(1/n)=e_n$ and $G(1/(n+1))=e_{n+1}$
, ensuring the pieces agree at every boundary $t=1/n$.

### Step 2: $G$ is weakly continuous everywhere

$G:\mathbb{R}\to(\ell^2,\text{weak})$ is continuous at every $t_0$:

- **$t_0\le 0$ or $t_0\ge 1$:** $G$ is locally constant, hence continuous.

- **$t_0\in\bigl(\frac{1}{n+1},\frac{1}{n}\bigr)$ (interior):** $G$ is a linear
interpolation between $e_n$ and $e_{n+1}$, hence norm-continuous (therefore weak
ly continuous).

- **$t_0 = 1/n$ (boundary):** From the left, $s_n(t)\to 0$, so $G(t)\to e_n$. Fr
om the right, $G(t)=(1-s_{n-1}(t))e_{n-1}+s_{n-1}(t)e_n$ with $s_{n-1}\to 1$, so
 $G(t)\to e_n$. Both one-sided limits equal $G(1/n)=e_n$. ✓

- **$t_0=0$:** For any $a=(a_k)\in\ell^2$, set $n=\lfloor 1/t\rfloor\to\infty$ a
s $t\to 0^+$. Then
$$\langle G(t),\, a\rangle = (1-s_n)\,a_n + s_n\,a_{n+1},$$
and $|\langle G(t),a\rangle|\le |a_n|+|a_{n+1}|\to 0$ since $a\in\ell^2\Rightarr
ow a_k\to 0$. From the left, $G(t)=0$. So $\langle G(t),a\rangle\to 0 = \langle
G(0),a\rangle$. ✓

### Step 3: $G$ is norm-discontinuous at $0$ but norm-continuous on $(0,\infty)$

**At $t_0=0$:** For every $t>0$,
$$\|G(t)\|^2 = (1-s_n)^2 + s_n^2 \ge \frac{(1-s_n+s_n)^2}{2} = \frac{1}{2},$$
so $\|G(t)-G(0)\|\ge 1/\sqrt{2}$ for all $t>0$. Not norm-continuous at $0$. ✓

**At $t_0=1/n$:** $\|G(t)-e_n\|^2 = 2s_n^2\to 0$ from the left; $\|G(t)-e_n\|^2=
2(1-s_{n-1})^2\to 0$ from the right. Norm-continuous. ✓

**At interior points:** $G$ is affine, hence norm-continuous. ✓

So $G$ is norm-discontinuous **only** at $t=0$.

### Step 4: Define $f:\ell^2\to\ell^2$ by $f(x)=G(x_1)$

where $x_1=\langle x,e_1\rangle$ is the first coordinate.

**$f$ is weakly continuous at every $x_0\in\ell^2$:** Let $V$ be a weakly open n
eighborhood of $f(x_0)=G((x_0)_1)$. Since $G:\mathbb{R}\to(\ell^2,\text{weak})$
is continuous, there exists $\delta>0$ with $|t-(x_0)_1|<\delta\Rightarrow G(t)\
in V$. The set
$$U=\{x\in\ell^2 : |x_1-(x_0)_1|<\delta\}$$
is weakly open (preimage of an open interval under the bounded linear functional
 $x\mapsto x_1$) and contains $x_0$. For $x\in U$, $f(x)=G(x_1)\in V$. ✓

**$f$ is norm-discontinuous at every $x_0$ with $(x_0)_1=0$:** We have $f(x_0)=G
(0)=0$. For any $\delta>0$, set $x'=x_0+\frac{\delta}{2}e_1$. Then $\|x'-x_0\|=\
delta/2<\delta$, but
$$\|f(x')-f(x_0)\|=\|G(\delta/2)\|\ge\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.$$
So $f$ is not norm-continuous at $x_0$. ✓

**Uncountably many discontinuities:** The set
$$\{x\in\ell^2 : x_1=0\}$$
is a closed hyperplane — an infinite-dimensional subspace of $\ell^2$ — and is t
herefore uncountable. ✓

---

## Conclusion

The infinite-dimensional Banach space $X=\ell^2$ admits the function $f(x)=G(x_1
)$ that is weakly continuous at every point of $X$ yet has uncountably many poin
ts of strong (norm) discontinuity (namely, the entire hyperplane $\{x_1=0\}$). T
he essential mechanism is that $G(t)\to 0$ weakly but not in norm as $t\to 0$, b
ecause the "mass" of $G(t)$ escapes to basis vectors $e_n$ with $n\to\infty$ — a
 phenomenon possible only in infinite dimensions.

Therefore, **such a Banach space exists**.
