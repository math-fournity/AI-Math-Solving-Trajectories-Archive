## Answer: No

The uniform limit of absolutely continuous functions need not be absolutely cont
inuous.

### Counterexample (Cantor function as uniform limit of absolutely continuous fu
nctions)

**Construction of the approximants.** Recall the standard Cantor set $C \subset
[0,1]$, obtained by repeatedly removing the open middle third. At stage $n$, the
 construction leaves $2^n$ closed intervals, each of length $3^{-n}$, and the Ca
ntor function $c$ is constant on each of the removed (open) middle-third interva
ls.

Define $f_n : [0,1] \to [0,1]$ to be the **piecewise-linear, continuous** functi
on that agrees with the Cantor function $c$ at every endpoint of the intervals r
emaining at stage $n$ (there are $2^n + 1$ such points) and is linear on each su
binterval between consecutive such points. Concretely, $f_n$ is the unique conti
nuous function that is affine on each of the $2^n$ surviving intervals (and affi
ne across the gaps), interpolating the values $c(k \cdot 3^{-n})$ at the relevan
t endpoints.

**Each $f_n$ is absolutely continuous.** Since $f_n$ is continuous and piecewise
 affine on $[0,1]$ with only finitely many pieces, it is Lipschitz (its derivati
ve is bounded — in fact $|f_n'| \le (\tfrac{3}{2})^n$ a.e., but boundedness is a
ll we need). Every Lipschitz function on a compact interval is absolutely contin
uous. Hence each $f_n$ is absolutely continuous.

**Uniform convergence $f_n \to c$.** On every surviving interval of stage $n$, t
he Cantor function $c$ varies by at most $2^{-n}$ (its total range is split into
 $2^n$ pieces of size $2^{-n}$), and $f_n$ is the linear interpolation between t
he endpoint values of $c$ on that interval. Since $c$ is monotone non-decreasing
, $f_n$ and $c$ agree at the endpoints and $c$ stays within the endpoint range,
so
$$
\|f_n - c\|_\infty \le 2^{-n} \to 0.
$$
Thus $f_n \to c$ uniformly.

**The limit $c$ is not absolutely continuous.** The Cantor set $C$ has Lebesgue
measure $m(C) = 0$ (it is the intersection of sets of measure $(2/3)^n \to 0$).
The Cantor function maps $C$ **onto** $[0,1]$: every $y \in [0,1]$ has a ternary
 expansion $y = \sum a_k 2^{-k}$ with $a_k \in \{0,1\}$, and the point $x = \sum
 (2a_k) 3^{-k} \in C$ satisfies $c(x) = y$. Hence
$$
m(c(C)) = m([0,1]) = 1 \neq 0 = m(C).
$$
A fundamental property of absolutely continuous functions is the **Lusin (N) con
dition**: an absolutely continuous function maps sets of measure zero to sets of
 measure zero. (Indeed, given $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $\delta > 0$ from the de
finition of absolute continuity; cover a null set $E$ by disjoint intervals of t
otal length $< \delta$, then the image has measure $< \varepsilon$.) Since $c$ s
ends the null set $C$ to a set of measure $1$, it violates the Lusin (N) conditi
on and therefore **cannot be absolutely continuous**.

### Conclusion

We have exhibited a sequence $\{f_n\}$ of absolutely continuous functions on $[0
,1]$ converging uniformly to the Cantor function $c$, which is not absolutely co
ntinuous. Therefore the answer is **No**: uniform convergence does not preserve
absolute continuity.
