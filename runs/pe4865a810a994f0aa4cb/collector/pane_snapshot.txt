i = 0$, and determine the maximum value.

## Proof

### General characterization (all $m$)

$f_m$ is continuous on the compact sphere $S^{k-1}$, so a maximum exists. Comput
ing the gradient on $\mathbb{R}^k$:

$$\nabla f_m(y) = m\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i \cdot y)^{m-1} x_i.$$

By Lagrange multipliers on $\|y\|^2=1$, a maximizer $y_m$ satisfies $\nabla f_m(
y_m) = 2\lambda\, y_m$, i.e.,

$$\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i \cdot y_m)^{m-1} x_i = \mu\, y_m, \qquad \mu = \frac{2\lambd
a}{m}.$$

Taking the inner product with $y_m$ and using $\|y_m\|=1$:

$$\mu = \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i \cdot y_m)^{m-1}(x_i \cdot y_m) = \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i \c
dot y_m)^m = f_m(y_m) = M_m.$$

So the **fixed-point (Z-eigenvalue) equation** is:

$$\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i \cdot y_m)^{m-1} x_i = M_m\, y_m, \qquad M_m = \sum_{
i=1}^n (x_i \cdot y_m)^m, \qquad \|y_m\|=1.}$$

Equivalently, $y_m$ is the **Z-eigenvector with the largest Z-eigenvalue** of th
e order-$m$ symmetric tensor $T_m := \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^{\otimes m}$, and $M_m$ is
 that largest Z-eigenvalue.

---

### Case $m = 1$: the zero-sum constraint makes it trivial

$$f_1(y) = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i \cdot y = \Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\Bigr)\cdot y = 0.$$

So $M_1 = 0$ and **every** unit vector is a maximizer. This is the key role of t
he constraint $\sum x_i = 0$.

---

### Case $m = 2$: eigenvalue of a matrix

$$f_2(y) = \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i \cdot y)^2 = y^\top\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i x_i^\to
p\right)\!y = y^\top A\, y,$$

where $A = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i x_i^\top$ is a $k\times k$ positive semidefinite mat
rix. The Rayleigh quotient gives:

$$\boxed{M_2 = \lambda_{\max}(A) = \lambda_{\max}\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i x_i^\t
op\right), \qquad y_2 = \text{top eigenvector of } A.}$$

Note $\operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum \|x_i\|^2$, so $M_2 \le \sum \|x_i\|^2$. The f
ixed-point equation $Ay = (y^\top A y)\,y$ is exactly the eigenvector equation $
Ay = \lambda y$ with $\lambda = y^\top A y$, confirming consistency.

---

### Case $k = 1$ (scalar case)

Here $y = \pm 1$ and $f_m(y) = y^m \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^m$.

- **Even $m$:** $y^m = 1$ regardless, so $M_m = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^m$ (both $y=\pm
1$ achieve it).
- **Odd $m$:** $f_m(\pm 1) = \pm\sum x_i^m$, so $M_m = \left|\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^m\
right|$, achieved by $y_m = \operatorname{sgn}\!\left(\sum x_i^m\right)$.

---

### Case $n = 2$

The constraint gives $x_2 = -x_1 =: -a$. Then:

$$f_m(y) = (a\cdot y)^m + (-a\cdot y)^m = (a\cdot y)^m\bigl(1 + (-1)^m\bigr).$$

- **Even $m$:** $f_m(y) = 2(a\cdot y)^m$, maximized at $y_m = \pm\, a/\|a\|$, gi
ving $M_m = 2\|a\|^m = 2\|x_1\|^m$.
- **Odd $m$:** $f_m \equiv 0$, so $M_m = 0$.

---

### All $x_i$ collinear (even $m$)

Write $x_i = c_i\, u$ with $\|u\|=1$ and $\sum c_i = 0$. Then $f_m(y) = (u\cdot
y)^m \sum c_i^m$. For even $m$, the maximum is at $y_m = \pm u$:

$$M_m = \sum_{i=1}^n |c_i|^m = \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i\|^m.$$

---

### Upper bound (even $m$)

For even $m$, $|x_i \cdot y| \le \|x_i\|\,\|y\| = \|x_i\|$, so:

$$M_m = \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i \cdot y_m)^m \le \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i\|^m,$$

with equality iff all $x_i$ are collinear (and $y_m$ aligns with them). For odd
$m$, since $f_m(-y) = -f_m(y)$ we have $M_m = \max_{\|y\|=1}|f_m(y)| \le \sum \|
x_i\|^m$ as well.

---

### Summary

| Setting | Maximum $M_m$ | Maximizer $y_m$ |
|---|---|---|
| $m=1$ (any $n,k$) | $0$ | any unit vector |
| $m=2$ (any $n,k$) | $\lambda_{\max}\!\bigl(\sum x_i x_i^\top\bigr)$ | top eige
nvector of $\sum x_i x_i^\top$ |
| General $m$ | largest Z-eigenvalue of tensor $T_m = \sum x_i^{\otimes m}$ | co
rresponding Z-eigenvector, satisfying $\sum (x_i\cdot y_m)^{m-1} x_i = M_m\, y_m
$ |
| $k=1$, even $m$ | $\sum x_i^m$ | $y = \pm 1$ |
| $k=1$, odd $m$ | $\left\lvert\sum x_i^m\right\rvert$ | $y = \operatorname{sgn}
(\sum x_i^m)$ |

| $n=2$, odd $m$ | $0$ | any unit vector |
| Collinear, even $m$ | $\sum \|x_i\|^m$ | along common direction |

The zero-sum constraint $\sum x_i = 0$ forces $M_1 = 0$ and, for $n=2$, forces $
M_m = 0$ for all odd $m$. For $m \ge 3$ and general configurations, the problem
is the **symmetric tensor eigenvalue problem** (known to be computationally hard
 for $m \ge 3$), and the answer is the largest Z-eigenvalue of $T_m = \sum x_i^{
\otimes m}$ with the maximizer given by the associated Z-eigenvector.
