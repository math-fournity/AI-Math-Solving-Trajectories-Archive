## Justification of the Derivation of Poisson's Integral Formula

**Setup.** We seek $u$ harmonic in the disk $D = \{(r,\theta): 0 \le r < a\}$ wi
th boundary value $u(a,\theta) = f(\theta)$, where $f$ is continuous and $2\pi$-
periodic. Laplace's equation in polar coordinates is
$$u_{rr} + \tfrac{1}{r}u_r + \tfrac{1}{r^2}u_{\theta\theta} = 0. \tag{1}$$

The goal is to justify each step leading to
$$u(r,\theta) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{a^2-r^2}{a^2-2ar\cos(\theta-\ph
i)+r^2}\,f(\phi)\,d\phi. \tag{P}$$

---

### Step 1 — Separation of variables

Assume $u(r,\theta)=R(r)\Theta(\theta)$. Substituting into (1) and dividing by $
R\Theta/r^2$ gives
$$\frac{r^2R''+rR'}{R} + \frac{\Theta''}{\Theta} = 0.$$
Since the first term depends only on $r$ and the second only on $\theta$, each m
ust be constant. Setting $\Theta''+\lambda\Theta=0$ and $r^2R''+rR'-\lambda R=0$
.

**Justification.** Equation (1) is separable in polar coordinates because the La
placian splits into a purely radial part and a purely angular part. Linearity of
 (1) later permits superposition of the separated solutions.

---

### Step 2 — Angular eigenvalues from periodicity

The angular equation $\Theta''+\lambda\Theta=0$ with the physical requirement $\
Theta(\theta+2\pi)=\Theta(\theta)$ (single-valuedness of $u$) forces $\lambda=n^
2$, $n=0,1,2,\dots$, giving
$$\Theta_n(\theta)=A_n\cos n\theta + B_n\sin n\theta.$$

**Justification.** Periodic boundary conditions on $[0,2\pi]$ form a regular Stu
rm–Liouville problem whose eigenvalues are exactly $n^2$; the eigenfunctions $\{
1,\cos n\theta,\sin n\theta\}$ are complete and orthogonal on $[0,2\pi]$.

---

### Step 3 — Radial equation and regularity at the origin

With $\lambda=n^2$ the radial equation $r^2R''+rR'-n^2R=0$ is Euler–Cauchy. Tryi
ng $R=r^m$ yields $m^2-n^2=0$, so $m=\pm n$ (for $n=0$: $R=C+D\ln r$). Regularit
y (boundedness) of $u$ at $r=0$ discards $\ln r$ and $r^{-n}$, leaving $R_n(r)=r
^n$.

**Justification.** The solution must be finite at the center of the disk; $\ln r
\to-\infty$ and $r^{-n}\to\infty$ as $r\to 0^+$, so their coefficients vanish. T
his is the standard regularity requirement for an interior problem.

---

### Step 4 — General solution and Fourier expansion of the boundary data

Superposition gives
$$u(r,\theta)=\frac{\alpha_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^n\b
igl(\alpha_n\cos n\theta+\beta_n\sin n\theta\bigr), \tag{2}$$
where the factor $(r/a)^n$ is inserted for convenience. Setting $r=a$ and using
$u(a,\theta)=f(\theta)$:
$$f(\theta)=\frac{\alpha_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(\alpha_n\cos n\theta+\be
ta_n\sin n\theta\bigr),$$
the Fourier series of $f$, with
$$\alpha_n=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(\phi)\cos n\phi\,d\phi,\qquad \beta_n=\fr
ac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(\phi)\sin n\phi\,d\phi.$$

**Justification.** Completeness/orthogonality of the trigonometric system (Step
2) guarantees the Fourier representation of $f$; for piecewise-$C^1$ periodic $f
$ the series converges to $f$ at points of continuity. The superposition in (2)
is valid because (1) is linear and each term is harmonic (each $r^n\cos n\theta$
, $r^n\sin n\theta$ satisfies (1), verified directly). For $r<a$ the series conv
erges absolutely and uniformly by comparison with $\sum(r/a)^n$.

---

### Step 5 — Substitute coefficients and interchange sum and integral

Inserting $\alpha_n,\beta_n$ into (2) and using $\cos n\theta\cos n\phi+\sin n\t
heta\sin n\phi=\cos n(\theta-\phi)$:
$$u(r,\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(\phi)\left[1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lef
t(\frac{r}{a}\right)^n\cos n(\theta-\phi)\right]d\phi. \tag{3}$$

**Justification.** The interchange of $\sum$ and $\int$ is permitted because the
 series converges *uniformly* in $\phi$ for fixed $r<a$: by the Weierstrass $M$-
test with $M_n=(r/a)^n$ and $\sum_{n\ge1}(r/a)^n<\infty$ (since $r/a<1$). Unifor
m convergence allows term-by-term integration over the finite interval $[0,2\pi]
$.

---

### Step 6 — Sum the series to obtain the Poisson kernel

Let $\rho=r/a\in(0,1)$ and $\alpha=\theta-\phi$. Using $\cos n\alpha=\operatorna
me{Re}(e^{in\alpha})$ and the geometric series (valid since $|\rho e^{i\alpha}|=
\rho<1$):
$$1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\rho^n\cos n\alpha = 1+2\operatorname{Re}\sum_{n=1}^{\in
fty}(\rho e^{i\alpha})^n = 1+2\operatorname{Re}\frac{\rho e^{i\alpha}}{1-\rho e^
{i\alpha}}.$$
Rationalizing,
$$\frac{\rho e^{i\alpha}}{1-\rho e^{i\alpha}}=\frac{\rho e^{i\alpha}-\rho^2}{1-2
\rho\cos\alpha+\rho^2},$$
whose real part is $\frac{\rho\cos\alpha-\rho^2}{1-2\rho\cos\alpha+\rho^2}$. Hen
ce
$$1+2\operatorname{Re}(\cdot)=\frac{1-2\rho\cos\alpha+\rho^2+2\rho\cos\alpha-2\r
ho^2}{1-2\rho\cos\alpha+\rho^2}=\frac{1-\rho^2}{1-2\rho\cos\alpha+\rho^2}.$$

**Justification.** The geometric series $\sum z^n = z/(1-z)$ is legitimate for $
|z|<1$; here $z=\rho e^{i\alpha}$ with $\rho<1$. The algebraic rationalization i
s exact. Substituting $\rho=r/a$ and multiplying numerator and denominator by $a
^2$:
$$\frac{1-(r/a)^2}{1-2(r/a)\cos(\theta-\phi)+(r/a)^2}=\frac{a^2-r^2}{a^2-2ar\cos
(\theta-\phi)+r^2}=:P(r,\theta-\phi).$$

Inserting this into (3) yields **Poisson's formula (P)**.

---

### Step 7 — Verify the formula actually solves the Dirichlet problem

**(a) Harmonicity of $u$ in $r<a$.** The kernel $P$ is harmonic in $(r,\theta)$
for $r<a$: indeed
$$P(r,\alpha)=\operatorname{Re}\frac{a+re^{i\alpha}}{a-re^{i\alpha}}$$
(up to the constant factor $1/2\pi$ absorbed into the integral), the real part o
f a holomorphic function of $z=re^{i\alpha}$, hence harmonic. Since $P$ and its
derivatives are smooth and bounded for $r\le r_0<a$, differentiation under the i
ntegral sign is justified, giving $\nabla^2 u = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int P\,(\nabla^2
f)\,d\phi$ — but more directly, $\nabla^2_{(r,\theta)}P=0$ implies $\nabla^2 u =
 \frac{1}{2\pi}\int (\nabla^2 P)\,f(\phi)\,d\phi=0$.

**Justification.** Differentiation under the integral is valid because $P$ is $C
^\infty$ and bounded on compact subsets of $r<a$ (dominated convergence). A conv
ergent integral of harmonic functions against a finite measure is harmonic.

**(b) Normalization.** Taking $f\equiv 1$, the unique bounded harmonic function
with boundary value $1$ is $u\equiv 1$, so by (P)
$$\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}P(r,\theta-\phi)\,d\phi = 1 \quad\text{for all } r<
a. \tag{4}$$

**Justification.** Uniqueness of the interior Dirichlet problem (maximum princip
le) certifies $u\equiv 1$ is the solution, hence (4) holds.

**(c) Boundary behavior $u\to f$ as $r\to a^-$.** For $\phi\ne\theta$, $P(r,\the
ta-\phi)\to 0$ as $r\to a^-$ (numerator $\to 0$, denominator $\to 2a^2(1-\cos(\t
heta-\phi))>0$). Near $\phi=\theta$, $P$ concentrates: using (4),
$$u(r,\theta)-f(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}P(r,\theta-\phi)\bigl[f(\phi)
-f(\theta)\bigr]d\phi.$$
Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $\delta>0$ with $|f(\phi)-f(\theta)|<\varepsilon$
for $|\phi-\theta|<\delta$ (continuity of $f$). Split the integral: on $|\phi-\t
heta|<\delta$ it is $<\varepsilon$ by (4) and $P>0$; on $|\phi-\theta|\ge\delta$
, $P\to 0$ uniformly as $r\to a^-$, so this part $\to 0$. Hence $\lim_{r\to a^-}
u(r,\theta)=f(\theta)$.

**Justification.** This is the standard approximate-identity argument: $P\ge 0$,
 $\int P\,d\phi=2\pi$, and $P$ concentrates at $\phi=\theta$ as $r\to a^-$. Posi
tivity of $P$ (numerator $a^2-r^2>0$, denominator $=(a-r)^2+2ar(1-\cos(\theta-\p
hi))\ge(a-r)^2>0$) is essential.
