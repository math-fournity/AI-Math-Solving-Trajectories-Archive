## Proof

**Yes**, such a Riemannian manifold exists. A simple example is the **flat cylin
der** $M = S^1 \times \mathbb{R}$ with the product Riemannian metric (which is f
lat and complete).

### Setup

Identify $S^1 = \mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}$ with angular coordinate $\theta$, and
 let $z$ be the coordinate on $\mathbb{R}$. Equip $M$ with the flat metric $ds^2
 = d\theta^2 + dz^2$. Take $p = (0, 0)$.

### Geodesics and distance

The geodesics from $p$ are the lifts of straight lines in the universal cover $\
widetilde{M} = \mathbb{R}^2$ (with coordinates $(\theta, z)$) starting from $(0,
0)$, projected back down to the cylinder. Concretely, for any integer $n \in \ma
thbb{Z}$, the curve
$$\gamma_n(t) = (n t,\; t) \quad \text{(or more generally any straight line)}$$
projects to a geodesic. More precisely, a geodesic from $p$ to a point $q = (\th
eta_0, z_0)$ lifts to a straight line from $(0,0)$ to $(\theta_0 + 2\pi n,\, z_0
)$ for some $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, and its length is
$$L_n = \sqrt{(\theta_0 + 2\pi n)^2 + z_0^2}.$$

The Riemannian distance from $p$ to $q = (\theta_0, z_0)$ is therefore
$$d(p, q) = \min_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} \sqrt{(\theta_0 + 2\pi n)^2 + z_0^2}.$$

### The antipodal line: infinitely many points with two minimizing geodesics

Consider the set of points $q_z = (\pi, z)$ for $z \in \mathbb{R}$ — the "antipo
dal line" at $\theta = \pi$.

For $q_z = (\pi, z)$, the two candidate lengths are:
$$L_0 = \sqrt{\pi^2 + z^2}, \qquad L_{-1} = \sqrt{(\pi - 2\pi)^2 + z^2} = \sqrt{
(-\pi)^2 + z^2} = \sqrt{\pi^2 + z^2}.$$

So $L_0 = L_{-1}$. For any other $n \neq 0, -1$, we have $|\pi + 2\pi n| > \pi$
(since $|n| \geq 1$ gives $|\pi + 2\pi n| \geq \pi$ with equality only at $n = 0
$ and $n = -1$), so $L_n > L_0$.

Therefore, for **every** $z \in \mathbb{R}$, the point $q_z = (\pi, z)$ has **ex
actly two** distinct minimizing geodesics from $p$:
- one lifting to the segment from $(0,0)$ to $(\pi, z)$ (wrapping zero times), a
nd
- one lifting to the segment from $(0,0)$ to $(-\pi, z)$ (wrapping once in the n
egative direction).

These two geodesics are distinct (they wrap around the cylinder in opposite dire
ctions), and both achieve the minimal length $\sqrt{\pi^2 + z^2}$.

### Conclusion

The set $\{q_z = (\pi, z) : z \in \mathbb{R}\}$ is an infinite (indeed uncountab
le) set of points, each admitting more than one minimizing geodesic from $p$. He
nce the flat cylinder $S^1 \times \mathbb{R}$ is a Riemannian manifold with a po
int $p$ such that infinitely many points $q$ have more than one minimizing geode
sic from $p$ to $q$.
