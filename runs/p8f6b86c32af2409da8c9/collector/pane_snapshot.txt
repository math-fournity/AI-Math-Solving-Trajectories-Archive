## Proof

**Claim:** $(I^2, J) = R$.

Since $I$ and $J$ are distinct maximal ideals, $I + J$ is an ideal strictly cont
aining $I$ (as $J \not\subseteq I$, otherwise $J \subseteq I$ would force $J = I
$ by maximality of $J$, contradicting distinctness). By maximality of $I$, we co
nclude
$$I + J = R.$$

Hence there exist $x \in I$ and $y \in J$ such that
$$x + y = 1.$$

Now compute $1 = 1^2 = (x+y)^2$:
$$1 = x^2 + 2xy + y^2.$$

We examine each summand:
- $x^2 \in I^2$ (since $x \in I$),
- $2xy \in J$ (since $y \in J$ and $J$ is an ideal),
- $y^2 \in J$ (since $y \in J$).

Therefore $2xy + y^2 \in J$, and
$$1 = \underbrace{x^2}_{\in I^2} + \underbrace{(2xy + y^2)}_{\in J} \in I^2 + J
= (I^2, J).$$

Since the ideal $(I^2, J)$ contains $1$, it must be the entire ring:
$$(I^2, J) = R. \qquad \blacksquare$$

**Remark.** The same argument with $(x+y)^n$ in place of $(x+y)^2$ shows more ge
nerally that comaximality $I + J = R$ implies $I^n + J = R$ for every $n \geq 1$
: all terms in the binomial expansion except $x^n$ contain a factor of $y$ and t
hus lie in $J$, while $x^n \in I^n$.
