## Proof

We determine whether $g \in W^{1,1}(0,1)$, i.e. whether $g \in L^1(0,1)$ and its
 weak derivative belongs to $L^1(0,1)$.

**Step 1: $g \in L^1(0,1)$.**

For $0 < x \le 1$, $|g(x)| = |x^2 \cos(1/x)| \le x^2$, and $g(0)=0$. Since $\lim
_{x\to 0^+} x^2 \cos(1/x) = 0 = g(0)$, $g$ is continuous on $[0,1]$, hence bound
ed and integrable. So $g \in L^1(0,1)$.

**Step 2: Classical derivative on $(0,1]$.**

For $x > 0$,
$$g'(x) = 2x\cos(1/x) + x^2\cdot(-\sin(1/x))\cdot(-1/x^2) = 2x\cos(1/x) + \sin(1
/x).$$

**Step 3: $g' \in L^1(0,1)$.**

- $\int_0^1 |2x\cos(1/x)|\,dx \le \int_0^1 2x\,dx = 1 < \infty$.
- For $\sin(1/x)$: substitute $u = 1/x$, $dx = -du/u^2$:
$$\int_0^1 |\sin(1/x)|\,dx = \int_1^\infty \frac{|\sin u|}{u^2}\,du \le \int_1^\
infty \frac{1}{u^2}\,du = 1 < \infty.$$

Hence $g' \in L^1(0,1)$.

**Step 4: $g'$ is the weak derivative of $g$.**

Let $\varphi \in C_c^\infty(0,1)$. Since $\varphi$ has compact support strictly
inside $(0,1)$, there exists $\varepsilon_0 > 0$ such that $\varphi \equiv 0$ on
 $(0,\varepsilon_0]\cup[1-\varepsilon_0,1)$. For $0 < \varepsilon < \varepsilon_
0$, integrate by parts on $[\varepsilon, 1]$:
$$\int_\varepsilon^1 g\,\varphi'\,dx = \big[g\varphi\big]_\varepsilon^1 - \int_\
varepsilon^1 g'\,\varphi\,dx.$$

Because $\varphi(1)=0$ and $\varphi(\varepsilon)=0$ (for $\varepsilon < \varepsi
lon_0$), the boundary term vanishes:
$$\int_\varepsilon^1 g\,\varphi'\,dx = -\int_\varepsilon^1 g'\,\varphi\,dx.$$

Letting $\varepsilon \to 0^+$, and using $g, g', \varphi \in L^1(0,1)$ (so the i
ntegrals converge absolutely over $(0,1)$):
$$\int_0^1 g\,\varphi'\,dx = -\int_0^1 g'\,\varphi\,dx, \qquad \forall\,\varphi
\in C_c^\infty(0,1).$$

This is precisely the definition of $g'$ being the weak derivative of $g$.

**Conclusion.**

Both $g \in L^1(0,1)$ and its weak derivative $g' = 2x\cos(1/x) + \sin(1/x) \in
L^1(0,1)$. Therefore

$$\boxed{g \in W^{1,1}(0,1).}$$
