**Key fact (under AC).** For infinite cardinals $\kappa, \lambda$ with $\lambda
\neq 0$, cardinal multiplication satisfies
$$\kappa \cdot \lambda = \max(\kappa, \lambda).$$
This is a standard consequence of the Axiom of Choice (equivalently, the Well-Or
dering Theorem): every infinite cardinal $\kappa$ satisfies $\kappa \cdot \kappa
 = \kappa$, and monotonicity then gives $\kappa \cdot \lambda = \max(\kappa,\lam
bda)$ for nonzero $\lambda$.

**Counterexample.** Let
- $A = \mathbb{R}$, so $|A| = \mathfrak{c} = 2^{\aleph_0}$,
- $B = \mathbb{N}$, so $|B| = \aleph_0$,
- $C = \mathbb{R}$, so $|C| = \mathfrak{c}$.

All three sets are infinite. Since $\aleph_0 \leq \mathfrak{c}$ and $\mathfrak{c

$$|A \times B| = \mathfrak{c} \cdot \aleph_0 = \max(\mathfrak{c}, \aleph_0) = \m

$$|A \times C| = \mathfrak{c} \cdot \mathfrak{c} = \max(\mathfrak{c}, \mathfrak{

Therefore $|A \times B| = |A \times C| = \mathfrak{c}$, and by the Well-Ordering

However,
$$|B| = \aleph_0 \neq \mathfrak{c} = |C|,$$
since by Cantor's theorem $\aleph_0 < 2^{\aleph_0} = \mathfrak{c}$.
