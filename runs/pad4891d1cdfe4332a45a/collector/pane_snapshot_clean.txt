## Solution

**Notation.** Let $S(m)$ denote the sum of distinct prime divisors of $m$ (with
$S(1)=0$). We seek all integers $n$ with $S(2^n+1)=S(n)$.

**Preliminary observations.** For $n<0$, $2^n+1$ is not an integer, so prime div
isors are undefined. For $n=0$, $2^0+1=2$ so $S(2)=2$, but $S(0)$ is undefined (
every prime divides $0$). So we need $n\geq 1$.

**Small cases.**
- $n=1$: $2^1+1=3$, $S(3)=3\neq 0=S(1)$. ✗
- $n=2$: $2^2+1=5$, $S(5)=5\neq 2=S(2)$. ✗
- $n=3$: $2^3+1=9=3^2$, $S(9)=3=S(3)$. ✓

**Key tool — Zsygmondy's theorem.** For coprime $a>b>0$ and exponent $m\geq 3$,
$a^m-b^m$ has a **primitive prime divisor** (a prime $p\mid a^m-b^m$ with $p\nmi
d a^k-b^k$ for $1\leq k<m$), with the unique exception $(a,b,m)=(2,1,6)$.

**Applying Zsygmondy to $2^{2n}-1$ with $a=2,b=1$, exponent $m=2n$.** For $n\geq
 2$ with $2n\neq 6$ (i.e., $n\neq 3$), there exists a primitive prime divisor $p
$ of $2^{2n}-1$ with $\operatorname{ord}_p(2)=2n$.

Since $p\mid 2^{2n}-1=(2^n-1)(2^n+1)$ and $\operatorname{ord}_p(2)=2n>n$ implies
 $p\nmid 2^n-1$, we conclude $p\mid 2^n+1$.

Moreover, $\operatorname{ord}_p(2)=2n\mid p-1$, so $p\geq 2n+1$.

**Bounding the two sides.** For any $n\geq 1$: if $n=p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_k^{a_k}$,
 then $S(n)=p_1+\cdots+p_k\leq p_1\cdots p_k\leq n$ (the sum of distinct primes
is at most their product, which is at most $n$), with equality $S(n)=n$ only whe
n $n$ is prime.

**Conclusion for $n\geq 2$, $n\neq 3$:** The primitive prime divisor $p\mid 2^n+
1$ satisfies $p\geq 2n+1$, so:
$$S(2^n+1)\geq p\geq 2n+1>n\geq S(n).$$
Thus $S(2^n+1)>S(n)$, and no solution exists.

**The exceptional case $n=3$:** Here $2n=6$ is exactly the Zsygmondy exception.
We verify directly: $2^3+1=9=3^2$, so $S(9)=3=S(3)$. This is a solution.

**Summary.** For $n=1$: no solution. For $n\geq 2$, $n\neq 3$: Zsygmondy forces
$S(2^n+1)>S(n)$. For $n=3$: direct verification gives equality. Negative $n$ and
 $n=0$ are excluded since $2^n+1$ is not a positive integer (or $S(0)$ is undefi
ned).

The unique integer satisfying the condition is $\boxed{n=3}$.
