## Proof

**Setup and uniqueness of $P$.**

In the polar decomposition $T = UP$, the positive semi-definite factor is always
 uniquely determined:
$$P = |T| = (T^*T)^{1/2}.$$
This holds regardless of normality or invertibility. So all questions of uniquen
ess concern $U$.

**Where $U$ is determined vs. free.**

Since $T = UP$ and $P \geq 0$, we have $T^*T = P^*U^*UP = P^2$, confirming $P =
(T^*T)^{1/2}$. The operator $U$ is determined on $(\ker P)^\perp = \overline{\op
eratorname{ran} P}$ by the relation
$$U(Px) = Tx, \quad \text{i.e., } U = T\,P^+ \text{ on } (\ker P)^\perp,$$
where $P^+$ is the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse. On $\ker P$, the value of $U$ is
 unconstrained by the equation $T = UP$ (since $P = 0$ there).

Since $\ker P = \ker(T^*T)^{1/2} = \ker T$, the freedom in choosing $U$ lies pre
cisely on $\ker T$.

**$U$ can always be extended to a unitary for normal $T$.**

The partial isometry in the standard polar decomposition has initial space $(\ke
r T)^\perp$ and final space $\overline{\operatorname{ran} T}$. To extend it to a
 unitary on the whole Hilbert space $H$, we need a unitary map from $\ker T$ ont
o $(\overline{\operatorname{ran} T})^\perp$.

For **normal** $T$, we use the key identity $\|Tx\| = \|T^*x\|$ (which follows f
rom $T^*T = TT^*$), giving $\ker T = \ker T^*$. Since $(\overline{\operatorname{
ran} T})^\perp = \ker T^* = \ker T$, we have
$$\dim \ker T = \dim(\overline{\operatorname{ran} T})^\perp,$$
so a unitary extension always exists. (Note: commutativity $UP = PU$ is automati
c for normal $T$ and does not further constrain $U$, since $P = 0$ on $\ker T$.)

**Characterization of uniqueness.**

$U$ is unique if and only if there is no freedom in its choice, i.e., if and onl
y if $\ker T = \{0\}$, equivalently, $T$ is **injective**.

**Relation to invertibility.**

- **($T$ invertible $\Rightarrow$ $U$ unique):** If $T$ is invertible (bijective
 with bounded inverse), then $\ker T = \{0\}$, so $U$ is unique. ✓

- **($U$ unique $\Rightarrow$ $T$ invertible):** This requires $\ker T = \{0\} \
Rightarrow T$ invertible. For a normal operator, $\ker T = \{0\}$ implies $\over
line{\operatorname{ran} T} = H$ (dense range, since $(\operatorname{ran} T)^\per
p = \ker T^* = \ker T = \{0\}$), but **dense range $\neq$ surjectivity** in infi
nite dimensions.

**Counterexample (infinite-dimensional).**

Let $H = \ell^2(\mathbb{N})$ and define
$$T = \operatorname{diag}\!\left(1,\, \tfrac{1}{2},\, \tfrac{1}{3},\, \dots\righ
t), \quad Te_n = \tfrac{1}{n} e_n.$$
Then:
- $T$ is diagonal with real entries, hence **self-adjoint** and positive semi-de
finite, so $T$ is **normal**.
- $\ker T = \{0\}$ (all diagonal entries are nonzero), so $T$ is **injective**.
- $P = |T| = T$ (since $T \geq 0$), and $U = I$ is the **unique** unitary satisf
ying $T = UP$.
- $T$ is **not surjective**: the sequence $y = (1/n)_{n \geq 1} \in \ell^2$ is n
ot in $\operatorname{ran} T$, since $Tx = y$ would require $x_n = 1$ for all $n$
, but $(1, 1, 1, \dots) \notin \ell^2$.
- Therefore $T$ is **not invertible**.

This gives a normal operator with **unique** $U$ that is **not invertible**, dis
proving the "only if" direction.

**Finite-dimensional case.**

In a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, injectivity, surjectivity, and bijectivit
y are equivalent for linear operators. Hence $\ker T = \{0\} \iff T$ invertible,
 and the equivalence "$U$ unique $\iff$ $T$ invertible" **does hold** in finite
dimensions.

## Conclusion

The statement **"$U$ is unique if and only if $T$ is invertible"** is:

- **True** in **finite-dimensional** Hilbert spaces (where injective $\iff$ inve
rtible).
- **False** in **general** (infinite-dimensional) Hilbert spaces.

The correct general characterization is:

> **$U$ is unique if and only if $T$ is injective (i.e., $\ker T = \{0\}$).**

The "if" direction ($T$ invertible $\Rightarrow$ $U$ unique) always holds, but t
he "only if" direction ($U$ unique $\Rightarrow$ $T$ invertible) fails in infini
te dimensions, as demonstrated by $T = \operatorname{diag}(1/n)$ on $\ell^2$, wh
ich is injective (hence $U = I$ is unique) but not surjective (hence not inverti
ble).
