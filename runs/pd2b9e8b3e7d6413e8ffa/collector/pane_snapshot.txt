## Counterexample: The line with two origins

Let $X = \mathbb{R}\times\{0,1\}$, equipped with the disjoint-union topology (i.
e. two disjoint copies of the real line). Since each copy of $\mathbb{R}$ is Hau
sdorff and a disjoint union of Hausdorff spaces is Hausdorff, $X$ is Hausdorff.

Define an equivalence relation $\sim$ on $X$ by
$$(x,0) \sim (x,1) \quad \text{for all } x \neq 0,$$
and every point is equivalent only to itself otherwise. Thus the only nontrivial
 equivalence classes are the singletons $\{(x,0),(x,1)\}$ for $x\neq 0$; the two
 points $(0,0)$ and $(0,1)$ each form their own singleton class.

Let $Y = X/{\sim}$ be the quotient space, and let $q:X\to Y$ be the quotient map
. Denote the two distinct points corresponding to the origins by
$$p_0 = q(0,0), \qquad p_1 = q(0,1).$$

## Claim: $Y$ is not Hausdorff

We show that $p_0$ and $p_1$ cannot be separated by disjoint open neighborhoods.

Recall that a set $U\subseteq Y$ is open iff $q^{-1}(U)$ is open in $X$ and is *
*saturated** (a union of $\sim$-equivalence classes).

Let $U$ be any open neighborhood of $p_0$ in $Y$. Then $q^{-1}(U)$ is an open sa

$$(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times\{0\} \subseteq q^{-1}(U).$$
By saturation, for every $x$ with $0<|x|<\varepsilon$, the point $(x,0)\in q^{-1
}(U)$ forces $(x,1)\in q^{-1}(U)$ as well (since $(x,0)\sim(x,1)$). Hence
$$\big((- \varepsilon,\varepsilon)\setminus\{0\}\big)\times\{1\} \subseteq q^{-1
