## Proof that the partition numbers $p(n)$ are not C-finite

**Definition.** A sequence $\{a(n)\}_{n \geq 0}$ is **C-finite** if it satisfies
 a linear recurrence with constant coefficients, equivalently, if its ordinary g
enerating function $\sum_{n \geq 0} a(n)\,x^n$ is a rational function $P(x)/Q(x)
$ with $P, Q \in \mathbb{C}[x]$, $Q(0) \neq 0$.

The proof proceeds in three steps: (1) establish a growth-rate dichotomy for C-f
inite sequences, (2) show $p(n)$ is superpolynomial, (3) show $p(n)$ is subexpon
ential. The contradiction proves $p(n)$ is not C-finite.

### Step 1: Growth-rate dichotomy for C-finite sequences

If $a(n)$ is C-finite, it satisfies $a(n) = c_1\, a(n{-}1) + \cdots + c_d\, a(n{
-}d)$ for all large $n$. The general solution is

$$a(n) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} P_i(n)\,\lambda_i^n,$$

where $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$ are the distinct roots of the characteristic
 polynomial $z^d - c_1 z^{d-1} - \cdots - c_d$, and $P_i$ is a polynomial of deg

**Claim.** If $a(n)$ is C-finite and not polynomially bounded, then $\limsup_{n\
to\infty} |a(n)|^{1/n} = \rho > 1$.

*Proof of claim.* If $\rho \leq 1$, then $|a(n)| \leq \sum_i |P_i(n)| \leq C\, n
^M$ (where $M = \max_i \deg P_i$), so $a(n)$ is polynomially bounded—contradicti
ng the hypothesis. Hence $\rho > 1$.

For the $\limsup$: the upper bound $|a(n)| \leq C\, n^M \rho^n$ gives $\limsup |
a(n)|^{1/n} \leq \rho$. For the lower bound, the terms with $|\lambda_i| = \rho$

mbda_i/\rho)^n$ is a non-zero quasi-polynomial (non-zero because the $\lambda_i/
\rho$ are distinct roots of unity times phases, and the $P_i$ are not all zero).

 are $O((\rho - \varepsilon)^n)$ for some $\varepsilon > 0$, hence negligible. S
o $|a(n)| \geq \frac{c}{2}\, n^m \rho^n$ for infinitely many $n$, giving $\limsu

**Dichotomy.** A C-finite sequence that is unbounded is either:
- **polynomially bounded** ($\rho \leq 1$, growth $O(n^M)$), or
- **exponentially growing** ($\rho > 1$, $\limsup |a(n)|^{1/n} = \rho > 1$).

There is no middle ground: no C-finite sequence can be superpolynomial yet subex

### Step 4: Contradiction

We have established:

1. $p(n) \to \infty$ (trivially, $p(n) \geq 1$ for all $n$ and $p(n)$ is increas
ing).
2. $p(n)$ is **superpolynomial** (Step 2): not bounded by any polynomial.
3. $p(n)$ is **subexponential** (Step 3): $\lim p(n)^{1/n} = 1$.

Suppose for contradiction that $p(n)$ is C-finite. Since $p(n)$ is not polynomia
lly bounded, by the dichotomy (Step 1) we must have $\limsup\, p(n)^{1/n} = \rho
 > 1$. But Step 3 gives $\lim\, p(n)^{1/n} = 1$, so $\limsup\, p(n)^{1/n} = 1 <
\rho$. **Contradiction.**

Therefore $p(n)$ is **not C-finite**. $\blacksquare$
