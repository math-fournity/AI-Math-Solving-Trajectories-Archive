## Counterexample

The answer is **no**: $L_1$ need not equal $L_2$. I construct an explicit counte
rexample.

**The sequences.** Let $x_k = (-1)^k$ for all $k \ge 1$, which is bounded. Take
$n_t = t$.

**Matrix $A$ (Cesàro):** Set
$$a_{n,k} = \begin{cases} 1/n & 1 \le k \le n, \\ 0 & k > n. \end{cases}$$

**Matrix $B$ (odd-index weighting):** Set
$$b_{n,k} = \begin{cases} 2/n & 1 \le k \le n \text{ and } k \text{ odd}, \\ 0 &
 \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

**Verification of hypotheses.**

*Nonnegativity:* Clear for both $a_{n,k}$ and $b_{n,k}$.

*Row sums tend to 1:*
$$\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_{n,k} = n \cdot \frac{1}{n} = 1 \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 1
.$$
$$\sum_{k=1}^\infty b_{n,k} = \frac{2}{n}\left\lceil \frac{n}{2}\right\rceil = \
begin{cases} 1 & n \text{ even},\\ (n+1)/n & n \text{ odd},\end{cases} \xrightar
row{n\to\infty} 1.$$

*Entrywise convergence to 0:* For each fixed $k$, $a_{n,k} = 1/n \to 0$ and $b_{
n,k} \le 2/n \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$.

**Computation of $L_1$:**
$$\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_{n,k}\,x_k = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^k = \begin{case
s} 0 & n \text{ even},\\ -1/n & n \text{ odd}.\end{cases} \xrightarrow{n\to\inft

So $L_1 = 0$.

**Computation of $L_2$:**
$$\sum_{k=1}^\infty b_{n,k}\,x_k = \frac{2}{n}\sum_{\substack{1 \le k \le n \\ k

So $L_2 = -1$.

**Conclusion.** Both limits exist along $n_t = t$, yet $L_1 = 0 \neq -1 = L_2$.
The equality $L_1 = L_2$ does not hold in general. $\blacksquare$
