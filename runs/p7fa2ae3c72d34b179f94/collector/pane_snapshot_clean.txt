## Proof that $D^2/S^1 \cong S^2$

**Answer leak check:** The problem asks "Is it $S^2$?" as a hypothesis to verify
 — this is a question, not a leaked solution. No proof, solution sketch, or answ
er value is embedded in the problem statement. Proceeding.

**Claim.** The quotient space $D^2/S^1$, obtained by collapsing the boundary cir
cle $S^1 = \partial D^2$ to a single point, is homeomorphic to the 2-sphere $S^2
$.

**Step 1: Construct a continuous surjection $f: D^2 \to S^2$ that is constant on
 $S^1$.**

Write points of $D^2$ in polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ with $0 \le r \le 1$, $
0 \le \theta < 2\pi$. Define $f: D^2 \to S^2 \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ by

$$f(r, \theta) = \bigl(\sin(\pi r)\cos\theta,\;\sin(\pi r)\sin\theta,\;-\cos(\pi
 r)\bigr).$$

This is the map that "wraps" the disk onto the sphere, sending the center to the
 south pole and the boundary to the north pole, using the polar angle $\phi = \p
i r$ (measured from the south pole) and the azimuthal angle $\theta$.

**Verification of key properties:**

- *(Well-defined and continuous at $r=0$):* At $r=0$, $\sin(\pi r)=0$, so $f(0,\
theta)=(0,0,-1)$ for every $\theta$ — the formula is independent of $\theta$ at
the center, matching the fact that the center is a single point. As $r \to 0$, $
f(r,\theta)\to(0,0,-1)$ uniformly in $\theta$, so $f$ is continuous at the cente
r. Away from $r=0$, $f$ is a composition of smooth functions. Hence $f$ is conti
nuous on all of $D^2$.

- *(Constant on $S^1$):* At $r=1$, $\sin(\pi)=0$ and $-\cos(\pi)=1$, so $f(1,\th
eta)=(0,0,1)$ for every $\theta$. The entire boundary $S^1$ maps to the north po
le. ✓

- *(Surjective):* Every point of $S^2$ has spherical coordinates $(\phi, \theta)
$ with $\phi\in[0,\pi]$, $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$. Setting $r = \phi/\pi \in [0,1]$ g
ives $f(r,\theta)$ equal to that point. The south pole ($\phi=0$, i.e., $r=0$) a
nd north pole ($\phi=\pi$, i.e., $r=1$) are both attained. ✓

- *(Injective on the open disk $\{r<1\}$):* For $0<r<1$, $\sin(\pi r)>0$, so the
 $\theta$-coordinate is recovered from the first two components, and $r$ is reco
vered from the third component via $r = \arccos(-z)/\pi$. The center $r=0$ maps
to the south pole $(0,0,-1)$, and no point with $r>0$ maps there (since $-\cos(\
pi r) = -1$ only at $r=0$). Thus $f$ is injective on $\{r < 1\}$. ✓

**Step 2: Factor through the quotient.**

Let $q: D^2 \to D^2/S^1$ be the quotient map. The equivalence classes are: singl
etons $\{p\}$ for each $p$ in the open disk, and the entire boundary $S^1$ as on
e class. Since $f$ is constant on $S^1$, the universal property of the quotient
topology yields a unique continuous map $\tilde{f}: D^2/S^1 \to S^2$ with $f = \
tilde{f} \circ q$.

**Step 3: $\tilde{f}$ is a bijection.**

- *Surjective:* $f$ is surjective, so $\tilde{f}$ is surjective.
- *Injective:* Distinct interior points map to distinct points of $S^2$ (injecti
vity on $\{r<1\}$), and the single boundary class maps to the north pole, which
is the image of no interior point. Hence distinct equivalence classes map to dis
tinct points. ✓

**Step 4: $\tilde{f}$ is a homeomorphism.**

- $D^2$ is compact (closed and bounded in $\mathbb{R}^2$).
- $D^2/S^1$ is the continuous image $q(D^2)$ of a compact space, hence **compact
**.
- $S^2$ is a subspace of the Hausdorff space $\mathbb{R}^3$, hence **Hausdorff**
.
- A continuous bijection from a compact space onto a Hausdorff space is a **home
omorphism** (a standard theorem: closed subsets of the compact domain are compac
t, hence their images are compact in the Hausdorff codomain, hence closed; so th
e inverse is continuous).

Therefore $\tilde{f}: D^2/S^1 \to S^2$ is a homeomorphism.

**Conclusion.** Yes, $D^2/S^1$ is homeomorphic to $S^2$. The quotient space obta
ined by collapsing the boundary of the disk to a point is the 2-sphere. $\blacks
quare$
