# Solution

**Answer: No** — under the standard definition of a near-ring (which includes a
multiplicative identity), a "silly-ring" cannot exist. Two-sided distributivity
together with a multiplicative identity forces the underlying additive group to
be Abelian.

## Proof

**Definition.** A *near-ring* is a structure $(N, +, \cdot)$ where:
1. $(N, +)$ is a group (not necessarily Abelian),
2. $(N, \cdot)$ is a semigroup,
3. Right distributivity holds: $(x + y) \cdot z = x \cdot z + y \cdot z$ for all
 $x, y, z \in N$,
4. There exists a multiplicative identity $1 \in N$ with $1 \cdot x = x$ for all
 $x \in N$ (left identity).

A "silly-ring" would be a near-ring additionally satisfying **left distributivit
y** ($x \cdot (y + z) = x \cdot y + x \cdot z$) while having a **non-Abelian** a
dditive group. We show this is impossible.

**Theorem.** If $(N, +, \cdot)$ is a near-ring (with left identity $1$) satisfyi
ng both left and right distributivity, then $(N, +)$ is Abelian.

**Proof.** Let $a, b \in N$ be arbitrary. Consider the expression $(1 + 1) \cdot
 (a + b)$, which we evaluate in two ways.

**Via right distributivity:**
$$(1 + 1) \cdot (a + b) = (1 + 1) \cdot a \;+\; (1 + 1) \cdot b.$$
Applying right distributivity again to each term:
$$(1 + 1) \cdot a = 1 \cdot a + 1 \cdot a = a + a,$$
$$(1 + 1) \cdot b = 1 \cdot b + 1 \cdot b = b + b,$$
where we used $1 \cdot x = x$ (left identity). Thus:
$$(1 + 1) \cdot (a + b) = (a + a) + (b + b). \tag{R}$$

**Via left distributivity:**
$$(1 + 1) \cdot (a + b) = 1 \cdot (a + b) \;+\; 1 \cdot (a + b).$$
Using $1 \cdot x = x$ again:
$$(1 + 1) \cdot (a + b) = (a + b) + (a + b). \tag{L}$$

**Equating (R) and (L):**
$$(a + a) + (b + b) = (a + b) + (a + b).$$

Writing this out with associativity (suppressing parentheses):
$$a + a + b + b = a + b + a + b.$$

**Left-cancel** $a$ (valid in any group):
$$a + b + b = b + a + b.$$

**Right-cancel** $b$:
$$a + b = b + a.$$

Since $a$ and $b$ were arbitrary, $(N, +)$ is Abelian. $\blacksquare$

**Conclusion.** Any near-ring (with multiplicative identity) satisfying two-side
d distributivity must have an Abelian additive group. Therefore a "silly-ring" —
 a near-ring with two-sided distributivity and a non-Abelian additive group — **
does not exist**.

**Remark on definitions.** Some authors define near-rings *without* requiring a
multiplicative identity. Under that weaker definition, the answer becomes trivia
lly **yes**: the *zero multiplication* $a \cdot b = 0$ on any non-Abelian group
(e.g., $S_3$) satisfies all the required axioms — it is a semigroup, both distri
butive laws hold vacuously ($0 = 0 + 0$), and the additive group is non-Abelian.
 However, this is a degenerate example. Under the standard definition (Pilz, 197
7) requiring a left identity — which is the definition that makes the theory of
near-rings structurally rich — the theorem above shows that no such structure ca
n exist.
