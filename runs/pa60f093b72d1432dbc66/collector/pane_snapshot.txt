## Proof

**Setup.** Let $p = a/c$ and $q = d/b$ (both non-zero reals, since $a,b,c,d \neq
 0$). The coefficient of $x^k$ in $(ax+b)^{1000} - (cx+d)^{1000}$ is:
$$\binom{1000}{k}\left(a^k b^{1000-k} - c^k d^{1000-k}\right) = \binom{1000}{k}
c^k b^{1000-k}\left(p^k - q^{1000-k}\right).$$

Since $\binom{1000}{k} c^k b^{1000-k} \neq 0$, the coefficient of $x^k$ vanishes
 if and only if:
$$p^k = q^{1000-k}. \tag{$\star$}$$

Setting $\rho = pq$ and $\gamma = q^{1000}$, equation $(\star)$ becomes $\rho^k
= \gamma$. Note $\gamma = q^{1000} > 0$ (since 1000 is even).

**Analysis of $\rho^k = \gamma$ over $k \in \{0, 1, \ldots, 1000\}$.**

**Case 1: $\rho = 1$ (i.e., $pq = 1$).** Then $\rho^k = 1$ for all $k$. If $\gam
ma = 1$ (i.e., $|q|=1$, so $q = \pm 1$ and $p = 1/q = \pm 1$): all 1001 coeffici
ents vanish, giving $P \equiv 0$ (this is $n=0$, not a natural number). If $\gam
ma \neq 1$: no coefficient vanishes, so $n = 1001$.

**Case 2: $\rho = -1$ (i.e., $pq = -1$).** Then $\rho^k = (-1)^k$. Since $\gamma
 > 0$, we need $(-1)^k = \gamma > 0$, forcing $\gamma = 1$ (i.e., $|q| = 1$) and
 $k$ even. Then all even $k \in \{0, 2, 4, \ldots, 1000\}$ satisfy $(\star)$: th
at's 501 values, giving $n = 1001 - 501 = 500$. If $\gamma \neq 1$: no solution,
 $n = 1001$.

**Case 3: $\rho > 0$, $\rho \neq 1$.** The function $k \mapsto \rho^k$ is strict
ly monotone, so $(\star)$ has **at most one** solution. Thus $|S| \in \{0, 1\}$,
 giving $n \in \{1001, 1000\}$.

**Case 4: $\rho < 0$, $\rho \neq -1$.** Then $\rho^k = (-1)^k |\rho|^k$. Since $
\gamma > 0$, we need $k$ even and $|\rho|^k = \gamma$. As $|\rho| \neq 1$ and $|
\rho| > 0$, the map $k \mapsto |\rho|^k$ is strictly monotone, yielding **at mos
t one** even solution. Thus $n \in \{1001, 1000\}$.

**Summary of possible $|S|$ values:** $\{0, 1, 501, 1001\}$, corresponding to $n
 \in \{1001, 1000, 500, 0\}$.

**Excluding $n = 0$:** When $|S| = 1001$, the polynomial is identically zero ($P
 \equiv 0$), which gives $n = 0$ — not a natural number. (This occurs when $(p,q
) \in \{(1,1), (-1,-1)\}$, corresponding to $ax+b = \pm(cx+d)$.)

**Achievability of $n \in \{500, 1000, 1001\}$:**

- **$n = 1001$:** Take $a=2, b=1, c=1, d=3$. Then $p=2, q=3$, $\rho=6, \gamma=3^
{1000}$. The equation $6^k = 3^{1000}$ gives $k = 1000 \cdot \frac{\ln 3}{\ln 6}
 \approx 613.1$, not an integer. No coefficient vanishes. ✓

- **$n = 1000$:** Take $a=2, b=1, c=1, d=2$. Then $p=2, q=2$, $\rho=4, \gamma=2^
{1000}$. The equation $4^k = 2^{1000}$ gives $k = 500$, a single vanishing coeff
icient. ✓

- **$n = 500$:** Take $a=-1, b=1, c=1, d=1$. Then $p=-1, q=1$, $\rho=-1, \gamma=
1$. All even $k$ satisfy $(\star)$. Explicitly:
$$P(x) = (1-x)^{1000} - (1+x)^{1000} = \sum_{k=0}^{1000} \binom{1000}{k}\bigl((-
1)^k - 1\bigr) x^k.$$
For even $k$: $(-1)^k - 1 = 0$; for odd $k$: $(-1)^k - 1 = -2 \neq 0$. Non-zero
coefficients appear at $k = 1, 3, 5, \ldots, 999$: exactly 500 coefficients. ✓

**No other values are possible** because in every case, the number of vanishing
coefficients is either 0, 1, 501, or 1001 — there is no way to obtain 2 or more
(but fewer than 501) vanishing coefficients, since $\rho^k = \gamma$ with $\rho
\notin \{-1, 0, 1\}$ has at most one solution, and $\rho \in \{-1, 1\}$ forces e
ither all/none or all-even/none.

## Answer
