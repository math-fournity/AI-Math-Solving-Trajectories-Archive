 \times n$ matrices $X$.

## Proof

We use **Sylvester's determinant identity**: for any $n \times n$ matrices $P, Q
$,
$$\det(I - PQ) = \det(I - QP).$$

**Key idea.** For a real parameter $t$, define
$$p(t) = \det(A + tI - MX), \qquad q(t) = \det(B + tI - XM).$$
Both are polynomials in $t$ of degree at most $n$. We show $p(t) = q(t)$ for all
 $t$ outside a finite set, hence $p \equiv q$, and evaluating at $t = 0$ gives t
he result.

---

**The intertwining relation is preserved under shifting.** Since $AM = MB$, we h
ave
$$(A + tI)M = AM + tM = MB + tM = M(B + tI) \tag{1}$$
for every $t$.

**The characteristic polynomials are preserved under shifting.** Since $\det(\la
mbda I - A) = \det(\lambda I - B)$, setting $\lambda = -t$ gives
$$\det(A + tI) = (-1)^n \det(-tI - A) = (-1)^n \chi_A(-t) = (-1)^n \chi_B(-t) =
\det(B + tI). \tag{2}$$

In particular, $A + tI$ is invertible if and only if $B + tI$ is invertible, and
 this fails for at most $n$ values of $t$ (the negatives of the eigenvalues of $
A$).

---

**Evaluation at generic $t$.** Fix $t_0$ such that $A + t_0 I$ (equivalently $B
+ t_0 I$) is invertible. From (1):
$$(A + t_0 I)^{-1} M = M (B + t_0 I)^{-1}. \tag{3}$$

Now compute:
$$p(t_0) = \det(A + t_0 I - MX) = \det(A + t_0 I)\;\det\!\big(I - (A+t_0 I)^{-1}
MX\big).$$

Using (3), $(A + t_0 I)^{-1}MX = M(B+t_0 I)^{-1}X$, so
$$p(t_0) = \det(A + t_0 I)\;\det\!\big(I - M(B+t_0 I)^{-1}X\big).$$

By **Sylvester's identity** (with $P = M$, $Q = (B+t_0 I)^{-1}X$):
$$\det\!\big(I - M(B+t_0 I)^{-1}X\big) = \det\!\big(I - (B+t_0 I)^{-1}XM\big).$$

Therefore
$$p(t_0) = \det(A + t_0 I)\;\det\!\big(I - (B+t_0 I)^{-1}XM\big).$$

On the other hand:
$$q(t_0) = \det(B + t_0 I - XM) = \det(B + t_0 I)\;\det\!\big(I - (B+t_0 I)^{-1}
XM\big).$$

By (2), $\det(A + t_0 I) = \det(B + t_0 I)$. Comparing the two expressions:
$$p(t_0) = q(t_0). \tag{4}$$

---

**Conclusion.** The polynomials $p(t)$ and $q(t)$ agree at every $t_0$ where $A
+ t_0 I$ is invertible. There are at most $n$ excluded values, so $p$ and $q$ ag
ree at infinitely many points. Two polynomials agreeing at infinitely many point
s are identical, so $p(t) = q(t)$ for all $t$.

Setting $t = 0$:
$$\det(A - MX) = p(0) = q(0) = \det(B - XM).$$

This holds for every real $n \times n$ matrix $X$. $\blacksquare$
