**Proof.**

We work over $\mathbb{K} \in \{\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}\}$. We identify the space
of $n \times n$ matrices over $\mathbb{K}$ with $\mathbb{K}^{n^2}$, equipped wit
h Lebesgue measure $\lambda$ (the $2n^2$-dimensional real Lebesgue measure when
$\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}$).

**Claim.** The set
$$\Sigma := \{A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n} : \det A = 0\}$$
of singular matrices has Lebesgue measure zero, hence is negligible.

---

**Key ingredient.** The determinant
$$\det : \mathbb{K}^{n^2} \to \mathbb{K}$$
is a polynomial in the $n^2$ entries of the matrix. It is a *nonzero* polynomial
, since $\det(I_n) = 1$.

We use the following standard fact:

> **Lemma.** Let $P \in \mathbb{K}[x_1, \dots, x_N]$ be a nonzero polynomial. Th
en its zero set $Z(P) = \{x \in \mathbb{K}^N : P(x) = 0\}$ has Lebesgue measure
zero in $\mathbb{K}^N \cong \mathbb{R}^N$ (or $\mathbb{R}^{2N}$ when $\mathbb{K}
=\mathbb{C}$).

*Proof of Lemma (by induction on $N$, the number of variables).* We treat the re
al case; the complex case follows by viewing $\mathbb{C}^N \cong \mathbb{R}^{2N}
$ and applying the real result to real and imaginary parts, or directly by the s
ame induction treating $\mathbb{C}$ as the base field (a nonzero polynomial in o
ne complex variable has finitely many zeros, hence measure zero).

**Base case $N=1$:** A nonzero polynomial in one variable has finitely many root
s, and a finite set has Lebesgue measure zero.

**Inductive step:** Assume the result for $N-1$ variables. Write
$$P(x_1, \dots, x_N) = \sum_{k=0}^{d} P_k(x_1, \dots, x_{N-1})\, x_N^k,$$
where $P_k \in \mathbb{K}[x_1, \dots, x_{N-1}]$. Since $P \neq 0$, at least one
$P_k \neq 0$; let $m$ be the largest index with $P_m \neq 0$.

For each $(x_1, \dots, x_{N-1}) \in \mathbb{K}^{N-1}$, define the "slice"
$$Z_{x'} := \{x_N \in \mathbb{K} : P(x', x_N) = 0\}, \quad x' = (x_1, \dots, x_{
N-1}).$$

- If $P_m(x') \neq 0$, then $P(x', \cdot)$ is a nonzero univariate polynomial of
 degree $m$, so $Z_{x'}$ is finite, hence has measure zero in $\mathbb{K}$.
- If $P_m(x') = 0$, then $Z_{x'} = \mathbb{K}$ (the whole line), which may have
positive measure.

By the induction hypothesis, the set $B := \{x' \in \mathbb{K}^{N-1} : P_m(x') =
 0\}$ has measure zero in $\mathbb{K}^{N-1}$, since $P_m$ is a nonzero polynomia
l in $N-1$ variables.

Now partition $Z(P)$:
$$Z(P) = \underbrace{\bigl\{(x', x_N) : x' \in B,\; x_N \in \mathbb{K}\bigr\}}_{
=: E_1} \;\cup\; \underbrace{\bigl\{(x', x_N) : x' \notin B,\; P(x', x_N) = 0\bi
gr\}}_{=: E_2}.$$

- $E_1 = B \times \mathbb{K}$. Since $\lambda_{N-1}(B) = 0$, by Fubini/Tonelli,
$\lambda_N(E_1) = \lambda_{N-1}(B) \cdot \lambda_1(\mathbb{K}) = 0$.

- $E_2$: For each $x' \notin B$, the slice $Z_{x'}$ has measure zero in $\mathbb
{K}$. By Fubini's theorem,
$$\lambda_N(E_2) = \int_{\mathbb{K}^{N-1}} \lambda_1\bigl(Z_{x'} \cap (\{x'\} \t
ext{ slice})\bigr)\, d\lambda_{N-1}(x') = \int_{\mathbb{K}^{N-1} \setminus B} 0\
, d\lambda_{N-1} = 0.$$

Therefore $\lambda_N(Z(P)) = \lambda_N(E_1) + \lambda_N(E_2) = 0$. $\square$

---

**Applying the Lemma.** The set of singular matrices is
$$\Sigma = Z(\det) = \{A \in \mathbb{K}^{n^2} : \det(A) = 0\}.$$

Since $\det$ is a nonzero polynomial on $\mathbb{K}^{n^2}$ (with $N = n^2$ varia
bles), the Lemma gives
$$\lambda(\Sigma) = 0.$$

---

**Conclusion.** The set of singular $n \times n$ matrices is a proper algebraic
subvariety of $\mathbb{K}^{n^2}$ (the zero locus of the nonzero polynomial $\det
$), and therefore has Lebesgue measure zero. In particular:

- Over $\mathbb{R}$: $\Sigma \subset \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ has $n^2$-dimensional Leb
esgue measure zero.
- Over $\mathbb{C}$: $\Sigma \subset \mathbb{C}^{n^2} \cong \mathbb{R}^{2n^2}$ h
as $2n^2$-dimensional Lebesgue measure zero.

Hence the set of singular matrices is **negligible** (measure zero) with respect
 to the Lebesgue measure on both $\mathbb{C}^{n^2}$ and $\mathbb{R}^{n^2}$. $\bl
acksquare$
