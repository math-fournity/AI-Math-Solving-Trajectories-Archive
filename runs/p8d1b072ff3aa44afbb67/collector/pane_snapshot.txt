## Proof

We are given
$$g(a)=\lim_{h\to 0^+}\int_h^{1-h}t^{-a}(1-t)^{a-1}\,dt,\qquad a\in(0,1),$$
and we wish to compute $g\!\left(\tfrac12\right)$.

**Step 1. Rewrite the integral via a substitution.**

In the integral set
$$t=\frac{1}{1+s},\qquad\text{so that}\qquad 1-t=\frac{s}{1+s},\qquad dt=-\frac{
ds}{(1+s)^2}.$$
As $t$ runs from $0$ to $1$, $s$ runs from $+\infty$ down to $0$. The truncated
interval $[h,1-h]$ corresponds to a truncated interval in $s$ that exhausts $(0,
\infty)$ as $h\to0^+$. Computing the integrand:
$$t^{-a}=(1+s)^{a},\qquad (1-t)^{a-1}=\left(\frac{s}{1+s}\right)^{a-1}=s^{a-1}(1
+s)^{1-a}.$$
Hence
$$t^{-a}(1-t)^{a-1}\,dt=(1+s)^{a}\,s^{a-1}(1+s)^{1-a}\cdot\frac{ds}{(1+s)^2}=s^{
a-1}(1+s)^{-1}\,ds.$$
Therefore
$$g(a)=\int_0^\infty\frac{s^{a-1}}{1+s}\,ds.$$
(The differentiability of $g$ on $(0,1)$, given in the hypothesis, is consistent
 with this representation: the integral converges for every $a\in(0,1)$ and depe
nds smoothly on $a$.)

**Step 2. Evaluate at $a=\tfrac12$.**

$$g\!\left(\tfrac12\right)=\int_0^\infty\frac{s^{-1/2}}{1+s}\,ds.$$
Use the substitution $s=u^2$ ($u>0$), so $ds=2u\,du$ and $s^{-1/2}=u^{-1}$:
$$g\!\left(\tfrac12\right)=\int_0^\infty\frac{u^{-1}}{1+u^2}\,2u\,du=2\int_0^\in
fty\frac{du}{1+u^2}.$$
The remaining integral is the standard arctangent integral:
$$\int_0^\infty\frac{du}{1+u^2}=\Big[\arctan u\Big]_0^\infty=\frac{\pi}{2}-0=\fr
ac{\pi}{2}.$$
Thus
$$g\!\left(\tfrac12\right)=2\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}=\pi.$$
