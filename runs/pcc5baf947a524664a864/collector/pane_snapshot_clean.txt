**Answer: Yes, $f = g$.**

**Proof:**

Since $|f(z)| = |g(z)|$ for all $z \in \overline{\mathbb{D}}$ (condition 1), the

e have $|f(z)| \sim C|z-a|^k$ and $|g(z)| \sim D|z-a|^\ell$; equality of moduli
for all $z$ near $a$ forces $k = \ell$. So $f$ and $g$ share the same zeros with
 the same multiplicities.

Since $f$ is analytic on a neighborhood of $\overline{\mathbb{D}}$ and $f(z_0) \
neq 0$ (so $f$ is not identically zero), $f$ has finitely many zeros in $\overli
ne{\mathbb{D}}$. Let $a_1, \ldots, a_m$ be the common zeros of $f$ and $g$ in $\
mathbb{D}$, listed with multiplicity. Form the finite Blaschke product:
$$B(z) = \prod_{j=1}^{m} \frac{z - a_j}{1 - \overline{a_j}\, z}.$$

Define $F(z) = f(z)/B(z)$ and $G(z) = g(z)/B(z)$. Then:

- $F$ and $G$ are analytic on $\mathbb{D}$ (the zeros of $B$ cancel the correspo
nding zeros of $f$ and $g$).
- $F$ and $G$ are **zero-free** in $\mathbb{D}$ (all interior zeros have been fa
ctored out).
- Since $|f(z)| = |g(z)|$ for all $z \in \overline{\mathbb{D}}$ and $|B(z)|$ is
a common factor, we have $|F(z)| = |G(z)|$ for all $z \in \mathbb{D}$.

Now consider $h(z) = F(z)/G(z)$. Since $G$ has no zeros in $\mathbb{D}$, $h$ is
analytic on $\mathbb{D}$, and:
$$|h(z)| = \frac{|F(z)|}{|G(z)|} = 1 \quad \text{for all } z \in \mathbb{D}.$$

By the **maximum modulus principle** (equivalently, the open mapping theorem), a
 nonconstant analytic function on a domain is an open map, so it cannot have con
stant modulus. Therefore $h$ must be constant: $h(z) = e^{i\theta}$ for some $\t
heta \in \mathbb{R}$.

This gives $F(z) = e^{i\theta}\, G(z)$ for all $z \in \mathbb{D}$, hence:
$$f(z) = e^{i\theta}\, g(z) \quad \text{for all } z \in \mathbb{D},$$
and by continuity, for all $z \in \overline{\mathbb{D}}$.

**Applying condition 2:** There exists $z_0 \in \mathbb{D}$ with $f(z_0) = g(z_0

$$e^{i\theta}\, g(z_0) = g(z_0).$$

Therefore $f(z) = g(z)$ for all $z \in \overline{\mathbb{D}}$.
