## Solution

**Yes**, such a $C^\infty$ function exists.

### Key tool: Borel's theorem

**Borel's theorem (Borel's lemma).** For any sequence of real numbers $(c_n)_{n
\geq 0}$, there exists a function $f \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ such that $f^{(n)
}(0) = c_n$ for every $n \geq 0$.

*Sketch of proof.* Let $\varphi \in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ be a bump function w
ith $\varphi \equiv 1$ on $[-1,1]$ and $\operatorname{supp}(\varphi) \subset [-2
,2]$. Choose a sequence $\varepsilon_n \downarrow 0$ decreasing so rapidly that
for every $k \geq 0$:
$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{|c_n|}{n!}\left\|\frac{d^k}{dx^k}\!\Big[x^n\,\varphi
(x/\varepsilon_n)\Big]\right\|_\infty < \infty.$$
This is achievable: for $n > k$, the $k$-th derivative of $x^n\varphi(x/\varepsi
lon_n)$ is bounded by $C_k\, n^k\, \varepsilon_n^{\,n-k}$, and the factor $\vare
psilon_n^{\,n-k} \to 0$ dominates, allowing each term to be made arbitrarily sma
ll. The finitely many terms with $n \leq k$ are harmless.

Define
$$f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{c_n}{n!}\, x^n\, \varphi(x/\varepsilon_n).$$
By the choice of $\varepsilon_n$, the series converges uniformly together with a
ll derivatives, so $f \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$. Near $x = 0$, we have $\varphi(
x/\varepsilon_n) = 1$ for all $n$, so each summand equals $\frac{c_n}{n!}x^n$ ne
ar $0$. The $k$-th derivative at $0$ of $\frac{c_n}{n!}x^n$ is $c_n$ if $k = n$
and $0$ otherwise. Term-by-term differentiation (justified by uniform convergenc
e of derivatives) gives $f^{(k)}(0) = c_k$. $\square$

### Construction

Set $c_n = (n!)^2$ for all $n \geq 0$. By Borel's theorem, there exists $f \in C
^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ with
$$f^{(n)}(0) = (n!)^2, \qquad n \geq 0.$$

The Maclaurin series of $f$ is:
$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}\,x^n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(n!
)^2}{n!}\,x^n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} n!\, x^n.$$

### Radius of convergence is zero

The coefficients are $a_n = n!$. By the **Cauchy–Hadamard theorem**, the radius
of convergence is:
$$R = \frac{1}{\limsup_{n\to\infty} |a_n|^{1/n}} = \frac{1}{\limsup_{n\to\infty}
 (n!)^{1/n}}.$$

By **Stirling's approximation**, $n! \sim \sqrt{2\pi n}\,(n/e)^n$, so:
$$(n!)^{1/n} \sim \frac{n}{e} \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} \infty.$$

Therefore $\limsup_{n\to\infty} (n!)^{1/n} = \infty$, which gives:
$$R = \frac{1}{\infty} = 0.$$

Equivalently, for any $x \neq 0$, the general term $n!\,|x|^n \to \infty$, so th
e series diverges at every $x \neq 0$; it converges only at $x = 0$.

### Conclusion

The function $f \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ constructed above (via Borel's theorem
 with the prescribed derivatives $f^{(n)}(0) = (n!)^2$) has a Maclaurin series $
\sum_{n=0}^\infty n!\,x^n$ with radius of convergence $R = 0$. Since $f$ is $C^\
infty$ on all of $\mathbb{R}$, it is in particular $C^\infty$ on $(-\varepsilon,
 \varepsilon)$ for any $\varepsilon > 0$.

**Therefore, such a $C^\infty$ function exists.**
