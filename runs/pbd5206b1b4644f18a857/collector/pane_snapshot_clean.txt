**Answer: Yes.** In fact, the action on just *two* of these four points already
uniquely determines the element of SO(3).

---

**Setup.** The quotient $S^2/\sigma$ identifies antipodal points, so its points
are lines through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (real projective plane $\mathbb{R
P}^2$). The four given points correspond to the four lines

$$L_1=\mathbb{R}(1,1,1),\quad L_2=\mathbb{R}(1,1,-1),\quad L_3=\mathbb{R}(1,-1,1
),\quad L_4=\mathbb{R}(1,-1,-1),$$

i.e. the four body‑diagonal directions of the cube. SO(3) acts on $S^2$ and this
 descends to an action on $\mathbb{RP}^2$ since every rotation commutes with the
 antipodal map $\sigma$.

**Key claim.** *The pointwise stabilizer in SO(3) of $L_1$ and $L_2$ is trivial.
* Since any two body diagonals make the same angle, the same then holds for any
pair, so the claim applies to any two of the four points.

**Proof of the claim.** Let $R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)$ fix $L_1$ as a line, i.e. $R(L_
1)=L_1$. The stabilizer of a line in SO(3) is exactly the subgroup of rotations
about that line, so $R=R_\theta$ is a rotation by some angle $\theta$ about the
axis $u=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1,1,1)$ (unit vector along $L_1$).

Now impose $R(L_2)=L_2$, i.e. $R_\theta(v_2)=\pm v_2$ for $v_2=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}
(1,1,-1)$. Using the Rodrigues formula
$$R_\theta v = \cos\theta\, v + (1-\cos\theta)(v\cdot u)\,u + \sin\theta\,(u\tim
es v),$$
the condition $R_\theta v_2 = \pm v_2$ gives
$$\sin\theta\,(u\times v_2) + (1-\cos\theta)\bigl((v_2\cdot u)u - v_2\bigr)=0.$$
Since $u$ and $v_2$ are linearly independent, $u\times v_2\neq 0$, forcing $\sin
\theta=0$, hence $\theta\in\{0,\pi\}$.

- $\theta=0$: $R=I$. ✓
- $\theta=\pi$: $R_\pi v_2 = -v_2 + 2(v_2\cdot u)u$. For this to equal $+v_2$ we
 would need $u\parallel v_2$; for it to equal $-v_2$ we would need $v_2\perp u$.
 But $v_2\cdot u = \tfrac{1}{3}(1+1-1)=\tfrac13\neq 0,1$, so neither holds. ✗

Thus $\theta=0$ and $R=I$. The pointwise stabilizer of $\{L_1,L_2\}$ is trivial.
 ∎

**Conclusion.** Suppose $R,R'\in\mathrm{SO}(3)$ have the same action on all four
 points, in particular $R(L_1)=R'(L_1)$ and $R(L_2)=R'(L_2)$. Then $R^{-1}R'$ fi
xes both $L_1$ and $L_2$ pointwise, so by the claim $R^{-1}R'=I$, i.e. $R=R'$.

Hence an element of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ **is** uniquely determined by its action on
 the four given points of $S^2/\sigma$ — indeed by its action on any two of them
.
