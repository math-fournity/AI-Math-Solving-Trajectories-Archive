Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether a flat, geodesically convex
 submanifold $S \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ always admits an isometric slice chart — a
 rigid motion mapping $S \cap U$ to a $k$-slice.

**Answer: No.** Such a chart does not exist in general. I provide a counterexamp
le and proof.

---

## Proof

**Key observation.** An isometry $\phi: U \to V$ between open subsets of $\mathb
b{R}^n$ (both carrying the standard Euclidean metric) must be a **rigid motion**
: $\phi(x) = Ax + b$ with $A \in O(n)$, $b \in \mathbb{R}^n$. This is classical
— any distance-preserving map on an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ extends unique
ly to a rigid motion of the entire space. Consequently, $\phi(S \cap U)$ being a
 $k$-slice requires $S \cap U$ to lie in a $k$-dimensional **affine** subspace o
f $\mathbb{R}^n$, i.e., $S$ must be **totally geodesic** ($II = 0$) near $p$.

The question thus reduces to: does flat (zero intrinsic curvature) + geodesicall
y convex imply totally geodesic? The answer is no.

### Counterexample: a patch of the unit cylinder

Consider the open patch of the unit cylinder in $\mathbb{R}^3$:
$$S = \bigl\{(\cos\theta,\, \sin\theta,\, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 : \theta \in (-\tf
rac{\pi}{2},\, \tfrac{\pi}{2}),\; z \in (-1,\, 1)\bigr\}.$$

This is a smooth 2-dimensional embedded submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

**$S$ is flat.** The parametrization $f(\theta,z) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, z)$
 yields tangent vectors $\partial_\theta = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0)$ and $\p
artial_z = (0,0,1)$, giving the induced metric:
$$g = \langle \partial_\theta, \partial_\theta\rangle\, d\theta^2 + \langle \par
tial_z, \partial_z\rangle\, dz^2 = d\theta^2 + dz^2.$$
This is the standard Euclidean metric on the $(\theta,z)$-plane, so the Riemanni
an curvature tensor vanishes identically. Hence $S$ is flat.

**$S$ is geodesically convex.** Under the coordinate map $(\theta, z) \mapsto f(
\theta,z)$, the submanifold $S$ corresponds to the open rectangle $R = (-\frac{\
pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}) \times (-1, 1) \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ with the flat metric
 $d\theta^2 + dz^2$. Since the Christoffel symbols vanish, geodesics are straigh
t lines in $(\theta, z)$-coordinates. The rectangle $R$ is a convex subset of $\
mathbb{R}^2$, so for any two points in $R$, the straight line segment joining th
em lies entirely in $R$. Therefore, for any $p, q \in S$, the (unique) minimizin
g geodesic in $S$ from $p$ to $q$ lies in $S$. Hence $S$ is geodesically convex.

**$S$ is not totally geodesic.** The second fundamental form is nonzero. The out
ward unit normal is $\nu = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)$, and:
$$\partial_\theta^2 f = (-\cos\theta, -\sin\theta, 0), \qquad \langle \partial_\
theta^2 f,\, \nu\rangle = -1.$$
So $II_{\theta\theta} = -1 \neq 0$. The principal curvature in the $\theta$-dire
ction is $1$; the principal curvature in the $z$-direction is $0$. Since $II \ne
q 0$, the submanifold $S$ is **not** contained in any 2-dimensional affine subsp
ace of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

**No isometric slice chart exists.** Suppose for contradiction that there exists
 an open neighborhood $U$ of some $p \in S$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ and an isometry $\
phi: U \to V$ such that $\phi(S \cap U)$ is a 2-slice in $V$. Then $\phi$ is a r
igid motion, so $\phi(S \cap U)$ lies in a 2-dimensional affine subspace $P$ of
$\mathbb{R}^3$. Since $\phi$ is affine, $S \cap U = \phi^{-1}(\phi(S \cap U))$ l
ies in the affine subspace $\phi^{-1}(P)$. But any open subset of $S$ (in the su
bmanifold topology) contains points where $II_{\theta\theta} = -1 \neq 0$, so $S
 \cap U$ cannot lie in any affine subspace — a subspace would force $II = 0$ eve
rywhere on $S \cap U$. Contradiction.

### Conclusion

The conditions "flat" (zero intrinsic Riemannian curvature) and "geodesically co
nvex" (an intrinsic metric condition) do **not** force the second fundamental fo
rm to vanish. The cylinder patch $S$ above is flat and geodesically convex yet h
as $II \neq 0$, so it cannot be mapped to a $k$-slice by any ambient isometry. T
herefore, an isometric slice chart need not exist.

The essential obstruction is that **flatness is intrinsic** while being a slice
under an ambient isometry is **extrinsic** (it requires $II = 0$, i.e., being to
tally geodesic). Geodesic convexity is also intrinsic and cannot bridge this gap
. $\blacksquare$
