## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, $A$ is necessarily a real matrix.

Write $A = B + iC$, where $B$ and $C$ are real $n\times n$ matrices (the real an
d imaginary parts of $A$). For any real vector $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$,

$$Ax = (B + iC)x = Bx + i\,Cx.$$

The hypothesis says that $Ax\in\mathbb{R}^n$ for every $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$. A com
plex vector is real if and only if its imaginary part vanishes, so

$$Cx = 0 \quad\text{for every } x\in\mathbb{R}^n.$$

In particular, taking $x = e_k$ (the $k$-th standard basis vector) for each $k =
 1,\dots,n$, we get $Ce_k = 0$, i.e. the $k$-th column of $C$ is zero. Since thi
s holds for every $k$, we conclude

$$C = 0.$$

Therefore $A = B$, a real matrix.

**Remark.** The Hermitian and nonnegative-definite hypotheses are not actually n
eeded for this conclusion: *any* complex matrix $A$ satisfying "$Ax$ is real for
 every real $x$" must be real. The given hypotheses are consistent with the conc
lusion (a real symmetric nonnegative-definite matrix is indeed Hermitian and non
negative-definite), but they play no essential role in forcing $A$ to be real.
