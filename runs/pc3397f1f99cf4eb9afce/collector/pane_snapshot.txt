## Proof

**Answer: No.** Knowing the marginal distributions of $X$ and $Y$ (exponential w
ith rates $p$ and $q$) together with the distribution of $X-Y$ does **not** forc
e $X$ and $Y$ to be independent. We exhibit an explicit dependent joint law with
 the same marginals and the same difference distribution.

---

### Setup

Let $f_0(x,y) = pq\,e^{-px-qy}\,\mathbf 1_{\{x\ge 0,\,y\ge 0\}}$ be the joint de
nsity in the independent case. Its marginals are $\mathrm{Exp}(p)$ and $\mathrm{
Exp}(q)$, and $Z = X-Y$ has the asymmetric Laplace density
$$
f_Z(z) = \frac{pq}{p+q}\,e^{-pz}\,(z\ge 0),\qquad f_Z(z)=\frac{pq}{p+q}\,e^{qz}\
,(z<0).
$$

We will perturb $f_0$ by a function $g$ that leaves the marginals, the total mas
s, **and** the distribution of $X-Y$ unchanged, while breaking independence.

---

### Constructing the perturbation

Fix constants $\alpha > p$ and $\beta > q$, and define $g$ on $[0,\infty)^2$ thr
ough its two‑dimensional (one‑sided) Laplace transform
$$
G(s,t)\;=\;\int_0^\infty\!\!\int_0^\infty g(x,y)\,e^{-sx-ty}\,dx\,dy
\;=\;\frac{s\,t\,(s+t)}{(s+\alpha)^3\,(t+\beta)^3}.
$$

Because the right‑hand side is **strictly proper** in each variable (degree $2<3
$) with all poles at the real negative points $s=-\alpha$, $t=-\beta$, the inver
se Laplace transform $g$ is a **genuine, real‑valued, continuous, integrable** f
unction on $[0,\infty)^2$ — a finite linear combination of terms $x^i y^j e^{-\a
lpha x-\beta y}$ with $0\le i,j\le 2$. Moreover $g\not\equiv 0$, since $G(1,1) =
 2/((1+\alpha)^3(1+\beta)^3)\neq 0$.

---

### Three annihilating properties

Write $h_X(x)=\int_0^\infty g(x,y)\,dy$ and $h_Y(y)=\int_0^\infty g(x,y)\,dx$.

**(i) Marginal of $X$ preserved.** $G(s,0)=0$ (factor $t$), so the Laplace trans
form of $h_X$ vanishes identically; by uniqueness, $h_X\equiv 0$.

**(ii) Marginal of $Y$ preserved.** $G(0,t)=0$ (factor $s$), so $h_Y\equiv 0$.

**(iii) Distribution of $X-Y$ preserved.** Let $D_g(z)$ denote the perturbation'
s contribution to the density of $Z=X-Y$:
$$
D_g(z)=\int_{\max(0,-z)}^\infty g(y+z,y)\,dy .
$$
Its bilateral Laplace transform is, after the change of variables $x=y+z$,
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} D_g(z)\,e^{-sz}\,dz
=\int_0^\infty\!\!\int_0^\infty g(x,y)\,e^{-s(x-y)}\,dx\,dy
=G(s,-s).
$$
But $G(s,-s)=0$ because of the factor $(s+t)$. This holds on the strip $-\alpha<
\mathrm{Re}\,s<\beta$, where $G(s,-s)$ is analytic. By uniqueness of the bilater
al Laplace transform for locally integrable functions, $D_g\equiv 0$.

**(iv) Total mass preserved.** $G(0,0)=0$, so $\iint g\,dx\,dy=0$.

---

### A valid non‑independent joint density

Define
$$
f_\varepsilon(x,y)=f_0(x,y)+\varepsilon\,g(x,y),\qquad x,y\ge 0.
$$

* **Mass** $=1+\varepsilon\cdot 0=1$.
* **Marginals** $=\mathrm{Exp}(p),\mathrm{Exp}(q)$ by (i),(ii).
* **Difference** $X-Y$ has density $f_Z+\varepsilon D_g=f_Z$ by (iii): exactly t
he asymmetric Laplace law of $X'-Y'$ with independent $X'\sim\mathrm{Exp}(p)$, $
Y'\sim\mathrm{Exp}(q)$.
* **Non‑negativity.** The ratio
  $$
  \frac{|g(x,y)|}{pq\,e^{-px-qy}}
  $$
  is continuous on $[0,\infty)^2$ and tends to $0$ as $x+y\to\infty$ (because $\
alpha>p$, $\beta>q$ make $g$ decay strictly faster than $f_0$), hence it is boun
ded by some $M<\infty$. For $|\varepsilon|<1/M$ we have $f_\varepsilon\ge 0$, so
 $f_\varepsilon$ is a bona fide joint density.

* **Not independent.** Independence would force $f_\varepsilon = f_X\cdot f_Y =
f_0$, i.e. $\varepsilon\,g\equiv 0$. Since $g\not\equiv 0$, any $\varepsilon\neq
 0$ (small enough) yields a **dependent** couple $(X,Y)$.

---

### Conclusion

There exist exponentially distributed (rates $p$, $q$) random variables $X,Y$ th
at are **dependent** yet whose difference $X-Y$ has exactly the same distributio
n as $X'-Y'$ for *independent* $X'\sim\mathrm{Exp}(p)$, $Y'\sim\mathrm{Exp}(q)$.
 Therefore the stated hypothesis does **not** imply that $X$ and $Y$ are indepen
dent.
