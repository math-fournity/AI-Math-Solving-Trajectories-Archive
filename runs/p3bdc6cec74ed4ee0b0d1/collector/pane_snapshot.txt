## Proof

**Claim.** The function $f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}$ is Lebesgue-integrable on
 $(0,1)$, and
$$\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}=\pi.$$

### Step 1. Measurability and positivity

On $(0,1)$ we have $x>0$ and $1-x>0$, hence $x(1-x)>0$, so $f$ is well defined,
continuous, strictly positive, and Borel-measurable. Being nonnegative, its Lebe
sgue integral equals its improper Riemann integral (monotone convergence / Fatou
-type argument via truncation). Thus it suffices to determine whether the improp
er integral converges and to compute it.

### Step 2. Integrability near the endpoints

The only potential singularities are at $x=0$ and $x=1$.

*Near $x=0$:* for $0<x\le\tfrac12$, we have $1-x\ge\tfrac12$, so
$$f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}\le\frac{1}{\sqrt{x/2}}=\sqrt{2}\,x^{-1/2}.$$
Since $\int_{0}^{1/2}x^{-1/2}\,dx=2\sqrt{x}\,\big|_{0}^{1/2}<\infty$, comparison
 shows $f$ is integrable near $0$.

*Near $x=1$:* by the substitution $x\mapsto 1-x$ (or directly, for $\tfrac12\le
x<1$ we have $x\ge\tfrac12$, giving $f(x)\le\sqrt{2}\,(1-x)^{-1/2}$), the same a
rgument shows $f$ is integrable near $1$.

On the compact subinterval $[\tfrac14,\tfrac34]$ the function is continuous, hen
ce bounded and integrable. Combining the three regions, $f\in L^{1}(0,1)$.

### Step 3. Computing the integral

Complete the square in the radicand:
$$x(1-x)=\tfrac14-\bigl(x-\tfrac12\bigr)^{2}=\tfrac14\Bigl(1-(2x-1)^{2}\Bigr),$$
so $\sqrt{x(1-x)}=\tfrac12\sqrt{1-(2x-1)^{2}}$. Therefore
$$\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}=2\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-(2x-1)^{2}
}}.$$

Substitute $u=2x-1$, $du=2\,dx$, so $dx=\tfrac12\,du$; as $x:0\to1$, $u:-1\to1$:
$$2\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-(2x-1)^{2}}}=2\cdot\frac12\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{du
}{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{du}{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}.$$

The antiderivative is $\arcsin u$:
$$\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{du}{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}=\Bigl[\arcsin u\Bigr]_{-1}^{1}=\frac{\p
i}{2}-\Bigl(-\frac{\pi}{2}\Bigr)=\pi.$$

### Step 4. Justifying the substitution under the Lebesgue integral

The substitution $u=2x-1$ is a $C^{1}$-diffeomorphism of $(0,1)$ onto $(-1,1)$ w
ith constant Jacobian $\tfrac12$, so the change-of-variables formula for Lebesgu
e integrals applies directly. Equivalently, one may apply it on truncated interv
als $[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]$ and pass to the limit via monotone convergence
 (justified by Step 2, which guarantees the limit is finite). Either route yield
s the same value $\pi$.

### Conclusion

The function $f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}$ is Lebesgue-integrable on $(0,1)$, a
nd
$$\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}=\pi.}$$
