## Counterexample

The answer is **no**: $\partial A \cap \partial B$ need not have $n$-dimensional
 Lebesgue measure $0$. I give an explicit counterexample in $\mathbb{R}^1$ (whic
h extends to $\mathbb{R}^n$ by product with a cube).

### Construction

Let $C \subset [0,1]$ be the **Smith–Volterra–Cantor (fat Cantor) set**: closed,
 nowhere dense, perfect, with Lebesgue measure $m(C) = \tfrac12 > 0$.

Its complement in $(0,1)$ is a countable disjoint union of open intervals (the "
gaps") $G_1, G_2, \dots$. Let $E = \{a_k, b_k\}_{k\ge 1}$ be the set of endpoint
s of the gaps. Since $C$ is perfect, $E$ is countable and **dense in $C$**.

**Lemma.** The gaps $\{G_k\}$ can be partitioned into two subfamilies $\{G_k : k
 \in P\}$ and $\{G_k : k \in Q\}$ (with $P \sqcup Q = \mathbb{N}$) so that the c
orresponding endpoint sets $E_P$ and $E_Q$ are **both dense in $C$**.

*Proof of lemma.* $C$ is a compact perfect metric space, so it has a countable b
ase $\{U_j\}$. Since $E$ is dense in the perfect set $C$, each $U_j \cap E$ is i
nfinite. Enumerate the requirements $(i,j) \in \{1,2\}\times\mathbb{N}$. At the
step for $(i,j)$, pick an as-yet-unassigned gap $G_k$ having an endpoint in $U_j
$ (possible since $U_j\cap E$ is infinite and only finitely many gaps have been
assigned so far) and assign $G_k$ to group $i$. Any leftover gaps are assigned a
rbitrarily. Each group then has an endpoint in every basic open set $U_j$, hence
 is dense in $C$. $\square$

Now define
$$A = \bigcup_{k\in P} G_k, \qquad B = \bigcup_{k\in Q} G_k.$$
Both are open and disjoint.

### Verifying the hypothesis

Because $E_P$ is dense in $C$, every point of $C$ is a limit point of $P$-gaps,
so $\overline{A} = A \cup C$. Likewise $\overline{B} = B \cup C$.

Then
$$A \cap \overline{B} = A \cap (B \cup C) = (A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C) = \varnothing
 \cup \varnothing = \varnothing,$$
since the gaps are pairwise disjoint and $A\subseteq (0,1)\setminus C$. Similarl
y $B\cap\overline{A}=\varnothing$. Hence
$$\bigl(\overline{A}\cap B\bigr)\cup\bigl(\overline{B}\cap A\bigr)=\varnothing.$
$

### Computing the boundaries

Since $A$ is open, $\operatorname{int}(A)=A$, and
$$\partial A = \overline{A}\setminus \operatorname{int}(A) = (A\cup C)\setminus
A = C.$$
Likewise $\partial B = C$. Therefore
$$\partial A \cap \partial B = C, \qquad m_1(\partial A\cap\partial B) = m_1(C)
= \tfrac12 > 0.$$

### Extension to $\mathbb{R}^n$

For $n\ge 2$, set $A' = A\times(0,1)^{n-1}$ and $B' = B\times(0,1)^{n-1}$. The s
ame separation argument gives $(\overline{A'}\cap B')\cup(\overline{B'}\cap A')=
\varnothing$, while $\partial A'\cap\partial B' \supseteq C\times(0,1)^{n-1}$ ha
s $n$-dimensional measure $\tfrac12\cdot 1^{n-1}=\tfrac12>0$.

### Conclusion

The condition $(\overline{A}\cap B)\cup(\overline{B}\cap A)=\varnothing$ does **
not** force $\partial A\cap\partial B$ to have $n$-dimensional Lebesgue measure
$0$. The fat-Cantor-set construction above yields $\partial A\cap\partial B = C$
 of positive measure.
