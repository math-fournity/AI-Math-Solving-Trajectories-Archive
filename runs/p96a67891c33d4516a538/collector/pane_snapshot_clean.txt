Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether, given a basis $w_1, \ldots
, w_n$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$, there exists a basis $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ such that for
every subset $S \subseteq \{1,\ldots,n\}$, the orthogonal complement of $\operat
orname{span}\{w_i : i \in S\}$ equals $\operatorname{span}\{v_i : i \in S^c\}$.

**Answer: Yes, such a basis always exists.**

---

**Step 1: Reduction to pairwise orthogonality.**

The condition for all subsets $S$ is equivalent to:
- (a) $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ is a basis, and
- (b) $v_k \perp w_i$ for all $k \neq i$.

*($\Rightarrow$)* Taking $S = \{i\}$: $(\operatorname{span}\{w_i\})^\perp = \ope
ratorname{span}\{v_k : k \neq i\}$, so $v_k \perp w_i$ for all $k \neq i$. The $
v_i$ form a basis by hypothesis.

*($\Leftarrow$)* If (a) and (b) hold, then for any $S$, every $v_k$ with $k \not
in S$ is orthogonal to every $w_i$ with $i \in S$ (since $k \neq i$). Thus $\ope
ratorname{span}\{v_k : k \notin S\} \subseteq (\operatorname{span}\{w_i : i \in
S\})^\perp$. Both sides have dimension $|S^c| = n - |S|$ (LHS: any subset of a b
asis is linearly independent; RHS: rank–nullity applied to the $|S|$-dimensional
 subspace $\operatorname{span}\{w_i : i \in S\}$). Hence equality holds.

**Step 2: Construction.**

For each $k$, define $V_k := (\operatorname{span}\{w_i : i \neq k\})^\perp$. Sin
ce $w_1, \ldots, w_n$ is a basis, $\operatorname{span}\{w_i : i \neq k\}$ has di
mension $n-1$, so $V_k$ is $1$-dimensional. Let $v_k$ be any nonzero vector in $
V_k$.

**Step 3: Orthogonality condition (b) holds.**

By construction, $v_k \in V_k = (\operatorname{span}\{w_i : i \neq k\})^\perp$,
so $v_k \perp w_i$ for all $i \neq k$. ✓

**Step 4: $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ is a basis (condition (a)).**

Let $W = [w_1 \mid \cdots \mid w_n]$ (columns are the $w_i$). Since $\{w_i\}$ is
 a basis, $W$ is invertible. The condition $v_k \in V_k$ means $w_i^T v_k = 0$ f
or all $i \neq k$, i.e., $W^T v_k$ is a scalar multiple of $e_k$. Since $v_k \ne
q 0$ and $W^T$ is invertible, $W^T v_k \neq 0$, so:
$$W^T v_k = \alpha_k\, e_k, \quad \alpha_k \neq 0.$$
(If $\alpha_k = 0$ then $v_k \perp w_k$ as well, making $v_k \perp$ all $w_i$, h
ence $v_k = 0$—contradiction.)

Therefore $v_k = \alpha_k (W^T)^{-1} e_k$. The vectors $(W^T)^{-1}e_1, \ldots, (
W^T)^{-1}e_n$ are the columns of the invertible matrix $(W^T)^{-1}$, hence form
a basis. Since each $v_k$ is a **nonzero** scalar multiple of $(W^T)^{-1}e_k$, t
he vectors $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ are also linearly independent, thus form a basis o
f $\mathbb{R}^n$. ✓

**Conclusion.**

By Steps 1–4, the basis $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ (where $v_k$ is the unique-up-to-scal
ar vector orthogonal to all $w_i$, $i \neq k$) satisfies
$$\bigl(\operatorname{span}\{w_i : i \in S\}\bigr)^\perp = \operatorname{span}\{
v_i : i \in S^c\}$$
for every subset $S \subseteq \{1, \ldots, n\}$. Equivalently, $v_k$ is proporti
onal to the $k$-th row of $W^{-1}$ (viewed as a column vector), which is always
well-defined and yields a basis.
