## Solution

**Answer: No, it does not follow that $f(t) = 0$ a.e. on $[0,T]$.**

### Analysis

The process $Y_t = Y_0 + \int_0^t f(s)\,ds$ is an absolutely continuous function
 of $t$ (assuming $f \in L^1([0,T])$ so the integral is well-defined). By the fu
ndamental theorem of calculus for Lebesgue integrals, $Y$ is differentiable a.e.
 with $Y'(t) = f(t)$ a.e.

The condition $\ell(E) = 0$ where $E = \{t \in [0,T] : Y_t = 0\}$ merely states
that the process spends **zero Lebesgue time** at the level $0$. This is an extr
emely weak condition — it is satisfied by virtually any function that does not v
anish on a set of positive measure. It places essentially no constraint on the d
erivative $f$.

### Counterexample

Take $Y_0 = 0$ and $f(t) = 1$ for all $t \in [0,T]$. Then:

$$Y_t = \int_0^t 1\,ds = t.$$

The zero set is:

$$E = \{t \in [0,T] : t = 0\} = \{0\},$$

so $\ell(E) = 0$. Yet $f(t) = 1 \neq 0$ for every $t$, and in particular $f \neq
 0$ a.e. on $[0,T]$.

### Why the implication fails

The condition $\ell(\{t : Y_t = 0\}) = 0$ is a statement about the **level set**
 of $Y$ at a single value ($0$). It says nothing about the **slope** of $Y$. A f
unction can cross (or avoid) the level $0$ while having any nonzero derivative.
For instance, any strictly monotone $Y$ satisfies $\ell(E) = 0$ (since $Y_t = 0$
 has at most one solution), regardless of what $f = Y'$ is.

More generally, for the implication "$f = 0$ a.e." to hold, one would need a con
dition that constrains $f$ itself — for example, that $Y_t = 0$ for **all** $t \
in [0,T]$ (which would indeed force $f = 0$ a.e. by the fundamental theorem of c
alculus), or some condition tying the occupation of level sets to the quadratic
variation (as in the occupation time formula for semimartingales, where $d\langl
e Y\rangle_t$ would need to be involved). The mere vanishing of the Lebesgue mea
sure of a single level set is far too weak.
