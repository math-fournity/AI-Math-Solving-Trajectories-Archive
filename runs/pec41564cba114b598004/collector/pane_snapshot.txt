## Proof

Let $P_k = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdots p_k$ (the $k$-th primor
ial). We show that for $k \geq 2$, $P_k - 1$ is never a nontrivial perfect power
 (i.e., $a^n$ with $a \geq 2$, $n \geq 2$).

### Step 1: The exponent $n$ must be odd.

Since $p_1 = 2$, $P_k$ is even, so $P_k - 1$ is odd. If $n$ were even, then $P_k
 - 1 = a^n = (a^{n/2})^2$ would be a perfect square. But for $k \geq 2$, $3 \mid
 P_k$, so $P_k - 1 \equiv -1 \equiv 2 \pmod{3}$, and $2$ is not a quadratic resi
due mod $3$. Contradiction. Thus $n$ is odd, so $n \geq 3$.

### Step 2: Reduction to prime exponent.

Let $q$ be any prime factor of $n$, and write $n = qm$. Set $b = a^m \geq 2$. Th
en $P_k - 1 = b^q$ where $q \geq 3$ is an odd prime. We henceforth work with $b^
q = P_k - 1$ (renaming $b$ back to $a$).

### Step 3: Key congruence analysis.

Since $p_i \mid P_k$ for all $i \leq k$, we have $a^q = P_k - 1 \equiv -1 \pmod{
p_i}$ for every prime $p_i \leq p_k$.

For each such prime $p$: $a^q \equiv -1 \pmod{p}$ means $a^{2q} \equiv 1$ but $a
^q \not\equiv 1 \pmod{p}$. So $\operatorname{ord}_p(a) \mid 2q$ but $\operatorna
me{ord}_p(a) \nmid q$. Since $q$ is prime, the only possibilities are:
- $\operatorname{ord}_p(a) = 2$, meaning $a \equiv -1 \pmod{p}$, or
- $\operatorname{ord}_p(a) = 2q$, meaning $2q \mid (p-1)$, i.e., $p \equiv 1 \pm
od{2q}$.

**Crucial observation:** No prime $p \leq 2q$ can satisfy $p \equiv 1 \pmod{2q}$
 (since that would require $p \geq 2q+1 > 2q$). Therefore, for **every** prime $
p \leq 2q$, we must have $a \equiv -1 \pmod{p}$.

### Step 4: Factoring via the cyclotomic identity.

Since $q$ is odd:
$$P_k = a^q + 1 = (a+1)\cdot Q, \quad \text{where } Q = a^{q-1} - a^{q-2} + \cdo
ts - a + 1 = \frac{a^q+1}{a+1}.$$

Since $P_k = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdots p_k$ is **squarefree**, and $(a+1) \cdot Q
 = P_k$, any prime dividing both $a+1$ and $Q$ would appear squared in $P_k$—imp
ossible. So $\gcd(a+1, Q) = 1$.

**Computing $\gcd(a+1, Q)$:** Modulo $a+1$, we have $a \equiv -1$, so $Q \equiv
\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}(-1)^j(-1)^{q-1-j} = q\cdot(-1)^{q-1} = q \pmod{a+1}$ (since $q-
1$ is even). Thus $\gcd(a+1, Q) \mid q$.

### Step 5: Case $q \leq p_k$ — Contradiction.

Since $q$ is prime and $q \leq p_k$, $q$ is one of the primes in $\{p_1, \ldots,
 p_k\}$. Also $q \leq 2q$, so by Step 3, $a \equiv -1 \pmod{q}$, giving $q \mid
(a+1)$.

From Step 4, $q \mid (a+1)$ implies $q \mid Q$ (since $Q \equiv q \equiv 0 \pmod
{q}$ when $a \equiv -1 \pmod{q}$). But then $q \mid \gcd(a+1, Q)$, and since $\g
cd(a+1,Q) \mid q$, we get $\gcd(a+1, Q) = q$. This means $q^2 \mid (a+1)\cdot Q
= P_k$, contradicting the squarefreeness of $P_k$.

### Step 6: Case $q > p_k$ — Contradiction.

Here $2q > 2p_k > p_k$, so $\min(2q, p_k) = p_k$. Every prime $p \leq p_k$ satis
fies $p < 2q$, hence $p \not\equiv 1 \pmod{2q}$, forcing $a \equiv -1 \pmod{p}$
for **all** primes $p \leq p_k$. Therefore $P_k \mid (a+1)$, so $a + 1 \geq P_k$
, i.e., $a \geq P_k - 1$.

But $a^q = P_k - 1$ with $q \geq 3$ gives $a = (P_k - 1)^{1/q} \leq (P_k - 1)^{1
/3}$. For $k \geq 2$, $P_k \geq 6$, so $P_k - 1 \geq 5 > 1$, and $(P_k-1)^{1/3}
< P_k - 1$. This contradicts $a \geq P_k - 1$.

### Conclusion.

Both cases lead to contradictions. Therefore, for $k \geq 2$, the number $p_1 p_
2 \cdots p_k - 1$ **cannot** be a perfect power.

For $k = 1$: $p_1 - 1 = 2 - 1 = 1 = 1^n$, which is only a trivial perfect power
(with base $a = 1$). Excluding this trivial case, the expression is **never** a
(nontrivial) perfect power for any $k \geq 1$.
