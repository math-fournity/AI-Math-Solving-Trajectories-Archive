## Proof

Let $L_1, L_2, \ldots, L_7$ be seven distinct lines in the Euclidean plane. We c
ount the number of (unordered) pairs of lines:

$$\binom{7}{2} = 21.$$

**Key observation.** Each pair of distinct lines is in one of two states:
- **Parallel**: the pair shares no common point, or
- **Intersecting**: the pair meets in exactly one point.

Crucially, a given pair of lines can be "charged" to **at most one** intersectio
n point (since two distinct non-parallel lines meet in exactly one point). Hence
, if we let $t_k$ denote the number of points at which **exactly** $k$ of the se
ven lines meet, then every point counted by $t_k$ accounts for exactly $\binom{k
}{2}$ pairs of lines, and these sets of pairs are **disjoint** across all inters
ection points. Therefore

$$\sum_{k \geq 2} \binom{k}{2}\, t_k \;\le\; 21, \tag{1}$$

since the left-hand side counts (a subset of) all intersecting pairs, and there
are at most 21 pairs in total.

**Applying the hypothesis.** We are told that
$$t_3 \geq 6 \qquad\text{and}\qquad t_2 \geq 4.$$

The six (or more) triple points alone account for at least
$$\binom{3}{2}\cdot t_3 \;\ge\; 3 \cdot 6 = 18$$
pairs of lines. The four (or more) double points alone account for at least
$$\binom{2}{2}\cdot t_2 \;\ge\; 1 \cdot 4 = 4$$
pairs of lines. Since a triple point and a double point are distinct points, and
 since any pair of lines determines at most one intersection point, the pairs ch
arged to the triple points and the pairs charged to the double points are **disj
oint**. Hence the total number of intersecting pairs is at least

$$18 + 4 = 22.$$

But this contradicts inequality (1), which requires at most 21 pairs.

**Conclusion.** The assumption that such a configuration exists forces at least
22 distinct pairs of lines to be intersecting, yet only 21 pairs of lines exist
in total. This is impossible. $\blacksquare$
