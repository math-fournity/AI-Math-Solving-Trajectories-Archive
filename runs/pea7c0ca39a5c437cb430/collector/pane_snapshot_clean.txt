## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, such a map exists.

### Construction

**Step 1: Linear rescaling.** Define $T: [0,10]^2 \to [0,2\pi]\times[-1,1]$ by
$$T(u,v) = \left(\frac{\pi u}{5},\;\frac{v}{5}-1\right).$$
This is a linear bijection with Jacobian determinant $|\det DT| = \frac{\pi}{5}\
cdot\frac{1}{5} = \frac{\pi}{25}$.

**Step 2: Lambert cylindrical equal-area projection.** Define $L: [0,2\pi]\times
[-1,1] \to S^2$ by
$$L(\theta, z) = \left(\sqrt{1-z^2}\cos\theta,\;\sqrt{1-z^2}\sin\theta,\;z\right
).$$

**Step 3: Compose and extend.** Set $\phi = L \circ T$ on $[0,10]^2$, and extend
 $\phi$ to all of $\mathbb{R}^2$ by mapping $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus [0,10]^2$ to
 any fixed point on $S^2$ (say the south pole). The condition only concerns Bore
l sets in $[0,10]^2$, so the extension is irrelevant.

### Jacobian Computation

The partial derivatives of $L$ are:
$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta} = \left(-\sqrt{1-z^2}\sin\theta,\;\sqrt{1-z^
2}\cos\theta,\;0\right),\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \left(\frac{-z\co
s\theta}{\sqrt{1-z^2}},\;\frac{-z\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1-z^2}},\;1\right).$$

Their cross product is $(\sqrt{1-z^2}\cos\theta,\;\sqrt{1-z^2}\sin\theta,\;z)$,
with magnitude
$$\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta}\times\frac{\partial L}{\partial z}\ri
ght\| = \sqrt{(1-z^2)\cos^2\theta + (1-z^2)\sin^2\theta + z^2} = \sqrt{1-z^2+z^2
} = 1.$$

So the Jacobian of $L$ is $J_L = 1$ (this is why it is called *equal-area*). By
the chain rule, the Jacobian of $\phi = L\circ T$ is
$$J_\phi = J_L \cdot |\det DT| = 1 \cdot \frac{\pi}{25} = \frac{\pi}{25},$$
which is **constant** on $(0,10)^2$.

### Injectivity on the Interior

The map $T$ is a bijection from $(0,10)^2$ onto $(0,2\pi)\times(-1,1)$. The Lamb
ert projection $L$ is injective on $(0,2\pi)\times(-1,1)$: given $(\sqrt{1-z^2}\
cos\theta, \sqrt{1-z^2}\sin\theta, z)$ with $z\in(-1,1)$ and $\theta\in(0,2\pi)$
, we recover $z$ from the third coordinate, then $\theta$ from the first two (si
nce $\sqrt{1-z^2}>0$ and $\theta\in(0,2\pi)$ is determined by $(\cos\theta,\sin\
theta)$). Therefore $\phi$ is injective on $(0,10)^2$.

### Boundary Analysis

The boundary $\partial([0,10]^2) = \{0,10\}\times[0,10] \cup [0,10]\times\{0,10\
}$ has Lebesgue measure $0$. Its image under $\phi$ is:
- $u=0$ and $u=10$: both map to the meridian $\theta=0$ (same as $\theta=2\pi$),
 a 1-dimensional curve.
- $v=0$: maps to the south pole ($z=-1$), a single point.
- $v=10$: maps to the north pole ($z=1$), a single point.

Thus $\phi(\partial([0,10]^2))$ is contained in the union of one meridian and tw
o points, which has $\sigma$-measure $0$.

### Verification of the Measure Condition

Let $A \in \mathcal{B}([0,10]^2)$ be any Borel set. Decompose:
$$A_0 = A \cap (0,10)^2, \qquad A_1 = A \cap \partial([0,10]^2).$$

**Boundary part:** $|A_1| = 0$ and $\phi(A_1) \subseteq \phi(\partial([0,10]^2))
$, so $\sigma(\phi(A_1)) = 0$.

**Interior part:** Since $\phi(A_0) \subseteq \phi(A) \subseteq \phi(A_0) \cup \
phi(A_1)$ and $\sigma(\phi(A_1))=0$:
$$\sigma(\phi(A_0)) \leq \sigma(\phi(A)) \leq \sigma(\phi(A_0)) + \sigma(\phi(A_
1)) = \sigma(\phi(A_0)),$$
so $\sigma(\phi(A)) = \sigma(\phi(A_0))$.

**Applying the area formula:** Since $\phi$ is smooth and injective on $(0,10)^2
$ with constant Jacobian $J_\phi = \frac{\pi}{25}$, the area formula gives:
$$\sigma(\phi(A_0)) = \int_{A_0} J_\phi \, d\lambda = \frac{\pi}{25}\,|A_0|.$$

Since $|A_0| = |A| - |A_1| = |A|$, we conclude:
$$\boxed{\sigma(\phi(A)) = \frac{\pi}{25}\,|A|}$$
for every Borel set $A \subseteq [0,10]^2$, with the constant $c = \frac{\pi}{25
}$ independent of $A$.

### Consistency Check

The total measure: $\sigma(\phi([0,10]^2)) = \frac{\pi}{25}\cdot 100 = 4\pi = \s
igma(S^2)$. The image covers all of $S^2$ (up to a $\sigma$-null set), which is
consistent with the Lambert projection being surjective onto $S^2$.
