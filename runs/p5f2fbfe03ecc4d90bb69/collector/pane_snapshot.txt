**Answer: No, it does not follow that $L = M$.** There exist strictly larger σ-a
lgebras $M \supsetneq L$ carrying a measure that extends Lebesgue measure and he
nce agrees with length on every closed interval.

---

### Step 1. The hypothesis forces $\mu$ to extend Lebesgue measure on all of $L$
.

Since $M \supseteq L \supseteq \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ and $\mu([a,b]) = b-a$ f
or every closed interval, the restriction $\mu\!\restriction_{\mathcal{B}(\mathb
b{R})}$ is a Borel measure agreeing with length on the generating π-system of cl
osed intervals. By the uniqueness part of the Carathéodory extension theorem (or
 Dynkin's π-λ theorem),

$$\mu\!\restriction_{\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})} = m\!\restriction_{\mathcal{B}(\ma
thbb{R})},$$

where $m$ is Lebesgue measure.

Now take any $E \in L$. By definition of the Lebesgue σ-algebra as the completio
n of the Borel σ-algebra, $E = B \cup N$ with $B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$, $
N \subseteq Z$, $Z \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$, and $m(Z) = 0$. Then $\mu(Z) =
m(Z) = 0$, so $0 \le \mu(N) \le \mu(Z) = 0$, i.e. $\mu(N) = 0$. Therefore

$$\mu(E) = \mu(B) + \mu(N) = m(B) + 0 = m(E).$$

Hence $\mu\!\restriction_L = m$. The hypothesis is thus precisely: *Lebesgue mea
sure $m$ on $L$ has been extended to a measure on some σ-algebra $M \supseteq L$
.*

### Step 2. Lebesgue measure admits strict extensions (Łoś–Marczewski).

The relevant tool is the **Łoś–Marczewski extension theorem**:

> Let $(X, \mathcal{F}, \mu)$ be a measure space and let $E \subseteq X$ with $E
 \notin \mathcal{F}$. Then $\mu$ can be extended to a measure on $\sigma(\mathca
l{F} \cup \{E\})$. More precisely, for every $t \in [\mu_*(E),\, \mu^*(E)]$ ther
e is a (unique) measure $\tilde\mu$ on $\sigma(\mathcal{F} \cup \{E\})$ extendin
g $\mu$ with $\tilde\mu(E) = t$, where
> $$\mu_*(E) = \sup\{\mu(B) : B \in \mathcal{F},\, B \subseteq E\}, \qquad \mu^*
(E) = \inf\{\mu(A) : A \in \mathcal{F},\, E \subseteq A\}.$$

The interval $[\mu_*(E), \mu^*(E)]$ is **always nonempty**, since for any $B \su
bseteq E \subseteq A$ with $B, A \in \mathcal{F}$ we have $\mu(B) \le \mu(A)$, a
nd taking the supremum over $B$ and infimum over $A$ gives $\mu_*(E) \le \mu^*(E
)$. Lebesgue measure is σ-finite ($\mathbb{R} = \bigcup_n [-n,n]$), so the theor
em applies.

### Step 3. Applying it to a Vitali set.

Let $V \subset [0,1]$ be a **Vitali set**, i.e. a set of representatives of $\ma
thbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}$ in $[0,1]$. Then $V \notin L$. Concretely:

- **Inner measure $m_*(V) = 0$.** If some measurable $B \subseteq V$ had $m(B) >
 0$, then by Steinhaus's theorem $B - B$ would contain an open neighborhood of $
0$, hence some nonzero rational $q$. But $B \subseteq V$ and $V$ contains at mos
t one point from each $\mathbb{Q}$-coset, so $(B - B) \cap \mathbb{Q} = \{0\}$ —
 contradiction.
- **Outer measure $m^*(V) > 0$.** Otherwise $m^*(V) = 0$ would force $m^*(V + q)
 = 0$ for every rational $q$, and since $[0,1] \subseteq \bigcup_{q \in \mathbb{
Q} \cap [-1,1]} (V + q)$, we would get $m([0,1]) = 0$, contradiction.

In particular $m_*(V) < m^*(V)$ (equivalently, $V$ is not Lebesgue measurable),
and the interval $[m_*(V), m^*(V)] = [0, m^*(V)]$ is nonempty.

Set $M := \sigma(L \cup \{V\})$. By the Łoś–Marczewski theorem (with $t = 0$, sa
y), Lebesgue measure $m$ on $L$ extends to a measure $\mu$ on $M$ with $\mu(V) =
 0$. Since $V \in M \setminus L$, we have $M \supsetneq L$.

### Step 4. Verification and conclusion.

The extended measure $\mu$ satisfies $\mu\!\restriction_L = m$, so in particular

$$\mu([a,b]) = m([a,b]) = b - a \qquad \text{for all } [a,b] \subset \mathbb{R}.
$$

Yet $M = \sigma(L \cup \{V\}) \supsetneq L$. We have produced a σ-algebra strict
ly larger than $L$ together with a measure on it that agrees with length on ever
y closed interval. Therefore the implication "$L \subseteq M$ and such a $\mu$ e
xists $\Longrightarrow L = M$" is **false**.

---

**Remark on the codomain.** The notation "$\mu : M \to \mathbb{R}$" is read here
 in the standard measure-theoretic sense of a $[0,\infty]$-valued (or $\overline
{\mathbb{R}}$-valued) countably additive measure; taken literally as *finite* re
al-valued, the hypothesis would already be contradictory, since $\mu(\mathbb{R})
 \ge \mu([-n,n]) = 2n \to \infty$ whereas $\mathbb{R} \in M$ would require $\mu(
\mathbb{R}) < \infty$. Under the standard (and only non-vacuous) interpretation,
 the answer above applies: **No, $L = M$ does not follow.**
