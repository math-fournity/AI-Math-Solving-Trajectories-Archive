## Proof

Let $\mathcal{B}((0,1])$ denote the Borel $\sigma$-algebra on the interval $(0,1
]$, equipped with the subspace topology inherited from $\mathbb{R}$. We must sho
w that the set of irrational numbers in $(0,1]$,
$$
I := (0,1] \setminus \mathbb{Q} = \{x \in (0,1] : x \notin \mathbb{Q}\},
$$
belongs to $\mathcal{B}((0,1])$.

**Step 1: Singletons are Borel.**

For any $a \in (0,1]$, the singleton $\{a\}$ is closed in the subspace topology
of $(0,1]$ (every singleton in a Hausdorff space is closed). Since the Borel $\s
igma$-algebra contains all closed sets, $\{a\} \in \mathcal{B}((0,1])$.

**Step 2: The rationals in $(0,1]$ form a Borel set.**

The set $\mathbb{Q} \cap (0,1]$ is countable, so we may enumerate it as $\mathbb
{Q} \cap (0,1] = \{q_1, q_2, q_3, \dots\}$. Then
$$
\mathbb{Q} \cap (0,1] = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} \{q_n\}.
$$
Each $\{q_n\}$ is Borel by Step 1, and a $\sigma$-algebra is closed under counta
ble unions. Therefore
$$
\mathbb{Q} \cap (0,1] \in \mathcal{B}((0,1]).
$$

**Step 3: The irrationals in $(0,1]$ are Borel.**

The set of irrationals in $(0,1]$ is the complement of the rationals within $(0,
1]$:
$$
I = (0,1] \setminus (\mathbb{Q} \cap (0,1]).
$$
Since $\mathcal{B}((0,1])$ is a $\sigma$-algebra on $(0,1]$, it is closed under
complementation (relative to $(0,1]$). Having established that $\mathbb{Q} \cap
(0,1] \in \mathcal{B}((0,1])$ in Step 2, it follows immediately that
$$
I = (0,1] \setminus (\mathbb{Q} \cap (0,1]) \in \mathcal{B}((0,1]).
$$

**Conclusion.** The Borel $\sigma$-algebra on $(0,1]$ does contain the set of al
l irrational numbers in $(0,1]$. The key facts used are: (i) every singleton is
closed and hence Borel; (ii) the rationals are countable, so they form a countab
le union of singletons, which is Borel; (iii) a $\sigma$-algebra is closed under
 complementation, so the irrationals—being the complement of the rationals withi
n $(0,1]$—are Borel.
