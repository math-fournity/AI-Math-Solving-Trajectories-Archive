## Evaluation of $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^{2}x}{x^{2}}\,dx$ by
complex analysis

**Step 1. Reduce to a standard form.**

Using $\sin^{2}x=\tfrac{1-\cos 2x}{2}$,

$$
I:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^{2}x}{x^{2}}\,dx
=\frac12\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos 2x}{x^{2}}\,dx.
$$

Substitute $u=2x$ ($dx=du/2$, $x=u/2$):

$$
I=\frac12\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos u}{(u/2)^{2}}\,\frac{du}{2}
=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos u}{u^{2}}\,du.
$$

So it suffices to prove

$$
J:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos u}{u^{2}}\,du=\frac{\pi}{2}.
$$

**Step 2. Choose the contour and the integrand.**

Consider

$$
f(z)=\frac{1-e^{iz}}{z^{2}},
$$

which is meromorphic on $\mathbb{C}$ with a single singularity at $z=0$. Near $0
$,

$$
e^{iz}=1+iz-\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\cdots\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
1-e^{iz}=-iz+\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\cdots,
$$

hence

$$
f(z)=\frac{-i}{z}+\frac12+O(z),
$$

so $z=0$ is a **simple pole** with residue $\operatorname{Res}_{z=0}f=-i$.

Let $C$ be the closed contour in the upper half-plane consisting of (see figure
mentally):
- $\Gamma_1$: the real segment $[\varepsilon,R]$,
- $\Gamma_2=C_R$: the large semicircle $|z|=R$, $\operatorname{Im}z\ge 0$, trave
rsed counterclockwise,
- $\Gamma_3$: the real segment $[-R,-\varepsilon]$,
- $\Gamma_4=C_\varepsilon$: the small semicircle $|z|=\varepsilon$, $\operatorna
me{Im}z\ge 0$, traversed **clockwise** (so that $0$ is excluded from the interio
r).

**Step 3. Apply Cauchy's theorem.**

The only singularity $z=0$ lies **outside** the contour (it is skirted by $C_\va
repsilon$), and $f$ is holomorphic inside $C$. Therefore

$$
\oint_{C}f(z)\,dz=0.
$$

**Step 4. The large arc $C_R$ vanishes as $R\to\infty$.**

For $z=Re^{i\theta}$ with $0\le\theta\le\pi$, $\operatorname{Im}z\ge 0$, so $|e^
{iz}|=e^{-\operatorname{Im}z}\le 1$, giving $|1-e^{iz}|\le 2$. Hence

$$
\left|\int_{C_R}f(z)\,dz\right|\le\frac{2}{R^{2}}\cdot\pi R=\frac{2\pi}{R}\xrigh
tarrow[R\to\infty]{}0.
$$

**Step 5. The small arc $C_\varepsilon$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$.**

Parametrize $C_\varepsilon$: $z=\varepsilon e^{i\theta}$, $\theta$ from $\pi$ to
 $0$ (clockwise). Using the Laurent expansion,

$$
\int_{C_\varepsilon}f(z)\,dz
=\int_{C_\varepsilon}\frac{-i}{z}\,dz+\int_{C_\varepsilon}\!\left(\frac12+O(z)\r
ight)dz.
$$

The second integral is $O(\varepsilon)\to 0$. For the first,

$$
\int_{C_\varepsilon}\frac{-i}{z}\,dz
=-i\int_{\pi}^{0}i\,d\theta=-i\cdot(-i\pi)=-\pi.
$$

Thus

$$
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{C_\varepsilon}f(z)\,dz=-\pi.
$$

**Step 6. The real-axis pieces combine into $2J$.**

$$
\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}\frac{1-e^{ix}}{x^{2}}\,dx+\int_{-R}^{-\varepsilon}\frac{1
-e^{ix}}{x^{2}}\,dx.
$$

In the second integral put $x=-t$:

$$
\int_{-R}^{-\varepsilon}\frac{1-e^{ix}}{x^{2}}\,dx
=\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}\frac{1-e^{-it}}{t^{2}}\,dt.
$$

Adding the two pieces,

$$
\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}\frac{(1-e^{ix})+(1-e^{-ix})}{x^{2}}\,dx
=\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}\frac{2-2\cos x}{x^{2}}\,dx
=2\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}\frac{1-\cos x}{x^{2}}\,dx.
$$

**Step 7. Take limits.**

Letting $R\to\infty$ and $\varepsilon\to 0$ in $\oint_C f\,dz=0$,

$$
2J+0+(-\pi)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad J=\frac{\pi}{2}.
$$

**Step 8. Conclude.**

$$
\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^{2}x}{x^{2}}\,dx
=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos u}{u^{2}}\,du
=J=\boxed{\dfrac{\pi}{2}}.
$$
