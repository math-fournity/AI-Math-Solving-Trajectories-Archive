## Proof

**Setup.** The sequence consists of all numbers of the form $P_{i,r} = p_i p_{i+
1}\cdots p_{i+r-1}$ for $i\geq 1$, $r\geq 1$, where $p_j$ is the $j$-th prime. W
hen $r=1$, these are primes; when $r\geq 2$, these are square-free products of s
uccessive primes. By unique factorization, all $P_{i,r}$ are distinct (equal pro
ducts would force equal prime sets, hence $r=s$ and $i=j$). Since $\mu(P_{i,r})=
(-1)^r$, the series equals

$$S = \sum_{r=1}^{\infty}(-1)^r \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \frac{\ln(P_{i,r})}{P_{i,r}}
 = \sum_{r=1}^{\infty} (-1)^r A_r, \qquad A_r := \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\ln(P_
{i,r})}{P_{i,r}}.$$

**Step 1: $A_1$ diverges.** $A_1 = \sum_{i=1}^\infty \frac{\ln p_i}{p_i}$. By Me
rtens' theorem (a consequence of the Prime Number Theorem),

$$\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\ln p}{p} = \ln x + O(1),$$

so $A_1 = +\infty$.

**Step 2: $A_r$ is finite for every $r\geq 2$, and $\sum_{r=2}^\infty |A_r|<\inf
ty$.**

Since $P_{i,r}\geq p_i^r$ and $\ln(P_{i,r})\leq r\ln p_{i+r-1}$, we have $\frac{
\ln(P_{i,r})}{P_{i,r}}\leq \frac{r\ln p_{i+r-1}}{p_i^r}$. By Bertrand's postulat
e, $p_{i+r-1}<2^{r-1}p_i$, so $\ln p_{i+r-1}<\ln p_i+(r-1)\ln 2$. Thus

$$A_r \leq r\sum_{i=1}^\infty \frac{\ln p_i+(r-1)\ln 2}{p_i^r}.$$

For $r\geq 3$ and $i\geq 2$ (so $p_i\geq 3$): $\frac{1}{p_i^r}\leq\frac{1}{p_i^3
}\cdot\frac{1}{3^{r-3}}$, giving

$$\sum_{i=2}^\infty \frac{r\ln p_i}{p_i^r}\leq \frac{r\cdot 3^3}{3^r}\sum_{i=2}^
\infty\frac{\ln p_i}{p_i^3}, \qquad \sum_{i=2}^\infty\frac{r(r-1)\ln 2}{p_i^r}\l
eq\frac{r(r-1)\ln 2\cdot 3^3}{3^r}\sum_{i=2}^\infty\frac{1}{p_i^3}.$$

Both sums on the right converge. For $i=1$: $P_{1,r}=p_r\#$ (the $r$-th primoria
l), and by PNT $\ln(p_r\#)\sim p_r$ while $p_r\#\sim e^{p_r}$, so $\frac{\ln(p_r
\#)}{p_r\#}\sim\frac{p_r}{e^{p_r}}\to 0$ super-exponentially.

Combining: for $r\geq 3$, $A_r = O\!\left(\frac{r^2}{3^r}\right)+O\!\left(\frac{
p_r}{e^{p_r}}\right)$, so $|A_r|$ decays exponentially. For $r=2$, $A_2\leq 2\su
m_i\frac{\ln p_i+\ln 2}{p_i^2}<\infty$ since $\sum\frac{\ln p_i}{p_i^2}$ converg
es. Therefore $\sum_{r=2}^\infty|A_r|<\infty$.

**Step 3: Partial sums diverge to $-\infty$.**

Define $S(x)=\sum_{N_k\leq x}\frac{\mu(N_k)}{N_k}\ln(N_k)$, the partial sum over
 all sequence elements $\leq x$. Then

$$S(x) = -\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\ln p}{p} + \sum_{r=2}^\infty (-1)^r A_r(x), \qqua
d A_r(x):=\sum_{P_{i,r}\leq x}\frac{\ln(P_{i,r})}{P_{i,r}}.$$

For each $r\geq 2$, $A_r(x)\leq A_r$ and $A_r(x)\to A_r$ as $x\to\infty$. Since
$\sum_{r=2}^\infty|A_r|<\infty$, by dominated convergence $\sum_{r=2}^\infty(-1)
^r A_r(x)\to \sum_{r=2}^\infty(-1)^r A_r =: C$ (a finite constant).

By Mertens' theorem, $\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\ln p}{p}=\ln x+O(1)$. Therefore

$$S(x) = -\ln x + O(1) + C + o(1) \;\longrightarrow\; -\infty \quad\text{as }x\t
o\infty.$$

Since the sequence $(N_k)$ is in increasing order, the $K$-th partial sum equals
 $S(N_K)$, and $N_K\to\infty$ as $K\to\infty$. Hence

$$\sum_{k=1}^K \frac{\mu(N_k)}{N_k}\ln(N_k) = S(N_K) \longrightarrow -\infty.$$

**Conclusion.** The series diverges (negatively) to $-\infty$. The primes contri
bute $-\sum_p\frac{\ln p}{p}\sim -\ln x\to-\infty$, while all products of two or
 more successive primes contribute only a bounded (indeed absolutely convergent)
 correction.
