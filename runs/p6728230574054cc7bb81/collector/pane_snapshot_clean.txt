## Proof

**Claim:** $x = \frac{1}{1+ia}$ belongs to the connected component of $G(A)$ con
taining $1$.

**Step 1: $x$ is well-defined and invertible.**

Since $a$ is self-adjoint ($a = a^*$), its spectrum satisfies $\sigma(a) \subset
eq \mathbb{R}$. By the spectral mapping theorem (applied via the continuous func
tional calculus, or directly: $\sigma(ia) = i\,\sigma(a)$ for the polynomial $z
\mapsto iz$), we have
$$\sigma(ia) = i\,\sigma(a) \subseteq i\mathbb{R}.$$
Hence
$$\sigma(1+ia) = 1 + \sigma(ia) \subseteq 1 + i\mathbb{R} = \{1+it : t \in \math
bb{R}\}.$$
Since $0 \notin 1 + i\mathbb{R}$ (the real part is always $1$), the element $1+i
a$ is invertible. Thus $x = (1+ia)^{-1}$ is a well-defined element of $G(A)$.

**Step 2: A continuous path in $G(A)$ from $1$ to $x$.**

Define, for $t \in [0,1]$,
$$\gamma(t) = \frac{1}{1 + t\,ia} = (1 + t\,ia)^{-1}.$$
We have $\gamma(0) = 1^{-1} = 1$ and $\gamma(1) = (1+ia)^{-1} = x$.

We must verify $\gamma(t) \in G(A)$ for every $t \in [0,1]$. Since $a$ is self-a
djoint, $t\,a$ is self-adjoint for real $t$, so $\sigma(ta) \subseteq \mathbb{R}
$ and $\sigma(t\,ia) = i\,\sigma(ta) \subseteq i\mathbb{R}$. Therefore
$$\sigma(1 + t\,ia) = 1 + \sigma(t\,ia) \subseteq 1 + i\mathbb{R},$$
which again excludes $0$. Hence $1 + t\,ia$ is invertible for every $t \in [0,1]
$, so $\gamma(t) \in G(A)$ throughout.

The map $t \mapsto 1 + t\,ia$ is continuous (norm-continuous, being affine in $t
$), and inversion $G(A) \to G(A)$, $y \mapsto y^{-1}$, is continuous in any unit
al Banach algebra. Therefore $\gamma : [0,1] \to G(A)$ is continuous.

**Conclusion.**

$\gamma$ is a continuous path in $G(A)$ joining $1 = \gamma(0)$ to $x = \gamma(1
)$. Hence $x$ lies in the connected component of $G(A)$ containing the identity.

(Remark: the hypothesis that $a \neq 0$ is not needed; the argument works for ev
ery self-adjoint $a$, including $a = 0$, in which case $x = 1$ trivially.)
