## Answer

### Setup and definitions

Let $G$ be a Lie group and define the **derived series** (chain of commutants) b
y
$$Q_0 = G, \qquad Q_{i+1} = [Q_i, Q_i] \quad (i \ge 0),$$
where $[Q_i,Q_i]$ denotes the commutator subgroup (the subgroup generated by all
 $xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$ with $x,y\in Q_i$). This gives a descending chain
$$G = Q_0 \supseteq Q_1 \supseteq Q_2 \supseteq \cdots$$
of closed, connected (when $G$ is connected) normal subgroups, each normal in th
e preceding one. At the Lie-algebra level the corresponding series is
$$\mathfrak{g}^{(0)}=\mathfrak{g},\qquad \mathfrak{g}^{(i+1)}=[\mathfrak{g}^{(i)
},\mathfrak{g}^{(i)}],$$
and for a connected Lie group one has $\operatorname{Lie}(Q_i)=\mathfrak{g}^{(i)
}$.

### What "termination" means

The chain **terminates** (stabilizes) if there exists an integer $n\ge 0$ such t
hat
$$Q_{n+1}=Q_n,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad [Q_n,Q_n]=Q_n.$$
From that point on the chain is constant: $Q_n=Q_{n+1}=Q_{n+2}=\cdots$. There ar
e exactly two qualitative outcomes:

1. **Termination at the identity:** $Q_n=\{e\}$ for some $n$. Then $Q_{n-1}$ is
abelian (since its commutator is trivial), $Q_{n-2}$ is solvable of derived leng
th $2$, and inductively $G=Q_0$ is solvable of derived length $\le n$.

2. **Termination at a non-trivial perfect subgroup:** $Q_n\neq\{e\}$ but $[Q_n,Q
_n]=Q_n$. Then $Q_n$ is a **perfect** Lie group (it equals its own commutator su
bgroup), and $G$ is **not** solvable. The subgroup $Q_n$ is the maximal perfect
subgroup reachable through the derived series — sometimes called the *perfect co
re* of the derived series.

In either case, stabilization is equivalent to $Q_n$ being perfect (the trivial
group is vacuously perfect), so termination is precisely the condition that the
derived series eventually reaches a perfect group.

### Significance for solvable Lie groups

**Definition (solvability).** A Lie group $G$ is **solvable** if its derived ser
ies terminates at the identity in finitely many steps:
$$\exists\, n\ge 0:\quad Q_n = \{e\}.$$
The smallest such $n$ is the **derived length** of $G$. Thus:

> *Termination of the chain of commutants at $\{e\}$ **is** the defining propert
y of a solvable Lie group.*

The significance is threefold:

**(a) Equivalence with Lie-algebra solvability.** For a connected Lie group $G$
with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$, the group is solvable if and only if $\mathfrak
{g}$ is solvable as a Lie algebra, i.e. $\mathfrak{g}^{(n)}=0$ for some $n$. Thi
s follows because $\operatorname{Lie}(Q_i)=\mathfrak{g}^{(i)}$ and a connected L
ie group is trivial iff its Lie algebra is $0$. Hence solvability can be checked
 purely at the infinitesimal level — a finite-dimensional linear-algebra computa
tion — rather than at the global group level.

**(b) Structural decomposition.** If the chain terminates at a non-trivial perfe
ct $Q_n$, then $G$ is not solvable, but the derived series still reveals the str
ucture: $Q_n$ is semisimple (perfect, and for connected compact groups, a produc
t of simple factors), and $G$ fits into an extension of $Q_n$ by the solvable "l
ayers" $Q_0/Q_1, Q_1/Q_2, \dots, Q_{n-1}/Q_n$, each of which is abelian. In part
icular, the **solvable radical** $\operatorname{Rad}(G)$ (the largest connected
solvable normal subgroup) captures the solvable part, and $G/\operatorname{Rad}(
G)$ is semisimple — this is the Levi decomposition. Termination of the derived s
eries at $\{e\}$ is exactly the statement $\operatorname{Rad}(G)=G$, i.e. $G$ is
 entirely solvable.

**(c) Triangulability and Lie's theorem.** For connected, simply-connected solva
ble Lie groups over $\mathbb{C}$ (and under mild hypotheses over $\mathbb{R}$),
Lie's theorem guarantees that every finite-dimensional representation is simulta
neously upper-triangularizable. This fundamental consequence — that solvable Lie
 groups behave like iterated extensions of abelian groups — is precisely what th
e terminating derived series expresses: the group is built up in finitely many l
ayers, each abelian, and this finite abelian tower is what makes the representat
ion theory tractable.

### Conclusion

The chain of commutants terminates when it reaches a perfect subgroup $Q_n=[Q_n,
Q_n]$. When that perfect subgroup is the trivial group $\{e\}$, the group $G$ is
 **solvable** — this is the very definition of solvability, equivalent to the so
lvability of the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$, and it underlies the Levi decomposi
tion and Lie's theorem. When the chain stabilizes at a non-trivial perfect subgr
oup, $G$ is not solvable, and the stable subgroup represents the semisimple (per
fect) core of the group, with the preceding layers forming the solvable part.
