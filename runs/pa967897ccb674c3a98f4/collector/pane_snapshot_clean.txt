## Counterexample

Take $f(t) = |t|^{-\alpha}$ for a fixed $\alpha$ with $0 < \alpha < 1$ (e.g., $\
alpha = \tfrac{1}{2}$). This is a locally integrable, tempered function, so its
Fourier transform is well-defined as a tempered distribution (and in fact as a f
unction away from the origin).

### Verification of the hypotheses

**Fourier transform.** For $\xi \ne 0$, using the standard integral $\int_0^\inf
ty t^{-\alpha}\cos(bt)\,dt = \Gamma(1-\alpha)\sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{\pi\alpha}{2}\bi
gr)\,b^{\alpha-1}$ (valid for $0<\alpha<1$, $b>0$):

$$\hat f(\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|t|^{-\alpha}e^{-2\pi i t\xi}\,dt = 2\int
_0^\infty t^{-\alpha}\cos(2\pi t\xi)\,dt = C_\alpha\,|\xi|^{\alpha-1},$$

where $C_\alpha = 2\,\Gamma(1-\alpha)\sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{\pi\alpha}{2}\bigr)(2\pi
)^{\alpha-1} > 0$, since $\Gamma(1-\alpha)>0$ and $\sin(\pi\alpha/2)>0$ for $0<\
alpha<1$.

At $\xi=0$, $\hat f(0)=\int|t|^{-\alpha}dt=+\infty$, consistent with $|\xi|^{\al
pha-1}\to+\infty$.

**Non-negativity.** $\hat f(\xi) = C_\alpha|\xi|^{\alpha-1}\ge 0$ for all $\xi$
(with $+\infty$ at the origin). ✓

**Divergent integral.** $\displaystyle\int\hat f(\xi)\,d\xi = C_\alpha\cdot 2\in
t_0^\infty \xi^{\alpha-1}\,d\xi = +\infty$, since $\alpha>0$ makes the integral
diverge at $+\infty$. ✓

### Failure of the conclusion

Fix any $M > 0$. The set in question is

$$\{t\in[-\epsilon,\epsilon]: f(t)>M\} = \{t\in[-\epsilon,\epsilon]: |t|^{-\alph
a}>M\} = \{t: |t|<M^{-1/\alpha}\}\cap[-\epsilon,\epsilon].$$

For all sufficiently small $\epsilon$, namely $\epsilon < M^{-1/\alpha}$, this s
et is the **entire** interval $[-\epsilon,\epsilon]$. Hence

$$\frac{\mu\bigl(\{t\in[-\epsilon,\epsilon]:f(t)>M\}\bigr)}{2\epsilon} = \frac{2
\epsilon}{2\epsilon} = 1 \quad\text{for all } \epsilon < M^{-1/\alpha}.$$

Therefore

$$\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^+}\frac{\mu\bigl(\{t\in[-\epsilon,\epsilon]:f(t)>M\}\bigr)
}{2\epsilon} = 1 \ne 0.$$

This holds for **every** $M>0$, so the "essential boundedness at zero" condition
 fails completely.

### Conclusion

The implication is **false**. The function $f(t)=|t|^{-\alpha}$ ($0<\alpha<1$) s
atisfies $\hat f\ge 0$ and $\int\hat f\,ds=+\infty$, yet for every $M>0$ the den
sity of $\{f>M\}$ near the origin equals $1$, not $0$. The divergence of $\int\h
at f$ reflects a genuine singularity of $f$ at the origin that occupies full mea
sure in every sufficiently small neighborhood.
