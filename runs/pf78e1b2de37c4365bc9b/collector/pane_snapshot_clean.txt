**Proof.**

Since $f_n \to f$ in $L^2[a,b]$, convergence in $L^2$ implies convergence in mea
sure. By the Riesz–F. Riesz theorem (every sequence converging in measure has a
subsequence converging almost everywhere), there exists a subsequence $\{f_{n_k}
\}$ such that

$$f_{n_k}(x) \to f(x) \quad \text{for a.e. } x \in [a,b]. \tag{1}$$

On the other hand, by hypothesis $f_n \to g$ pointwise on $[a,b]$, so in particu
lar the subsequence satisfies

$$f_{n_k}(x) \to g(x) \quad \text{for every } x \in [a,b]. \tag{2}$$

Combining (1) and (2), for almost every $x \in [a,b]$ both limits hold, and uniq
ueness of pointwise limits gives

$$f(x) = g(x) \quad \text{for a.e. } x \in [a,b].$$

Thus $f - g = 0$ a.e. on $[a,b]$. Since $f$ and $g$ are both continuous on $[a,b
]$, the function $h = f - g$ is continuous and vanishes a.e. A continuous functi
on that vanishes on a dense set (and a.e. implies dense, since the complement of
 a null set is dense) vanishes everywhere. Therefore

$$f(x) = g(x) \quad \text{for all } x \in [a,b].$$

Hence $f = g$. $\blacksquare$
