Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether "every subsequence fails to
 converge" implies the range set is discrete, in a general topological space.

## Answer: No.

The implication does **not** hold in general topological spaces. Here is a count
erexample.

### Construction

Let $\mathcal{U}$ be a free ultrafilter on $\mathbb{N}$. Define $X = \mathbb{N}
\cup \{p\}$ (where $p \notin \mathbb{N}$) with the topology:
- Every subset of $\mathbb{N}$ is open (points of $\mathbb{N}$ are isolated).
- A set containing $p$ is open if and only if it has the form $\{p\} \cup A$ wit
h $A \in \mathcal{U}$.

**This is a valid topology:** Finite intersections of $p$-neighborhoods give $\{
p\} \cup (A \cap B)$ with $A \cap B \in \mathcal{U}$ (filter property); arbitrar
y unions and intersections with subsets of $\mathbb{N}$ are handled since $\math
cal{U}$ is upward-closed and every subset of $\mathbb{N}$ is open.

**The space is $T_1$:** $\{p\}$ is closed since $\mathbb{N}$ is open; $\{n\}$ is
 closed for each $n$ since $\mathbb{N} \setminus \{n\}$ is cofinite, hence in $\
mathcal{U}$ (free ultrafilters contain all cofinite sets), making $(\mathbb{N}\s
etminus\{n\})\cup\{p\}$ open.

### The sequence

Define $x_1 = p$ and $x_n = n-1$ for $n \geq 2$. The range is $S = \{x_n : n \in
 \mathbb{N}\} = \{p\} \cup \mathbb{N} = X$.

### $S$ is not discrete

The point $p$ is not isolated: $\{p\}$ is not open (since $\emptyset \notin \mat
hcal{U}$). Every open neighborhood of $p$ is $\{p\} \cup A$ with $A \in \mathcal
{U}$, $A \neq \emptyset$, so it contains points of $\mathbb{N}$.

### No subsequence converges

Let $(x_{n_k})$ be any subsequence.

**Case 1: The subsequence excludes $p$.** Then it is an injective sequence in $\
mathbb{N}$ with infinite range $B \subseteq \mathbb{N}$.

- *Convergence to $p$?* By the ultrafilter property, either $B \in \mathcal{U}$
or $\mathbb{N}\setminus B \in \mathcal{U}$.
  - If $\mathbb{N}\setminus B \in \mathcal{U}$: all terms lie in $B$, so the sub
sequence is **never** in $\mathbb{N}\setminus B$ — not eventually in this neighb
orhood of $p$.
  - If $B \in \mathcal{U}$: partition $B = B_1 \sqcup B_2$ into two infinite set
s. By the ultrafilter property, $B_1 \in \mathcal{U}$ or $B_2 \in \mathcal{U}$.
If $B_1 \in \mathcal{U}$, then infinitely many terms lie in $B_2 \subseteq \math
bb{N}\setminus B_1$, so the subsequence is not eventually in $B_1$. Symmetricall
y for $B_2$.

  In all cases, the subsequence does not converge to $p$.

- *Convergence to some $m \in \mathbb{N}$?* Since $m$ is isolated, $\{m\}$ is op
en, and convergence to $m$ requires the subsequence to be eventually in $\{m\}$,
 i.e., eventually constant at $m$. But the subsequence is injective, so this nev
er happens.

**Case 2: The subsequence includes $p$ (i.e., $n_1 = 1$).** The tail $(x_{n_k})_
{k \geq 2}$ is an injective sequence in $\mathbb{N}$ with infinite range $B$. Th
e identical argument as Case 1 shows the tail does not converge to $p$ or to any
 isolated point. Adding the single initial term $p$ does not affect convergence
(convergence depends only on the tail).

### Conclusion

Every subsequence of $(x_n)$ fails to converge, yet $S = \{x_n : n \in \mathbb{N
}\}$ is **not** discrete. Therefore, the implication is **false** in general top
ological spaces. $\square$

### Remark

In **first-countable** spaces (in particular, all metric spaces), the implicatio
n *does* hold: if $x_k$ is a non-isolated point of $S$, a countable neighborhood
 base $U_1 \supseteq U_2 \supseteq \cdots$ at $x_k$ allows selecting $x_{n_j} \i
n U_j \cap (S \setminus \{x_k\})$ with strictly increasing indices, yielding a s
ubsequence converging to $x_k$. The counterexample exploits the failure of first
 countability — the free ultrafilter topology at $p$ has no countable base.
