## Proof

**Definition (S-small).** A set $E \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is *S-small* (has Lebes
gue measure zero) if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists a countable collec
tion of open intervals $\{I_n\}$ such that
$$E \subseteq \bigcup_{n} I_n \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_{n} |I_n| < \varepsilo
n.$$

**Claim.** There exists an uncountable S-small subset of $\mathbb{R}$.

**Construction (the Cantor middle-thirds set).** Define a nested sequence of clo
sed sets $C_0 \supseteq C_1 \supseteq C_2 \supseteq \cdots$ as follows. Set $C_0
 = [0,1]$. Given $C_{n-1}$, which is a union of $2^{n-1}$ closed intervals each
of length $3^{-(n-1)}$, obtain $C_n$ by deleting the open middle third of each o
f those intervals. Thus $C_n$ is a union of $2^n$ closed intervals each of lengt
h $3^{-n}$. The **Cantor set** is
$$C = \bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty} C_n.$$

---

**Step 1: $C$ is S-small (measure zero).**

For each $n$, the set $C_n$ (and hence $C$) is covered by $2^n$ intervals of len
gth $3^{-n}$, with total length
$$L_n = 2^n \cdot 3^{-n} = \left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right)^n.$$
Given any $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $n$ large enough that $(2/3)^n < \varepsilon
$. Enlarging each of the $2^n$ constituent closed intervals of $C_n$ slightly to
 an open interval (adding, say, $\varepsilon' / 2^n$ to each, for a tiny $\varep
silon'$), we obtain a countable open cover of $C$ with total length at most $(2/
3)^n + \varepsilon' < \varepsilon$ (choosing $n$ and $\varepsilon'$ appropriatel
y). Hence $C$ is S-small. $\square$

---

**Step 2: $C$ is uncountable.**

Every $x \in [0,1]$ admits a ternary (base-3) expansion
$$x = 0.a_1 a_2 a_3 \cdots \quad (a_i \in \{0,1,2\}).$$
A point $x$ belongs to $C$ if and only if it has a ternary expansion using only
the digits $0$ and $2$ (the digit $1$ corresponds to falling into a deleted midd
le third at some stage). Define the map
$$\varphi : C \longrightarrow [0,1], \qquad \varphi\!\left(0.a_1 a_2 a_3 \cdots\
right)_{\text{base }3} = 0.b_1 b_2 b_3 \cdots{}_{\text{base }2}, \quad b_i = a_i
 / 2.$$
That is, replace each ternary digit $a_i \in \{0,2\}$ by the binary digit $a_i/2
 \in \{0,1\}$, and read the result as a binary expansion. This map $\varphi$ is
a **surjection** from $C$ onto $[0,1]$: given any binary expansion $0.b_1 b_2 \c
dots$, the number with ternary expansion $0.(2b_1)(2b_2)\cdots$ lies in $C$ and
maps to it.

Since $\varphi: C \to [0,1]$ is surjective and $[0,1]$ is uncountable, $C$ is un
countable. $\square$

---

**Conclusion.** The Cantor set $C \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is simultaneously uncoun
table and S-small (it has Lebesgue measure zero, being coverable by intervals of
 arbitrarily small total length). Therefore, **yes**, uncountable S-small subset
s of the real line exist.
