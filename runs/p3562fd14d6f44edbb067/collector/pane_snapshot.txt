Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether, for any $N \geq 1$, there
exists $f(N)$ such that for all $x \geq f(N)$, the integers $x+1, \ldots, x+N$ a
re multiplicatively independent (no nontrivial integer-exponent product equals 1
).

## Proof

The answer is **YES**.

**Setup.** Let $N \geq 1$ and let $\mathcal{P} = \{p \text{ prime} : p \leq N-1\
}$ (empty when $N=1$). For each $i \in \{1,\ldots,N\}$ and $x \geq 1$, decompose
 $x+i = y_i \cdot s_i$, where $s_i = \prod_{p \in \mathcal{P}} p^{v_p(x+i)}$ is
the "$\mathcal{P}$-part" and $y_i = (x+i)/s_i$ is the "$\mathcal{P}$-free part"
(all prime factors of $y_i$ are $\geq N$). Note that $y_i = 1$ exactly when $x+i
$ is $(N-1)$-smooth.

**Key observation (private primes).** If a prime $p \geq N$ divides both $x+i$ a
nd $x+j$ with $i \neq j$, then $p \mid (j-i)$, so $p \leq |j-i| \leq N-1 < p$, a
 contradiction. Therefore the $y_i$ are **pairwise coprime**, and every prime $p
 \geq N$ dividing some $x+i$ divides no other $x+j$.

**Lemma.** *If at most one $y_i$ equals $1$, then $x+1, \ldots, x+N$ are multipl
icatively independent.*

*Proof.* Suppose $\prod_{i=1}^N (x+i)^{a_i} = 1$ with $a_i \in \mathbb{Z}$, i.e.
, $\prod y_i^{a_i} \cdot \prod s_i^{a_i} = 1$. For any $j$ with $y_j > 1$, pick
a prime $p \mid y_j$. Then $p \geq N$, $p$ divides no other $y_k$ (pairwise copr
imality), and $p$ divides no $s_k$ (those involve only primes $< N$). So the $p$
-adic valuation of the entire product is $a_j \cdot v_p(y_j)$, which must equal
$0$, giving $a_j = 0$. This holds for every $j$ with $y_j > 1$. Since at most on
e $y_i = 1$, we obtain $a_j = 0$ for all $j \neq i$ (or all $j$ if none equals $
1$). If some $y_i = 1$, the relation reduces to $(x+i)^{a_i} = 1$, and since $x+
i \geq 2$, we get $a_i = 0$. $\square$

**Bounding the exceptional set.** It remains to show that for sufficiently large
 $x$, at most one of $x+1, \ldots, x+N$ is $(N-1)$-smooth. Two of them are both
$(N-1)$-smooth only if there exist $i < j$ with $d = j - i \in \{1, \ldots, N-1\
}$ such that both $x+i$ and $x+j$ are $(N-1)$-smooth, i.e., we need pairs of $(N
-1)$-smooth numbers differing by $d$.

For each fixed $d$, consider the equation $u - v = d$ with $u, v$ both $(N-1)$-s
mooth. Set $S = \mathcal{P} \cup \{p \text{ prime} : p \mid d\}$. Then $u/d$ and
 $-v/d$ are $S$-units, and the equation becomes $u/d + (-v/d) = 1$, an $S$-unit
equation. By the **$S$-unit theorem** (Evertse, 1984; equivalently, a consequenc
e of the Schmidt Subspace Theorem), for any finite set of primes $S$ and any non
zero constant $c$, the equation $x + y = c$ has **finitely many** solutions in $
S$-units. Therefore, for each $d$, there are finitely many pairs of $(N-1)$-smoo
th numbers differing by $d$.

Since there are only $N-1$ possible values of $d$, the total number of pairs of
$(N-1)$-smooth numbers with difference at most $N-1$ is finite. Hence the set of
 $x$ for which two or more of $x+1, \ldots, x+N$ are $(N-1)$-smooth is **finite*
*.

**Conclusion.** Let $M$ be the largest $x$ such that two or more of $x+1, \ldots
, x+N$ are $(N-1)$-smooth (set $M = 0$ if none exists). Set $f(N) = \max(M+1, 1)
$. Then for every $x \geq f(N)$, at most one $y_i = 1$, and by the Lemma, $x+1,
\ldots, x+N$ are multiplicatively independent. $\blacksquare$
