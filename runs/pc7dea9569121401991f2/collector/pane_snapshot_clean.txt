## Proof

**Step 1: Every element of $G$ has modulus 1, so $G \subseteq S^1$.**

Since $G$ is a compact subset of $\mathbb{C}$, it is bounded; say $|g| \le M$ fo
r all $g \in G$. Take any $g \in G$. Because $G$ is a subgroup, $g^n \in G$ for
every $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, so $|g|^n = |g^n| \le M$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

- If $|g| > 1$, then $|g|^n \to \infty$ as $n \to +\infty$, contradicting bounde
dness.
- If $|g| < 1$, then $|g|^n \to 0$ as $n \to +\infty$. Since $G$ is compact (hen
ce closed in $\mathbb{C}$) and $g^n \in G$ with $g^n \to 0$, we would get $0 \in
 G$, contradicting $G \subseteq \mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$.

Therefore $|g| = 1$ for every $g \in G$, i.e. $G \subseteq S^1$.

**Step 2: $G$ is a closed subgroup of $S^1$.**

$G$ is compact in $\mathbb{C}$, hence closed in $\mathbb{C}$, hence closed in $S
^1$. So $G$ is a closed subgroup of $S^1$.

**Step 3: The only closed subgroups of $S^1$ are the finite cyclic groups (roots
 of unity of finite order) and $S^1$ itself.**

Consider the universal covering homomorphism $\exp \colon \mathbb{R} \to S^1$, $
t \mapsto e^{2\pi i t}$, and set $H := \exp^{-1}(G)$. Since $\exp$ is continuous
 and $G$ is closed, $H$ is a **closed subgroup of $(\mathbb{R}, +)$**.

A classical result states that every closed subgroup of $\mathbb{R}$ is one of:
$$\{0\}, \qquad \mathbb{R}, \qquad a\mathbb{Z} \text{ for some } a > 0.$$

- If $H = \{0\}$, then $G = \{1\}$, which is finite.
- If $H = \mathbb{R}$, then $G = \exp(\mathbb{R}) = S^1$.
- If $H = a\mathbb{Z}$, then $G = \exp(a\mathbb{Z}) = \{e^{2\pi i a n} : n \in \
mathbb{Z}\}$.
  - If $a \in \mathbb{Q}$, write $a = p/q$ in lowest terms; then $G$ is the grou
p of $q$-th roots of unity, which is **finite**.
  - If $a \notin \mathbb{Q}$, then $\{an \bmod 1 : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is dense
in $[0,1)$ (Kronecker's theorem), so $\exp(a\mathbb{Z})$ is **dense in $S^1$** b
ut not equal to $S^1$. In particular $\exp(a\mathbb{Z})$ is **not closed** in $S
^1$, contradicting the fact that $G$ is closed. So this case cannot occur for a
closed subgroup.

Hence every closed subgroup of $S^1$ is either finite or equal to $S^1$.

**Step 4: Conclusion.**

$G$ is an infinite closed subgroup of $S^1$. By Step 3, the only infinite closed
 subgroup of $S^1$ is $S^1$ itself. Therefore

$$\boxed{G = S^1}.$$

So yes, $G$ must be equal to the unit circle $S^1$.
