**Proof.**

Yes, $M^{1/2}$ is invertible.

Since $M \in \mathcal{B}(F)^+$ is invertible, $M^{-1} \in \mathcal{B}(F)$ exists
 and is bounded. We claim that $M^{-1}$ is also a positive operator. Indeed, sin
ce $M$ is positive and invertible, its spectrum satisfies $\sigma(M) \subseteq [
m, \|M\|]$ for some $m > 0$ (because $0 \notin \sigma(M)$ by invertibility, and
the spectrum of a positive operator is contained in $[0,\infty)$). By the spectr
al mapping theorem, $\sigma(M^{-1}) = \{\lambda^{-1} : \lambda \in \sigma(M)\} \
subseteq (0, 1/m]$, so $M^{-1}$ is self-adjoint with non-negative spectrum, henc
e $M^{-1} \in \mathcal{B}(F)^+$.

Since $M^{-1}$ is positive, it has a unique positive square root $(M^{-1})^{1/2}
 \in \mathcal{B}(F)^+$.

Now observe:
$$M^{1/2} \cdot (M^{-1})^{1/2} \cdot (M^{-1})^{1/2} \cdot M^{1/2} = M^{1/2} \, M
^{-1} \, M^{1/2} = M^{1/2} M^{-1/2} M^{-1/2} M^{1/2}.$$

More directly, since $M^{1/2}$ and $M^{-1}$ are both functions of the same self-
adjoint operator $M$ (via the continuous functional calculus), they commute. Spe
cifically, $M^{1/2}$ is a limit of polynomials in $M$, so $M^{1/2}$ commutes wit
h $M$, and hence with $M^{-1}$. Therefore $M^{1/2}$ also commutes with $(M^{-1})
^{1/2}$ (which is a function of $M^{-1}$, and $M^{-1}$ is a function of $M$).

Thus:
$$M^{1/2} \cdot (M^{-1})^{1/2} = (M^{-1})^{1/2} \cdot M^{1/2}.$$

Moreover, both $M^{1/2}$ and $(M^{-1})^{1/2}$ are functions of the same self-adj
oint operator $M$, so by the functional calculus:
$$M^{1/2} \cdot (M^{-1})^{1/2} = (M \cdot M^{-1})^{1/2} = I^{1/2} = I.$$

Hence $(M^{-1})^{1/2}$ is a two-sided inverse of $M^{1/2}$, and since $(M^{-1})^
{1/2} \in \mathcal{B}(F)$ is bounded, $M^{1/2}$ is invertible with
$$\left(M^{1/2}\right)^{-1} = (M^{-1})^{1/2}.$$

Therefore, $M^{1/2}$ is invertible. $\blacksquare$
