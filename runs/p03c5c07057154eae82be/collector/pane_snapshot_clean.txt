**Answer: Yes — the only such subfield is ℝ itself.**

**Theorem.** The only locally compact subfield of ℝ (with the subspace topology)
 is ℝ. In particular, ℝ is an uncountable, locally compact subfield of itself, a
nd no proper subfield of ℝ is locally compact.

**Proof.**

Let $F \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be a subfield that is locally compact in the subspa
ce topology inherited from $\mathbb{R}$. We prove $F = \mathbb{R}$ in four steps
.

**Step 1: $F$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$.**
Since $F$ is a subfield of $\mathbb{R}$, it contains $0$ and $1$, and therefore
contains the prime subfield $\mathbb{Q}$. Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathb
b{R}$, so is $F$; hence $\overline{F} = \mathbb{R}$.

**Step 2: $F$ is open in $\mathbb{R}$.**
We use the standard fact: *a subspace $Y$ of a Hausdorff space $X$ is locally co
mpact (in the subspace topology) if and only if $Y$ is locally closed in $X$*, i
.e., $Y$ is open in $\overline{X}(Y)$.

(Quick justification of the forward direction: for $y \in Y$, local compactness
gives an open-in-$Y$ neighborhood $V = Y \cap W$ ($W$ open in $X$) of $y$ with $
\operatorname{cl}_Y(V)$ compact, hence compact in $X$ (since $X$ is Hausdorff),
hence closed in $X$. One checks $\operatorname{cl}_Y(V) = Y \cap \operatorname{c
l}_X(V)$, so $y \in W \cap \operatorname{cl}_X(V) \subseteq Y$, with $W \cap \op
eratorname{cl}_X(V)$ open in $\overline{X}(Y)$. Thus $Y$ is open in $\overline{X
}(Y)$.)

Applying this with $X = \mathbb{R}$ and $Y = F$: since $\overline{\mathbb{R}}(F)
 = \mathbb{R}$ by Step 1, $F$ is open in $\mathbb{R}$.

**Step 3: $F$ is closed in $\mathbb{R}$.**
$F$ is an additive subgroup of $\mathbb{R}$. By Step 2, $F$ is an *open* subgrou
p of the topological group $(\mathbb{R}, +)$. An open subgroup $H$ of any topolo
gical group $G$ is closed: the complement
$$G \setminus H = \bigcup_{g \notin H} gH$$
is a union of (open) cosets, hence open; so $H$ is closed.

**Step 4: $F = \mathbb{R}$.**
$F$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$ (Step 1) and closed in $\mathbb{R}$ (Step 3), so $F
 = \mathbb{R}$.

**Conclusion.** The unique locally compact subfield of $\mathbb{R}$ is $\mathbb{
R}$ itself. Since $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable and locally compact, **yes**, such
 a subfield exists — and it is the only one. Every proper subfield of $\mathbb{R
}$ fails to be locally compact (proper subfields are dense but not open, hence n
ot locally closed, hence not locally compact; in particular uncountable proper s
ubfields such as those obtained by dropping one element from a transcendence bas
is are not locally compact).
