## Fourier Transform of $e^{\frac{i}{2}\langle Qx,x\rangle}$

**Convention.** We use $\widehat{f}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}f(x)\,e^{-i\langle x
,\xi\rangle}\,dx$.

### Step 1. Diagonalization

Since $Q$ is real symmetric, there exists an orthogonal matrix $P$ ($P^TP=I$, $|
\det P|=1$) such that
$$Q = P^T D P, \qquad D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n),$$
with $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_r>0$ and $\lambda_{r+1},\dots,\lambda_n<0$. Set $y
=Px$ and $\eta=P\xi$; since $P$ is orthogonal, $dx=dy$ and $\langle x,\xi\rangle
=\langle y,\eta\rangle$, $\langle Qx,x\rangle=\langle Dy,y\rangle=\sum_j \lambda
_j y_j^2$. Hence
$$\widehat{f}(\xi)=\prod_{j=1}^n \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{\frac{i}{2}\lambda_j
 y_j^2}\,e^{-iy_j\eta_j}\,dy_j.$$

### Step 2. One-dimensional Fresnel integral

For $a\neq 0$ and $\zeta\in\mathbb{R}$, complete the square:
$$\frac{a}{2}y^2 - y\zeta = \frac{a}{2}\Big(y-\frac{\zeta}{a}\Big)^2 - \frac{\ze
ta^2}{2a}.$$
Therefore
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{\frac{i}{2}ay^2}e^{-iy\zeta}\,dy = e^{-i\zeta^2/(2a
)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{\frac{i}{2}au^2}\,du.$$

The Fresnel integral is evaluated by rotating the contour: for $\operatorname{sg
n}(a)=+1$ rotate to $u=e^{i\pi/4}t$, for $\operatorname{sgn}(a)=-1$ rotate to $u
=e^{-i\pi/4}t$; in both cases (justified by Cauchy's theorem on a wedge with the
 arc contribution vanishing):
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{\frac{i}{2}au^2}\,du = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{|a|}}\;e^{
i\frac{\pi}{4}\operatorname{sgn}(a)}.$$

So
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{\frac{i}{2}ay^2}e^{-iy\zeta}\,dy = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi
}{|a|}}\;e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}\operatorname{sgn}(a)}\;e^{-i\zeta^2/(2a)}.$$

### Step 3. Assemble the product

Applying Step 2 with $a=\lambda_j$, $\zeta=\eta_j$:
$$\widehat{f}(\xi)=\prod_{j=1}^n \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{|\lambda_j|}}\;e^{i\frac{\pi}
{4}\operatorname{sgn}(\lambda_j)}\;e^{-i\eta_j^2/(2\lambda_j)}.$$

**Amplitude.** $\prod_j \sqrt{2\pi/|\lambda_j|} = (2\pi)^{n/2}/\sqrt{|\det D|} =
 (2\pi)^{n/2}/\sqrt{|\det Q|}$ (since $|\det P|=1$).

**Phase.** $\prod_j e^{i\pi\operatorname{sgn}(\lambda_j)/4} = \exp\!\Big(\frac{i
\pi}{4}\sum_j\operatorname{sgn}(\lambda_j)\Big) = \exp\!\Big(\frac{i\pi}{4}\big(
r-(n-r)\big)\Big)=e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}(2r-n)}.$

**Quadratic exponent.** $\sum_j \eta_j^2/\lambda_j = \langle D^{-1}\eta,\eta\ran
gle$. Since $Q^{-1}=P^T D^{-1}P$ and $\eta=P\xi$,
$$\langle D^{-1}\eta,\eta\rangle = \langle P^T D^{-1}P\xi,\xi\rangle = \langle Q
^{-1}\xi,\xi\rangle.$$

### Result

$$\boxed{\widehat{f}(\xi)=\frac{(2\pi)^{n/2}}{\sqrt{|\det Q|}}\;\exp\!\Big(\frac
{i\pi}{4}(2r-n)\Big)\;\exp\!\Big(-\frac{i}{2}\langle Q^{-1}\xi,\xi\rangle\Big).}
$$

**Remarks.**
- $2r-n = r-(n-r)$ is the **signature** of $Q$; the phase factor $e^{i\pi(2r-n)/
4}$ depends only on the signature, not on the magnitudes of the eigenvalues.
- The result is the analytic continuation of the real Gaussian formula $\widehat
{\,e^{-\frac12\langle Ax,x\rangle}\,}(\xi)=(2\pi)^{n/2}(\det A)^{-1/2}e^{-\frac1
2\langle A^{-1}\xi,\xi\rangle}$ (valid for $A>0$) to $A=-iQ$, with the square ro
ot branch fixed by the signature, yielding the Fresnel phase.
- The transform is again a pure chirp $e^{-\frac{i}{2}\langle Q^{-1}\xi,\xi\rang
le}$ (the Fourier transform maps a quadratic phase with matrix $Q$ to a quadrati
c phase with matrix $-Q^{-1}$, up to the constant prefactor).
