## Solution

Introduce polar coordinates $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, with $r = \sqr
t{x^2+y^2}$, $r \geq 0$.

**Radial dynamics.** Using $\dot r = \dfrac{x\dot x + y\dot y}{r}$:

$$
x\dot x + y\dot y = x\!\left(-y - \frac{x(r^2-2)}{r}\right) + y\!\left(x - \frac
{y(r^2-2)}{r}\right)
= -xy + yx - \frac{(x^2+y^2)(r^2-2)}{r}
= -\frac{r^2(r^2-2)}{r} = -r(r^2-2).
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{\dot r = \frac{-r(r^2-2)}{r} = r(2-r^2).}
$$

**Angular dynamics.** Using $\dot\theta = \dfrac{x\dot y - y\dot x}{r^2}$:

$$
x\dot y - y\dot x = x\!\left(x - \frac{y(r^2-2)}{r}\right) - y\!\left(-y - \frac
{x(r^2-2)}{r}\right)
= x^2 + y^2 - \frac{xy(r^2-2)}{r} + \frac{xy(r^2-2)}{r} = r^2.
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{\dot\theta = 1.}
$$

**Finding periodic orbits.** The system decouples in polar coordinates. Since $\
dot\theta \equiv 1 \neq 0$, every trajectory (away from the origin) winds around
 with strictly increasing angle. A closed orbit therefore requires $r$ to return
 to its starting value after one full revolution; but $r$ evolves autonomously v
ia $\dot r = r(2-r^2)$, independent of $\theta$. Thus a closed orbit exists **if
f** $r$ is constant along it, i.e. **iff** $r$ is an equilibrium of the scalar O
DE $\dot r = r(2-r^2)$.

The equilibria of $\dot r = r(2-r^2)$ are $r = 0$ and $r = \sqrt{2}$ (since $r\g
e 0$). The value $r=0$ corresponds to the origin, which is a singular point of t
he original system (the vector field is undefined there because of the $1/\sqrt{
x^2+y^2}$ factor), not a limit cycle.

The value $r = \sqrt{2}$ gives a genuine periodic orbit: the circle

$$
\boxed{x^2 + y^2 = 2}
$$

on which $\dot r = 0$ and $\dot\theta = 1$, so it is traversed once every period
 $T = 2\pi$.

**Stability (confirming it is a limit cycle).** For $0 < r < \sqrt 2$, $2 - r^2
> 0$ so $\dot r > 0$: trajectories spiral outward. For $r > \sqrt 2$, $2 - r^2 <
 0$ so $\dot r < 0$: trajectories spiral inward. Hence the circle $r = \sqrt 2$
attracts all nearby trajectories from both sides — it is a **stable limit cycle*
*.

**Uniqueness.** Any limit cycle must, by the argument above, be a positive equil
ibrium of $\dot r = r(2-r^2)$. The only positive equilibrium is $r = \sqrt 2$. T
herefore the limit cycle is unique.

## Conclusion

The system has exactly one limit cycle: the circle

$$
x^2 + y^2 = 2,
$$

which is a stable limit cycle traversed counter-clockwise with period $2\pi$.
