## Proof

Recall that a set $S \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ has **content (Jordan content) zero**
 if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists a *finite* collection of rectangles
 covering $S$ whose total area is less than $\varepsilon$.

Let $\varepsilon > 0$ be given. Choose an integer $N$ with $N > 2/\varepsilon$,
so that
$$\frac{1}{N} < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}.$$

Decompose
$$C = C_{\le N} \cup C_{> N},$$
where
$$C_{\le N} = \bigcup_{q=1}^{N}\;\bigcup_{\substack{0\le p\le q\\ \gcd(p,q)=1}}
\{p/q\}\times[0,\,1/q], \qquad
C_{> N} = \bigcup_{q>N}\;\bigcup_{\substack{0\le p\le q\\ \gcd(p,q)=1}} \{p/q\}\
times[0,\,1/q].$$

**Covering $C_{>N}$.** For every $q > N$ and every admissible $p$, the segment $
\{p/q\}\times[0,1/q]$ satisfies $1/q < 1/N$, hence lies inside the single rectan
gle
$$[0,1]\times[0,\,1/N],$$
which has area $1/N < \varepsilon/2$. Thus $C_{>N}$ is covered by one rectangle
of area $<\varepsilon/2$.

**Covering $C_{\le N}$.** This is a *finite* union of vertical segments (finitel
y many $q$, and for each $q$ at most $q+1$ choices of $p$). Set
$$w = \frac{\varepsilon}{4N}.$$
For each segment $\{p/q\}\times[0,1/q]$ with $1\le q\le N$, cover it by the (clo
sed) rectangle
$$R_{p,q} = \Big([\,p/q - w/2,\; p/q + w/2\,]\cap[0,1]\Big)\times[0,\,1/q],$$
which has width at most $w$ and height $1/q$, hence area at most $w/q$.

The number of admissible $p$ for a given $q$ is at most $q+1$, so the total area
 of all these rectangles is
$$\sum_{q=1}^{N}\sum_{p} \frac{w}{q} \;\le\; \sum_{q=1}^{N}(q+1)\cdot\frac{w}{q}
 \;\le\; \sum_{q=1}^{N} 2w \;=\; 2wN \;=\; 2\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{4N}\cdot N \
;=\; \frac{\varepsilon}{2}.$$

(Here we used $(q+1)/q \le 2$ for $q\ge 1$.)

**Combining.** The set $C$ is covered by the single rectangle enclosing $C_{>N}$
 together with the finitely many rectangles $R_{p,q}$ enclosing $C_{\le N}$. The
 total number of rectangles is finite, and their total area is strictly less tha
n
$$\frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} = \varepsilon.$$

Since $\varepsilon > 0$ was arbitrary, $C$ has content zero. $\blacksquare$
