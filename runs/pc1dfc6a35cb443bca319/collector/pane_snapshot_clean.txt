Yes, it is possible. Here is a simple example.

**Example.** Let $X = \mathbb{R}$ with the absolute value norm $|\cdot|$, which
is a (one-dimensional, real) Banach space. Define the linear operator
$$T : X \to X, \qquad T(x) = 2x.$$

**Linearity.** For all $x, y \in X$ and $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$,
$$T(\alpha x + \beta y) = 2(\alpha x + \beta y) = \alpha(2x) + \beta(2y) = \alph
a T(x) + \beta T(y),$$
so $T$ is linear.

**Norm.** The operator norm is
$$\|T\| = \sup_{x \neq 0} \frac{|T(x)|}{|x|} = \sup_{x \neq 0} \frac{|2x|}{|x|}
= \sup_{x \neq 0} 2 = 2 > 1.$$

**Invertibility.** The operator $T$ is bijective: it is injective since $T(x) =
0 \Rightarrow 2x = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0$, and surjective since for any $y \in X$,
 taking $x = y/2$ gives $T(x) = y$. Its inverse is the bounded linear operator
$$T^{-1}(y) = \tfrac{1}{2} y, \qquad \|T^{-1}\| = \tfrac{1}{2} < \infty.$$
Since $X$ is Banach and $T^{-1}$ is bounded, $T$ is invertible as a bounded line
ar operator on $X$.

Thus $T$ is an invertible linear operator on a Banach space with $\|T\| = 2 > 1$
.

**Remark.** The same construction works on any Banach space $X$: the operator $T
 = \lambda I$ (scalar multiple of the identity) with $\lambda > 1$ satisfies $\|
T\| = |\lambda| > 1$ and is invertible with bounded inverse $T^{-1} = \lambda^{-
1} I$. This shows the phenomenon is completely general — having operator norm la
rger than $1$ is in no way incompatible with invertibility. (Norm $> 1$ merely m
eans $T$ *expands* some vectors; it says nothing about injectivity or surjectivi
ty.)
