# Proof

**Claim:** Yes. If \(A \in M_n(\mathbb{C})\) is nonsingular and similar to \(A^k
\) for every \(k = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\), then \(1\) is the only eigenvalue of \(A\)
.

---

**Setup.** Let \(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n\) be the eigenvalues of \(A\) (wit
h multiplicity), and let \(p_k = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i^k = \operatorname{tr}(A^
k)\). Since similar matrices have equal trace and determinant, the hypothesis gi
ves:

- **(Trace condition)** \(p_k = p_1 =: c\) for all \(k \ge 1\).
- **(Determinant condition)** \(\det(A) = \det(A^k) = \det(A)^k\) for all \(k\);
 taking \(k=2\) yields \(\det(A) = 1\).

---

**Step 1. Every eigenvalue is a root of unity.**

Since \(A \sim A^k\), the multiset of eigenvalues of \(A^k\), namely \(\{\lambda
_1^k, \ldots, \lambda_n^k\}\), equals the multiset \(S = \{\lambda_1, \ldots, \l
ambda_n\}\) for every \(k \ge 1\). Thus the map \(z \mapsto z^k\) permutes \(S\)
. In particular, for any \(\lambda \in S\), we have \(\lambda^k \in S\) for all
\(k \ge 1\), so the orbit \(\{\lambda, \lambda^2, \lambda^3, \ldots\}\) is conta
ined in the finite set \(S\). Hence \(\lambda\) has finite multiplicative order,
 i.e., \(\lambda\) is a root of unity. In particular \(|\lambda| = 1\) (so \(A\)
 is nonsingular is consistent) and the characteristic polynomial \(P(x) = \prod_
{i=1}^n (x - \lambda_i)\) is a product of cyclotomic polynomials, hence has **in
teger coefficients**.

---

**Step 2. Newton's identities determine the characteristic polynomial.**

Newton's identities express the elementary symmetric polynomials \(e_k\) in term
s of power sums:
\[
k\, e_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} (-1)^{j-1} e_{k-j}\, p_j, \qquad e_0 = 1.
\]
Since \(p_1 = p_2 = \cdots = p_n = c\), a straightforward induction gives
\[
e_k = \binom{c}{k} := \frac{c(c-1)\cdots(c-k+1)}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \ldots, n
.
\]
Therefore
\[
P(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k \binom{c}{k} x^{n-k}.
\]

---

**Step 3. Two key constraints: \(\binom{c}{n} = 1\) and \(P(1) = 0\).**

*Determinant.* The constant term of \(P\) is \((-1)^n e_n = (-1)^n \det(A) = (-1
)^n\). Since \(e_n = \binom{c}{n}\), we get
\[
\binom{c}{n} = 1. \tag{D}
\]
In particular \(c \neq 0\) (for \(n \ge 1\), \(\binom{0}{n} = 0 \neq 1\)).

*Trace for all \(k\).* The power sums \(p_m\) for \(m > n\) are determined by th
e linear recurrence from \(P\):
\[
p_m = e_1 p_{m-1} - e_2 p_{m-2} + \cdots + (-1)^{n-1} e_n\, p_{m-n}.
\]
With \(p_1 = \cdots = p_n = c\), this gives
\[
p_{n+1} = c\bigl(e_1 - e_2 + \cdots + (-1)^{n-1} e_n\bigr) = c\bigl(1 - P(1)\big
r).
\]
The hypothesis requires \(p_{n+1} = c\). Since \(c \neq 0\), we must have
\[
P(1) = 0. \tag{T}
\]
(Once \(P(1) = 0\), the same recurrence gives \(p_{n+2} = c\), and by induction
\(p_m = c\) for all \(m \ge 1\), so no further constraints arise.)

---

**Step 4. \(P(1) = 0\) forces \(c \in \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}\).**

Using the binomial identity \(\sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k \binom{c}{k} = (-1)^n \binom
{c-1}{n}\),
\[
P(1) = (-1)^n \binom{c-1}{n} = 0 \implies (c-1)(c-2)\cdots(c-n) = 0.
\]
Hence \(c \in \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}\); in particular \(c\) is a **positive integer
** with \(1 \le c \le n\).

---

**Step 5. Nonsingularity forces \(c = n\); all eigenvalues equal \(1\).**

With \(c\) a positive integer and \(1 \le c \le n\), the generalized binomial co
efficients become ordinary ones, and
\[
P(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k \binom{c}{k} x^{n-k} = x^{n-c}(x-1)^c.
\]
The roots are \(0\) (with multiplicity \(n - c\)) and \(1\) (with multiplicity \
(c\)). Since \(A\) is **nonsingular**, \(0\) is not an eigenvalue, so \(n - c =
0\), i.e.,
\[
c = n.
\]
Therefore \(P(x) = (x - 1)^n\), and **every eigenvalue of \(A\) is \(1\)**. \(\b
lacksquare\)
