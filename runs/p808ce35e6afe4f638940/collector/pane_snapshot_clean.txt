**Answer: Yes, $f(x)$ must have a root in $F$.**

**Proof.**

Let $p = \deg f$ (prime) and let $L$ be the splitting field of $f$ over $F$. Let
 $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_p$ be the roots of $f$ in $L$ (with multiplicity).

**Step 1: Each root generates the entire splitting field.**

For any root $\alpha_i$, take $K = F(\alpha_i)$. Then $f$ has a root in $K$, so
by hypothesis $f$ splits in $K$. Thus every root of $f$ lies in $F(\alpha_i)$, g
iving $L \subseteq F(\alpha_i)$. Since $F(\alpha_i) \subseteq L$ trivially, we c
onclude
$$F(\alpha_i) = L \quad \text{for every root } \alpha_i. \tag{$\star$}$$

**Step 2: Separable case.**

Suppose $f$ is separable (i.e., $f' \neq 0$; this covers $\operatorname{char}(F)
 = 0$ and $\operatorname{char}(F) \neq p$). Then $L/F$ is Galois; set $G = \oper
atorname{Gal}(L/F)$ with $|G| = [L:F]$. The group $G$ acts on the set of $p$ **d
istinct** roots $\{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_p\}$.

By $(\star)$, $[F(\alpha_i) : F] = [L:F] = |G|$. By orbit–stabilizer, $[F(\alpha
_i):F] = |G|\,/\,|\operatorname{Stab}(\alpha_i)|$, so $|\operatorname{Stab}(\alp
ha_i)| = 1$ for every $i$. Thus $G$ acts **freely**: every orbit has size $|G|$.

The orbits partition the $p$ roots into $m$ orbits, each of size $|G|$, so $p =
m \cdot |G|$. Since $f$ is reducible over $F$ and squarefree (separable), it has
 at least two distinct irreducible factors, so the number of orbits $m \geq 2$.
Since $p$ is prime and $|G|$ divides $p$ with $m \geq 2$, we must have $|G| = 1$
.

Therefore $L = F$: $f$ splits completely over $F$, and in particular $f$ has a r
oot in $F$.

**Step 3: Inseparable case.**

Suppose $f$ is not separable. Then $f' = 0$, which forces $\operatorname{char}(F
) = p$ (since $\deg f = p$ is prime, the only way $f' \equiv 0$ is if every expo
nent is divisible by $p$, and degree $p$ forces $f$ to be linear in $x^p$). So (
up to a leading coefficient) $f(x) = x^p - c$ for some $c \in F$.

Over $\bar{F}$, we have $f(x) = (x - \alpha)^p$ where $\alpha^p = c$. Since $f$
is reducible over $F$, write $f = gh$ with $1 \leq \deg g \leq p-1$ and $g, h \i
n F[x]$. In $\bar{F}[x]$, we must have $g(x) = (x - \alpha)^a$ for some $1 \leq
a \leq p-1$. The coefficient of $x^{a-1}$ in $(x-\alpha)^a$ is $-a\alpha$. Since
 $g \in F[x]$, we need $a\alpha \in F$. Because $1 \leq a \leq p-1$ and $\operat
orname{char}(F) = p$, the integer $a$ is nonzero (hence invertible) in $F$, so $
\alpha \in F$.

Thus $f(x) = (x - \alpha)^p$ with $\alpha \in F$, and $f$ has root $\alpha \in F
$.

**Conclusion.** In both the separable and inseparable cases, the hypotheses forc
e $f$ to have a root in $F$. $\blacksquare$
