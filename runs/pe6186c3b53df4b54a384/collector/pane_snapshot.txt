## Solution

Let $a_n = \dfrac{n}{(\log n)^r}$ (for $n$ large enough that $\log n > 0$, i.e.,
 $n > 1$). We seek $p \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ such that $\left|a_n^{\,p}\r
ight| \le C$ for all $n > N$.

Since $a_n > 0$ for $n > 1$, we have $a_n^{\,p} = \dfrac{n^p}{(\log n)^{rp}}$.

### Case 1: $p > 0$

We show $a_n^{\,p} \to \infty$, hence the sequence is unbounded.

For any $p > 0$ and any $r \in \mathbb{R}$, write
$$a_n^{\,p} = \frac{n^p}{(\log n)^{rp}}.$$

The key fact is that **logarithmic growth is slower than any positive power**: f
or every $\varepsilon > 0$,
$$\frac{(\log n)^{rp}}{n^\varepsilon} \to 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$
(This follows from L'Hôpital's rule: $\frac{(\log x)^k}{x^\varepsilon} \to 0$ fo
r any $k \in \mathbb{R}$, $\varepsilon > 0$.)

Choose $\varepsilon = p/2 > 0$. Then $(\log n)^{rp} = o\!\left(n^{p/2}\right)$,
so
$$a_n^{\,p} = \frac{n^p}{(\log n)^{rp}} \ge \frac{n^p}{n^{p/2}} = n^{p/2} \to \i
nfty \quad \text{(for } n \text{ large enough)}.$$

Thus **no $p > 0$ works**, for any value of $r$.

### Case 2: $p < 0$

Write $p = -q$ with $q > 0$. Then
$$a_n^{\,p} = \left(\frac{n}{(\log n)^r}\right)^{-q} = \frac{(\log n)^{rq}}{n^{q
}}.$$

Again using $\frac{(\log n)^{rq}}{n^q} \to 0$ (logarithmic growth is $o(n^q)$ fo
r any $q > 0$), we get
$$a_n^{\,p} \to 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$

A sequence converging to $0$ is bounded: there exists $N$ such that for all $n >
 N$, $|a_n^{\,p}| \le 1$, and the finitely many terms $n \le N$ are bounded by a
 constant. Hence $|a_n^{\,p}| \le C$ for all $n > N$.

Thus **every $p < 0$ works**, for any value of $r$.

### The Relationship Between $r$ and $p$

The relationship is:
$$\boxed{p < 0 \quad (\text{for any } r \in \mathbb{R}).}$$

The value of $r$ is **irrelevant** to boundedness: for any $r \in \mathbb{R}$ an
d any $p < 0$, the sequence $\left\{\left(\frac{n}{(\log n)^r}\right)^p\right\}$
 is bounded (in fact, converges to $0$). For any $r \in \mathbb{R}$ and any $p >
 0$, the sequence is unbounded (diverges to $+\infty$).

**Intuition:** The base $a_n = n/(\log n)^r \to \infty$ for every $r$ (polynomia
l growth always dominates logarithmic growth). Raising a quantity tending to $+\
infty$ to a negative power yields something tending to $0$; raising it to a posi
tive power yields something tending to $+\infty$. The parameter $r$ only affects
 the *rate* at which $a_n \to \infty$, not the qualitative fact that it does, so
 $r$ plays no role in the boundedness question.
