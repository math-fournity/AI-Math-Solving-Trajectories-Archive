## Proof

**Claim:** No non-trivial two-variable harmonic function can vanish on a curve w
ith a cusp.

**Key tool:** Every harmonic function in two variables is locally the real part
of a holomorphic function.

**Setup.** Let $f(x,y)$ be a non-trivial harmonic function on a domain $\Omega \
subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$. Identify $\mathbb{R}^2$ with $\mathbb{C}$ via $z = x+iy$
. Since $\Omega$ is locally simply connected, for every point $p \in \Omega$ the
re exists a neighborhood $U$ and a holomorphic function $g$ on $U$ such that $f
= \operatorname{Re}(g)$ on $U$.

**Local structure of the zero set.** Fix a point $p$ with $f(p)=0$, so $g(p) = i
\alpha$ for some real $\alpha$ (since $\operatorname{Re}(g(p))=0$). Replacing $g
$ by $g - i\alpha$, we may assume $g(p)=0$.

*Case 1: $\nabla f(p) \neq 0$.* Then $g'(p) \neq 0$ (since $\nabla f = 0$ iff $g
' = 0$ at a zero of $g$). By the implicit function theorem, the zero set $\{f=0\
}$ is a smooth real-analytic curve near $p$.

*Case 2: $\nabla f(p) = 0$.* Then $g$ has a zero of order $n \geq 2$ at $p$. Wri
te
$$g(z) = (z-p)^n \, h(z), \quad h(p) \neq 0.$$
Since $h(p)\neq 0$, on a sufficiently small neighborhood we can choose a holomor
phic branch $\varphi(z) = h(z)^{1/n}$. Define
$$w = (z-p)\,\varphi(z).$$
Since $w'(p) = \varphi(p) = h(p)^{1/n} \neq 0$, the map $z \mapsto w$ is a **bih
olomorphism** from a neighborhood of $p$ to a neighborhood of $0$. In the $w$-co
ordinate:
$$f = \operatorname{Re}(g) = \operatorname{Re}(w^n).$$
Writing $w = re^{i\theta}$, the zero set is $\operatorname{Re}(w^n) = r^n \cos(n
\theta) = 0$, i.e., the $n$ rays
$$\theta = \frac{\pi/2 + k\pi}{n}, \quad k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1.$$
These are $n$ **smooth** straight lines through the origin. Pulling back through
 the biholomorphism, the zero set of $f$ near $p$ is a union of $n$ smooth real-
analytic arcs through $p$.

**Conclusion of local analysis.** At every point, the zero set of a non-trivial
harmonic function is locally a finite union of smooth real-analytic arcs (a sing
le smooth arc if $\nabla f \neq 0$, or $n \geq 2$ smooth arcs crossing if $\nabl
a f = 0$).

**Why a cusp cannot occur.** A cusp on a curve (e.g., the semicubical cusp $y^2
= x^3$ at the origin) is a singular point where a *single branch* of the curve f
ails to be a smooth embedded 1-manifold. Concretely, the cusp $y^2=x^3$ is the i
mage of $t \mapsto (t^2, t^3)$; it is topologically a line but is **not** smooth
ly embedded at the origin (the parametrization has vanishing derivative at $t=0$
, and no reparametrization makes it a smooth embedding).

Now suppose, for contradiction, that $f$ vanishes on a curve $C$ having a cusp a
t a point $p$. Then $C \subseteq \{f=0\}$, and in particular $p \in \{f=0\}$. By
 the local analysis above, $\{f=0\}$ near $p$ is a finite union of smooth real-a
nalytic arcs $A_1, \dots, A_n$ through $p$. Since $C$ is a single branch near $p
$ (a cusp is a single-branch singularity), $C$ near $p$ must be contained in one
 of these arcs, say $A_1$. But $A_1$ is a **smooth** real-analytic arc, while $C
$ is **not smooth** at $p$. A non-smooth curve cannot be contained in a smooth a
rc — contradiction.

Therefore, no non-trivial harmonic function can vanish on a curve with a cusp. $
\blacksquare$
