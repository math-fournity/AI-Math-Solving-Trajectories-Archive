The statement is **false**.

## Counterexample

Define the three sequences $f, g, h: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{N}$ as follo
ws:

- $f(n) = n$
- $g(n) = n^2$
- $h(n) = \lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor + 1$ (with $h(0)=h(1)=1$ to keep values in $\
mathbb{N}$)

**Verification of the hypotheses.**

1. **$f, g, h$ are increasing and tend to infinity.** Clearly $f(n)=n$ and $g(n)
=n^2$ are strictly increasing and unbounded. For $h$, $\lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor$
 is non-decreasing and increases by $1$ each time $n$ crosses a power of $2$, so
 $h$ is non-decreasing (increasing in the sense required) and $h(n) \to \infty$.

2. **$f(n) = o(g(n))$.** We have
$$\frac{f(n)}{g(n)} = \frac{n}{n^2} = \frac{1}{n} \to 0,$$
so $f(n) = o(g(n))$.

**The conclusion fails.** Compute the ratio
$$\frac{h(f(n))}{h(g(n))} = \frac{h(n)}{h(n^2)} = \frac{\lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor
 + 1}{\lfloor \log_2(n^2) \rfloor + 1}.$$

For $n = 2^k$ (a power of $2$), this becomes
$$\frac{k + 1}{\lfloor 2k \rfloor + 1} = \frac{k+1}{2k+1} \to \frac{1}{2} \neq 0
.$$

Since the ratio $h(f(n))/h(g(n))$ does **not** tend to $0$ along the subsequence
 $n = 2^k$, it cannot tend to $0$ in general. Hence
$$h(f(n)) \neq o(h(g(n))).$$

## Why the intuition fails

The little-$o$ relation $f = o(g)$ is preserved under composition with $h$ only
when $h$ grows *fast enough* (e.g., polynomially or faster) so that the multipli
cative gap between $f$ and $g$ is amplified. When $h$ grows slowly (logarithmica
lly, as above), the gap $g(n)/f(n) \to \infty$ is *compressed* by $h$ into a bou
nded additive gap, and the ratio $h(f(n))/h(g(n))$ stays bounded away from $0$.
Thus the implication

$$f(n) = o(g(n)) \;\Longrightarrow\; h(f(n)) = o(h(g(n)))$$

does **not** hold for arbitrary increasing $h: \mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$ tending
to infinity.
