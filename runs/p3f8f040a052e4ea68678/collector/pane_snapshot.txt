## Proof

**Answer: No.** The condition $a_n = O(1/|n|^k)$ does **not** imply that $f$ is
$k$ times differentiable. We construct an explicit counterexample.

---

### Key Distinction

The standard sufficient condition for $k$-times continuous differentiability is
**absolute summability** of the weighted coefficients:

$$\sum_{n\neq 0} |n|^k |a_n| < \infty \implies f \in C^k.$$

The condition $a_n = O(1/|n|^k)$ is far weaker: it gives $|n|^k|a_n| = O(1)$, bu
t the series $\sum |n|^k|a_n|$ may diverge (e.g., like the harmonic series). We
exploit this gap by concentrating the coefficient mass on a **sparse subsequence
**, so the pointwise bound $O(1/|n|^k)$ holds while the derivative series diverg
es.

---

### Counterexample

Fix an integer $k \geq 1$. Define the $2\pi$-periodic function

$$f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\sin\!\bigl(n^{k+1}\, x\bigr)}{n^{k(k+1)}}.$$

**Step 1: The series converges and $f$ is well-defined and continuous.**

Since $k(k+1) \geq 2 > 1$, the Weierstrass $M$-test gives uniform convergence:

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left|\frac{\sin(n^{k+1}x)}{n^{k(k+1)}}\right| \leq \sum_{
n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^{k(k+1)}} < \infty.$$

Hence $f$ is continuous (and $2\pi$-periodic).

**Step 2: The Fourier coefficients satisfy $a_m = O(1/|m|^k)$.**

Writing $f(x) = \sum_{m} c_m\, e^{imx}$, the only nonzero coefficients occur at
$m = \pm n^{k+1}$ for positive integers $n$:

$$c_{n^{k+1}} = \frac{1}{2i\, n^{k(k+1)}}, \qquad c_{-n^{k+1}} = -\frac{1}{2i\,
n^{k(k+1)}}, \qquad c_m = 0 \text{ otherwise.}$$

When $m = n^{k+1}$, we have $n^{k(k+1)} = (n^{k+1})^k = m^k$, so

$$|c_m| = \frac{1}{2\,m^k} \quad \text{when } m = n^{k+1}, \qquad |c_m| = 0 \qua
d \text{otherwise.}$$

Therefore $|c_m| \leq \frac{1}{2|m|^k}$ for all $m \neq 0$, i.e., $a_m = O(1/|m|
^k)$.

**Step 3: $f$ is not $k$ times differentiable.**

Suppose for contradiction that $f$ is $k$ times differentiable with $f^{(k)} \in
 L^1([0,2\pi])$ (the standard notion underlying Fourier-analytic differentiabili
ty). Then the Fourier coefficients of $f^{(k)}$ are $(im)^k c_m$, and by the **R
iemann–Lebesgue lemma**, any $L^1$ function's Fourier coefficients must tend to
$0$:

$$\bigl|(im)^k c_m\bigr| \xrightarrow{|m|\to\infty} 0.$$

However, along the subsequence $m = n^{k+1}$:

$$\bigl|(im)^k c_m\bigr| = |m|^k \cdot \frac{1}{2\,m^k} = \frac{1}{2} \quad \tex
t{for all } n \geq 1.$$

This does **not** tend to $0$ — contradiction. Hence $f^{(k)} \notin L^1$, and $
f$ cannot be $k$ times differentiable in any sense that yields an integrable $k$
-th derivative.

**Remark on pointwise differentiability.** The formal $k$-th derivative series i
s

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{d^k}{dx^k}\frac{\sin(n^{k+1}x)}{n^{k(k+1)}} = \sum_{
n=1}^{\infty} \operatorname{trig}(n^{k+1}x),$$

where $\operatorname{trig}$ is a sine or cosine. The terms $\operatorname{trig}(
n^{k+1}x)$ do not tend to $0$ for generic $x$, so the series diverges — consiste
nt with $f$ failing to be $k$-times differentiable pointwise as well. (For $k=1$
 this recovers the classical **Riemann nondifferentiable function** $R(x)=\sum \
sin(n^2 x)/n^2$.)

---

### Conclusion

The pointwise decay condition $a_n = O(1/|n|^k)$ is insufficient to guarantee $k
$-fold differentiability. What is required is the **summability** condition $\su
m |n|^k|a_n|<\infty$ (or $a_n = O(1/|n|^{k+1+\varepsilon})$ for some $\varepsilo
n>0$), which forces absolute convergence of the $k$-th derivative series. By pla
cing coefficient mass of size $\sim 1/m^k$ on the sparse set $m = n^{k+1}$, we s
atisfy the pointwise bound while violating summability, and the Riemann–Lebesgue
 lemma certifies the impossibility of a $k$-th derivative.
