## Proof

We claim that $\dfrac{s_n}{\sqrt{n}} \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$.

**Setup.** Since $x \in \ell^2(\mathbb{N})$, we have $\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} x_i^2
=: L < \infty$. Let $\varepsilon > 0$ be arbitrary.

**Tail control.** Choose $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that
$$\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty} x_i^2 < \varepsilon^2.$$
This is possible since the tail of a convergent series tends to $0$.

**Split the partial sum.** For $n > N$, write
$$s_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i}_{=:\, S_N} + \under
brace{\sum_{i=N+1}^{n} x_i}_{=:\, R_n}.$$

**Bound each piece.** By the triangle inequality,
$$\frac{|s_n|}{\sqrt{n}} \le \frac{|S_N|}{\sqrt{n}} + \frac{|R_n|}{\sqrt{n}}.$$

- *Head term.* $S_N$ is a fixed finite sum (independent of $n$), so
$$\frac{|S_N|}{\sqrt{n}} \longrightarrow 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$

- *Tail term.* By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to $R_n = \sum_{i=N+1}^{
n} x_i \cdot 1$,
$$|R_n| \le \left(\sum_{i=N+1}^{n} x_i^2\right)^{\!1/2}\!\left(\sum_{i=N+1}^{n}
1^2\right)^{\!1/2} = \left(\sum_{i=N+1}^{n} x_i^2\right)^{\!1/2}\!\sqrt{n - N}.$
$
Since $\sum_{i=N+1}^{n} x_i^2 \le \sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty} x_i^2 < \varepsilon^2$ a
nd $n - N \le n$,
$$\frac{|R_n|}{\sqrt{n}} \le \varepsilon \cdot \frac{\sqrt{n-N}}{\sqrt{n}} \le \
varepsilon.$$

**Combine.** Taking the limsup as $n \to \infty$,
$$\limsup_{n \to \infty} \frac{|s_n|}{\sqrt{n}} \le 0 + \varepsilon = \varepsilo
n.$$

Since $\varepsilon > 0$ was arbitrary, we conclude
$$\limsup_{n \to \infty} \frac{|s_n|}{\sqrt{n}} = 0,$$
and since $\frac{|s_n|}{\sqrt{n}} \ge 0$, it follows that
$$\frac{s_n}{\sqrt{n}} \longrightarrow 0.$$

**Remark (sharpness of the normalization).** The normalization $\sqrt{n}$ is the
 natural scale here: by Cauchy–Schwarz, $\frac{|s_n|}{\sqrt{n}} \le \left(\sum_{
i=1}^{n} x_i^2\right)^{1/2} \le \sqrt{L}$, so the sequence is always bounded. Th
e argument above shows the bound can be driven to $0$ by separating a finite hea
d (killed by $\sqrt{n}$) from a small tail (controlled by $\ell^2$). The result
is best possible in the sense that for $x_i = 1/i \in \ell^2$, one has $s_n \sim
 \log n$, so $s_n/\sqrt{n} \to 0$ but only barely; and the Kronecker-type normal
ization $n$ would not give convergence in general.
