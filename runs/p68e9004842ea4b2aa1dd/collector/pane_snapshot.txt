## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, such a function exists.

**Construction.** Let $a < b$ and define $f: [a, b) \to \mathbb{R}$ by
$$f(x) = -\sqrt{b - x}.$$

We verify the three required properties.

**1. Differentiability.** The function $g(t) = \sqrt{t}$ is differentiable on $(
0, \infty)$, and $b - x > 0$ for all $x \in [a, b)$. By the chain rule,
$$f'(x) = -\frac{d}{dx}(b-x)^{1/2} = -\tfrac{1}{2}(b-x)^{-1/2}\cdot(-1) = \frac{
1}{2\sqrt{b-x}},$$
which exists for every $x \in [a, b)$ (including one-sided differentiability at
$a$). Hence $f$ is differentiable on $[a, b)$.

**2. The derivative tends to infinity.** As $x \to b^{-}$, we have $b - x \to 0^
{+}$, so $\sqrt{b-x} \to 0^{+}$, and therefore
$$f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{b-x}} \longrightarrow +\infty.$$

**3. Boundedness.** For every $x \in [a, b)$, we have $0 < b - x \leq b - a$, so
$$0 \leq \sqrt{b-x} \leq \sqrt{b-a},$$
which gives
$$-\sqrt{b-a} \leq f(x) = -\sqrt{b-x} < 0.$$
Thus $f$ is bounded on $[a, b)$, with $\sup f = 0$ (not attained) and $\inf f =
-\sqrt{b-a}$ (attained at $x = a$).

**Remark on why this is consistent with the Mean Value Theorem.** One might worr
y that $f'(x) \to +\infty$ forces $f$ to blow up. The MVT gives, for $x < y$ in
$[a,b)$:
$$f(y) - f(x) = f'(\xi)(y - x) \geq M(y-x)$$
once $f' \geq M$ on $[x, y)$. But fixing $x$ and letting $y \to b$ only yields $
f(y) \geq f(x) + M(y - x) \to f(x) + M(b-x)$, a *finite* bound for each fixed $M
$. The divergence of $f'$ is "too slow" to make the total increment $\int_x^b f'
(t)\,dt = -f(x) = \sqrt{b-x}$ diverge — precisely because $f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sq
rt{b-x}}$ is integrable near $b$ even though it diverges pointwise. $\blacksquar
e$
