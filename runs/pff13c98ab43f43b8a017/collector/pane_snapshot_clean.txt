Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether, for every irrational $x \i
n [1,2]$, one can define a sequence of rational numbers converging to $\log_2 x$
.

**Answer: No.** Such a sequence cannot be defined for every irrational $x \in [1
,2]$.

---

**Proof.**

We interpret "a sequence of rational numbers can be defined" in the standard eff
ective/computable sense: there exists an algorithm that produces a sequence of r
ationals converging to the target. This is the framework of *computable real num
bers* — a real number $y$ is **computable** if there exists a computable sequenc
e of rationals $(q_n)$ with $|q_n - y| \leq 2^{-n}$ for all $n$.

**Step 1: There exist non-computable irrational numbers in $[1,2]$.**

A real number is computable if and only if some Turing machine can approximate i
t to any prescribed precision $2^{-n}$. Since there are only countably many Turi
ng machines, the set of computable reals is countable. The set of irrationals in
 $[1,2]$ is uncountable. Therefore, uncountably many irrationals in $[1,2]$ are
non-computable.

**Step 2: If $x \in [1,2]$ is non-computable, then $\log_2 x$ is non-computable.
**

Suppose for contradiction that $\log_2 x$ is computable. The function $f(y) = 2^
y$ is a computable function on $[0,1]$: it can be effectively approximated to an
y precision via its Taylor expansion (with computable approximations of the cons
tant $\ln 2$, which is itself a computable real). Since $x = 2^{\log_2 x} = f(\l
og_2 x)$, and a computable function applied to a computable input yields a compu
table output, $x$ would be computable — contradicting our assumption that $x$ is
 non-computable. Hence $\log_2 x$ is non-computable.

**Step 3: A non-computable real admits no effectively convergent computable rati
onal sequence.**

By the definition of computability for reals, $y$ is computable if and only if t
here exists a computable sequence of rationals converging to $y$ with an effecti
ve (computable) convergence rate. Since $\log_2 x$ is non-computable (Step 2), n
o such effectively convergent computable sequence of rationals can converge to i
t.

**Step 4: The obstruction is even stronger — countability alone suffices.**

Even if we relax the requirement and ask only for a computable sequence of ratio
nals converging to $\log_2 x$ (with no computable convergence rate), the answer
remains negative for most $x$. The set of computable sequences of rationals is c
ountable (one per Turing machine), and each convergent sequence has at most one
limit. Thus the set of reals that are limits of computable sequences of rational
s is at most countable. On the other hand, $\log_2 : [1,2] \to [0,1]$ is a conti
nuous, strictly increasing bijection, hence injective. Since the irrationals in
$[1,2]$ are uncountable, the set $\{\log_2 x : x \text{ irrational in } [1,2]\}$
 is uncountable. Therefore, for uncountably many irrational $x \in [1,2]$, the v
alue $\log_2 x$ is not the limit of *any* computable sequence of rationals whats
oever.

**Conclusion.**

For every non-computable irrational $x \in [1,2]$ — and such $x$ exist in uncoun
table abundance — no effectively definable sequence of rational numbers converge
s to $\log_2 x$. The non-computability of $x$ propagates through the (computable
) map $\log_2$ to render $\log_2 x$ non-computable, placing it outside the reach
 of any algorithmic rational approximation. Therefore, such a sequence **cannot*
* be defined for every irrational $x \in [1,2]$.
