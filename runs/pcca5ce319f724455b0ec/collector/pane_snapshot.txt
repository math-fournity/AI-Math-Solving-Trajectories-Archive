## Proof

We have $Y_n = \frac{1}{n^3}\sum_{k=1}^n \frac{X_k^2}{k}$, where $X_k \sim \math
rm{Uniform}(0,k)$ are independent.

**Key observation: a deterministic bound.** Since each $X_k \in (0,k)$, we have
$0 < X_k^2 < k^2$, hence

$$0 < \frac{X_k^2}{k} < k.$$

Summing over $k = 1, \dots, n$:

$$0 < \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{X_k^2}{k} < \sum_{k=1}^n k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}.$$

Dividing by $n^3$:

$$0 < Y_n = \frac{1}{n^3}\sum_{k=1}^n \frac{X_k^2}{k} < \frac{n(n+1)}{2n^3} = \f
rac{n+1}{2n^2}.$$

**Taking the limit.** As $n \to \infty$,

$$\frac{n+1}{2n^2} = \frac{1}{2n} + \frac{1}{2n^2} \longrightarrow 0.$$

By the squeeze theorem (applied pathwise, for every realization of the sequence
$\{X_k\}$),

$$Y_n \longrightarrow 0.$$

**Conclusion.** The convergence holds for *every* sample point $\omega$ in the u
nderlying probability space — it is in fact *sure* convergence, which is stronge
r than almost sure convergence. The independence and the specific uniform distri
bution play no role beyond guaranteeing the support constraint $X_k \in (0,k)$.

The sequence $\{Y_n\}$ converges almost surely (indeed surely), and

$$\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty} Y_n = 0}.$$
