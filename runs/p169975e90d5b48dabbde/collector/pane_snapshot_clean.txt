## Solution

The matrix $A[k]$ has entries $A[k]_{m,n} = e^{(2\pi i\, mn)/k}$, where $m,n$ ra
nge over a complete residue system modulo $k$ (e.g. $0,1,\dots,k-1$). This is pr
ecisely the (unnormalized) **Discrete Fourier Transform matrix**.

### Claim

The inverse is

$$A[k]^{-1}_{m,n} \;=\; \frac{1}{k}\,e^{-(2\pi i\, mn)/k}.$$

That is, $A[k]^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{k}\,\overline{A[k]} = \tfrac{1}{k}\,A[k]^{*}$ (c
onjugate transpose, since $A[k]$ is symmetric so transpose = itself).

### Proof

Let $B$ be the candidate matrix with entries $B_{n,p} = \tfrac{1}{k}e^{-(2\pi i\
, np)/k}$. We compute the $(m,p)$-entry of the product $A[k]\,B$:

$$(A[k]\,B)_{m,p} \;=\; \sum_{n=0}^{k-1} A[k]_{m,n}\,B_{n,p} \;=\; \frac{1}{k}\s
um_{n=0}^{k-1} e^{(2\pi i\, mn)/k}\,e^{-(2\pi i\, np)/k}.$$

Combining exponents:

$$(A[k]\,B)_{m,p} \;=\; \frac{1}{k}\sum_{n=0}^{k-1} e^{(2\pi i\, n(m-p))/k}.$$

This is a **finite geometric sum** with ratio $r = e^{(2\pi i\,(m-p))/k}$.

**Case 1: $m \equiv p \pmod{k}$.** Then $r = e^{0} = 1$, and the sum equals $k$,
 giving

$$(A[k]\,B)_{m,p} = \frac{1}{k}\cdot k = 1.$$

**Case 2: $m \not\equiv p \pmod{k}$.** Then $r \neq 1$ (since $0 < |m-p| < k$ ma
kes $r$ a nontrivial $k$-th root of unity). The geometric sum gives

$$\sum_{n=0}^{k-1} r^{n} = \frac{r^{k}-1}{r-1} = \frac{e^{2\pi i\,(m-p)}-1}{r-1}
 = \frac{1-1}{r-1} = 0,$$

so $(A[k]\,B)_{m,p} = 0$.

Combining both cases:

$$(A[k]\,B)_{m,p} = \delta_{m,p} = (I)_{m,p}.$$

Hence $A[k]\,B = I$, which proves $B = A[k]^{-1}$.

### Orthogonality Principle

The key fact underlying the proof is the **orthogonality of roots of unity**:

$$\sum_{n=0}^{k-1} e^{(2\pi i\, n\, j)/k} = \begin{cases} k & \text{if } j \equi
v 0 \pmod{k},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

The columns of $A[k]$ are mutually orthogonal (as vectors in $\mathbb{C}^{k}$),
each with squared norm $k$. Normalizing by $\tfrac{1}{k}$ and conjugating (to re
verse the sign in the exponent) yields the inverse.

### Final Answer

$$\boxed{A[k]^{-1}_{m,n} \;=\; \frac{1}{k}\,e^{-(2\pi i\, mn)/k}}$$

Equivalently, $A[k]^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{k}\,\overline{A[k]}$, i.e. the inverse is $
\frac{1}{k}$ times the complex conjugate of $A[k]$.
