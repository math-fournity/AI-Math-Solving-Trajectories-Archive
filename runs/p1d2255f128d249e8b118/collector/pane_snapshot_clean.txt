Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether $g(x) = \int_0^1 f(t)\,t^x\
,dt$ is necessarily real-analytic on $(-1,\infty)$, given $f:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ is $
C^\infty$ (but not necessarily real-analytic).

**Answer: Yes, $g$ is necessarily real-analytic on $(-1,\infty)$.**

In fact, only boundedness and measurability of $f$ are needed — the smoothness h
ypothesis is far more than required. The analyticity comes entirely from the ker
nel $t^x = e^{x\ln t}$.

---

**Proof.**

**Step 1: Setup and domain.** For $x > -1$, the integral converges since $t^x$ i
s integrable near $t=0$ (as $\int_0^1 t^x\,dt = \frac{1}{x+1} < \infty$) and $f$
 is bounded. So $g$ is well-defined on $(-1,\infty)$.

**Step 2: Differentiation under the integral sign.** We claim that $g$ is $C^\in
fty$ on $(-1,\infty)$ with
$$g^{(n)}(x) = \int_0^1 f(t)\,t^x\,(\ln t)^n\,dt, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$

Fix a compact subinterval $[a,b] \subset (-1,\infty)$ with $a > -1$. For $x \in
[a,b]$, the $n$-th derivative of the integrand $f(t)\,t^x$ with respect to $x$ i
s $f(t)\,t^x\,(\ln t)^n$, which is dominated by
$$|f(t)|\,t^a\,|\ln t|^n \leq t^a\,|\ln t|^n.$$
This dominating function is integrable on $[0,1]$: substituting $u = -\ln t$,
$$\int_0^1 t^a\,|\ln t|^n\,dt = \int_0^\infty u^n\,e^{-(a+1)u}\,du = \frac{n!}{(
a+1)^{n+1}} < \infty,$$
since $a+1 > 0$. By the dominated convergence theorem, differentiation under the
 integral is justified, giving the formula for $g^{(n)}$.

**Step 3: Taylor series representation.** Fix $x_0 \in (-1,\infty)$. For $t \in
(0,1]$ and $x$ with $|x - x_0| < x_0 + 1$, expand:
$$t^x = t^{x_0}\,e^{(x-x_0)\ln t} = t^{x_0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(x-x_0)^n\,
(\ln t)^n}{n!}.$$

We interchange sum and integral. The partial sums are dominated as follows: for
$|x - x_0| \leq r < x_0 + 1$,
$$\left|f(t)\,t^{x_0}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{(x-x_0)^n(\ln t)^n}{n!}\right| \leq t^{
x_0}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{r^n\,|\ln t|^n}{n!} \leq t^{x_0}\,e^{r\,|\ln t|} = t^{x_
0 - r}.$$

Since $x_0 - r > x_0 - (x_0+1) = -1$, the function $t^{x_0 - r}$ is integrable o
n $[0,1]$. By dominated convergence:
$$g(x) = \int_0^1 f(t)\,t^{x_0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(x-x_0)^n(\ln t)^n}{n!}\
,dt = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(x-x_0)^n}{n!}\underbrace{\int_0^1 f(t)\,t^{x_0}(
\ln t)^n\,dt}_{=\,g^{(n)}(x_0)}.$$

Therefore:
$$g(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{g^{(n)}(x_0)}{n!}(x - x_0)^n \qquad \text{for
} |x - x_0| < x_0 + 1.$$

**Step 4: Conclusion.** The Taylor series of $g$ at $x_0$ converges to $g(x)$ in
 the open interval $(x_0 - (x_0+1),\; x_0 + (x_0+1)) = (-1,\; 2x_0+1)$, which is
 an open neighborhood of $x_0$ contained in $(-1,\infty)$. Since $x_0 \in (-1,\i
nfty)$ was arbitrary, $g$ is real-analytic at every point of $(-1,\infty)$.

**Remark.** The radius of convergence of the Taylor series at $x_0$ is at least
$x_0 + 1$, which is exactly the distance from $x_0$ to the boundary point $-1$.
The smoothness of $f$ plays no essential role — only $|f(t)| \leq 1$ (boundednes
s) and integrability were used. The real-analyticity of $g$ is inherited from th
e real-analyticity of the kernel $t^x = e^{x\ln t}$ in the variable $x$, preserv
ed under integration against a bounded measurable weight.
