### Step 1: Locate the dominant singularity

$F(x)=\dfrac{1}{(2-x-x^{5})^{2011}}$ is singular where $2-x-x^5=0$, i.e. $x^5+x-
2=0$.

**$x=1$ is a root:** $1+1-2=0$. ✓

Factoring: $x^5+x-2=(x-1)(x^4+x^3+x^2+x+2)$.

**Claim:** $x=1$ is the unique root of minimum modulus, and all other roots sati
sfy $|x|>1$.

*Proof.* For $|x|<1$: $|x^5+x|\le |x|^5+|x|<1+1=2$, so $x^5+x\ne 2$; no root ins
ide the open unit disk. On $|x|=1$, equality $|x^5+x|=2$ holds only when $x^5$ a
nd $x$ point in the same direction, i.e. $x^4=1$, giving $x\in\{1,-1,i,-i\}$. Ch
ecking: $x=1\Rightarrow 2$ ✓ (root); $x=-1\Rightarrow -2\ne 2$; $x=\pm i$ give n
on-real values $\ne 2$. So $x=1$ is the **only** root on $|x|=1$, and it is the
unique singularity of minimal modulus $\rho=1$. ∎

### Step 2: Local behavior at $x=1$

Expand $2-x-x^5$ near $x=1$ with $u=1-x$:
$$x^5=(1-u)^5=1-5u+10u^2-10u^3+5u^4-u^5,$$
$$2-x-x^5=2-(1-u)-(1-5u+10u^2-\cdots)=6u-10u^2+10u^3-5u^4+u^5=u\,h(x),$$
where $h(x)=6-10u+10u^2-5u^3+u^4$ is analytic near $x=1$ with $h(1)=6\ne 0$.

Thus
$$F(x)=\frac{1}{(1-x)^{2011}\,h(x)^{2011}}=G(x)\cdot(1-x)^{-2011},\qquad G(x):=h
(x)^{-2011}.$$

$G$ is analytic at $x=1$ (the nearest zero of $h$ is a root of $2-x-x^5=0$ other
 than $1$, hence of modulus $>1$), with
$$G(1)=6^{-2011}.$$

### Step 3: Extract asymptotics of $a_n=[x^n]F(x)$

Using $(1-x)^{-2011}=\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge 0}\binom{n+2010}{2010}x^n$ and $\bi
nom{n+2010}{2010}=\dfrac{(n+1)(n+2)\cdots(n+2010)}{2010!}\sim\dfrac{n^{2010}}{20
10!}$:

Since $G$ is analytic at $x=1$ (radius of convergence strictly larger than $1$),
 the singularity-analysis transfer theorem gives
$$a_n \sim G(1)\binom{n+2010}{2010}\sim \frac{1}{6^{2011}}\cdot\frac{n^{2010}}{2
010!}.$$

The correction from $G(x)-G(1)=O(1-x)$ contributes $O(n^{2009})$, which is negli
gible compared to $n^{2010}$.

### Step 4: Identify $(c,d)$

$$\frac{a_n}{n^{2010}}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{1}{6^{2011}\cdot 2010!}.$$

So $d=2010$ and $c=\dfrac{1}{6^{2011}\cdot 2010!}$, both positive reals.

**Final Answer:** $\boxed{\left(\dfrac{1}{6^{2011}\cdot 2010!},\;2010\right)}$
