## Proof

**Answer: Yes, two different polynomials of the same degree $d$ can appear in th
e factorization of $x^{p^n}-x$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$.**

### Key fact

The polynomial $x^{p^n}-x$ is precisely the product of all distinct monic irredu
cible polynomials in $\mathbb{F}_p[x]$ whose degree divides $n$. This is a stand
ard result: the roots of $x^{p^n}-x$ in its splitting field are exactly the elem
ents of $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$, and the minimal polynomial over $\mathbb{F}_p$ of an
y $\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ has degree $d$ where $d\mid n$ (since $\mathbb{F}_
{p^d}=\mathbb{F}_p(\alpha)\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ iff $d\mid n$). Collecting
all these minimal polynomials gives the factorization.

### Counting irreducibles of a fixed degree

The number of monic irreducible polynomials of degree $d$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$ is
 given by the classical formula

$$N_p(d)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k\mid d}\mu(k)\,p^{d/k},$$

where $\mu$ is the Möbius function. For $d\geq 2$ this number is generally large
r than $1$.

### Concrete example

Take $p=2$ and $d=3$. Then

$$N_2(3)=\frac{1}{3}\bigl(\mu(1)\cdot 2^3+\mu(3)\cdot 2^1\bigr)=\frac{1}{3}(8-2)
=2.$$

Indeed, the two monic irreducible cubics over $\mathbb{F}_2$ are

$$f_1(x)=x^3+x+1,\qquad f_2(x)=x^3+x^2+1.$$

Both are irreducible (neither has a root in $\mathbb{F}_2$, and being cubic this
 suffices), and they are distinct.

Now take $n=3$ (so $d=3\mid n=3$). The factorization of $x^{2^3}-x=x^8-x$ over $
\mathbb{F}_2$ is

$$x^8-x=x(x+1)(x^3+x+1)(x^3+x^2+1).$$

Here $f_1$ and $f_2$ are **two different polynomials of the same degree $d=3$**,
 and both appear in the factorization.

### General justification

For any prime $p$ and any $d\geq 2$ with $d\mid n$, the count $N_p(d)$ equals th
e number of distinct monic irreducible factors of degree $d$ in $x^{p^n}-x$. Sin
ce

$$N_p(d)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k\mid d}\mu(k)\,p^{d/k}\geq\frac{1}{d}\bigl(p^d-p^{d/2
}\cdot(\text{number of nontrivial divisors})\bigr),$$

and more directly $N_p(d)\geq\frac{p^d-p^{d-1}-p^{d/2}-\cdots}{d}>1$ for all $p$
 and all $d\geq 2$ (one checks $N_p(2)=\frac{p^2-p}{2}\geq 1$, with equality onl
y when $p=2$; and $N_p(d)\geq 2$ for all $d\geq 3$), there are in general **mult
iple** distinct irreducible polynomials of the same degree $d$ contributing to t
he factorization.

The only case where a given degree $d\mid n$ contributes a **single** irreducibl
e factor is $d=1$ (giving $x$ and $x-1,\dots,x-(p-1)$, actually $p$ linear facto
rs) — and even then there are $p$ of them. For $d=2$ with $p=2$, there is exactl
y one irreducible quadratic ($x^2+x+1$), but this is the unique exception; for $
p\geq 3$ or $d\geq 3$ there are always at least two.

### Conclusion

Two different polynomials of the same degree $d$ **can** appear in the factoriza
tion of $x^{p^n}-x$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$, and in fact this is the generic situati
on: the number of distinct monic irreducible polynomials of degree $d$ over $\ma
thbb{F}_p$ is $N_p(d)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k\mid d}\mu(k)p^{d/k}$, which is greater
than $1$ for essentially all $d\geq 2$ (the sole exception being $d=2$, $p=2$).
The explicit example $x^8-x=x(x+1)(x^3+x+1)(x^3+x^2+1)$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$ exhi
bits two distinct irreducible cubics in the same factorization.
