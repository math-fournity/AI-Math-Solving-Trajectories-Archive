## Solution

We find all real polynomials $P(x,y)$ satisfying
$$P(x,y)P(z,t)=P(xz-yt,\,xt+yz).\tag{$\ast$}$$

The answer is:
$$\boxed{P\equiv 0 \quad\text{or}\quad P(x,y)=(x^{2}+y^{2})^{n}\ \text{for some
integer }n\ge 0.}$$

---

### Step 1. Two constant solutions and a normalization

Set $(z,t)=(1,0)$ in $(\ast)$:
$$P(x,y)\,P(1,0)=P(x,y)\qquad\forall x,y.$$
Hence either $P\equiv 0$, or $P(1,0)=1$. The zero polynomial clearly works, so *
*assume from now on $P(1,0)=1$**.

Set $(x,y)=(0,0)$ in $(\ast)$:
$$P(0,0)\,P(z,t)=P(0,0)\qquad\forall z,t.$$
So either $P(0,0)=1$ (forcing $P\equiv 1$, which works and equals $(x^{2}+y^{2})
^{0}$), or $P(0,0)=0$. **Assume $P(0,0)=0$**, so $P$ is nonconstant.

### Step 2. $P$ is homogeneous

Write $P=\sum_{k=0}^{d}P_{k}$ with $P_{k}$ homogeneous of total degree $k$ and $
P_{d}\neq 0$, $d\ge 1$. We have $P_{0}=P(0,0)=0$.

The right–hand side $P(xz-yt,xt+yz)$: each $P_{k}$, being homogeneous of degree
$k$, becomes under substitution a polynomial that is homogeneous of degree $k$ i
n $(x,y)$ **and** degree $k$ in $(z,t)$ — i.e. of bidegree $(k,k)$.

The left–hand side $P(x,y)P(z,t)=\sum_{i,j}P_{i}(x,y)P_{j}(z,t)$ has bidegree $(
i,j)$ whenever $P_{i},P_{j}\neq 0$.

Equating bidegree supports: if two distinct degrees $p<q$ had $P_{p},P_{q}\neq 0
$, then bidegree $(p,q)$ would occur on the left but is impossible on the right
(which only has $(k,k)$). Contradiction. Hence **exactly one** $P_{k}$ is nonzer
o, i.e. $P$ is homogeneous of degree $d\ge 1$.

### Step 3. Complex coordinates

Introduce $u=x+iy$, $v=x-iy$ (so $x=(u+v)/2$, $y=(u-v)/(2i)$) and similarly $u'=
z+it$, $v'=z-it$. Complex multiplication satisfies
$$(x+iy)(z+it)=xz-yt+i(xt+yz),$$
so the right–hand argument of $(\ast)$ corresponds to $(uu',\,vv')$. Define
$$Q(u,v):=P\!\bigl(\tfrac{u+v}{2},\,\tfrac{u-v}{2i}\bigr).$$
Then $Q$ is homogeneous of degree $d$ in $(u,v)$ and $(\ast)$ becomes
$$Q(u,v)\,Q(u',v')=Q(uu',\,vv').\tag{$\ast\ast$}$$

Write $Q(u,v)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}c_{k}\,u^{k}v^{d-k}$. Expanding $(\ast\ast)$:
$$\sum_{a,b}c_{a}c_{b}\,u^{a}v^{d-a}\,u'^{\,b}v'^{\,d-b}
=\sum_{m}c_{m}\,u^{m}u'^{\,m}\,v^{d-m}v'^{\,d-m}.$$
Since $u,v,u',v'$ are independent over $\mathbb C$, comparing the coefficient of
 $u^{a}v^{d-a}u'^{\,b}v'^{\,d-b}$ gives
$$c_{a}c_{b}=0\ \ (a\neq b),\qquad c_{m}^{2}=c_{m}.$$
Thus at most one $c_{m}$ is nonzero, and that one equals $1$. So
$$Q(u,v)=u^{m}v^{d-m}\quad\text{for some }m\in\{0,\dots,d\}.$$

### Step 4. Reality forces $m=d/2$

$P$ has real coefficients, so $Q(u,v)=Q(v,u)$ (conjugation swaps $u\leftrightarr
ow v$ and fixes $P$). But $u^{m}v^{d-m}=v^{m}u^{d-m}$ forces $m=d-m$, i.e. $d=2m
$. Hence $d$ is even, say $d=2n$ with $n\ge 1$, and
$$Q(u,v)=(uv)^{n}\implies P(x,y)=(x^{2}+y^{2})^{n}.$$

### Step 5. Verification

For $P(x,y)=(x^{2}+y^{2})^{n}$:
$$(xz-yt)^{2}+(xt+yz)^{2}=x^{2}z^{2}+y^{2}t^{2}+x^{2}t^{2}+y^{2}z^{2}=(x^{2}+y^{
2})(z^{2}+t^{2}),$$
so $P(xz-yt,xt+yz)=\bigl((x^{2}+y^{2})(z^{2}+t^{2})\bigr)^{n}=P(x,y)P(z,t)$. ✓

The case $n=0$ gives $P\equiv 1$, already accounted for; $P\equiv 0$ was handled
 in Step 1.

---

**All solutions:** $P\equiv 0$, or $P(x,y)=(x^{2}+y^{2})^{n}$ for some integer $
n\ge 0$.
