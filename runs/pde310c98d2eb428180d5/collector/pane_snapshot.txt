## Identifying $\chi$

The conditions on $\chi$ — namely $\chi(1)=1$, $\chi(mn)=\chi(m)\chi(n)$, and no
nconstancy — make $\chi$ a **nontrivial group homomorphism** from $(\mathbb{Z}/p
\mathbb{Z})^*$ to $\{-1,1\}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

Since $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^*$ is cyclic of order $p-1$, such homomorphisms
exist iff $2\mid(p-1)$, i.e., $p$ is odd. There is exactly one nontrivial homomo
rphism (sending a primitive root $g$ to $-1$), which is precisely the **Legendre
 symbol** $\chi(a)=\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)$.

For $p=2$: the domain $\{1\}$ has one element, so no nonconstant function to $\{
-1,1\}$ exists. Thus $p=2$ is excluded.

## Evaluating the sum

For odd prime $p$, the sum is:
$$S = \sum_{a=1}^{p-1} a^{\chi(a)} = \sum_{\substack{a \in QR \\ \chi(a)=1}} a^1
 \;+\; \sum_{\substack{a \in QNR \\ \chi(a)=-1}} a^{-1} = \sum_{a\in QR} a \;+\;
 \sum_{a\in QNR} a^{-1} \pmod{p}$$

**Key fact:** The inversion map $\phi: a\mapsto a^{-1}$ preserves quadratic resi
duosity.

*Proof.* Let $g$ be a primitive root mod $p$. Then $a$ is a QR iff $a=g^{2k}$ fo
r some $k$. In that case $a^{-1}=g^{-2k}=g^{2(p-1-k)}$, which is also an even po
wer of $g$, hence a QR. Conversely, if $a^{-1}$ is a QR, then $a=(a^{-1})^{-1}$
is a QR by the same argument. ∎

Since $\phi$ is a bijection on $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^*$ that maps QR→QR and
QNR→QNR, restricting to QNR gives a bijection QNR→QNR. Therefore:
$$\sum_{a\in QNR} a^{-1} = \sum_{a\in QNR} a$$

Substituting back:
$$S = \sum_{a\in QR} a + \sum_{a\in QNR} a = \sum_{a=1}^{p-1} a = \frac{p(p-1)}{
2}$$

For any odd prime $p$, $\frac{p(p-1)}{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p}$.

## Verification

- $p=3$: $\chi(1)=1,\chi(2)=-1$. Sum $= 1^1+2^{-1}=1+2=3\equiv 0\pmod{3}$. ✓
- $p=5$: QRs $\{1,4\}$, QNRs $\{2,3\}$. Sum $=1+4+3+2=10\equiv 0\pmod{5}$. ✓
- $p=7$: QRs $\{1,2,4\}$, QNRs $\{3,5,6\}$. Sum $=1+2+4+5+3+6=21\equiv 0\pmod{7}
