## Proof

We compute the intersection $\mathbb{R}P^2 \cap \mathbb{C}P^1$ inside $\mathbb{C
}P^2$.

**Setup.** The real projective plane is
$$\mathbb{R}P^2 = \{[x_0:x_1:x_2] \mid x_i \in \mathbb{R}\} \subset \mathbb{C}P^
2.$$
The complex line (projective hyperplane) is
$$\mathbb{C}P^1 = \{[z_0:z_1:z_2] \mid a_0 z_0 + a_1 z_1 + a_2 z_2 = 0\}, \quad
a_i \in \mathbb{C}.$$
A point of $\mathbb{R}P^2$ lies on this line iff
$$a_0 x_0 + a_1 x_1 + a_2 x_2 = 0, \qquad x_i \in \mathbb{R}.$$

**Splitting into real and imaginary parts.** Write $a_j = \alpha_j + i\,\beta_j$
 with $\alpha_j, \beta_j \in \mathbb{R}$. Since the $x_j$ are real, the single c
omplex equation becomes **two real equations**:
$$\alpha_0 x_0 + \alpha_1 x_1 + \alpha_2 x_2 = 0, \tag{R}$$
$$\beta_0 x_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 = 0. \tag{I}$$

Each is a real linear form on $\mathbb{R}^3$, i.e., a projective line in $\mathb
b{R}P^2$. The intersection $\mathbb{R}P^2 \cap \mathbb{C}P^1$ is the intersectio
n of these two projective lines in $\mathbb{R}P^2$.

**Generic case (the complex line is not defined over $\mathbb{R}$).** The two re
al linear forms $(\alpha_0,\alpha_1,\alpha_2)$ and $(\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2)$ a
re linearly independent over $\mathbb{R}$ — equivalently, the vector $(a_0,a_1,a
_2)$ is **not** a real scalar multiple of a real vector, i.e., the complex line
is not defined over $\mathbb{R}$. Two independent linear equations on $\mathbb{R
}^3$ cut out a **1-dimensional** subspace (a line through the origin), which cor
responds to exactly **one point** in $\mathbb{R}P^2$.

**Verification by example.** Take $a_0 = 1,\; a_1 = i,\; a_2 = 0$. The equation
$x_0 + i\,x_1 = 0$ gives $x_0 = 0$ and $x_1 = 0$, so the unique solution is $[0:
0:1]$. ✓

Take $a_0 = 1,\; a_1 = 1+i,\; a_2 = i$. Then (R): $x_0 + x_1 = 0$; (I): $x_1 + x
_2 = 0$. The solution is $[−1:1:−1]$, a single point. ✓

**Degenerate case (line defined over $\mathbb{R}$).** If all $a_j$ share the sam
e complex argument, i.e., $(\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2) = \lambda(\alpha_0,\alpha_1
,\alpha_2)$ for some $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$, then (R) and (I) are proportional
 and define the same projective line in $\mathbb{R}P^2$. The intersection is the
n an entire $\mathbb{R}P^1$ (infinitely many points). This is the non-generic, s
pecial case where the complex hyperplane is actually defined over $\mathbb{R}$.

**Conclusion.** For a generic complex line $\mathbb{C}P^1 \subset \mathbb{C}P^2$
 (not defined over $\mathbb{R}$), the intersection with $\mathbb{R}P^2$ consists
 of exactly

$$\boxed{1}$$

point.
