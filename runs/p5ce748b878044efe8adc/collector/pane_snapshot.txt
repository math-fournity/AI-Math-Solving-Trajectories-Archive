## Solution

**Example.** Let $X$ be the **Cantor set** $C \subset [0,1]$.

We prove $C^2 \cong C^3$ (in fact $C^n \cong C$ for every $n \geq 1$).

---

### Step 1. $C$ is a compact metric space with at least two points.

$C$ is a closed subset of the compact interval $[0,1]$, hence compact. It inheri
ts the Euclidean metric, so it is a compact metric space. It contains $0$ and $1
$ (among uncountably many other points), so it has at least two points.

### Step 2. $C \cong \{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}$ (Cantor space).

Every $x \in C$ admits a ternary expansion $x = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} a_k 3^{-k}$
with each $a_k \in \{0,2\}$ (the endpoints of removed intervals have two expansi
ons, but choosing the one ending in all $0$s or all $2$s consistently gives a bi
jection). Define
$$\varphi : C \to \{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}, \qquad \varphi(x) = (a_1/2,\, a_2/2,\, a
_3/2,\, \dots).$$
This is a bijection. Both $C$ (subspace of $[0,1]$) and $\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}$ (
countable product of finite discrete spaces, hence compact Hausdorff) are compac
t Hausdorff, so the continuous bijection $\varphi$ is a homeomorphism.

### Step 3. $\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}} \times \{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}} \cong \{0,1\}^{\ma
thbb{N}}$.

There is a bijection $\mathbb{N} \sqcup \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$, e.g. the int
erleaving map $2k \mapsto k$, $2k+1 \mapsto k$ (or more explicitly, send the $k$
-th coordinate of the first factor to coordinate $2k$ and the $k$-th coordinate
of the second factor to coordinate $2k+1$). This induces a homeomorphism
$$\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}} \times \{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}} \;\cong\; \{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}
 \sqcup \mathbb{N}} \;\cong\; \{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}.$$
Concretely, the interleaving map
$$(b_1, b_2, \dots),\, (c_1, c_2, \dots) \;\longmapsto\; (b_1, c_1, b_2, c_2, \d
ots)$$
is a homeomorphism $\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}} \times \{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}} \to \{0,1\}
^{\mathbb{N}}$.

### Step 4. Conclusion.

Combining Steps 2 and 3:
$$C^2 \cong \{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}} \times \{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}} \cong \{0,1\}^{\mat
hbb{N}} \cong C.$$
Iterating, $C^3 \cong C^2 \times C \cong C \times C \cong C$. Therefore
$$C^2 \cong C \cong C^3,$$
so in particular $C^2 \cong C^3$.

The Cantor set $C$ is the desired compact metric space with at least two points
satisfying $X^2 \cong X^3$. $\blacksquare$
