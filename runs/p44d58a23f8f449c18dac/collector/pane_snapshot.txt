## Proof

The answer depends on $n$:
- **$n = 1$: No** such function exists.
- **$n \geq 2$: Yes**, such a function exists.

### Case $n = 1$: No such function exists

$B^1 = (-1,1)$. Suppose $f:(-1,1)\to(-1,1)$ is continuous and open, but not inje
ctive. Then there exist $a < b$ in $(-1,1)$ with $f(a)=f(b)$.

**Subcase 1: $f$ is constant on $[a,b]$.** Then for any open subinterval $U \sub
set (a,b)$, $f(U)$ is a single point, which is not open in $(-1,1)$. This contra
dicts $f$ being open.

**Subcase 2: $f$ is not constant on $[a,b]$.** By continuity, $f$ attains a maxi
mum $M$ and minimum $m$ on $[a,b]$. Since $f$ is not constant, either $M > f(a)$
 or $m < f(a)$.

- If $M > f(a) = f(b)$, the maximum is attained at some interior point $c \in (a
,b)$ with $f(c) = M$. For any sufficiently small open interval $U$ around $c$, $
f(U) \subseteq (-\infty, M]$ and $M \in f(U)$. Thus $f(U)$ has a greatest elemen
t $M$ and cannot be open in $\mathbb{R}$ (any open set containing $M$ must conta
in points $> M$).

- If $m < f(a)$, the symmetric argument applies at the interior minimum.

Both subcases contradict $f$ being open. Therefore every continuous open map $f:
(-1,1)\to(-1,1)$ is injective. $\square$

### Case $n \geq 2$: Such a function exists

**Construction.** Identify $\mathbb{R}^n \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{R}^{n-2
}$, writing points as $(z, w)$ with $z \in \mathbb{C}$, $w \in \mathbb{R}^{n-2}$
. Define
$$f(z, w) = (z^2, w).$$

**$f$ maps $B^n$ into $B^n$:** Let $(z,w) \in B^n$, so $|z|^2 + \|w\|^2 < 1$. Se
t $a = |z|^2$, $b = \|w\|^2$, so $a + b < 1$ with $a, b \geq 0$. Then
$$|z^2|^2 + \|w\|^2 = |z|^4 + \|w\|^2 = a^2 + b.$$
Since $0 \leq a < 1$, we have $a^2 \leq a$, so $a^2 + b \leq a + b < 1$. Thus $f
(z,w) \in B^n$.

**$f$ is continuous:** The map $z \mapsto z^2$ is a polynomial (hence continuous
), and $w \mapsto w$ is continuous. Their product is continuous.

**$f$ is open:** We use two facts:

*Fact 1: $g:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$, $g(z)=z^2$, is an open map.* For $z_0 \neq
 0$, $g'(z_0) = 2z_0 \neq 0$, so $g$ is a local diffeomorphism near $z_0$ and ma
ps neighborhoods of $z_0$ to neighborhoods of $z_0^2$. For $z_0 = 0$, the image
of the open disk $\{z : |z| < r\}$ is $\{w : |w| < r^2\}$, an open disk. Since $
g$ maps a neighborhood of every point to an open set, $g$ is open.

*Fact 2: The product of two open maps is open.* If $g:X\to Y$ and $h:W\to Z$ are
 open, then $g\times h: X\times W \to Y\times Z$ sends basic open sets $U\times
V$ to $g(U)\times h(V)$, which is open. Since basic open sets form a basis, $g\t
imes h$ is open.

Applying Fact 2 with $g(z) = z^2$ (open by Fact 1) and $h(w) = w$ (the identity,
 trivially open), the map $f = g \times h$ is open.

**$f$ is not injective:** For any $z \neq 0$, the points $(z, w)$ and $(-z, w)$
are distinct (since $z \neq -z$ when $z \neq 0$), but $f(z,w) = (z^2, w) = ((-z)
^2, w) = f(-z, w)$. For instance, $(\tfrac{1}{2}, 0, \dots, 0)$ and $(-\tfrac{1}
{2}, 0, \dots, 0)$ are distinct points of $B^n$ with the same image. $\square$

---

**Conclusion.** A continuous, open, non-injective map $f: B^n \to B^n$ exists if
 and only if $n \geq 2$.
