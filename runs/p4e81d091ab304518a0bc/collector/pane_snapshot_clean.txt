# Proof

Let $X$ be a normed vector space, $X^*$ its continuous dual, $X^{**} = (X^*)^*$
its double dual, and $J: X \to X^{**}$ the canonical isometric embedding defined
 by $J(x)(\varphi) = \varphi(x)$ for $\varphi \in X^*$.

**The two topologies.** The weak* topology on $X^*$ is $\sigma(X^*, X) = \sigma(
X^*, J(X))$, the coarsest topology making all maps $\varphi \mapsto \varphi(x) =
 J(x)(\varphi)$ continuous for $x \in X$. The weak topology on $X^*$ is $\sigma(
X^*, X^{**})$, the coarsest topology making all maps $\varphi \mapsto \Phi(\varp
hi)$ continuous for $\Phi \in X^{**}$.

Since $J(X) \subseteq X^{**}$, the weak* topology is generated by a subset of th
e functionals generating the weak topology. Hence the weak* topology is **always
** coarser than (or equal to) the weak topology on $X^*$:
$$\sigma(X^*, J(X)) \;\leq\; \sigma(X^*, X^{**}).$$

**Key fact (dual of a weak topology).** For any subspace $Y \subseteq X^{**}$ th
at separates points of $X^*$, the continuous dual of the locally convex space $(
X^*, \sigma(X^*, Y))$ is exactly $Y$ itself. This is a standard consequence of t
he Mackey–Arens theorem (or directly: a linear functional on $X^*$ is $\sigma(X^
*,Y)$-continuous iff it lies in $Y$).

We verify the separation hypothesis for both cases:
- $J(X)$ separates points of $X^*$: if $\varphi \neq 0$, pick $x \in X$ with $\v
arphi(x) \neq 0$; then $J(x)(\varphi) \neq 0$.
- $X^{**}$ separates points of $X^*$ trivially, being the full algebraic/continu
ous dual.

Therefore:
$$\bigl(X^*,\, \sigma(X^*, J(X))\bigr)^* = J(X) \qquad\text{(weak* topology)},$$
$$\bigl(X^*,\, \sigma(X^*, X^{**})\bigr)^* = X^{**} \qquad\text{(weak topology)}
.$$

**The implication.** The hypothesis says the weak* topology is "not strictly wea
ker" than the weak topology. Since weak* $\leq$ weak always holds, "not strictly
 weaker" forces
$$\sigma(X^*, J(X)) = \sigma(X^*, X^{**}).$$

Equal topologies have identical continuous duals, so
$$J(X) = X^{**}.$$

This is precisely the statement that the canonical embedding $J: X \to X^{**}$ i
s **surjective**, which is the definition of $X$ being **reflexive**.

(Remark: since $X^{**}$ is always a Banach space, $J(X) = X^{**}$ automatically
forces $X$ to be complete; thus a reflexive normed space is necessarily a Banach
 space.)

**Conclusion.** Yes. If the weak* topology on $X^*$ is not strictly weaker than
the weak topology on $X^*$, then $X$ is reflexive.
