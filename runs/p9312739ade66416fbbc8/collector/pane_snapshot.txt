## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, $f \geq 0$ almost everywhere.

**Step 1: $F$ is absolutely continuous.**

Since $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, the indefinite integral $F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x
} f(t)\,dt$ is absolutely continuous on every compact interval $[a,b]$. Indeed,
for any $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such that whenever $\{(a_i,b
_i)\}$ are disjoint intervals with $\sum (b_i - a_i) < \delta$,
$$\sum_i |F(b_i) - F(a_i)| = \sum_i \left|\int_{a_i}^{b_i} f(t)\,dt\right| \leq
\sum_i \int_{a_i}^{b_i} |f(t)|\,dt = \int_{\bigcup_i (a_i,b_i)} |f(t)|\,dt < \va
repsilon,$$
where the last inequality uses absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral of $
|f| \in L^1$.

**Step 2: A non-decreasing absolutely continuous function has non-negative deriv
ative a.e.**

A classical theorem (Lebesgue) states that any monotone function is differentiab
le a.e., and its derivative is non-negative a.e. when the function is non-decrea
sing. Since $F$ is non-decreasing by hypothesis, $F'(x) \geq 0$ for almost every
 $x$.

**Step 3: $F' = f$ a.e.**

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus for Lebesgue integrals, an absolutely con
tinuous function satisfies $F(x) = F(a) + \int_a^x F'(t)\,dt$ and $F' \in L^1_{\
text{loc}}$. Moreover, for an indefinite integral of an $L^1_{\text{loc}}$ funct
ion, the derivative equals the integrand a.e.:

$$F'(x) = f(x) \quad \text{for a.e. } x \in \mathbb{R}.$$

(This is the standard differentiation theorem: the derivative of the indefinite
Lebesgue integral recovers the integrand at every Lebesgue point of $f$, and alm
ost every point is a Lebesgue point of an $L^1_{\text{loc}}$ function.)

**Step 4: Conclusion.**

Combining Steps 2 and 3:
$$f(x) = F'(x) \geq 0 \quad \text{for a.e. } x \in \mathbb{R}.$$

Therefore $f$ is non-negative almost everywhere with respect to the Lebesgue mea
sure. $\blacksquare$
