Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether convergence of an unordered
 sum over an uncountable index set implies convergence of every countable sub-su
m, in a general normed vector space.

**Answer: Not necessarily.** The result holds in Banach spaces (complete normed
spaces) but fails in incomplete normed spaces. Below I give the proof of the pos
itive result in the Banach case, and an explicit counterexample in the incomplet
e case.

---

## Preliminaries: Definition

The unordered sum $\sum_{i \in J} x_i$ **converges** to $s \in X$ if for every $
\varepsilon > 0$ there exists a finite set $F_0 \subseteq J$ such that for every
 finite $F$ with $F_0 \subseteq F \subseteq J$,
$$\left\|\sum_{i \in F} x_i - s\right\| < \varepsilon.$$

Equivalently, the net of finite partial sums (directed by inclusion) converges t
o $s$.

A useful consequence: if $\sum_{i \in J} x_i$ converges, then $\{i \in J : \|x_i
\| \geq \varepsilon\}$ is finite for every $\varepsilon > 0$ (given $F_0$ as abo
ve, any $j \notin F_0$ with $\|x_j\| \geq \varepsilon$ yields a contradiction by
 comparing $F_0$ and $F_0 \cup \{j\}$). Hence only countably many $x_i$ are nonz
ero.

## Positive Result: Banach Space Case

**Theorem.** If $X$ is a Banach space and $\sum_{i \in J} x_i$ converges, then $
\sum_{i \in I} x_i$ converges for every countable $I \subseteq J$.

**Proof.** Suppose $\sum_{i \in J} x_i = s$. Let $I \subseteq J$ be countable an
d let $\varepsilon > 0$. Choose finite $F_0 \subseteq J$ such that $\|\sum_{i \i
n F} x_i - s\| < \varepsilon/2$ for all finite $F \supseteq F_0$. Set $G_0 = F_0
 \cap I$.

For any two finite sets $G_1, G_2$ with $G_0 \subseteq G_1, G_2 \subseteq I$, th
e sets $F_0 \cup G_1$ and $F_0 \cup G_2$ are finite subsets of $J$ containing $F
_0$, so:
$$\left\|\sum_{i \in F_0 \cup G_1} x_i - s\right\| < \varepsilon/2, \qquad \left
\|\sum_{i \in F_0 \cup G_2} x_i - s\right\| < \varepsilon/2.$$

Since $G_k \subseteq I$, we have $G_k \setminus F_0 = G_k \setminus G_0$, so:
$$\sum_{i \in F_0 \cup G_k} x_i = \sum_{i \in F_0} x_i + \sum_{i \in G_k \setmin
us G_0} x_i = \sum_{i \in F_0} x_i + \sum_{i \in G_k} x_i - \sum_{i \in G_0} x_i
.$$

Subtracting the two expressions:
$$\left\|\sum_{i \in G_1} x_i - \sum_{i \in G_2} x_i\right\| = \left\|\sum_{i \i
n F_0 \cup G_1} x_i - \sum_{i \in F_0 \cup G_2} x_i\right\| \leq \varepsilon.$$

Thus the net of partial sums over $I$ is Cauchy. Since $X$ is complete, every Ca
uchy net in $X$ converges (extract a Cauchy subsequence from the net, which conv
erges by completeness; the full net converges to the same limit). Therefore $\su
m_{i \in I} x_i$ converges. $\blacksquare$

## Counterexample: Incomplete Normed Space

The result **fails** in a normed space that is not complete.

**Setup.** Let $X = c_{00}$, the space of real sequences with finitely many nonz
ero terms, equipped with the $\ell^2$ norm $\|x\|_2 = \left(\sum_k |x_k|^2\right
)^{1/2}$. This is a normed vector space but is **not complete** (its completion
is $\ell^2$).

Let $J = \mathbb{R}$ (uncountable). Let $e_k$ denote the $k$-th standard basis v
ector. Define:
$$x_{2k-1} = \tfrac{1}{k}\, e_k, \qquad x_{2k} = -\tfrac{1}{k}\, e_k, \qquad k =
 1, 2, 3, \ldots$$
and $x_i = 0$ for $i \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{N}$.

**Claim 1.** $\sum_{i \in J} x_i$ converges to $0$ in $c_{00}$.

*Proof.* Let $\varepsilon > 0$. Choose $N$ with $\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{
k^2} < \varepsilon^2$. Set $F_0 = \{1, 2, \ldots, 2N\}$. For any finite $F \sups
eteq F_0$:

The pairs $(x_{2k-1}, x_{2k}) = (\frac{1}{k}e_k, -\frac{1}{k}e_k)$ for $k \leq N
$ are both in $F_0 \subseteq F$ and cancel, contributing $0$. The remaining term
s lie in $\{2k-1, 2k : k > N\}$, and for each $k > N$, at most one of the pair i
s in $F \setminus F_0$, contributing either $0$, $\frac{1}{k}e_k$, or $-\frac{1}
{k}e_k$ in coordinate $k$. These are orthogonal, so:

$$\left\|\sum_{i \in F} x_i\right\|_2^2 = \sum_{\substack{k > N \\ |\{2k-1,2k\}
\cap (F \setminus F_0)| = 1}} \frac{1}{k^2} \leq \sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{
k^2} < \varepsilon^2.$$

Hence $\|\sum_{i \in F} x_i\|_2 < \varepsilon$, proving convergence to $0 \in c_
{00}$. $\checkmark$

**Claim 2.** For the countable set $I = \{1, 3, 5, 7, \ldots\} = \{2k-1 : k \in
\mathbb{N}\} \subseteq J$, the sum $\sum_{i \in I} x_i$ does **not** converge in
 $c_{00}$.

*Proof.* For finite $G \subseteq I$, we have $\sum_{i \in G} x_i = \sum_{k\,:\,2
k-1 \in G} \frac{1}{k}\, e_k$. This net converges in $\ell^2$ to
$$t = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k}\, e_k = \left(1,\, \tfrac{1}{2},\, \tfrac{
1}{3},\, \ldots\right) \in \ell^2,$$
since for $G_0 = \{1, 3, \ldots, 2N-1\}$ and $G \supseteq G_0$:
$$\left\|\sum_{i \in G} x_i - t\right\|_2^2 = \sum_{\substack{k > N \\ 2k-1 \not
in G}} \frac{1}{k^2} \leq \sum_{k>N} \frac{1}{k^2} \to 0.$$

Now $t \notin c_{00}$ (it has infinitely many nonzero components). If the net co
nverged to some $s' \in c_{00}$, then since $c_{00} \hookrightarrow \ell^2$ is a
n isometric embedding, $s'$ would also be the $\ell^2$-limit. By uniqueness of l
imits in $\ell^2$, we would have $s' = t$, contradicting $t \notin c_{00}$.

Therefore $\sum_{i \in I} x_i$ does not converge in $X = c_{00}$. $\checkmark$

---

## Conclusion

In a general normed vector space $X$, convergence of $\sum_{i \in J} x_i$ does *
*not** guarantee convergence of $\sum_{i \in I} x_i$ for every countable $I \sub
seteq J$. The implication holds if and only if $X$ is complete (a Banach space):
 completeness ensures the Cauchy net of sub-partial-sums converges, while the co
unterexample above exploits incompleteness of $c_{00}$ to produce a countable su
b-sum whose limit escapes the space.
