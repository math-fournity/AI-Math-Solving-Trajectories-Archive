**Answer:** $S_n$ is not an integral domain for **every** non-negative integer $

## Proof

**Setup.** For each $n \geq 0$, I construct two functions $f, g \in C^n([0,1])$
such that $f \cdot g \equiv 0$, yet neither $f$ nor $g$ is identically zero. Thi
s exhibits a pair of zero divisors, proving $S_n$ is not an integral domain.

**Construction.** Let $c = \tfrac{1}{2}$. Define:

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} (c - x)^{n+1} & 0 \leq x \leq c, \\ 0 & c < x \leq 1, \en
d{cases} \qquad g(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & 0 \leq x \leq c, \\ (x - c)^{n+1} & c <
 x \leq 1. \end{cases}$$

**Claim 1: $f \in C^n([0,1])$.**

On $[0, c)$, $f(x) = (c-x)^{n+1}$ is a polynomial, hence smooth. On $(c, 1]$, $f
 \equiv 0$, also smooth. It remains to verify smoothness at $x = c$.

For $0 \leq k \leq n$, the $k$-th derivative on the left side is:

$$f^{(k)}(x) = (-1)^k \frac{(n+1)!}{(n+1-k)!}(c - x)^{n+1-k}, \quad x < c,$$

and $f^{(k)}(x) = 0$ for $x > c$. Since $n + 1 - k \geq 1$, we have:

$$\lim_{x \to c^-} f^{(k)}(x) = (-1)^k \frac{(n+1)!}{(n+1-k)!} \cdot 0^{n+1-k} =
 0 = \lim_{x \to c^+} f^{(k)}(x).$$

Thus each $f^{(k)}$ is continuous at $c$ for $k = 0, 1, \ldots, n$, so $f \in C^
n([0,1])$.

**Claim 2: $g \in C^n([0,1])$.**

By the identical argument (symmetric construction), $g^{(k)}(x) = \frac{(n+1)!}{
(n+1-k)!}(x-c)^{n+1-k}$ for $x > c$ and $g^{(k)}(x) = 0$ for $x < c$. The same l
imit argument shows $g^{(k)}$ is continuous at $c$ for all $k \leq n$. Hence $g
\in C^n([0,1])$.

**Claim 3: $f \cdot g \equiv 0$.**

- For $x \leq c$: $g(x) = 0$, so $f(x)g(x) = 0$.
- For $x > c$: $f(x) = 0$, so $f(x)g(x) = 0$.
- At $x = c$: $f(c) = 0$ and $g(c) = 0$.

Therefore $f \cdot g \equiv 0$ on $[0,1]$.

**Claim 4: Neither $f$ nor $g$ is identically zero.**

$$f(0) = \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n+1} \neq 0, \qquad g(1) = \left(\tfrac{1}{2
}\right)^{n+1} \neq 0.$$

**Conclusion.** For every non-negative integer $n$, the functions $f$ and $g$ ar
e nonzero elements of $S_n = C^n([0,1])$ whose product is zero. Thus $S_n$ has z
ero divisors and is **not** an integral domain for any $n \geq 0$.

The note that "$S_1$ and $S_2$ are not the answer" confirms that the answer is n
ot the restricted set $\{1, 2\}$ — rather, **every** non-negative integer $n$ is
 a value for which $S_n$ fails to be an integral domain. The key insight is that
 the exponent $n+1$ in the construction ensures exactly $n$ continuous derivativ
es at the junction point $c = \frac{1}{2}$, while the $(n+1)$-th derivative jump
s — this works uniformly for all $n$.
