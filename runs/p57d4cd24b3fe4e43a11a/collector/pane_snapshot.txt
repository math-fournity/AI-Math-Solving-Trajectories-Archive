### Step 1: Determine the degree of $P$

Let $P$ be a polynomial of degree $d$. Then $P(x)-1$ has degree $d$, and $P(P(x)
-1)$ has degree $d \cdot d = d^2$. The right-hand side $1 + x^{36}$ has degree $
36$, so

$$d^2 = 36 \implies d = 6.$$

### Step 2: Reduce to a self-composition equation

Set $Q(x) = P(x) - 1$. Then the equation becomes

$$P(P(x)-1) = P(Q(x)) = Q(Q(x)) + 1 = 1 + x^{36},$$

so we need a degree-6 polynomial $Q$ satisfying

$$Q(Q(x)) = x^{36}.$$

### Step 3: Leading coefficient

Write $Q(x) = cx^6 + \cdots$. The leading term of $Q(Q(x))$ is $c \cdot (cx^6)^6
 = c^7 x^{36}$. Since $Q(Q(x)) = x^{36}$, we get $c^7 = 1$, so $c = 1$ (over the
 reals).

### Step 4: Uniqueness — all roots of $Q$ coincide

Let $r_1, \dots, r_k$ be the **distinct** roots of $Q$, with multiplicities $m_1
, \dots, m_k$. Then

$$Q(Q(x)) = \prod_{i=1}^{k} \bigl(Q(x) - r_i\bigr)^{m_i}.$$

Since $Q(Q(x)) = x^{36}$ has $x = 0$ as its **only** root (with multiplicity $36
$), each factor $Q(x) - r_i$ must itself be a monomial in $x$, i.e.,

$$Q(x) - r_i = c_i\, x^{6}$$

(since $Q(x)-r_i$ has degree $6$, all its roots must be $0$, forcing the form $c
_i x^6$).

But $Q$ is a single polynomial, so all the expressions $c_i x^6 + r_i$ must be i
dentical. Hence there is only **one** distinct root $r$ (with multiplicity $6$),
 and

$$Q(x) = c\, x^6 + r, \qquad c = 1.$$

### Step 5: Pin down $r$

With $Q(x) = x^6 + r$ and $Q(r) = 0$ (since $r$ is a root):

$$r^6 + r = 0 \implies r(r^5 + 1) = 0.$$

- **Case $r = 0$:** $Q(x) = x^6$, and $Q(Q(x)) = (x^6)^6 = x^{36}$. ✓

- **Case $r \neq 0$, $r^5 = -1$:** Then $Q(Q(x)) = (x^6 + r)^6 + r$. Expanding,
the $x^{30}$-coefficient is $6r \neq 0$, which cannot match $x^{36}$. ✗

Therefore $Q(x) = x^6$ is the **unique** real solution.

### Step 6: Recover $P$ and compute $P(2)$

$$P(x) = Q(x) + 1 = x^6 + 1.$$

**Verification:** $P(P(x)-1) = P(x^6) = (x^6)^6 + 1 = x^{36} + 1.$ ✓

$$\boxed{P(2) = 2^6 + 1 = 64 + 1 = 65}.$$
