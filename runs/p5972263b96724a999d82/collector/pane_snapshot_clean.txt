## Solution

We solve the initial value problem
$$u_t = u_{xx} - u, \quad x \in \mathbb{R},\ t>0, \qquad u(x,0)=f(x), \qquad \li
m_{|x|\to\infty}u(x,t)=0.$$

### Step 1. Remove the reaction term

Set
$$u(x,t) = e^{-t}\,v(x,t).$$

Differentiating:
$$u_t = e^{-t}(v_t - v), \qquad u_{xx} = e^{-t}\,v_{xx}.$$

Substituting into $u_t = u_{xx} - u$:
$$e^{-t}(v_t - v) = e^{-t}v_{xx} - e^{-t}v \;\Longrightarrow\; v_t = v_{xx}.$$

The initial condition becomes
$$v(x,0) = u(x,0) = f(x),$$
and the decay condition $\lim_{|x|\to\infty}v(x,t)=0$ is inherited from that of
$u$.

### Step 2. Solve the standard heat equation

The Cauchy problem $v_t = v_{xx}$, $v(x,0)=f(x)$, on the whole line has the well
-known solution given by convolution with the heat kernel
$$\Phi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\,e^{-x^2/(4t)}, \qquad t>0.$$

Namely,
$$v(x,t) = (\Phi(\cdot,t)*f)(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi(x-y,t)\,f(y)\,dy =
 \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-(x-y)^2/(4t)}\,f(y)\,dy.$$

### Step 3. Recover $u$

Multiplying back by $e^{-t}$:

$$\boxed{\;u(x,t) = \frac{e^{-t}}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp\!\l
eft(-\frac{(x-y)^2}{4t}\right) f(y)\,dy,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},\ t>0.\;}$$

### Verification

1. **PDE.** Since $v$ solves $v_t=v_{xx}$, the computation in Step 1 (run in rev
erse) shows $u_t = u_{xx}-u$. ✓

2. **Initial condition.** As $t\to 0^+$, the heat kernel $\Phi(\cdot,t)$ is an a
pproximate identity, so $v(x,t)\to f(x)$ pointwise (at every continuity point of
 $f$), and $e^{-t}\to 1$. Hence $u(x,t)\to f(x)$. ✓

3. **Decay at infinity.** For each fixed $t>0$, $\Phi(x-y,t)\to 0$ as $|x|\to\in
fty$ uniformly in $y$ on compact sets; combined with the integrability/boundedne
ss of $f$ and the $e^{-t}$ factor, $\lim_{|x|\to\infty}u(x,t)=0$. ✓

4. **Regularity.** For $t>0$, $v$ (hence $u$) is $C^\infty$ in $(x,t)$ by the sm
oothing property of the heat kernel, provided $f$ is, e.g., bounded and continuo
us.

### Assumptions on $f$

The formula is valid under standard hypotheses guaranteeing the convolution to b
e well defined and the heat equation to be classically solved — for instance, $f
\in C(\mathbb{R})\cap L^\infty(\mathbb{R})$, or more generally $f$ continuous an
d satisfying a growth condition such as $|f(x)|\le Me^{ax^2}$ with $a$ small eno
ugh. Under $f\in L^1(\mathbb{R})\cap C(\mathbb{R})$ the solution is classical fo
r $t>0$ and continuous up to $t=0$.
