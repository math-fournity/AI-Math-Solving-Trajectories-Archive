Looking at this problem, I need to prove that for polynomials $f, g$ whose value
s at $1, 2, \ldots, n$ trace out a regular $n$-gon, at least one has degree $\ge
q n-1$.

**Proof.**

Let the regular $n$-gon have center $(a, b)$, circumradius $R > 0$, and starting
 angle $\theta_0$. The vertices in counterclockwise order are:
$$(f(k), g(k)) = (a + R\cos\theta_k,\; b + R\sin\theta_k), \quad \theta_k = \the
ta_0 + \frac{2\pi(k-1)}{n}, \quad k = 1, \ldots, n.$$

**Step 1: Complex reformulation.** Define $h(x) = f(x) + i\,g(x)$, $c = a + ib$,
 and $\omega = e^{2\pi i/n}$. Set $p(x) = h(x) - c$. Then:
$$p(k) = h(k) - c = R\,e^{i\theta_k} = R\,e^{i\theta_0}\,\omega^{k-1} = \alpha\,
\omega^{k-1}, \quad k = 1, \ldots, n,$$
where $\alpha = R\,e^{i\theta_0} \neq 0$.

**Step 2: Key recurrence.** For $k = 1, \ldots, n-1$:
$$p(k+1) = \alpha\,\omega^k = \omega \cdot \alpha\,\omega^{k-1} = \omega\, p(k).
$$

**Step 3: Constructing the divisibility.** Define $q(x) = p(x+1) - \omega\, p(x)
$. By Step 2, $q(k) = 0$ for $k = 1, 2, \ldots, n-1$. Therefore:
$$\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(x - k) \;\Big|\; q(x).$$

**Step 4: Degree of $q$.** Let $d = \deg(p)$ and write $p(x) = a_d x^d + \cdots$
 with $a_d \neq 0$. Then:
$$p(x+1) = a_d(x+1)^d + \cdots = a_d x^d + d\,a_d x^{d-1} + \cdots,$$
$$q(x) = p(x+1) - \omega\,p(x) = a_d(1 - \omega)\,x^d + \cdots.$$

Since $n \geq 3$, we have $\omega = e^{2\pi i/n} \neq 1$, so $a_d(1-\omega) \neq
 0$. Thus $\deg(q) = d$.

(Note: $q$ is not the zero polynomial, since that would force $a_d = \omega\, a_
d$, i.e., $(1-\omega)a_d = 0$, contradicting $a_d \neq 0$ and $\omega \neq 1$.)

**Step 5: Conclusion.** Since $\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(x-k)$ has degree $n-1$ and divi
des $q(x)$ which has degree $d$, we need $d \geq n-1$.

It remains to confirm $d = \max(\deg f, \deg g)$. Since $p(x) = f(x) + i\,g(x) -
 c$ and $f, g$ have real coefficients, the leading coefficient of $p$ at degree
$\max(\deg f, \deg g)$ is $\operatorname{lc}(f) + i\,\operatorname{lc}(g)$ (taki
ng $\operatorname{lc} = 0$ for the polynomial of smaller degree). This complex n
umber is zero only if both real and imaginary parts vanish, which is impossible.
 Hence:
$$\deg(p) = \max(\deg f, \deg g) \geq n - 1.$$

Therefore at least one of $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ has degree greater than or equal to
$n - 1$. $\blacksquare$
