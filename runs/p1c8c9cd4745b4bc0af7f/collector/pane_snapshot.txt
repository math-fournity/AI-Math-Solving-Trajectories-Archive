## Definitions and Setup

Let $s \in \{0,1\}^n$ be a random bit sequence of length $n$, drawn uniformly at
 random (each of the $2^n$ sequences equally likely with probability $2^{-n}$).
Let $f: \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}^n$ be a reversible (bijective) function with inver
se $f'$, satisfying $f'(f(s)) = s$ for all $s$. The **compression rate** is defi
ned as the ratio of the average length of the compressed representation to the l
ength of the input, i.e., $\rho = \frac{\mathbb{E}[L(f(s))]}{n}$, where $L(\cdot
)$ is the length of an optimal encoding.

## Claim

**The theoretical maximum average compression rate is $\rho = 1$ (i.e., no compr
ession is achievable on average).**

## Proof

**Step 1: A random bit sequence has maximum entropy.**

The Shannon entropy of a uniformly random $n$-bit sequence is:
$$H(s) = -\sum_{s \in \{0,1\}^n} 2^{-n} \log_2(2^{-n}) = n \text{ bits.}$$

This is the maximum possible entropy for an $n$-bit sequence — each bit carries
exactly 1 bit of information, with no redundancy.

**Step 2: A bijection preserves entropy.**

Since $f$ is a bijection (reversible with inverse $f'$), it is a permutation of
the space $\{0,1\}^n$. A permutation is a one-to-one relabeling that preserves t
he probability mass function. If $s$ is uniform, then $f(s)$ is also uniform:
$$\Pr[f(s) = y] = \Pr[s = f'(y)] = 2^{-n} \quad \text{for all } y \in \{0,1\}^n.
$$

Therefore:
$$H(f(s)) = H(s) = n \text{ bits.}$$

The output $f(s)$ has exactly the same entropy as the input $s$. No information
is created or destroyed by a bijection.

**Step 3: Shannon's Source Coding Theorem gives a tight lower bound.**

By Shannon's source coding theorem, for any lossless encoding of a source with e
ntropy $H$, the expected code length $L$ satisfies:
$$\mathbb{E}[L] \geq H,$$
with equality achievable (up to at most 1 bit) by optimal codes. Since $H(f(s))
= n$:
$$\mathbb{E}[L(f(s))] \geq n.$$

The minimum achievable average encoded length of $f(s)$ is $n$ bits — exactly th
e original length. Thus:
$$\rho = \frac{\mathbb{E}[L(f(s))]}{n} \geq \frac{n}{n} = 1.$$

No encoding of $f(s)$ can be shorter than $n$ bits on average. The compression r
ate cannot be below 1.

**Step 4: The rate of 1 is achievable (trivially).**

The identity encoding (outputting $f(s)$ directly without further compression) a
chieves $\mathbb{E}[L] = n$, giving $\rho = 1$. This is optimal. Therefore the *
*maximum average compression rate is exactly $\rho = 1$** — meaning no net compr
ession.

**Step 5: Kolmogorov complexity confirms incompressibility.**

From the algorithmic information theory perspective: a truly random $n$-bit sequ
ence $s$ has Kolmogorov complexity $K(s) \approx n$ (with high probability over
the choice of $s$). Since $f$ is a computable bijection with computable inverse
$f'$:
$$K(f(s)) \geq K(s) - |f'| \approx n - O(1),$$
because $s = f'(f(s))$ can be reconstructed from $f(s)$ plus the fixed program f
or $f'$. Thus $f(s)$ is also algorithmically random and incompressible. No rever
sible transformation can reduce the Kolmogorov complexity of a random sequence b
y more than a constant.

## Impact of Pattern Identification and Mapping in Modern Compression

Modern compression algorithms (LZ77, LZW, Huffman, arithmetic coding, PPM, etc.)
 achieve compression by:

1. **Pattern identification**: detecting repeated substrings, statistical regula
rities, and non-uniform symbol distributions.
2. **Mapping**: assigning shorter codes to more frequent patterns and longer cod
es to rarer ones (e.g., Huffman coding), or referencing earlier occurrences (LZ
dictionary methods).

These methods exploit **redundancy** — departures from maximum entropy. They wor
k because real-world data (text, images, audio) is highly non-uniform and contai
ns exploitable structure.

For a **truly random bit sequence**, however:

- There are no repeated patterns beyond what chance dictates (no exploitable red
undancy).
- The symbol distribution is already uniform, so variable-length codes provide n
o net gain.
- Dictionary methods find no recurring substrings to reference.

Crucially, applying a bijection $f$ **cannot create** patterns or redundancy whe
re none existed. A bijection is a relabeling — it permutes the elements but pres
erves the uniform distribution. Pattern identification on $f(s)$ finds the same
(lack of) structure as on $s$. Any mapping $f$ provides is already information-n
eutral: the "dictionary" needed to invert $f$ (i.e., $f'$ itself) costs as much
to specify as it saves.

In fact, any **fixed** reversible mapping $f$ that one might hope compresses cer
tain inputs must, by the pigeonhole principle on a bijection over a finite space
, **expand** other inputs by exactly as much as it compresses some. Averaged ove
r a uniform distribution, these effects cancel perfectly, yielding net zero comp
ression. This is a direct consequence of the bijection preserving the measure.

## Conclusion

The theoretical maximum average compression rate for a reversible function $f(s)
$ (with $f'(f(s)) = s$) applied to a random bit sequence is:

$$\boxed{\rho = 1}$$

No average compression is achievable. A random bit sequence is incompressible (m
aximum Shannon entropy, near-maximal Kolmogorov complexity), and a bijection pre
serves both. Modern compression algorithms' pattern identification and mapping t
echniques are powerless against true randomness — they exploit redundancy, and a
 bijection over a uniform distribution neither possesses nor can introduce explo
itable redundancy. The gains on some inputs are exactly offset by losses on othe
rs, yielding a net compression rate of exactly 1.
