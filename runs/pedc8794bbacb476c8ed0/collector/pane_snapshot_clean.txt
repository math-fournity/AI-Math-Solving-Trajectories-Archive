## Proof

**Claim.** Yes. Every finite group of odd order is isomorphic to a subgroup of $
A_n$ (in fact, of $A_{|G|}$).

**Proof.** Let $G$ be a finite group of odd order, and set $n = |G|$.

By Cayley's theorem, the left-regular action of $G$ on itself gives an injective
 homomorphism
$$\rho : G \hookrightarrow S_n, \qquad \rho(g)(h) = gh.$$
It remains to show that $\rho(G) \subseteq A_n$.

Fix $g \in G$ and let $d = \operatorname{ord}(g)$. Since $d \mid |G|$ and $|G|$
is odd, $d$ is **odd**.

The cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$ acts on $G$ (on the left) freely: if $g^
k \cdot h = h$, then $g^k = e$, so $d \mid k$. Hence every orbit of $\langle g \
rangle$ on $G$ has size exactly $d$. Consequently the permutation $\rho(g)$ deco
mposes into
$$\frac{n}{d} \text{ disjoint cycles, each of length } d.$$

A cycle of length $d$ has sign $(-1)^{d-1}$. Since $d$ is odd, $d - 1$ is even,
so each cycle is an **even** permutation. The product of even permutations is ev
en, so
$$\operatorname{sgn}(\rho(g)) = (+1)^{n/d} = +1.$$

This holds for every $g \in G$ (the identity is trivially even). Therefore
$$\rho(G) \subseteq A_n,$$
and since $\rho$ is injective, $G \cong \rho(G) \le A_n$. $\blacksquare$
