**Setup.** Let $V_n$ denote the vector space of real polynomials of degree $\le
n$. Equip $V$ with the inner product $\langle f, g\rangle = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(
x)g(x)\,dx$. The conditions
$$\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x^k p(x)\,dx = 0,\qquad 0\le k\le 6,$$
say precisely that $p$ is orthogonal to $V_6$.

**Uniqueness up to scale.** The space $V_7$ has dimension $8$, and $V_6\subset V
_7$ has dimension $7$. The orthogonal complement $V_6^\perp \cap V_7$ is therefo
re $1$-dimensional. Hence every degree-$7$ polynomial orthogonal to $V_6$ is a s
calar multiple of a single such polynomial. The scalar is then pinned down by th
e normalization $p(\pi)=\sqrt 3$. So $p$ is uniquely determined.

**Identifying the orthogonal polynomial.** The Legendre polynomials $P_n(t)$ are
 orthogonal on $[-1,1]$ with $\int_{-1}^{1} P_m P_n = 0$ for $m\ne n$, and $\deg
 P_n = n$. Substituting $t = x/\pi$ (so $dx = \pi\,dt$) shows that $Q_n(x) := P_
n(x/\pi)$ are orthogonal on $[-\pi,\pi]$. In particular
$$Q_7(x) = P_7(x/\pi)$$
is a degree-$7$ polynomial orthogonal to every polynomial of degree $\le 6$ on $
[-\pi,\pi]$. By the uniqueness above,
$$p(x) = c\,P_7(x/\pi)$$
for some real constant $c$.
