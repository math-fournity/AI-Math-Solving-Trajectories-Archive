## Proof

**Claim.** Yes. The Tietze extension theorem applies to a normal space (compact
Hausdorff is not required), and it in fact yields an extension to the *entire* s
pace, which is strictly stronger than extending merely to an open neighborhood o
f the closed subset.

---

**Step 1 — State the theorem precisely.**

The Tietze Extension Theorem (Urysohn–Tietze) reads:

> Let $X$ be a **normal** topological space and let $A\subseteq X$ be a **closed
** subset. Every continuous function $f:A\to\mathbb{R}$ (equivalently $f:A\to[a,
b]$) admits a continuous extension $F:X\to\mathbb{R}$ (respectively $F:X\to[a,b]
$) with $F|_A=f$.

The hypothesis is **normality** — the property that any two disjoint closed sets
 can be separated by disjoint open neighborhoods. Compact Hausdorff is *not* the
 hypothesis; it is merely one common sufficient condition for normality, since e
very compact Hausdorff space is normal.

**Step 2 — Normality $\not\Rightarrow$ compact Hausdorff.**

There are many normal spaces that are not compact Hausdorff:

- Every **metrizable** space is normal (a standard theorem). In particular $\mat
hbb{R}$ with its usual topology is normal, but $\mathbb{R}$ is not compact.
- Every **regular Lindelöf** space is normal; e.g. $\mathbb{R}$ is regular and L
indelöf, hence normal, yet not compact.
- Discrete spaces of arbitrary cardinality are normal (trivially, every subset i
s clopen) but are compact only when finite.

Thus "normal" is a genuinely weaker hypothesis than "compact Hausdorff," and the
 Tietze theorem operates in this broader setting.

**Step 3 — The theorem extends to all of $X$, hence to any open neighborhood.**

Given a closed $A\subseteq X$ and continuous $f:A\to\mathbb{R}$, Tietze produces
 $F:X\to\mathbb{R}$ continuous with $F|_A=f$. Since $A$ is closed in a normal (h
ence $T_1$, under the usual convention that normal = $T_4$) space, $X\setminus A
$ is open. Pick any open neighborhood $U$ of $A$ — for instance $U=X$ itself, or
 any smaller open set containing $A$. The restriction $F|_U:U\to\mathbb{R}$ is c
ontinuous and extends $f$.

In particular, taking $U=X$ already gives the desired extension to an open neigh
borhood (the whole space is an open neighborhood of $A$). If one insists on a *p
roper* open neighborhood, one may use the normality of $X$ once more: since $A$
is closed and (if $A\neq X$) $X\setminus A$ is a nonempty open set, for any poin
t $x_0\notin A$ the closed set $\{x_0\}$ and $A$ are disjoint closed sets, so by
 normality there exist disjoint open sets $V\supseteq A$ and $W\ni x_0$; then $U
:=X\setminus\overline{W}$ (or simply $V$) is an open neighborhood of $A$ properl
y contained in $X$, and $F|_U$ is the desired extension.

**Step 4 — Conclusion.**

The compact Hausdorff assumption is irrelevant to the applicability of Tietze's
theorem. Normality alone suffices, and the theorem delivers an extension to the
whole space $X$ — which automatically restricts to an extension on any open neig
hborhood of $A$. Therefore the Tietze extension theorem **can** be applied in th
e stated situation, regardless of whether $X$ is compact Hausdorff.
