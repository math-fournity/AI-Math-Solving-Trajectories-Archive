## Proof

The statement is **true**.

Let $f: c_0 \to \mathbb{C}$ be a positive linear functional satisfying: whenever
 $g_n \to 0$ in $c_0$ (i.e. $\|g_n\|_\infty \to 0$), we have $f(g_n) \to 0$.

**Step 1. The hypothesis makes $f$ norm-continuous (bounded).**

The condition "$f(g_n)\to 0$ whenever $g_n \to 0$ in $c_0$" is precisely sequent
ial continuity of $f$ at $0$. For a linear map between normed spaces, sequential
 continuity at $0$ is equivalent to boundedness: if $f$ were unbounded, there wo
uld exist $h_n$ with $\|h_n\|_\infty \le 1$ and $|f(h_n)| \to \infty$; setting $
g_n = h_n/\sqrt{|f(h_n)|}$ gives $\|g_n\|_\infty \to 0$ yet $|f(g_n)| = \sqrt{|f
(h_n)|} \to \infty$, contradicting the hypothesis. Hence $f$ is bounded, with $\
|f\| < \infty$.

**Step 2. Positivity gives $f(e_n) \ge 0$ and a lower bound on $\|f\|$.**

Let $e_n = \mathbf{1}_{\{n\}} \in c_0$. Since $e_n \ge 0$ componentwise and $f$
is positive, $a_n := f(e_n) \ge 0$ (a non-negative real). For each $N$, define
$$x^{(N)} = \sum_{n=1}^{N} e_n = (1,1,\dots,1,0,0,\dots) \in c_0.$$
Then $x^{(N)} \ge 0$ and $\|x^{(N)}\|_\infty = 1$. By linearity,
$$f\!\bigl(x^{(N)}\bigr) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} f(e_n) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n.$$
Since $f$ is positive and $\|x^{(N)}\|_\infty = 1$ with $x^{(N)}\ge 0$,
$$\sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n = f\!\bigl(x^{(N)}\bigr) \le \|f\|\cdot\|x^{(N)}\|_\infty =
 \|f\|.$$

**Step 3. Conclusion.**

The inequality $\sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n \le \|f\|$ holds for every $N$. Letting $N \t
o \infty$,
$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f(e_n) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n \le \|f\| < \infty.$$

Thus $\sum f(\mathbf{1}_{\{n\}})$ is finite. $\blacksquare$
