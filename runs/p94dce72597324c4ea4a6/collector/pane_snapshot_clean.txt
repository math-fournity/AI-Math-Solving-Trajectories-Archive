# Solution

**Answer: No.** The implication $|A|+|C|=|B|+|C| > |C| \Rightarrow |A|=|B|$ is *
*not provable in ZF** (Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory without the Axiom of Choice).
 We exhibit a model of ZF with a counterexample.

---

## The Counterexample

Work in the **basic Cohen model** (Cohen's first model of $\mathsf{ZF}+\neg\math
sf{AC}$), in which there exists an **infinite Dedekind-finite set** $D$ (a set t
hat is infinite yet admits no injection $\mathbb{N}\hookrightarrow D$). The exis
tence of such a set in this model is a classical result of Cohen.

Set:
- $A = D$,
- $B = D \sqcup \{p\}$ (disjoint union with a singleton, $p\notin D$),
- $C = \mathbb{N}$.

We verify the three required properties.

### Claim 1: $|A|+|C| = |B|+|C|$

We have $|A|+|C| = |D\sqcup\mathbb{N}|$ and $|B|+|C| = |D\sqcup\{p\}\sqcup\mathb
b{N}|$.

The set $D\sqcup\mathbb{N}$ contains $\mathbb{N}$ as a subset, hence is **Dedeki
nd-infinite**. A standard fact: for any Dedekind-infinite set $X$, $|X|=|X|+1$.
Indeed, if $\{x_0,x_1,x_2,\dots\}\subseteq X$ is countably infinite, the map
$$p\mapsto x_0,\quad x_n\mapsto x_{n+1},\quad x\mapsto x\text{ (for all other }x
\in X\text{)}$$
is a bijection $X\sqcup\{p\}\to X$.

Applying this to $X = D\sqcup\mathbb{N}$ (which is Dedekind-infinite):
$$|D\sqcup\mathbb{N}| = |D\sqcup\mathbb{N}|+1 = |D\sqcup\mathbb{N}\sqcup\{p\}| =
 |D\sqcup\{p\}\sqcup\mathbb{N}|.$$
Therefore $|A|+|C|=|B|+|C|$. $\checkmark$

### Claim 2: Both sides are strictly larger than $|C|=\aleph_0$

We need $|D\sqcup\mathbb{N}|>\aleph_0$, i.e., $D\sqcup\mathbb{N}$ does not injec
t into $\mathbb{N}$. If it did, then $D\subseteq D\sqcup\mathbb{N}$ would inject
 into $\mathbb{N}$, making $D$ countable. But a countably infinite set is Dedeki
nd-infinite (it admits an injection from $\mathbb{N}$), contradicting the Dedeki
nd-finiteness of $D$. Hence $|D\sqcup\mathbb{N}|>\aleph_0$. $\checkmark$

### Claim 3: $|A|\neq|B|$

We have $|A|=|D|$ and $|B|=|D|+1$. Suppose for contradiction that $|D|=|D|+1$, s
o there is a bijection $h:D\sqcup\{p\}\to D$. Let $d_0 = h(p)\in D$. Then $h\big
|_D : D \to D\setminus\{d_0\}$ is a bijection (since $h^{-1}(d_0)=p\notin D$). T
his exhibits $D$ as equipotent to a proper subset of itself, making $D$ **Dedeki
nd-infinite**—contradicting the Dedekind-finiteness of $D$.

Therefore $|D|\neq|D|+1$, i.e., $|A|\neq|B|$. $\checkmark$

---

## Why the Argument Fails Under AC

Under the Axiom of Choice, every infinite cardinal $\kappa$ satisfies $\kappa+\g
amma = \max(\kappa,\gamma)$ for infinite $\gamma$. Thus $|A|+|C|=|B|+|C|>\!|C|$
forces $\max(|A|,|C|)=\max(|B|,|C|)>|C|$, giving $|A|=\max(|A|,|C|)=\max(|B|,|C|
)=|B|$.

Without AC, the absorption law $\kappa+\gamma=\max(\kappa,\gamma)$ fails. The co
unterexample exploits this: $C=\mathbb{N}$ makes $A\sqcup C$ Dedekind-infinite (
allowing it to absorb the extra element from $B=A\sqcup\{p\}$), while $A$ itself
 remains Dedekind-finite (so $|A|\neq|A|+1=|B|$).

---

## Conclusion

Since the basic Cohen model is a model of $\mathsf{ZF}$ in which sets $A,B,C$ ex
ist with $|A|+|C|=|B|+|C|>|C|$ yet $|A|\neq|B|$, the implication is **not a theo
rem of ZF**. The answer is **no**: $|A|+|C|=|B|+|C|>|C|$ does not imply $|A|=|B|
$ in the absence of the axiom of choice.
