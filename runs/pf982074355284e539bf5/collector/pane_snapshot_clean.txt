# Proof that Pinsker's Inequality holds on a compact metric space

**Answer: Yes.** Pinsker's Inequality holds for the total variation distance $V(
p,q)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sup_{A\subseteq X}|p(A)-q(A)|$ when $X$ is a compact metric sp
ace (with its Borel $\sigma$-algebra) and $p,q$ are Borel probability measures.
In fact, the result holds for **any** two probability measures on **any** measur
able space; compactness of $X$ is not needed.

---

## Statement

For probability measures $p, q$ on a measurable space $(X, \mathcal{F})$,

$$V(p,q) \;\le\; \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\,D(p\,\|\,q)},$$

where $D(p\,\|\,q) = \int \log\!\tfrac{dp}{dq}\,dp$ is the Kullback–Leibler dive
rgence (with $D=+\infty$ if $p\not\ll q$).

---

## Proof

### Step 0 — Trivial case

If $p \not\ll q$, then $D(p\,\|\,q)=+\infty$ and the inequality holds vacuously.
 Hence assume $p \ll q$ and set $h = \frac{dp}{dq} \ge 0$, so that $\int h\,dq =
 1$.

### Step 1 — Total variation as an integral

Let $\mu = p + q$ and write $f = dp/d\mu$, $g = dq/d\mu$. Then

$$V(p,q) = \tfrac{1}{2}\sup_{A\in\mathcal{F}}|p(A)-q(A)| = \tfrac{1}{2}\int_X |f
-g|\,d\mu = \tfrac{1}{2}\int_X |h-1|\,dq.$$

The supremum is attained by the measurable set $A^* = \{h \ge 1\} = \{f \ge g\}$
, since $p(A)-q(A) = \int_A(f-g)\,d\mu$ is maximized exactly where $f-g \ge 0$.
(On a compact metric space, $h$ is Borel measurable, so $A^*$ is a Borel set.)

### Step 2 — Data-processing (log-sum) reduction to the binary case

For any measurable $A$, partition $X = A \sqcup A^c$ and apply the **log-sum ine
quality** $\sum_i a_i\log\frac{a_i}{b_i} \ge \bigl(\sum a_i\bigr)\log\frac{\sum
a_i}{\sum b_i}$ to the two pieces:

$$D(p\,\|\,q) = \int_A h\log h\,dq + \int_{A^c} h\log h\,dq \;\ge\; p(A)\log\fra
c{p(A)}{q(A)} + p(A^c)\log\frac{p(A^c)}{q(A^c)}.$$

The right-hand side is the **binary KL divergence** $D_{\mathrm{Bern}}\bigl(p(A)
\,\|\,q(A)\bigr)$. Thus for every $A$:

$$D(p\,\|\,q) \;\ge\; D_{\mathrm{Bern}}\bigl(p(A)\,\|\,q(A)\bigr). \tag{1}$$

### Step 3 — Binary Pinsker inequality

**Claim.** For all $a, b \in [0,1]$,

$$(a-b)^2 \;\le\; 2\Bigl[a\log\tfrac{a}{b} + (1-a)\log\tfrac{1-a}{1-b}\Bigr] = 2
\,D_{\mathrm{Bern}}(a\,\|\,b). \tag{2}$$

*Proof of claim.* By symmetry in the roles of $a$ and $b$ (note $D_{\mathrm{Bern
}}(a\|b) = D_{\mathrm{Bern}}(1-a\|1-b)$ and $(a-b)^2 = (b-a)^2$), it suffices to
 consider $a \ge b$. Fix $b$ and define

$$\varphi(a) = a\log\tfrac{a}{b} + (1-a)\log\tfrac{1-a}{1-b} - \tfrac{1}{2}(a-b)
^2, \qquad a \in [b,1].$$

We show $\varphi(a) \ge 0$. Compute:

$$\varphi(b) = 0, \qquad \varphi'(a) = \log\frac{a(1-b)}{b(1-a)} - (a-b), \qquad
 \varphi'(b) = 0,$$

$$\varphi''(a) = \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{1-a} - 1 = \frac{1}{a(1-a)} - 1.$$

Since $a(1-a) \le \tfrac{1}{4}$ for $a\in(0,1)$, we have $\frac{1}{a(1-a)} \ge 4
 > 1$, so $\varphi''(a) > 0$ on $(0,1)$. Therefore $\varphi$ is **strictly conve
x**, with $\varphi(b)=0$ and $\varphi'(b)=0$, which forces $\varphi(a) \ge 0$ fo
r all $a \in [b,1]$. $\square$

### Step 4 — Combine

For any measurable $A$, using (1) and (2):

$$(p(A)-q(A))^2 \;\le\; 2\,D_{\mathrm{Bern}}\bigl(p(A)\,\|\,q(A)\bigr) \;\le\; 2
\,D(p\,\|\,q).$$

Taking the supremum over all measurable $A$ (attained at $A^* = \{h\ge 1\}$):

$$(2V)^2 = \Bigl(\sup_A |p(A)-q(A)|\Bigr)^2 \;\le\; 2\,D(p\,\|\,q).$$

Hence

$$\boxed{V(p,q) \;\le\; \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\,D(p\,\|\,q)}.} \qquad \blacksquare$$

---

## Remarks

- **No topological hypothesis is used.** The proof is purely measure-theoretic:
it uses only the Radon–Nikodym theorem, the log-sum inequality, and a one-variab
le convexity argument. Compactness of $X$ plays no role; the inequality holds on
 every measurable space.
- **The supremum is attained.** On a compact metric space (or any measurable spa
ce), the set $A^*=\{dp/dq\ge 1\}$ is measurable and achieves $\sup_A |p(A)-q(A)|
$, so the definition of $V$ via a supremum coincides with the integral form $\tf
rac12\int|f-g|\,d\mu$.
- **Sharpness.** The constant $\tfrac12$ inside the square root is optimal: equa
lity is approached by pairs of Bernoulli measures with parameters $(\epsilon, 0)
$ as $\epsilon\to 0$.
