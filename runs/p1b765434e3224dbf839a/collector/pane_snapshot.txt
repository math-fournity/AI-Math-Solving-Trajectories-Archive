## Proof

### Step 1: Relation to $\zeta(s)$ for $\Re(s) > 1$

For $\Re(s) > 1$, the Euler product gives $\zeta(s) = \prod_p (1 - p^{-s})^{-1}$
. Taking reciprocals:

$$\frac{1}{\zeta(s)} = \prod_p (1 - p^{-s}) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\mu(n)}{
n^s}$$

The expansion of the Euler product into the Dirichlet series follows from the mu
ltiplicativity of $\mu$. The series converges **absolutely** for $\Re(s) > 1$ si
nce $|\mu(n)| \leq 1$ and $\sum n^{-\sigma}$ converges for $\sigma > 1$.

### Step 2: Abscissa of convergence

Let $M(x) = \sum_{n \leq x} \mu(n)$. By Abel (partial) summation:

$$\sum_{n=1}^{N} \frac{\mu(n)}{n^s} = \frac{M(N)}{N^s} + s\int_1^N \frac{M(x)}{x
^{s+1}}\,dx$$

The abscissa of convergence $\sigma_c$ of the Dirichlet series $\sum \mu(n) n^{-
s}$ satisfies:

$$\sigma_c = \limsup_{x \to \infty} \frac{\log |M(x)|}{\log x}$$

### Step 3: $\sigma_c = \Theta := \sup\{\Re(\rho) : \zeta(\rho) = 0\}$

**Upper bound ($\sigma_c \leq \Theta$):** The explicit formula for $M(x)$ (via P
erron's formula applied to $1/\zeta(s)$) expresses $M(x)$ as a sum over non-triv
ial zeros $\rho$ of $\zeta$:

$$M(x) = \sum_{\rho} \frac{x^{\rho}}{\rho\,\zeta'(\rho)} + \text{(lower order te
rms)}$$

Each zero $\rho$ contributes a term of magnitude $O(x^{\Re(\rho)})$. Standard co
ntour-shifting arguments give $M(x) = O(x^{\Theta + \epsilon})$ for every $\epsi
lon > 0$, hence $\sigma_c \leq \Theta$.

**Lower bound ($\sigma_c \geq \Theta$):** Suppose $\sigma_c < \Theta$. Then the
Dirichlet series $\sum \mu(n) n^{-s}$ converges for all $\Re(s) > \sigma_c$, and
 converges to an **analytic** function on this half-plane (a fundamental propert
y of Dirichlet series). But this function equals $1/\zeta(s)$ for $\Re(s) > 1$,
so by analytic continuation it equals $1/\zeta(s)$ for $\Re(s) > \sigma_c$. Howe
ver, since $\sigma_c < \Theta$, there exists a zero $\rho$ of $\zeta$ with $\Re(
\rho) > \sigma_c$, at which $1/\zeta(s)$ has a **pole** — contradicting analytic
ity. Therefore $\sigma_c \geq \Theta$.

Combining: $\sigma_c = \Theta$.

### Step 4: Known bounds on $\Theta$

- **$\Theta \geq 1/2$ (Hardy, 1914):** Hardy proved that $\zeta(s)$ has infinite
ly many zeros on the critical line $\Re(s) = 1/2$. Hence $\Theta \geq 1/2$.
- **$\Theta < 1$ (Hadamard / de la Vallée-Poussin, 1896):** The Prime Number The
orem is equivalent to the non-vanishing of $\zeta(s)$ on $\Re(s) = 1$, giving $\
Theta < 1$.

### Step 5: Convergence at $s = 1$

Since $\Theta < 1$ (by PNT), we have $s = 1 > \Theta = \sigma_c$, so the series
**converges** at $s = 1$.

**Value:** For $s > 1$, the sum equals $1/\zeta(s)$. As $s \to 1^+$, $\zeta(s) \
to +\infty$ (simple pole), so $1/\zeta(s) \to 0$. By Abel's theorem for Dirichle
t series (continuity from the right at the boundary of convergence), the sum at
$s = 1$ equals:

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\mu(n)}{n} = \lim_{s \to 1^+} \frac{1}{\zeta(s)} = 0
$$

This result is in fact **equivalent to the Prime Number Theorem**.

### Step 6: Divergence for $0 < s \leq 1/2$

Since $\Theta \geq 1/2$ (Hardy's theorem), $\sigma_c \geq 1/2$.

- **For $0 < s < 1/2$:** We have $s < 1/2 \leq \sigma_c$, so the series **diverg
es**.
- **For $s = 1/2$:** If $\Theta > 1/2$, then $s = 1/2 < \sigma_c$ and the series
 diverges. If $\Theta = 1/2$ (RH holds), then $s = 1/2 = \sigma_c$ is on the bou
ndary of convergence. The zeros $\rho = 1/2 + i\gamma$ on the critical line cont
ribute oscillating terms $\frac{x^{1/2+i\gamma}}{\rho\,\zeta'(\rho)}$ to $M(x)$,
 giving $M(x) = \Omega(x^{1/2})$. By partial summation at $s = 1/2$:

$$\sum_{n \leq N} \frac{\mu(n)}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{M(N)}{\sqrt{N}} + \frac{1}{2}\
int_1^N \frac{M(x)}{x^{3/2}}\,dx$$

The term $M(N)/\sqrt{N}$ does not converge (it oscillates with non-vanishing amp
litude). The integral, substituting the contribution from a zero $\rho = 1/2 + i
\gamma$, contains $\int_1^N x^{-1+i\gamma}\,dx = \frac{N^{i\gamma}-1}{i\gamma}$,
 which oscillates without converging. Hence the series **diverges** at $s = 1/2$
 unconditionally.

### Step 7: The region $1/2 < s < 1$

The series converges for $s > \Theta$ and diverges for $s \leq \Theta$.

- **If the Riemann Hypothesis holds ($\Theta = 1/2$):** The series converges for
 all $s > 1/2$, hence for **all** $1/2 < s < 1$.
- **If RH fails ($\Theta > 1/2$):** The series converges for $\Theta < s < 1$ an
d diverges for $1/2 < s \leq \Theta$.

The statement **"the series converges for every real $s > 1/2$"** is equivalent
to the Riemann Hypothesis.

## Conclusion

The series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \mu(n)\,n^{-s}$ converges if and only if $s > \T
heta$, where $\Theta = \sup\{\Re(\rho) : \zeta(\rho) = 0\} \in [1/2, 1)$ is the
supremum of the real parts of the zeros of the Riemann zeta function.

| Range of $s$ | Convergence | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| $s > 1$ | Converges absolutely; sum $= 1/\zeta(s)$ | Euler product |
| $s = 1$ | **Converges** conditionally to $0$ | PNT ($\Theta < 1$); equivalent
to PNT |
| $1/2 < s < 1$ | Converges iff $s > \Theta$; converges for all such $s$ **iff R
H** | $\sigma_c = \Theta$; RH $\Leftrightarrow$ $\Theta = 1/2$ |
| $s = 1/2$ | **Diverges** (unconditionally) | $\Theta \geq 1/2$; zeros on criti
cal line force $M(x) = \Omega(x^{1/2})$ |
| $0 < s < 1/2$ | **Diverges** (unconditionally) | $s < 1/2 \leq \Theta = \sigma
_c$ |

**For $s = 1$:** The series converges (to $0$), by the Prime Number Theorem.

**For $0 < s < 1$:** The series diverges for $0 < s \leq 1/2$ unconditionally. F
or $1/2 < s < 1$, convergence is equivalent to the Riemann Hypothesis — the deep
est open problem in mathematics. Unconditionally, the series converges for $s >
\Theta$ and diverges for $s \leq \Theta$, where $\Theta \in [1/2, 1)$ is unknown
.
