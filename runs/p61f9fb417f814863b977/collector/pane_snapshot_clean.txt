## Proof

**Model clarification.** A Toeplitz matrix $T \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ satis
fies $T_{ij} = a_{i-j}$ for a sequence of parameters $\{a_m\}_{m=-(n-1)}^{p-1}$.
 The statement that entries are "i.i.d. from a continuous random variable" is in
terpreted in the standard way for random Toeplitz matrices: the $n+p-1$ diagonal
 parameters $a_{-(n-1)}, \dots, a_{p-1}$ are i.i.d. from a continuous distributi
on. (Note: the matrix entries themselves are *not* independent, since entries on
 the same diagonal are identical. If the matrix were truly i.i.d. and not Toepli
tz, the result would be even easier.)

**Claim:** With probability 1, every set of $n$ columns of $T$ is linearly indep
endent.

---

### Step 1: Reduction to a polynomial non-vanishing problem

Fix any $n$ distinct column indices $1 \le j_1 < j_2 < \dots < j_n \le p$. The $
n \times n$ submatrix $M$ formed by these columns has entries:

$$M_{ik} = a_{i - j_k}, \quad i = 1, \dots, n,\ k = 1, \dots, n.$$

The determinant $\det(M)$ is a polynomial in the variables $\{a_m\}$. Since the
$a_m$'s are independent continuous random variables, a standard measure-theoreti
c fact gives:

> **Fact:** If $P(x_1, \dots, x_N)$ is a polynomial that is not identically zero
, and $x_1, \dots, x_N$ are independent random variables with continuous distrib
utions, then $\Pr[P(x_1, \dots, x_N) \ne 0] = 1$.

Thus it suffices to show that $\det(M)$ is **not the zero polynomial**.

---

### Step 2: Exhibiting a nonsingular specialization

We exhibit a specific assignment of the $a_m$'s making $\det(M) \ne 0$. Set $a_m
 = P(m)$ for a polynomial $P$ of degree exactly $n-1$. Then:

$$M_{ik} = P(i - j_k).$$

Write $P(x) = \sum_{r=0}^{n-1} c_r x^r$ with $c_{n-1} \ne 0$. By the binomial th
eorem:

$$P(i - j_k) = \sum_{r=0}^{n-1} c_r (i - j_k)^r = \sum_{r=0}^{n-1} c_r \sum_{\el
l=0}^{r} \binom{r}{\ell} i^\ell (-j_k)^{r-\ell} = \sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1} i^\ell \,
Q_\ell(j_k),$$

where we define:

$$Q_\ell(x) = \sum_{r=\ell}^{n-1} c_r \binom{r}{\ell} (-x)^{r-\ell}.$$

This yields the **matrix factorization**:

$$M = A \cdot B^\top,$$

where:
- $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ with $A_{i\ell} = i^\ell$ (the Vandermonde mat
rix in nodes $1, 2, \dots, n$),
- $B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ with $B_{k\ell} = Q_\ell(j_k)$.

---

### Step 3: Both factors are nonsingular

**$A$ is nonsingular.** $A$ is the Vandermonde matrix with distinct nodes $1, 2,
 \dots, n$:

$$\det(A) = \prod_{1 \le i < j \le n}(j - i) \ne 0.$$

**$B$ is nonsingular.** Each $Q_\ell$ is a polynomial of degree $n - 1 - \ell$ w
ith leading coefficient $c_{n-1}\binom{n-1}{\ell}(-1)^{n-1-\ell} \ne 0$ (since $
c_{n-1} \ne 0$). The polynomials $Q_0, Q_1, \dots, Q_{n-1}$ have **distinct degr
ees** $n-1, n-2, \dots, 0$, hence are linearly independent and form a basis for
the space of polynomials of degree $\le n-1$.

The matrix $B$ evaluates these $n$ linearly independent polynomials at $n$ disti
nct points $j_1, \dots, j_n$. If $B^\top \alpha = 0$ for some $\alpha \in \mathb
b{R}^n$, then the polynomial $\sum_\ell \alpha_\ell Q_\ell$ (of degree $\le n-1$
) vanishes at $n$ distinct points, forcing it to be the zero polynomial. By line
ar independence, $\alpha = 0$. Hence $B$ is nonsingular.

---

### Step 4: Conclusion

Since $\det(M) = \det(A) \cdot \det(B) \ne 0$ under the assignment $a_m = P(m)$,
 the determinant polynomial is **not identically zero**. By the Fact in Step 1,
for any fixed set of $n$ columns:

$$\Pr\big[\det(M) \ne 0\big] = 1.$$

There are $\binom{p}{n}$ possible choices of $n$ columns — a **finite** number.
The intersection of finitely many events of probability 1 still has probability
1. Therefore:

$$\Pr\big[\text{every set of } n \text{ columns of } T \text{ is linearly indepe
ndent}\big] = 1.$$

$\blacksquare$
