**Problem.** Let $P(x)$ be a polynomial of degree $n$ with $P(x)=Q(x)P''(x)$, $Q
$ quadratic. Show: if $P$ has at least two distinct roots, then it has $n$ disti
nct roots.

**Proof.**

Write $P(x)=a\prod_{i=1}^{k}(x-r_i)^{m_i}$ where $r_1,\dots,r_k$ are the distinc
t roots, $m_i\ge 1$, and $\sum m_i=n$. We prove: if $k\ge 2$ then $m_i=1$ for al
l $i$ (i.e., $k=n$).

**Step 1. A root of $P$ with multiplicity $\ge 2$ forces $Q$ to have a double ro
ot there.**

Suppose $r_i$ is a root of multiplicity $m_i\ge 2$. Write $P(x)=(x-r_i)^{m_i}g(x
)$, $g(r_i)\ne 0$. Then

$$P''(x)=(x-r_i)^{m_i-2}\bigl[m_i(m_i-1)g(x)+2m_i(x-r_i)g'(x)+(x-r_i)^2g''(x)\bi
gr].$$

The bracket at $x=r_i$ equals $m_i(m_i-1)g(r_i)\ne 0$. From $P=QP''$, dividing b
y $(x-r_i)^{m_i-2}$:

$$(x-r_i)^2 g(x)=Q(x)\bigl[m_i(m_i-1)g(x)+2m_i(x-r_i)g'(x)+(x-r_i)^2g''(x)\bigr]
.$$

Evaluating at $x=r_i$: $0=Q(r_i)\cdot m_i(m_i-1)g(r_i)$, so $Q(r_i)=0$. Since th
e bracket is nonzero at $r_i$, the multiplicity of $r_i$ as a root of the RHS eq
uals its multiplicity as a root of $Q$; the LHS has multiplicity exactly $2$. He
nce $(x-r_i)^2\mid Q(x)$. Since $\deg Q=2$, this forces

$$Q(x)=c\,(x-r_i)^2,\qquad c\ne 0.$$

**Step 2. When $Q(x)=c\,(x-r)^2$, the polynomial $P$ has at most one distinct ro
ot.**

Set $u=x-r$ and write $P=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_j u^j$. The identity $P=QP''$ becomes

$$\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_j u^j = c\,u^2\sum_{j=2}^{n}j(j-1)a_j u^{j-2}=c\sum_{j=2}^{n}j
(j-1)a_j u^j.$$

Comparing coefficients of $u^j$:

- $j=0$: $a_0=0$.
- $j=1$: $a_1=0$.
- $j\ge 2$: $a_j\bigl(1-c\,j(j-1)\bigr)=0$, so $a_j\ne 0$ only if $c\,j(j-1)=1$.

The equation $c\,j(j-1)=1$, i.e., $j^2-j-\tfrac{1}{c}=0$, has two roots $j_1,j_2
$ with $j_1+j_2=1$ (Vieta's). Since $j_1+j_2=1$, **at most one** of them can be
$\ge 2$ (both $\ge 2$ would give sum $\ge 4$). Moreover, for $a_j\ne 0$ we need
$j$ to be a non-negative integer $\ge 2$. Hence at most one coefficient $a_j$ wi
th $j\ge 2$ is nonzero, and $a_0=a_1=0$. Therefore

$$P(x)=a_j\,(x-r)^j$$

for a single $j\ge 2$. This polynomial has exactly **one** distinct root, namely
 $x=r$.

**Step 3. Conclusion.**

If any root $r_i$ of $P$ had multiplicity $m_i\ge 2$, Steps 1–2 imply $P$ has at
 most one distinct root, contradicting the hypothesis $k\ge 2$. Therefore every
root is simple: $m_i=1$ for all $i$, and

$$n=\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_i=k.$$

So $P$ has exactly $n$ distinct roots. $\blacksquare$
