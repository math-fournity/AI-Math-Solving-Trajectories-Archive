## Proof

Let $(X, x_0)$ be an arbitrary pointed topological space (no separation axioms a
ssumed). We write $\pi_n(X,x_0) = [(I^n, \partial I^n),\, (X, x_0)]$, the set of
 homotopy classes of maps $I^n \to X$ sending $\partial I^n$ to $x_0$, where hom
otopies are required to keep $\partial I^n$ at $x_0$ throughout. We verify that
the standard proof of abelianness for $n \ge 2$ goes through without any Hausdor
ff hypothesis on $X$.

### Step 1 — Homotopy is an equivalence relation

- **Reflexivity**: the constant homotopy $H(t,s) = f(t)$ is continuous for any t
arget $X$.
- **Symmetry**: reversing the homotopy parameter, $H'(t,s) = H(t, 1-s)$, is comp
osition of $H$ with a continuous self-map of $I$; valid for any $X$.
- **Transitivity**: given $H_1: f \simeq g$ and $H_2: g \simeq h$, we paste them
 at $s = 1/2$. This uses the **pasting lemma**: if $A, B$ are closed in the doma
in $Y$ with $Y = A \cup B$, and continuous maps $A \to X$, $B \to X$ agree on $A
 \cap B$, the combined map is continuous.

The pasting lemma is a statement about the **domain** (the pieces $A, B$ must be
 closed in $Y$); the **target** $X$ is arbitrary. Its proof uses the closed-set
characterization of continuity: $h^{-1}(C) = f^{-1}(C) \cup g^{-1}(C)$ is closed
 in $Y$ because $f^{-1}(C)$ is closed in $A$ (hence in $Y$, since $A$ is closed
in $Y$) and similarly for $g^{-1}(C)$. This characterization holds for every top
ological space $X$, Hausdorff or not. In our application $Y = I^n \times I$ and
the pieces $\{s \le 1/2\}$, $\{s \ge 1/2\}$ are closed in $Y$. No condition on $
X$ is invoked.

### Step 2 — The group operation is well-defined

The sum $[f] + [g]$ is defined by pinching $I^n$ along the first coordinate: pre
compose $f$ with the first half and $g$ with the second half. If $f \simeq f'$ v
ia $F$ and $g \simeq g'$ via $G$, then $f + g \simeq f' + g'$ via the homotopy t
hat applies $F$ on the first half and $G$ on the second half—again assembled by
the pasting lemma on the domain. No property of $X$ beyond continuity of the con
stituent maps is used.

### Step 3 — Group axioms

- **Identity**: the constant map $c(t) = x_0$. The homotopy $f + c \simeq f$ (an
d $c + f \simeq f$) is given by a continuous reparametrization of the domain—sli
ding the "active" portion. This is composition of $f$ with a continuous map $I^n
 \times I \to I^n$, valid for any $X$.
- **Associativity**: $(f+g)+h \simeq f+(g+h)$ by reparametrizing the first coord
inate. Same mechanism; no condition on $X$.
- **Inverses**: define $\bar f(t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n) = f(1 - t_1, t_2, \dots, t_
n)$. The homotopy $f + \bar f \simeq c$ progressively shrinks the support of $f$
 to the boundary, which is a continuous map $I^n \times I \to X$ built from $f$
by precomposition. No condition on $X$.

### Step 4 — Two operations and the Eckmann–Hilton argument

For $n \ge 2$ we may pinch along coordinate $1$ (giving $+_1$) or along coordina
te $2$ (giving $+_2$). Each is a group operation on $\pi_n(X, x_0)$ with the **s
ame** identity (the class of the constant map), by the same arguments as Steps 2
–3 applied to the second coordinate.

**Interchange law.** We must show
$$
(f +_1 g) +_2 (h +_1 k) \;=\; (f +_2 h) +_1 (g +_2 k).
$$
Both sides are represented by the map that places $f, g, h, k$ in the four quadr
ants of the $(t_1, t_2)$-plane (with the remaining coordinates untouched) and se
nds the complement of the four quadrants to $x_0$. The two expressions differ on
ly by how the domain is subdivided for the purpose of *describing* the same map;
 they are literally the same continuous function $I^n \to X$. The verification t
hat this function is continuous is an application of the pasting lemma on the do
main $I^n$ (the four closed quadrants), which—as established in Step 1—requires
no hypothesis on $X$.

**Eckmann–Hilton.** With two unital binary operations $+_1, +_2$ on a set, each
a homomorphism for the other (the interchange law), and sharing the same unit $e
$, the algebraic argument gives:
$$
f +_1 g = (f +_2 e) +_1 (e +_2 g) = (f +_1 e) +_2 (e +_1 g) = f +_2 g,
$$
so $+_1 = +_2$, and
$$
f +_1 g = f +_2 g = (e +_2 f) +_1 (g +_2 e) = (e +_1 g) +_2 (f +_1 e) = g +_1 f,
$$
so the operation is **commutative**. This is pure algebra—no topology enters.

### Step 5 — Audit of topological tools

Every topological ingredient in the proof is:

| Tool | Where used | Depends on $X$ being Hausdorff? |
|---|---|---|
| Continuity of composition $f \circ \varphi$ with $\varphi: I^n \to I^n$ | Repa
rametrization, identity, inverses, interchange | No — composition of continuous
maps is continuous for any target |
| Pasting lemma (closed pieces in domain) | Transitivity of homotopy, well-defin
edness of $+$, continuity of the 4-quadrant map | No — the hypothesis is on the
**domain** (pieces closed in $I^n$); the target is arbitrary |
| Constant map $I^n \to x_0$ is continuous | Identity element | No — the preimag
e of any open set is either $\emptyset$ or $I^n$ |

No other topological tool is invoked. In particular:

- **No uniqueness-of-limits argument** is used (that is where Hausdorffness woul
d matter, but homotopy theory does not take limits in the target).
- **No separation axiom on $X$** is needed for the closed-set or open-set charac
terizations of continuity.
- **No quotient-map property of the target** is required; the pinch map is a quo
tient map on the *domain* $I^n$, and the universal property of quotient maps (a
map constant on fibers factors through the quotient) holds for **any** target $X
$.

### Conclusion

The standard proof that $\pi_n(X, x_0)$ is abelian for $n \geq 2$—via the Eckman
n–Hilton argument applied to the two coordinate-pinch operations—uses only (a) c
ontinuity of compositions, (b) the pasting lemma on the domain $I^n$, and (c) pu
re algebra. None of these require the target $X$ to satisfy any separation axiom
. Therefore **the proof remains valid for any pointed space $(X, x_0)$, Hausdorf
f or not.** $\blacksquare$
