## Commutator Subgroups of $S_3$ and $S_4$

**Notation.** For a group $G$, its commutator (derived) subgroup is $[G,G] = \la
ngle\, aba^{-1}b^{-1} \mid a,b \in G \,\rangle$. We write permutations in cycle
notation and compose right-to-left.

**Key fact (used for both).** For any three distinct symbols $a,b,c$, the commut
ator of the transpositions $(ab)$ and $(ac)$ is the 3-cycle $(abc)$:

$$[(ab),(ac)] = (ab)(ac)(ab)^{-1}(ac)^{-1} = (ab)(ac)(ab)(ac),$$

since transpositions are self-inverse. Computing the product $(ab)(ac)$ (apply $
(ac)$ first, then $(ab)$): $a \mapsto c \mapsto c$, $c \mapsto a \mapsto b$, $b
\mapsto b \mapsto a$, so $(ab)(ac) = (a\,c\,b) = (acb)$. Hence

$$[(ab),(ac)] = (acb)(acb) = (acb)^2 = (abc).$$

Thus **every 3-cycle $(abc)$ is a commutator of two transpositions** in $S_n$ (w
henever $a,b,c \in \{1,\dots,n\}$ are distinct).

### General upper bound: $[S_n, S_n] \subseteq A_n$ for $n \ge 2$

The sign homomorphism $\operatorname{sgn} : S_n \to \{\pm 1\} \cong \mathbb{Z}_2
$ is surjective with kernel $A_n$, so $S_n / A_n \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$, which is a
belian. The commutator subgroup is contained in the kernel of every homomorphism
 onto an abelian group, so

$$[S_n, S_n] \subseteq A_n \qquad (n \ge 2).$$

### Case $n = 3$: $[S_3, S_3] = A_3$

Here $A_3 = \{e, (1\,2\,3), (1\,3\,2)\} \cong \mathbb{Z}_3$, the cyclic subgroup
 generated by $(1\,2\,3)$.

- **Upper bound.** By the general bound above, $[S_3, S_3] \subseteq A_3$.
- **Lower bound.** Using the key fact with $a=1, b=2, c=3$:
  $$[(1\,2),(1\,3)] = (1\,2\,3) \in [S_3, S_3].$$
  Since $(1\,2\,3)$ generates $A_3$, we get $A_3 \subseteq [S_3, S_3]$.

Combining both inclusions:

$$\boxed{[S_3, S_3] = A_3 = \{e,\,(1\,2\,3),\,(1\,3\,2)\} \cong \mathbb{Z}_3.}$$

### Case $n = 4$: $[S_4, S_4] = A_4$

The alternating group $A_4$ has order $12$ and is generated by its 3-cycles. (In
deed every element of $A_4$ is a product of 3-cycles: the identity is empty, the
 eight 3-cycles are themselves, and the three double-transpositions $(1\,2)(3\,4
), (1\,3)(2\,4), (1\,4)(2\,3)$ satisfy e.g. $(1\,2)(3\,4) = (1\,3\,2)(1\,3\,4)$,
 a product of two 3-cycles. So the 3-cycles generate $A_4$.)

- **Upper bound.** By the general bound, $[S_4, S_4] \subseteq A_4$.
- **Lower bound.** Every 3-cycle $(a\,b\,c)$ with $\{a,b,c\} \subseteq \{1,2,3,4
\}$ distinct is, by the key fact, the commutator $[(a\,b),(a\,c)]$ of two transp
ositions in $S_4$. Hence all eight 3-cycles lie in $[S_4, S_4]$. Since the 3-cyc
les generate $A_4$, we obtain $A_4 \subseteq [S_4, S_4]$.

Combining both inclusions:

$$\boxed{[S_4, S_4] = A_4.}$$

Explicitly, $A_4 = \{e\} \cup \{\text{8 three-cycles}\} \cup \{(1\,2)(3\,4),\,(1
\,3)(2\,4),\,(1\,4)(2\,3)\}$, a group of order $12$.
