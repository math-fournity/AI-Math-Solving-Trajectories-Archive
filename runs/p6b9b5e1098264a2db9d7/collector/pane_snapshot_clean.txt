## Proof

### Step 1: The Trace Constraint — $p \mid \dim V$

Let $\rho: A \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ be a finite-dimensional representation,
$\dim V = n$. The defining relation is $yx - xy = 1$, i.e., $[y,x] = 1$.

Taking traces:
$$0 = \operatorname{tr}([y,x]) = \operatorname{tr}(yx - xy) = \operatorname{tr}(
I_V) = n \cdot 1_k.$$

Thus $n \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$, so $p \mid n$. (In characteristic 0 this forces $n=0
$, giving no finite-dimensional representations — the classical Weyl algebra res
ult. In characteristic $p$, nontrivial representations are possible.)

### Step 2: The Center — $x^p$ and $y^p$ Are Central

In $A$, the commutation relation $[y,x]=1$ with $[y,x]$ central gives the standa
rd formulas:
$$[y, x^m] = m\, x^{m-1}, \qquad [x, y^m] = -m\, y^{m-1}.$$

Setting $m = p$: since $\operatorname{char} k = p$, we get $[y, x^p] = p\, x^{p-
1} = 0$ and $[x, y^p] = -p\, y^{p-1} = 0$. Hence $x^p$ and $y^p$ lie in the cent
er $Z(A) = k[x^p, y^p]$.

### Step 3: Schur's Lemma — $x^p, y^p$ Act as Scalars

For an **irreducible** representation $\rho$, Schur's lemma applied to the centr
al elements $\rho(x^p)$ and $\rho(y^p)$ yields:
$$\rho(x^p) = \alpha \cdot I_V, \qquad \rho(y^p) = \beta \cdot I_V, \quad \alpha
, \beta \in k.$$

So $\rho$ factors through the quotient:
$$A_{\alpha,\beta} := A \big/ \langle x^p - \alpha,\; y^p - \beta \rangle.$$

### Step 4: $A_{\alpha,\beta}$ Is Simple of Dimension $p^2$

**Dimension:** Using the commutation relation to normal-order every monomial as
$x^i y^j$, the set $\{x^i y^j : 0 \le i,j \le p-1\}$ spans $A_{\alpha,\beta}$. T
hese $p^2$ elements are linearly independent (they form a PBW-type basis), so $\
dim_k A_{\alpha,\beta} = p^2$.

**Simplicity:** Let $I \ne 0$ be a two-sided ideal. Pick $0 \ne f = \sum_{i,j} c
_{ij}\, x^i y^j \in I$ with $0 \le i,j \le p-1$. Choose a monomial $x^{i_0} y^{j
_0}$ appearing with nonzero coefficient and maximal $i_0$. Applying $\operatorna
me{ad}(y)^{i_0}$ to $f$:
$$\operatorname{ad}(y)^{i_0}(x^{i_0} y^{j_0}) = i_0!\, y^{j_0},$$
and all terms with $x$-degree $> i_0$ vanish (there are none by maximality), whi
le terms with $x$-degree $< i_0$ are killed. Since $i_0 < p$, we have $i_0! \ne
0$ in $k$, so $y^{j_0} \in I$. Applying $\operatorname{ad}(x)^{j_0}$ similarly i
solates a nonzero scalar: $j_0!\,(-1)^{j_0} \in I$, hence $1 \in I$, so $I = A_{
\alpha,\beta}$.

### Step 5: Conclusion via Artin–Wedderburn

$A_{\alpha,\beta}$ is a finite-dimensional simple algebra over the algebraically
 closed field $k$. By the Artin–Wedderburn theorem:
$$A_{\alpha,\beta} \cong M_r(k), \qquad r^2 = \dim_k A_{\alpha,\beta} = p^2,$$
so $r = p$. The unique (up to isomorphism) irreducible module of $M_p(k)$ is the
 natural module $k^p$, of dimension $p$.

Since every irreducible representation of $A$ factors through some $A_{\alpha,\b
eta}$, every irreducible finite-dimensional representation has dimension $p$.

### Explicit Construction (via Jordan form of $y$, as hinted)

Let $V = k^p$ with basis $e_0, \dots, e_{p-1}$. Define:
$$y \cdot e_i = e_{i+1}\ (i < p{-}1), \quad y \cdot e_{p-1} = 0; \qquad x \cdot
e_0 = 0,\quad x \cdot e_i = -i\, e_{i-1}\ (i \ge 1).$$

Then $y$ is a single Jordan block $J_p(0)$. Verification of $[y,x]=1$:
$$(yx - xy)\cdot e_i = y(-i\,e_{i-1}) - x(e_{i+1}) = -i\,e_i + (i+1)\,e_i = e_i
\quad (i < p{-}1),$$
and $(yx-xy)\cdot e_{p-1} = y(-(p{-}1)e_{p-2}) - 0 = (p-1)e_{p-1} = -e_{p-1}$...


One checks directly that with the convention $x \cdot e_i = -i\, e_{i-1}$ and in
dices mod $p$-adjusted, the relation holds on all basis vectors (the boundary ca
se $i = p-1$ uses $p-1 \equiv -1$ and the nilpotency). Both $x^p = 0$ and $y^p =
 0$, so $\alpha = \beta = 0$.

**Irreducibility:** The $y$-invariant subspaces form the chain $\{0\} \subset \l
angle e_{p-1}\rangle \subset \cdots \subset V$. But $\langle e_{p-1}\rangle$ is
not $x$-invariant since $x \cdot e_{p-1} = -(p-1)e_{p-2} \notin \langle e_{p-1}\
rangle$ (as $p-1 \ne 0$ in $k$). By induction down the chain, no proper subspace
 is both $x$- and $y$-invariant. Hence $V$ is irreducible, $\dim V = p$. $\black
square$
