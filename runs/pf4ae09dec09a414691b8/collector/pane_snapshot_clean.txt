# Proof

## Setup and Notation

Let $(V, g)$ be a real vector space with a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear for
m $g$. Let $\{e_i\}_{i=1}^n$ and $\{f_j\}_{j=1}^n$ be two orthonormal bases, mea
ning $g(e_i, e_j) = \epsilon_i \delta_{ij}$ and $g(f_i, f_j) = \epsilon'_i \delt
a_{ij}$ with $\epsilon_i, \epsilon'_i \in \{+1, -1\}$.

Let $A$ be the change-of-basis matrix: $f_j = \sum_i A_{ij}\, e_i$. The $j$-th c
olumn of $A$ is the coordinate vector of $f_j$ in the $\{e_i\}$ basis.

Let $G = \operatorname{diag}(\epsilon_1, \dots, \epsilon_n)$ and $G' = \operator
name{diag}(\epsilon'_1, \dots, \epsilon'_n)$ be the matrices of $g$ in the two b
ases.

## Part 1: Columns are NOT unit-length orthogonal in general

The orthonormality of $\{f_j\}$ translates to:

$$g(f_i, f_j) = A_i^T G\, A_j = \epsilon'_i\, \delta_{ij}$$

or in matrix form:

$$A^T G\, A = G'. \tag{1}$$

This says the columns of $A$ are orthogonal and of "$g$-length" $\pm 1$ **with r
espect to the scalar product $g$**, not with respect to the standard Euclidean i
nner product. The Euclidean orthonormality of columns would require $A^T A = I$,
 which is a different condition.

**Counterexample.** Consider $V = \mathbb{R}^2$ with $g = \operatorname{diag}(1,
 -1)$ (the Minkowski plane). The standard basis $e_1 = (1,0)$, $e_2 = (0,1)$ is
orthonormal with $g(e_1,e_1)=1$, $g(e_2,e_2)=-1$.

A Lorentz boost by rapidity $\phi$ gives another orthonormal basis:

$$f_1 = (\cosh\phi,\, \sinh\phi), \qquad f_2 = (\sinh\phi,\, \cosh\phi).$$

**Verification:** $g(f_1,f_1) = \cosh^2\phi - \sinh^2\phi = 1$, $\;g(f_2,f_2) =
\sinh^2\phi - \cosh^2\phi = -1$, $\;g(f_1,f_2) = \cosh\phi\sinh\phi - \sinh\phi\
cosh\phi = 0$. So $\{f_1, f_2\}$ is orthonormal.

The change-of-basis matrix is:

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} \cosh\phi & \sinh\phi \\ \sinh\phi & \cosh\phi \end{pmatri
x}.$$

The Euclidean lengths of the columns are $\sqrt{\cosh^2\phi + \sinh^2\phi} = \sq
rt{\cosh 2\phi} \neq 1$ (for $\phi \neq 0$), and the Euclidean dot product of th
e columns is $\sinh 2\phi \neq 0$. Thus the columns are **neither unit-length no
r orthogonal** in the Euclidean sense.

In the positive-definite case ($G = I$), Eq. (1) reduces to $A^T A = I$ (since $
G' = I$ as well), so Euclidean orthonormality of columns holds. The indefinite c
ase breaks this because $G \neq I$.

**Conclusion:** In the general scalar product case, the matrix representation do
es **not** have unit-length orthogonal columns (in the Euclidean sense).

## Part 2: The determinant is $\pm 1$

Taking the determinant of both sides of Eq. (1):

$$\det(A^T)\,\det(G)\,\det(A) = \det(G').$$

Since $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$:

$$\det(A)^2 = \frac{\det(G')}{\det(G)}. \tag{2}$$

Now, by **Sylvester's law of inertia**, the signature of a non-degenerate symmet
ric bilinear form is an invariant: any two orthonormal bases of the same form $g
$ have the same number of $+1$'s and $-1$'s on the diagonal. Therefore $G$ and $
G'$ have the same signature, which implies:

$$\det(G') = \det(G) = \prod_{i=1}^n \epsilon_i = (-1)^{n_-},$$

where $n_-$ is the number of negative entries (the index of $g$).

Substituting into Eq. (2):

$$\det(A)^2 = \frac{\det(G)}{\det(G)} = 1.$$

Since $A$ is a real matrix, $\det(A)$ is real, and $\det(A)^2 = 1$ implies:

$$\boxed{\det(A) = \pm 1.}$$

**Remark on the necessity of Sylvester's law.** Without the signature invariance
, Eq. (2) would only give $\det(A)^2 = \pm 1$. The value $-1$ is impossible for
a real squared quantity, so if the signatures differed, no such change-of-basis
would exist at all — which is precisely the content of Sylvester's law. The law
guarantees the signatures match, the ratio is $+1$, and the determinant is $\pm
1$.

## Summary

1. **No**, in the general (indefinite) scalar product case, the columns of the c
hange-of-basis matrix are not unit-length and orthogonal in the Euclidean sense.
 They are orthonormal only with respect to $g$ itself, which is a weaker/differe
nt condition when $g$ is not positive-definite.

2. **Yes**, the determinant of any change-of-basis between two orthonormal bases
 is $\pm 1$. This follows from $A^T G A = G'$ together with Sylvester's law of i
nertia, which forces $\det(G) = \det(G')$, yielding $\det(A)^2 = 1$.
