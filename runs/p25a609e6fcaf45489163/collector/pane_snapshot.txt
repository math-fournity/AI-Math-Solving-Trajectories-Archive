## Verification of Stokes' Theorem by Direct Computation

**Problem.** Compute $\oint_C -y^3\,dx + x^3\,dy - z^3\,dz$, where $C$ is the in
tersection of the cylinder $x^2+y^2=1$ and the plane $x+y+z=1$, and show it equa
ls $\dfrac{3\pi}{2}$.

---

### Step 1. Parametrize the curve $C$

The cylinder $x^2+y^2=1$ is parametrized by
$$x=\cos t,\qquad y=\sin t,\qquad t\in[0,2\pi].$$
Substituting into the plane equation $x+y+z=1$ gives
$$z = 1 - x - y = 1 - \cos t - \sin t.$$
Hence a parametrization of $C$ (traversed once as $t$ goes from $0$ to $2\pi$) i
s
$$\mathbf{r}(t) = \bigl(\cos t,\;\sin t,\;1-\cos t-\sin t\bigr),\qquad 0\le t\le
 2\pi.$$

### Step 2. Compute the differentials

$$\frac{dx}{dt}=-\sin t,\qquad \frac{dy}{dt}=\cos t,\qquad \frac{dz}{dt}=\sin t
- \cos t.$$

### Step 3. Substitute into the line integral

With $P=-y^3$, $Q=x^3$, $R=-z^3$:

$$\oint_C P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\Bigl[P\,\frac{dx}{dt}+Q\,\frac{dy}
{dt}+R\,\frac{dz}{dt}\Bigr]dt.$$

Computing each term:

- $P\,\dfrac{dx}{dt} = -\sin^3 t\cdot(-\sin t) = \sin^4 t$
- $Q\,\dfrac{dy}{dt} = \cos^3 t\cdot\cos t = \cos^4 t$
- $R\,\dfrac{dz}{dt} = -\bigl(1-\cos t-\sin t\bigr)^3(\sin t-\cos t)$

So the integral splits into two parts:

$$I = \underbrace{\int_0^{2\pi}\!\bigl(\sin^4 t+\cos^4 t\bigr)\,dt}_{I_1} \;\und
erbrace{-\;\int_0^{2\pi}\!\bigl(1-\cos t-\sin t\bigr)^3(\sin t-\cos t)\,dt}_{I_2
}.$$

### Step 4. Evaluate $I_1$

Using the identity
$$\sin^4 t + \cos^4 t = \bigl(\sin^2 t+\cos^2 t\bigr)^2 - 2\sin^2 t\cos^2 t = 1
- \tfrac12\sin^2(2t),$$
and $\sin^2(2t)=\dfrac{1-\cos(4t)}{2}$, we get
$$\sin^4 t+\cos^4 t = 1 - \tfrac14\bigl(1-\cos(4t)\bigr)=\tfrac34+\tfrac14\cos(4
t).$$

Therefore
$$I_1=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\Bigl(\tfrac34+\tfrac14\cos(4t)\Bigr)dt = \tfrac34\cdot 2\p
i + \tfrac14\cdot 0 = \frac{3\pi}{2}.$$

### Step 5. Evaluate $I_2$

Set $u(t)=1-\cos t-\sin t$. Then
$$\frac{du}{dt}=\sin t - \cos t,$$
which is exactly the second factor. Hence $I_2$ is an exact differential:
$$I_2 = -\int_0^{2\pi} u^3\,\frac{du}{dt}\,dt = -\int_{u(0)}^{u(2\pi)} u^3\,du =
 -\Bigl[\frac{u^4}{4}\Bigr]_{u(0)}^{u(2\pi)}.$$

Since
$$u(0)=1-1-0=0,\qquad u(2\pi)=1-1-0=0,$$
we obtain
$$I_2 = -\frac{0-0}{4}=0.$$

### Step 6. Combine

$$I = I_1 + I_2 = \frac{3\pi}{2}+0 = \boxed{\dfrac{3\pi}{2}}.$$

---

### Consistency check via Stokes' theorem

The vector field is $\mathbf{F}=(-y^3,\,x^3,\,-z^3)$, with
$$\nabla\times\mathbf{F}=\bigl(0-0,\;0-0,\;3x^2+3y^2\bigr)=(0,0,3(x^2+y^2)).$$

The surface $S$ is the planar disk $x+y+z=1$ inside the cylinder, with upward un
it normal
$$\mathbf{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}(1,1,1).$$

By Stokes' theorem,
$$\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}=\iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\math
bf{n}\,dS = \iint_S \frac{3(x^2+y^2)}{\sqrt3}\,dS.$$

Since $dS = \sqrt3\,dA$ (the plane has normal $(1,1,1)$ of length $\sqrt3$, so $
dS=\sqrt3\,dA$ over the projection onto the $xy$-plane), and $x^2+y^2$ is the po
lar radius squared on the unit disk:
$$\iint_S \frac{3(x^2+y^2)}{\sqrt3}\cdot\sqrt3\,dA = 3\iint_{x^2+y^2\le 1}(x^2+y
^2)\,dA = 3\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^1 r^2\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta = 3\cdot 2\pi\cdot\fr
ac14=\frac{3\pi}{2}.$$

This agrees with the direct line-integral computation, confirming Stokes' theore
m. $\blacksquare$

### PROOF COMPLETE
