## Proof

The answer is **no**. We construct a counterexample with $d=1$.

**Setup.** Let $\{h_n\}_{n=0}^\infty$ be the Hermite functions, an orthonormal b
asis of $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ and eigenfunctions of $-\Delta+|x|^2$ with eigenvalues
 $2n+1$. Under the unitary map $f\mapsto(\langle f,h_n\rangle_{L^2})_{n\ge0}$, w
e identify:
- $L^2(\mathbb{R})\cong\ell^2$,
- $H^1(\mathbb{R})\cong\ell^2_1:=\{(c_n):\sum(1+n)|c_n|^2<\infty\}$,
- $H^{-1}(\mathbb{R})\cong\ell^2_{-1}:=\{(c_n):\sum(1+n)^{-1}|c_n|^2<\infty\}$.

Both the $L^2$ inner product and the $H^{-1}$–$H^1$ duality pairing correspond t
o $\sum c_n\overline{d_n}$ (convergent by Cauchy–Schwarz in the respective weigh
ted spaces). The Fourier transform is an isomorphism preserving this pairing, so
 the hats in the problem statement are immaterial.

**Construction of $X$.** Define $\alpha=(0,\,1,\,2^{-1/4},\,3^{-1/4},\ldots)$, i
.e. $\alpha_0=0$, $\alpha_n=n^{-1/4}$ for $n\ge1$. Then:
- $\alpha\in\ell^2_{-1}$: $\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-1/2}(1+n)^{-1}\le\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-3/2}
<\infty$,
- $\alpha\notin\ell^2$: $\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-1/2}=\infty$.

Set
$$X=\Big\{d\in\ell^2_1:\; d_0=0\;\text{and}\;\sum_{n\ge1}\alpha_n\,d_n=0\Big\}.$
$

This is a closed subspace of $\ell^2_1$ (hence a vector space in $H^1$), being t
he intersection of the kernels of two continuous linear functionals on $\ell^2_1
$: $\ell_0(d)=d_0$ (corresponding to $e_0\in\ell^2_{-1}$) and $\ell_\alpha(d)=\s
um_{n\ge1}\alpha_n d_n$ (corresponding to $\alpha\in\ell^2_{-1}$). These are ind
ependent ($e_0\in\ker\ell_\alpha\setminus\ker\ell_0$, $e_1\in\ker\ell_0\setminus
\ker\ell_\alpha$), so $X$ has codimension $2$ in $\ell^2_1$.

**Computing $X^{\perp_{L^2}}$.** We claim $X^{\perp_{L^2}}=\operatorname{span}\{
e_0\}$.

The inclusion $e_0\in X^{\perp_{L^2}}$ is immediate since $d_0=0$ for all $d\in
X$.

Conversely, let $c\in\ell^2$ with $\langle c,d\rangle=0$ for all $d\in X$. Write
 $c=c_0 e_0+c'$ with $c'=(0,c_1,c_2,\ldots)\in\ell^2$. Then $c'$ annihilates $X$
, i.e. the $\ell^2$-continuous functional $L_{c'}(d)=\langle c',d\rangle$ vanish
es on $X=\ker\ell_\alpha\cap V$ where $V=\{d\in\ell^2_1:d_0=0\}$.

Since $\ell_\alpha$ is a nonzero functional on $V$ (as $\ell_\alpha(e_1)=1$), an
y linear functional on $V$ vanishing on $\ker\ell_\alpha$ is a scalar multiple o
f $\ell_\alpha$: $L_{c'}=\lambda\,\ell_\alpha$ for some $\lambda$. But $L_{c'}$
is $\ell^2$-continuous (because $c'\in\ell^2$) while $\ell_\alpha$ is **not** $\
ell^2$-continuous (because $\alpha\notin\ell^2$). Therefore $\lambda=0$, giving
$c'=0$ and $c=c_0 e_0$.

Thus $X^{\perp_{L^2}}=\operatorname{span}\{e_0\}$, which is **1-dimensional** (f
inite). ✓

**Computing $X^{\perp_{H^{-1}}}$.** Since $X$ is a closed subspace of $\ell^2_1$
 of codimension $2$, its annihilator in $(\ell^2_1)^*=\ell^2_{-1}$ is $2$-dimens
ional, spanned by the two defining functionals:

$$X^{\perp_{H^{-1}}}=\operatorname{span}\{e_0,\,\alpha\}.$$

Since $\alpha\in\ell^2_{-1}\setminus\ell^2$, the element $\alpha$ lies in $X^{\p
erp_{H^{-1}}}\setminus X^{\perp_{L^2}}$.

**Conclusion.** We have $X^{\perp_{L^2}}=\operatorname{span}\{e_0\}$ (finite-dim
ensional, $\dim=1$) but $X^{\perp_{H^{-1}}}=\operatorname{span}\{e_0,\alpha\}$ (
$\dim=2$, strictly larger, containing $\alpha\notin L^2$). Hence

$$X^{\perp_{H^{-1}}}\neq X^{\perp_{L^2}}.$$

The finite-dimensionality of $X^{\perp_{L^2}}$ does **not** imply $X^{\perp_{H^{
-1}}}=X^{\perp_{L^2}}$.
