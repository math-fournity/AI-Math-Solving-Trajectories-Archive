## Answer

The statement is **false**. The inclusion $\langle X\rangle H \subseteq \langle
X, H\rangle$ always holds, but equality requires $\langle X\rangle H$ to be a su
bgroup, which fails in general.

### Proof

Recall that for subgroups $A, B \le G$, the product set $AB = \{ab : a \in A, b
\in B\}$ is itself a subgroup **if and only if** $AB = BA$. In particular, $\lan
gle X\rangle H$ need not be a subgroup, and when it is not, it cannot equal $G =
 \langle X, H\rangle$.

**Counterexample.** Let $G = S_3$, the symmetric group on $\{1,2,3\}$. Set
$$H = \{e, (12)\}, \qquad X = \{(13)\}.$$
Then $\langle X\rangle = \{e, (13)\}$, and
$$\langle X, H\rangle = \langle (12), (13)\rangle = S_3,$$
since the two transpositions $(12)$ and $(13)$ generate all of $S_3$ (their prod
uct is a 3-cycle, and a transposition together with a 3-cycle generates $S_3$).

Now compute the product set $\langle X\rangle H$:
$$\langle X\rangle H = \{e,\,(13)\}\cdot\{e,\,(12)\} = \{e,\,(12),\,(13),\,(13)(
12)\}.$$
Using right-to-left composition,
$$(13)(12):\; 1\mapsto 2,\; 2\mapsto 3,\; 3\mapsto 1,$$
so $(13)(12) = (123)$. Hence
$$\langle X\rangle H = \{e,\,(12),\,(13),\,(123)\},$$
which has **4 elements**. Since $|S_3| = 6$, this set is missing $(23)$ and $(13
2)$. Moreover, by Lagrange's theorem a subgroup of $S_3$ must have order dividin
g $6$, and $4\nmid 6$, so $\langle X\rangle H$ is not even a subgroup.

Therefore $\langle X\rangle H \ne S_3 = \langle X, H\rangle = G$, even though $G
 = \langle X, H\rangle$.

**Conclusion.** The implication $G = \langle X, H\rangle \;\Longrightarrow\; G =
 \langle X\rangle H$ does **not** hold in general. The obstruction is that $\lan
gle X\rangle H$ may fail to be a subgroup (equivalently, $\langle X\rangle$ may
fail to normalize $H$). Equality holds precisely when $\langle X\rangle H$ is a
subgroup, i.e., when $\langle X\rangle H = H\langle X\rangle$ (for instance when
 $H \trianglelefteq G$, in which case $\langle X\rangle H = \langle X, H\rangle$
 is guaranteed).
