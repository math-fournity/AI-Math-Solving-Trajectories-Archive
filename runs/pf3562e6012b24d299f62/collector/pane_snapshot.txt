## Proof

**Setup.** Let $X = \prod_{\alpha \in A} X_\alpha$ equipped with the product top
ology, and let $V \subseteq X$ be a nonempty open set. We interpret $S = \{\alph
a \in A : \pi_\alpha(V) \neq X_\alpha\}$, where $\pi_\alpha : X \to X_\alpha$ is
 the canonical projection.

**Step 1: $S$ is always finite.**

Since $V$ is open in the product topology, we can write $V = \bigcup_{i \in I} B
_i$ where each $B_i$ is a basic open set of the form $B_i = \prod_{\alpha \in A}
 U_\alpha^{(i)}$, with each $U_\alpha^{(i)}$ open in $X_\alpha$ and $U_\alpha^{(
i)} = X_\alpha$ for all but finitely many $\alpha$. The index set $I$ is nonempt
y since $V$ is nonempty.

For each $i \in I$, define $F_i = \{\alpha \in A : U_\alpha^{(i)} \neq X_\alpha\
}$. By definition of the product topology, each $F_i$ is **finite**.

Now, the projection of $V$ onto coordinate $\alpha$ is:
$$\pi_\alpha(V) = \bigcup_{i \in I} \pi_\alpha(B_i) = \bigcup_{i \in I} U_\alpha
^{(i)}.$$

**Key observation:** If $\alpha \notin F_i$ for some $i_0 \in I$, then $U_\alpha
^{(i_0)} = X_\alpha$, so $\pi_\alpha(V) \supseteq X_\alpha$, which forces $\pi_\
alpha(V) = X_\alpha$, and thus $\alpha \notin S$.

By contrapositive, $\alpha \in S$ implies $\alpha \in F_i$ for **every** $i \in
I$. Therefore:
$$S \subseteq \bigcap_{i \in I} F_i.$$

Since $I \neq \emptyset$, pick any $i_0 \in I$. Then:
$$S \subseteq \bigcap_{i \in I} F_i \subseteq F_{i_0}.$$

Since $F_{i_0}$ is finite, $S$ is finite. $\quad\checkmark$

**Step 2: Every finite cardinality is achievable.**

For any $n \in \mathbb{N}$, take $A = \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$, each $X_\alpha = \{0
, 1\}$ with the discrete topology, and $V = \{0\} \times \{0\} \times \cdots \ti
mes \{0\} = \prod_{\alpha=1}^n \{0\}$. This is a basic open set (each factor $\{
0\}$ is open in the discrete topology, and only finitely many—indeed all $n$—coo
rdinates are restricted).

Then $\pi_\alpha(V) = \{0\} \neq \{0,1\} = X_\alpha$ for every $\alpha \in A$, s
o $S = A$ and $|S| = n$.

**Step 3: $S$ cannot be infinite.**

Suppose for contradiction that $S$ is infinite. Pick any $i_0 \in I$ (which exis
ts since $V \neq \emptyset$). Since $F_{i_0}$ is finite and $S$ is infinite, the
re exists $\alpha \in S \setminus F_{i_0}$. Then $U_\alpha^{(i_0)} = X_\alpha$,
so $\pi_\alpha(V) \supseteq X_\alpha = \pi_\alpha(V)$, giving $\pi_\alpha(V) = X
_\alpha$. But this contradicts $\alpha \in S$. $\quad\checkmark$

**Conclusion.**

$S$ is always finite ($|S| < \aleph_0$), yet $|S|$ can be any finite cardinal $n
$. The supremum of all achievable cardinalities is $\aleph_0$.

$$\boxed{|S| < \aleph_0 \text{ always; the maximum possible cardinality is } \al
eph_0 \text{ (supremum over all configurations, not achieved).}}$$

The essential reason is that the product topology restricts basic open sets to d
iffer from the full space in only finitely many coordinates, and this finiteness
 propagates to arbitrary unions: any coordinate left unrestricted by even one ba
sic open set in the union has full projection.
