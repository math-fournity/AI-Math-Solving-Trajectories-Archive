## Proof

The answer is **yes**, and it follows directly from Lebesgue's differentiation t
heorem, without even needing the hypothesis on $G$.

**Key theorem (Lebesgue):** Every monotone (non-decreasing or non-increasing) re
al-valued function on an interval is differentiable almost everywhere with respe
ct to Lebesgue measure.

**Application to $F$:** Since $F: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is non-decreasing,
$F$ is monotone. For each $n \geq 1$, restrict $F$ to the closed interval $[-n,
n]$. By Lebesgue's differentiation theorem, $F$ is differentiable almost everywh
ere on $[-n, n]$. Let

$$E_n = \{x \in [-n, n] : F \text{ is not differentiable at } x\}.$$

Each $E_n$ has Lebesgue measure zero. The set of all points where $F$ fails to b
e differentiable is

$$E = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} E_n,$$

which is a countable union of measure-zero sets, hence has measure zero. Therefo
re $F$ is differentiable almost everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$.

**Remark on the hypothesis:** The assumption that $G$ is differentiable a.e. is
in fact redundant. Indeed, $G$ is itself non-decreasing: if $x_1 < x_2$, then $\
{F(y) : y > x_2\} \subseteq \{F(y) : y > x_1\}$, so

$$G(x_1) = \inf_{y > x_1} F(y) \leq \inf_{y > x_2} F(y) = G(x_2).$$

Since $G$ is non-decreasing, Lebesgue's theorem already guarantees that $G$ is d
ifferentiable a.e. — the stated hypothesis adds no information. Both $F$ and $G$
 are differentiable a.e. purely by virtue of being monotone functions.
