Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the expression $Q(t) = \sum
_i v_i(1,0,\ldots,0)\, v_i(t, \sqrt{1-t^2}, 0, \ldots, 0)$ by itself suffices to
 conclude that $Q(t)$ is a polynomial in $t$ of degree exactly $k$.

**Answer: No.** The expression alone is insufficient. While $Q(t)$ is in fact a
polynomial of degree exactly $k$, this cannot be read off from the given express
ion without additional argument. Below is the proof.

---

## 1. Why the expression alone is insufficient

Each $v_i$ is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $k$ in $n$ variables. Restricti
ng to the $(x_1, x_2)$-plane (setting $x_3 = \cdots = x_n = 0$), we may write:

$$v_i(x_1, x_2, 0, \ldots, 0) = \sum_{j=0}^{k} c_{i,j}\, x_1^{k-j}\, x_2^{j}$$

Substituting $x_1 = t$, $x_2 = \sqrt{1-t^2}$:

$$v_i(t, \sqrt{1-t^2}, 0, \ldots, 0) = \sum_{j=0}^{k} c_{i,j}\, t^{k-j}\, (1-t^2
)^{j/2}$$

For **odd** $j$, the factor $(1-t^2)^{j/2} = (1-t^2)^{(j-1)/2}\sqrt{1-t^2}$ is *
*not a polynomial** in $t$. Therefore each individual summand $v_i(1,0,\ldots,0)
\, v_i(t, \sqrt{1-t^2}, 0, \ldots, 0)$ is generally not a polynomial in $t$, and
 the expression as written involves the non-polynomial quantity $\sqrt{1-t^2}$.

One can decompose:

$$Q(t) = P(t) + \sqrt{1-t^2}\cdot R(t)$$

where $P(t), R(t)$ are polynomials in $t$ (collecting even and odd powers of $\s
qrt{1-t^2}$ respectively). For $Q(t)$ to be a polynomial, one needs $R(t) \equiv
 0$, but **this cancellation is not evident from the expression** — it requires
additional structure.

---

## 2. $Q(t)$ is in fact a polynomial of degree exactly $k$ (requires additional
argument)

The proof uses three ingredients not visible in the expression: **(a)** $O(n)$-i
nvariance of the kernel, **(b)** $O(n-1)$-invariance of $K(e_1, \cdot)$, and **(
c)** harmonicity of $K(e_1, \cdot)$.

### 2a. Polynomiality via reflection symmetry

Since $Q(\langle x, y \rangle) = \sum_i v_i(x) v_i(y) =: K(x,y)$, the kernel $K$
 is $O(n)$-invariant: $K(gx, gy) = K(x,y)$ for all $g \in O(n)$. Consider the re
flection $\sigma: x_2 \mapsto -x_2$, which fixes $e_1 = (1,0,\ldots,0)$. Then:

$$Q(t) = K(e_1, (t, \sqrt{1-t^2}, 0, \ldots, 0)) = K(e_1, \sigma(t, \sqrt{1-t^2}
, 0, \ldots, 0)) = K(e_1, (t, -\sqrt{1-t^2}, 0, \ldots, 0))$$

Averaging the two representations:

$$Q(t) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_i v_i(e_1)\Big[v_i(t, \sqrt{1-t^2}, 0, \ldots, 0) + v_i
(t, -\sqrt{1-t^2}, 0, \ldots, 0)\Big]$$

The bracketed term is the **even part** of $v_i$ in $x_2$, involving only even p
owers of $\sqrt{1-t^2}$, hence only even powers of $(1-t^2)$, which are polynomi
als in $t$. Each term has degree at most $k$ (since $t^{k-j}(1-t^2)^{j/2}$ with
$j$ even has degree $k-j + j = k$). Therefore $Q(t)$ is a polynomial of degree $
\leq k$.

### 2b. Structure via $O(n-1)$-invariance

The stabilizer of $e_1$ in $O(n)$ is $O(n-1)$, acting on $(y_2, \ldots, y_n)$. B
y invariance, $K(e_1, y)$ is $O(n-1)$-invariant. By the fundamental theorem of $
O(n-1)$-invariant polynomials, $K(e_1, y)$ is a polynomial in $y_1$ and $|y'|^2
= y_2^2 + \cdots + y_n^2$. Being homogeneous of degree $k$:

$$K(e_1, y) = \sum_{\substack{a + 2b = k \\ a, b \geq 0}} \alpha_{a,b}\, y_1^a\,
 (|y'|^2)^b$$

On $S^{n-1}$, $|y'|^2 = 1 - y_1^2$, so:

$$Q(t) = \sum_{a+2b=k} \alpha_{a,b}\, t^a\, (1-t^2)^b$$

Each term $t^a(1-t^2)^b$ has degree $a + 2b = k$, confirming $\deg Q \leq k$.

### 2c. Degree exactly $k$ via harmonicity

Since $K(e_1, \cdot) \in H$ (it is a linear combination of the $v_i$), it is har
monic: $\Delta_y K(e_1, y) = 0$. Computing $\Delta = \partial_{y_1}^2 + \Delta_{
y'}$ on each term:

- $\partial_{y_1}^2 \big[y_1^a (|y'|^2)^b\big] = a(a-1)\, y_1^{a-2} (|y'|^2)^b$
- $\Delta_{y'} \big[(|y'|^2)^b\big] = 2b(n + 2b - 3)\, (|y'|^2)^{b-1}$

Setting $\Delta P = 0$ and matching terms of the same $(a, b)$ with $a + 2b = k-
2$ yields the recurrence:

$$\alpha_{a+2,\, b}\,(a+2)(a+1) + \alpha_{a,\, b+1}\, 2(b+1)(n + 2b - 1) = 0$$

Starting from $\alpha_{k, 0}$, this determines all coefficients:

$$\alpha_{k-2b,\, b} = \frac{(-1)^b\, k!}{(k-2b)!\, 4^b\, b!\, \left(\frac{n-1}{
2}\right)_b}\, \alpha_{k,0}$$

where $\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)_b = \prod_{j=0}^{b-1}\frac{n-1+2j}{2}$ is the
Pochhammer symbol (rising factorial), which is **positive** for $n \geq 2$.

Now, $\alpha_{k,0}$ is the coefficient of $y_1^k$ in $K(e_1, y)$, which equals:

$$\alpha_{k,0} = \sum_i v_i(e_1) \cdot [\text{coeff. of } y_1^k \text{ in } v_i(
y)] = \sum_i v_i(e_1)^2 = K(e_1, e_1)$$

By the reproducing property, $K(e_1, e_1) = \|K(\cdot, e_1)\|^2 \geq 0$, and $K(
e_1, e_1) = 0$ would imply $f(e_1) = 0$ for all $f \in H$, which is false (e.g.,
 the zonal harmonic at $e_1$ is nonzero). So $\alpha_{k,0} > 0$.

The coefficient of $t^k$ in $Q(t) = \sum_b \alpha_{k-2b,b}\, t^{k-2b}(1-t^2)^b$
is (extracting the leading term $(-1)^b t^k$ from each $(1-t^2)^b$):

$$[t^k]\, Q(t) = \sum_{b=0}^{\lfloor k/2 \rfloor} (-1)^b\, \alpha_{k-2b,\, b} =
\alpha_{k,0}\, k! \sum_{b=0}^{\lfloor k/2 \rfloor} \frac{1}{(k-2b)!\, 4^b\, b!\,
 \left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)_b}$$

Every term in this sum is **strictly positive** (since $\alpha_{k,0} > 0$ and $\
left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)_b > 0$ for $n \geq 2$). Therefore $[t^k]\, Q(t) > 0$,
and $\deg Q = k$ exactly.

---

## 3. Conclusion

$Q(t)$ **is** a polynomial of degree exactly $k$ — but this fact **cannot be con
cluded from the expression** $Q(t) = \sum_i v_i(1,0,\ldots,0)\, v_i(t, \sqrt{1-t
^2}, 0, \ldots, 0)$ alone. The expression involves $\sqrt{1-t^2}$, which is not
a polynomial, so:

1. **Polynomiality** requires the reflection-symmetry argument (from $O(n)$-inva
riance of $K$) to show the non-polynomial parts cancel.
2. **Degree exactly $k$** requires the $O(n-1)$-invariant structure, the harmoni
city recurrence, and the positivity of $K(e_1, e_1)$.

These are substantial additional arguments that go well beyond what the expressi
on displays. The expression, taken at face value, presents $Q(t)$ as a sum of te
rms each involving $\sqrt{1-t^2}$, and neither the polynomial nature nor the exa
ct degree is apparent without invoking the symmetry and harmonicity structure of
 the kernel.
