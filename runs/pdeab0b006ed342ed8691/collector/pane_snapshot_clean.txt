## Answer

**No.** The condition $f \circ f = \mathrm{id}$ does **not** imply that $f$ is c
ontinuous, and hence does not imply that $f$ is a homeomorphism. Below is a coun
terexample and the analysis.

---

### Key observation

If $f \circ f = \mathrm{id}$ and $f$ is bijective, then $f^{-1} = f$. Therefore

$$
f \text{ is a homeomorphism} \iff f \text{ is continuous} \iff f^{-1} \text{ is
continuous}.
$$

So the entire question reduces to: **must an involutive bijection be continuous?
** The answer is no.

---

### Counterexample

Let $X = \mathbb{R}$ with the standard (Euclidean) topology. Define $f \colon \m
athbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ by

$$
f(x) = \begin{cases}
1, & x = 0,\\
0, & x = 1,\\
x, & x \notin \{0,1\}.
\end{cases}
$$

That is, $f$ swaps $0$ and $1$ and fixes every other point.

**$f$ is bijective.** It is its own inverse: applying $f$ twice returns every po
int to itself, so $f \circ f = \mathrm{id}$, which gives both injectivity and su
rjectivity directly.

**$f \circ f = \mathrm{id}$.** Immediate from the definition: swapping $0 \leftr
ightarrow 1$ twice is the identity, and every other point is fixed.

**$f$ is not continuous.** Consider the open set $U = (0.5,\, 1.5) \subseteq \ma
thbb{R}$. We compute the preimage $f^{-1}(U)$:

- For $x \notin \{0,1\}$: $f(x) = x$, so $x \in f^{-1}(U) \iff x \in (0.5,1.5)$,
 i.e. $x \in (0.5,1)\cup(1,1.5)$ (excluding $1$).
- For $x = 0$: $f(0) = 1 \in U$, so $0 \in f^{-1}(U)$.
- For $x = 1$: $f(1) = 0 \notin U$, so $1 \notin f^{-1}(U)$.

Hence
$$
f^{-1}(U) = \{0\} \cup (0.5,1) \cup (1,1.5).
$$

This set is **not open** in $\mathbb{R}$: the point $0$ is isolated in $f^{-1}(U
)$ (there is no open interval around $0$ contained in $f^{-1}(U)$, since points
near $0$ other than $0$ itself are mapped to themselves and lie outside $(0.5,1.
5)$). Since the preimage of an open set is not open, $f$ is not continuous.

---

### Conclusion

We have exhibited a bijective involution $f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ (w
ith $f\circ f = \mathrm{id}$) on a topological space that is **not continuous**.
 Since $f^{-1} = f$, the inverse is also not continuous, so $f$ is not a homeomo
rphism.

Therefore the implication

$$
\bigl(f \text{ bijective and } f\circ f = \mathrm{id}\bigr) \;\Longrightarrow\;
f \text{ is a homeomorphism}
$$

is **false** in general. The algebraic condition $f \circ f = \mathrm{id}$ only
forces $f^{-1} = f$; it imposes no topological regularity. Continuity must be as
sumed (or derived from additional hypotheses on $X$ or $f$) separately.
