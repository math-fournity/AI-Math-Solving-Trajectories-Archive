## Proof

The answer is **No**. Conditions (a) and (b) do not imply that $f$ is differenti
able.

**Counterexample:** Let $f(x) = |x|$.

**Verification of condition (a):** We show $\lim_{h\to 0}|f(x+h)-f(x-h)|=0$ for
every $x\in\mathbb{R}$.

- **At $x=0$:** $|f(h)-f(-h)| = \big||h|-|h|\big| = 0$ for all $h\neq 0$, so the
 limit is $0$.
- **At $x\neq 0$:** Choose $|h|<|x|/2$, so that $x+h$ and $x-h$ share the sign o
f $x$. If $x>0$, then $f(x+h)-f(x-h)=(x+h)-(x-h)=2h$, hence $|f(x+h)-f(x-h)|=2|h
|\to 0$. The case $x<0$ is identical.

**Verification of condition (b):** We show $\lim_{h\to 0}|f(x+h)+f(x-h)-2f(x)|=0
$ for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$.

- **At $x=0$:** $|f(h)+f(-h)-2f(0)|=|h|+|h|-0=2|h|\to 0$.
- **At $x\neq 0$:** For $|h|<|x|/2$, both $x+h$ and $x-h$ share the sign of $x$.
 If $x>0$, then $f(x+h)+f(x-h)-2f(x)=(x+h)+(x-h)-2x=0$. The case $x<0$ is identi
cal.

**Non-differentiability at $x=0$:** The derivative at $x=0$ would be
$$f'(0)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(0+h)-f(0)}{h}=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{|h|}{h},$$
which does not exist since the right-hand limit is $+1$ and the left-hand limit
is $-1$.

**Conclusion:** The function $f(x)=|x|$ satisfies both conditions (a) and (b) fo
r every $x\in\mathbb{R}$, yet fails to be differentiable at $x=0$. Therefore, co
nditions (a) and (b) together do **not** imply that $f$ is differentiable. $\bla
cksquare$
