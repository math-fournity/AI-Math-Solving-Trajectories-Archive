Looking at this problem, I need to find the number of ways to decompose $\mathbb

## Proof

**Step 1: Identify the group structure.**

Every positive rational number has a unique prime factorization $q = \prod_p p^{
n_p}$ where $p$ ranges over all primes, $n_p \in \mathbb{Z}$, and only finitely
many $n_p$ are nonzero. The map $q \mapsto (n_p)_p$ is a group isomorphism:
$$\mathbb{Q}^+ \cong \bigoplus_{p \text{ prime}} \mathbb{Z},$$
the free abelian group of countable rank. Note that $\mathbb{Q}^+$ is **countabl
e**.

**Step 2: Translate the condition.**

The condition that every element of $\mathbb{Q}^+$ is uniquely expressible as $a
b$ with $a \in A$, $b \in B$ means the multiplication map $A \times B \to \mathb
b{Q}^+$ is a bijection. This is equivalent to:
- $A \cdot B = \mathbb{Q}^+$ (surjectivity), and
- $A \cap B = \{1\}$ (injectivity),

i.e., $\mathbb{Q}^+ = A \times B$ is an **internal direct product** decompositio
n, with both $A$ and $B$ proper.

**Step 3: Lower bound — construct $2^{\aleph_0}$ distinct pairs.**

Let $\mathcal{P}$ be the set of primes (countably infinite). For each subset $S
\subseteq \mathcal{P}$ with $\emptyset \neq S \neq \mathcal{P}$, define:
$$A_S = \left\{\prod_{p \in S} p^{n_p} : n_p \in \mathbb{Z}, \text{ finitely man
y nonzero}\right\}, \quad B_S = \left\{\prod_{p \notin S} p^{n_p} : n_p \in \mat
hbb{Z}, \text{ finitely many nonzero}\right\}.$$

These are subgroups of $\mathbb{Q}^+$ (closed under multiplication and inverses)
. Both are proper since $S \neq \emptyset$ and $S \neq \mathcal{P}$.

- **$A_S \cap B_S = \{1\}$**: If $q \in A_S \cap B_S$, then $q$'s prime factoriz
ation uses only primes in $S$ and only primes not in $S$, so all exponents are z
ero, giving $q = 1$.
- **$A_S \cdot B_S = \mathbb{Q}^+$**: Every $q \in \mathbb{Q}^+$ has a unique pr
ime factorization, which splits uniquely into the part supported on $S$ (an elem
ent of $A_S$) and the part supported on $\mathcal{P} \setminus S$ (an element of
 $B_S$).

So each such $S$ yields a valid pair $(A_S, B_S)$.

**Distinctness**: If $S \neq S'$, say $p \in S \setminus S'$, then $p \in A_S$ b
ut $p \notin A_{S'}$, so $A_S \neq A_{S'}$, hence $(A_S, B_S) \neq (A_{S'}, B_{S

The number of subsets $S$ with $\emptyset \neq S \neq \mathcal{P}$ is $2^{\aleph
_0} - 2 = 2^{\aleph_0}$. This gives **at least $2^{\aleph_0}$** distinct pairs.

**Step 4: Upper bound — at most $2^{\aleph_0}$ pairs.**

Since $\mathbb{Q}^+$ is countable, it has at most $2^{\aleph_0}$ subsets, hence
at most $2^{\aleph_0}$ subgroups. The number of ordered pairs of subgroups is at
 most $2^{\aleph_0} \times 2^{\aleph_0} = 2^{\aleph_0}$. Therefore the number of
 valid pairs is **at most $2^{\aleph_0}$**.

**Step 5: Conclusion.**

Combining the lower and upper bounds, the number of distinct pairs $(A, B)$ is e
xactly:
$$\boxed{2^{\aleph_0}}$$
i.e., the cardinality of the continuum (uncountably many).
