I'll work under the natural assumption that $p, q$ are positive integers (so tha

## Claim

The necessary and sufficient condition is $\gcd(p, q) = 1$.

---

## Proof of Necessity ($\gcd(p,q)=1$ is required)

Suppose $d = \gcd(p, q) > 1$. Write $p = da$ and $q = db$ with $a, b$ positive i
ntegers. Set $x = 2^{1/d}$.

- **$x$ is irrational:** Since $d > 1$ and $2$ is not a perfect $d$-th power, $2
^{1/d} \notin \mathbb{Q}$.
- **$x^p$ is rational:** $x^p = (2^{1/d})^{da} = 2^a \in \mathbb{Q}$.
- **$x^q$ is rational:** $x^q = (2^{1/d})^{db} = 2^b \in \mathbb{Q}$.

Thus we have an irrational $x$ with both $x^p$ and $x^q$ rational, violating the
 reverse implication. Therefore $\gcd(p, q) = 1$ is **necessary**. $\square$

---

## Proof of Sufficiency ($\gcd(p,q)=1$ is enough)

Assume $\gcd(p, q) = 1$. We must show both directions of the equivalence.

**Forward direction ($\Rightarrow$):** If $x \in \mathbb{Q}$, then for positive
integers $p, q$, the values $x^p$ and $x^q$ are rational (the rationals are clos
ed under multiplication; $0^p = 0$ for $p \geq 1$). So $x^p, x^q \in \mathbb{Q}$
.

**Reverse direction ($\Leftarrow$):** Suppose $x^p \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $x^q \in
\mathbb{Q}$.

- **Case $x = 0$:** Then $x \in \mathbb{Q}$ trivially.

- **Case $x \neq 0$:** Since $\gcd(p, q) = 1$, by **Bézout's identity** there ex
ist integers $a, b$ such that
$$ap + bq = 1.$$
Then:
$$x = x^{1} = x^{ap + bq} = x^{ap} \cdot x^{bq} = (x^p)^a \cdot (x^q)^b.$$

Since $x \neq 0$ and $p, q \geq 1$, we have $x^p \neq 0$ and $x^q \neq 0$. There
fore:
- If $a \geq 0$: $(x^p)^a$ is a rational number (rational base, non-negative int
eger exponent).
- If $a < 0$: $(x^p)^a = \frac{1}{(x^p)^{|a|}}$, which is rational since $x^p$ i
s a **nonzero** rational.
- The same reasoning applies to $(x^q)^b$.

Thus $(x^p)^a$ and $(x^q)^b$ are both rational, so their product $x$ is rational
. $\square$

---

## Conclusion

The necessary and sufficient condition is:

$$\boxed{\gcd(p,\, q) = 1}$$

**Necessity:** If $\gcd(p,q) = d > 1$, the irrational number $x = 2^{1/d}$ satis
fies $x^p = 2^{p/d}, x^q = 2^{q/d} \in \mathbb{Q}$, providing a counterexample.

**Sufficiency:** If $\gcd(p,q) = 1$, Bézout's identity $ap + bq = 1$ lets us wri
te $x = (x^p)^a(x^q)^b$ for $x \neq 0$, expressing $x$ as a product of rationals
; and $x = 0$ is trivially rational.
