## Proof

Let $f(x) = x^3 - 10x^2 + 29x - 25$.

### Step 1: $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ with three distinct real roots,
 all $> 1$.

By the rational root theorem, possible rational roots are $\pm 1, \pm 5, \pm 25$
. We compute $f(1)=-5$, $f(-1)=-65$, $f(5)=-5$, $f(-5)=-545$, $f(25)=10075$, non
e zero. Being a cubic with no rational root, $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}
$.

For real roots: $f(1)=-5<0$, $f(2)=1>0$, $f(3)=-1<0$, $f(5)=-5<0$, $f(6)=5>0$. B
y IVT, roots lie in $(1,2)$, $(2,3)$, and $(5,6)$—three distinct positive roots,
 all $>1$.

### Step 2: The Galois group is cyclic of order 3.

Substituting $x = u + 10/3$ gives the depressed cubic $u^3 - \frac{13}{3}u - \fr
ac{65}{27} = 0$ (with $p = -13/3$, $q = -65/27$). Its discriminant is:
$$\Delta = -4p^3 - 27q^2 = \frac{8788}{27} - \frac{4225}{27} = \frac{4563}{27} =
 169 = 13^2.$$
Since $\Delta$ is a perfect square, the Galois group is $A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\
mathbb{Z}$, and the splitting field equals $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ for any root $\a
lpha$. Let $\sigma$ be the generator with $\sigma(\alpha)=\beta$, $\sigma(\beta)
=\gamma$, $\sigma(\gamma)=\alpha$, where $\gamma$ is the third root.

### Step 3: For any two distinct roots $\alpha, \beta$, the ratio $\beta/\alpha$
 is irrational.

Suppose $\beta/\alpha = q \in \mathbb{Q}$, $q \neq 1$. Since $\beta = q\alpha$ i
s a root: $(q\alpha)^3 - 10(q\alpha)^2 + 29(q\alpha) - 25 = 0$. Using $\alpha^3
= 10\alpha^2 - 29\alpha + 25$:
$$10q^2(q-1)\alpha^2 - 29q(q^2-1)\alpha + 25(q^3-1) = 0.$$
Factoring out $(q-1) \neq 0$:
$$10q^2\alpha^2 - 29q(q+1)\alpha + 25(q^2+q+1) = 0.$$
This is a $\mathbb{Q}$-linear relation among $1, \alpha, \alpha^2$. But $\alpha$
 has degree 3 over $\mathbb{Q}$ (since $f$ is irreducible), so $\{1, \alpha, \al
pha^2\}$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb{Q}$. Thus $10q^2 = 0$, forcing $
q = 0$, but then $25(q^2+q+1) = 25 \neq 0$. **Contradiction.**

### Step 4: For any two distinct roots $\alpha, \beta$, the numbers $1, 1/\alpha
, 1/\beta$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb{Q}$.

Suppose $p + q/\alpha + r/\beta = 0$ for $(p,q,r) \in \mathbb{Q}^3 \setminus \{0
\}$, i.e., $p\alpha\beta + q\beta + r\alpha = 0$.

- **$q = 0$:** $\alpha(p\beta + r) = 0$. Since $\alpha \neq 0$, $p\beta + r = 0$
. If $p \neq 0$, then $\beta = -r/p \in \mathbb{Q}$, contradicting irrationality
. If $p = 0$, then $r = 0$: trivial.

- **$p = 0, q \neq 0$:** $q\beta + r\alpha = 0$ gives $\beta/\alpha = -r/q \in \
mathbb{Q}$, contradicting Step 3.

- **$p \neq 0, q \neq 0$:** Since $p\beta + r \neq 0$ (else $\beta \in \mathbb{Q
}$), we get $\alpha = -q\beta/(p\beta + r)$. Applying $\sigma$ and $\sigma^2$:
$$\beta = \frac{-q\gamma}{p\gamma + r}, \qquad \gamma = \frac{-q\alpha}{p\alpha
+ r}.$$
These yield:
$$p\alpha\beta + r\alpha + q\beta = 0 \quad\text{(i)},\quad p\beta\gamma + r\bet
a + q\gamma = 0 \quad\text{(ii)},\quad p\gamma\alpha + r\gamma + q\alpha = 0 \qu
ad\text{(iii)}.$$
Adding all three: $p(\alpha\beta+\beta\gamma+\gamma\alpha) + (q+r)(\alpha+\beta+
\gamma) = 0$, i.e., $29p + 10(q+r) = 0$, so $r = -(29p+10q)/10$.

Substituting into (i): $10p\alpha\beta - 29p\alpha - 10q\alpha + 10q\beta = 0$,
giving $p\alpha(10\beta - 29) = 10q(\alpha - \beta)$. Similarly from (ii) and (i
ii): $p\beta(10\gamma-29) = 10q(\beta-\gamma)$ and $p\gamma(10\alpha-29) = 10q(\
gamma-\alpha)$.

Setting $T = 10q/p \in \mathbb{Q}$, the Möbius transformation $\varphi(x) = \fra
c{(T+29)x}{10x + T}$ sends $\alpha \mapsto \beta \mapsto \gamma \mapsto \alpha$,
 so $\varphi$ has order 3. Its fixed points satisfy $10x^2 - 29x = 0$, giving $x
 = 0$ and $x = 29/10$. The multiplier at $x = 0$ is $\varphi'(0) = (T+29)/T \in
\mathbb{R}$. But a Möbius transformation of order 3 (not the identity) has multi
plier equal to a primitive cube root of unity $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$ or $\omega
^2$ at its fixed points—both **non-real**. Since $(T+29)/T \in \mathbb{R}$, this
 is a **contradiction**.

All cases yield contradictions, so $1, 1/\alpha, 1/\beta$ are linearly independe
nt over $\mathbb{Q}$.

### Step 5: Infinitely many common positive integers via Weyl's equidistribution
 theorem.

For $x > 1$ irrational and $m$ a positive integer, $m \in S(x)$ iff there exists
 a positive integer $n$ with $\lfloor nx \rfloor = m$, iff the interval $[m/x, (
m+1)/x)$ contains an integer. Since $x$ is irrational, $m/x$ is never an integer
, and the interval has length $1/x < 1$, so:
$$m \in S(x) \iff \{m/x\} \geq 1 - 1/x,$$
where $\{\cdot\}$ denotes fractional part. Note $1 - 1/x \in (0,1)$ since $x > 1
$.

By Step 4, for any two distinct roots $\alpha, \beta$, the numbers $1, 1/\alpha,
 1/\beta$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb{Q}$. By **Weyl's equidistributi
on theorem**, the sequence $\bigl(\{m/\alpha\}, \{m/\beta\}\bigr)_{m \geq 1}$ is
 equidistributed in $[0,1)^2$.

The region $\{(u, v) \in [0,1)^2 : u \geq 1 - 1/\alpha,\; v \geq 1 - 1/\beta\}$
is a rectangle of area $\frac{1}{\alpha} \cdot \frac{1}{\beta} > 0$. By equidist
ribution, the set of positive integers $m$ with $m \in S(\alpha) \cap S(\beta)$
has natural density $\frac{1}{\alpha\beta} > 0$, and is therefore **infinite**.

### Conclusion

This holds for **any** pair of distinct roots. Choosing, for instance, $\alpha \
in (1,2)$ and $\beta \in (2,3)$, we conclude that infinitely many positive integ
ers appear in both $S(\alpha)$ and $S(\beta)$. $\blacksquare$
