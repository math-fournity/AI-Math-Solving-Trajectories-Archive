## Proof

**Answer: Yes, such a function $v \in X$ exists.**

**Construction.** Since $u \in W^{2,2}(\Omega)$, we have $u'' \in L^2(\Omega)$.
Define
$$f := \max(0, u'') = (u'')^+.$$
Because $0 \leq f \leq |u''|$ pointwise a.e. and $u'' \in L^2(\Omega)$, we get $
f \in L^2(\Omega)$ (with $\|f\|_{L^2} \leq \|u''\|_{L^2}$).

We now solve the two-point boundary value problem
$$v'' = f \text{ in } \Omega, \qquad v(0) = v(1) = 0.$$

Integrating twice and imposing the boundary conditions gives the explicit formul
a
$$v(x) = \int_0^x (x-t)\,f(t)\,dt \;-\; x\int_0^1 (1-t)\,f(t)\,dt.$$

**Verification that $v \in W^{2,2}(\Omega)$.** Differentiating under the integra
l (valid since $f \in L^2 \subset L^1$ on the bounded interval $(0,1)$):
$$v'(x) = \int_0^x f(t)\,dt - \int_0^1 (1-t)\,f(t)\,dt,$$
$$v''(x) = f(x) \quad \text{a.e.}$$
Hence $v'' = f \in L^2(\Omega)$. Also $v'$ is absolutely continuous (it is an in
definite integral of an $L^1$ function), so $v' \in W^{1,2}(\Omega) \subset L^2(
\Omega)$, and $v$ is continuous on $[0,1]$, hence $v \in L^2(\Omega)$. Therefore
 $v \in W^{2,2}(\Omega)$.

**Verification that $v \in W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)$.** From the formula, $v(0) = 0$ an
d
$$v(1) = \int_0^1 (1-t)\,f(t)\,dt - \int_0^1 (1-t)\,f(t)\,dt = 0.$$
In one dimension, $W^{1,2}(\Omega) \hookrightarrow C^0(\overline{\Omega})$, and
$W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)$ coincides with the subspace of $W^{1,2}(\Omega)$ functions w
hose continuous representatives vanish at the endpoints. Since $v \in W^{1,2}(\O
mega)$ and $v(0)=v(1)=0$, we conclude $v \in W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)$.

**Conclusion.** The function $v$ defined above belongs to $X = W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)
 \cap W^{2,2}(\Omega)$ and satisfies $v'' = \max(0,u'')$ a.e. in $\Omega$. The o
nly ingredient used is that $u'' \in L^2(\Omega)$, so its positive part remains
in $L^2(\Omega)$, which is exactly the regularity needed to integrate twice and
stay within $W^{2,2}$.
