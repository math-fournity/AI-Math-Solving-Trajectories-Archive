Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether a countable family of bound
ed continuous functions can force weak convergence of any sequence of probabilit
y measures whose integrals against all family members converge.

**Answer: No, such a countable family does not exist (for $d \geq 1$).**

## Proof

Let $\{f_k\}_{k=1}^{\infty}$ be **any** countable family of continuous, bounded,
 real-valued functions on $\mathbb{R}^d$ (with $d \geq 1$). We construct a seque
nce of Borel probability measures for which every integral converges, yet the se
quence does not converge weakly to any probability measure.

**Construction.** Since $d \geq 1$, choose points $x_n = (n, 0, \ldots, 0) \in \
mathbb{R}^d$ so that $\|x_n\| \to \infty$. Consider the Dirac measures $\delta_{
x_n}$.

**Diagonal subsequence argument.** For each $k$, since $f_k$ is bounded, the seq
uence $\{f_k(x_n)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ is bounded in $\mathbb{R}$. We extract a sub
sequence along which **all** these sequences converge simultaneously:

- Since $\{f_1(x_n)\}$ is bounded, by Bolzano–Weierstrass there is a subsequence
 $\sigma^{(1)}$ of $\mathbb{N}$ with $f_1(x_{\sigma^{(1)}(j)})$ convergent.
- Inductively, given $\sigma^{(k-1)}$, the sequence $\{f_k(x_{\sigma^{(k-1)}(j)}
)\}$ is bounded, so there exists a subsequence $\sigma^{(k)}$ of $\sigma^{(k-1)}
$ along which $f_k(x_{\sigma^{(k)}(j)})$ converges.
- Set $n_j = \sigma^{(j)}(j)$. For each fixed $k$ and all $j \geq k$, the index
$n_j$ lies in the range of $\sigma^{(k)}$, so $\{n_j\}_{j \geq k}$ is a subseque
nce of $\sigma^{(k)}$. Hence $f_k(x_{n_j})$ converges as $j \to \infty$.

**Integrals converge.** Set $\mu_j = \delta_{x_{n_j}}$. Then:
$$\int f_k \, d\mu_j = f_k(x_{n_j}) \longrightarrow L_k \quad \text{for every }
k.$$
So the hypothesis is satisfied: the sequence of integrals converges for each fun
ction in the family.

**No weak limit exists.** Since $\|x_{n_j}\| \to \infty$ (as a subsequence of $x
_n$), the sequence $\{\mu_j\}$ is **not tight**: for any compact $K \subset \mat
hbb{R}^d$ (which is bounded), $x_{n_j} \notin K$ for all sufficiently large $j$,
 so $\mu_j(K) = 0$ for large $j$. Thus for $\varepsilon < 1$, no compact $K$ sat
isfies $\mu_j(K) \geq 1 - \varepsilon$ for all $j$.

By **Prohorov's theorem**, a sequence of probability measures on $\mathbb{R}^d$
that converges weakly to a probability measure must be tight (relative compactne
ss is necessary for convergence). The contrapositive: since $\{\mu_j\}$ is not t
ight, it cannot converge weakly to any Borel probability measure. In fact, no su
bsequence can converge weakly to a probability measure either.

**Conclusion.** For every countable family $\{f_k\}$ of continuous, bounded, rea
l-valued functions on $\mathbb{R}^d$ ($d \geq 1$), there exists a sequence of Bo
rel probability measures (namely $\mu_j = \delta_{x_{n_j}}$ with $\|x_{n_j}\| \t
o \infty$, subselected via the diagonal argument) such that all integrals conver
ge, yet the sequence fails to converge weakly to any probability measure. Theref
ore, no such countable family exists.

The essential obstruction is that $\mathbb{R}^d$ is not compact: mass can escape
 to infinity, and no countable family of bounded test functions can prevent this
 — because boundedness guarantees that a diagonal subsequence can always be foun
d along which all integrals converge, regardless of where the mass goes.
