## Proof

We prove the contrapositive. Suppose $\hat{f}(\gamma)$ does **not** change sign
infinitely often as $|\gamma|\to\infty$. Then there exists $A>0$ such that $\hat
{f}(\gamma)$ has a constant sign for all $|\gamma|\geq A$: either $\hat{f}(\gamm
a)\geq 0$ or $\hat{f}(\gamma)\leq 0$ for all $|\gamma|\geq A$.

Since $f$ is real and even, $\hat{f}$ is real and even, so "sign" is well-define
d.

### Case 1: $\hat{f}(\gamma)\geq 0$ for all $|\gamma|\geq A$.

**Fejér means.** For $T>0$, define the Fejér mean at the origin:
$$\sigma_T(0)=\int_{-T}^{T}\hat{f}(\gamma)\left(1-\frac{|\gamma|}{T}\right)d\gam
ma.$$
(Here $e^{2\pi i\gamma\cdot 0}=1$, and the integrand is real since $\hat{f}$ is
real.)

By the Fourier-integral analogue of Fejér's theorem, $\sigma_T(x)=(f*F_T)(x)$ wh
ere $F_T$ is the (non-negative) Fejér kernel, an approximate identity satisfying
 $\int F_T=1$, $F_T\geq 0$, and $F_T(x)\to 0$ uniformly off the origin. Since $f
(0+)$ and $f(0-)$ exist, the approximate identity converges to the average of th
e one-sided limits:

$$\sigma_T(0)\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{f(0+)+f(0-)}{2}\quad\text{as }T\to\infty.
\tag{1}$$

**Splitting the integral.** For $T>A$, write
$$\sigma_T(0)=\underbrace{\int_{-A}^{A}\hat{f}(\gamma)\Bigl(1-\frac{|\gamma|}{T}
\Bigr)\,d\gamma}_{I_T}\;+\;\underbrace{\int_{A\leq|\gamma|\leq T}\hat{f}(\gamma)
\Bigl(1-\frac{|\gamma|}{T}\Bigr)\,d\gamma}_{J_T}.$$

- **The inner part $I_T$.** Since $f\in L^1$, the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma gives $
\hat{f}\in C_0(\mathbb{R})$; in particular $\hat{f}$ is bounded on $[-A,A]$. By
dominated convergence,
$$I_T\;\longrightarrow\; C:=\int_{-A}^{A}\hat{f}(\gamma)\,d\gamma,\qquad |C|<\in
fty. \tag{2}$$

- **The tail $J_T$.** For $|\gamma|\geq A$ we have $\hat{f}(\gamma)\geq 0$, and
for $|\gamma|\leq T$ we have $(1-|\gamma|/T)\geq 0$, so $J_T\geq 0$. Define
$$h_T(\gamma)=\hat{f}(\gamma)\Bigl(1-\frac{|\gamma|}{T}\Bigr)^{\!+}\,\mathbf{1}_
{\{|\gamma|\geq A\}}.$$
For each fixed $\gamma$ with $|\gamma|\geq A$, the factor $(1-|\gamma|/T)^+$ inc
reases to $1$ as $T\to\infty$, and $\hat{f}(\gamma)\geq 0$, so $h_T(\gamma)\near
row \hat{f}(\gamma)\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|\gamma|\geq A\}}$ pointwise. By the **monoto
ne convergence theorem**,
$$J_T\;\nearrow\;\int_{|\gamma|\geq A}\hat{f}(\gamma)\,d\gamma\;\in\;[0,\infty].
 \tag{3}$$

**Combining (1), (2), (3):**
$$\frac{f(0+)+f(0-)}{2}=C+\int_{|\gamma|\geq A}\hat{f}(\gamma)\,d\gamma.$$
The left-hand side is finite, and $C$ is finite, so the tail integral must be fi
nite:
$$\int_{|\gamma|\geq A}\hat{f}(\gamma)\,d\gamma<\infty.$$
Together with the boundedness of $\hat{f}$ on $[-A,A]$, this gives $\hat{f}\in L
^1(\mathbb{R})$.

**Fourier inversion.** Since $\hat{f}\in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, the Fourier inversion
 theorem applies: the function
$$g(x):=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{f}(\gamma)\,e^{2\pi i\gamma x}\,d\gamma$$
is continuous (in fact uniformly continuous) and $g=f$ almost everywhere.

**Concluding $f(0+)=f(0-)$.** Since $f=g$ a.e. and $g$ is continuous: choose any
 sequence $x_n\downarrow 0$ with $f(x_n)=g(x_n)$ (possible since $\{f\neq g\}$ h
as measure zero, so such points are dense). Then
$$f(0+)\;\xleftarrow{\text{def}}\;\lim_{n\to\infty}f(x_n)=\lim_{n\to\infty}g(x_n
)=g(0),$$
by continuity of $g$. Similarly, choosing $x_n\uparrow 0$ with $f(x_n)=g(x_n)$ g
ives $f(0-)=g(0)$. Therefore
$$f(0+)=f(0-).$$

### Case 2: $\hat{f}(\gamma)\leq 0$ for all $|\gamma|\geq A$.

Apply Case 1 to the function $-f$, which is also real, even, and summable, with
$(-f)(0+)=-f(0+)$ and $(-f)(0-)=-f(0-)$ both existing, and $\widehat{(-f)}=-\hat
{f}\geq 0$ for $|\gamma|\geq A$. Case 1 yields $(-f)(0+)=(-f)(0-)$, hence $f(0+)
=f(0-)$.

### Conclusion

In both cases, if $\hat{f}(\gamma)$ is eventually of one sign (i.e., does not ch
ange sign infinitely often as $|\gamma|\to\infty$), then $f(0+)=f(0-)$. Taking t

$$\boxed{f(0+)\neq f(0-)\;\Longrightarrow\;\hat{f}(\gamma)\text{ changes sign in
finitely often as }|\gamma|\to\infty,}$$

which is equivalent to the stated disjunction: **either $f(0-)=f(0+)$, or $\hat{
