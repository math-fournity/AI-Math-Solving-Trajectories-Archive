**Proof.** Suppose, for a contradiction, that $R$ is not simple. Since $R$ is fi
nite, among all nonzero proper ideals there is one of minimal cardinality; call
it $I$. Then $I$ is a minimal nonzero ideal of $R$.

By hypothesis the subring generated by $I\cup\{1\}$ is $R$. Since $I$ is an idea
l (hence a subring) and $1$ is the identity, this subring is
$$I+\mathbb{Z}\cdot 1=\{i+n\cdot 1:i\in I,\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\}.$$
So
$$R=I+\mathbb{Z}\cdot 1. \tag{1}$$
Consequently $R/I$ is generated as a ring by $1+I$, hence is a quotient of $\mat
hbb{Z}$ and is commutative.

Since $I$ is minimal, $I^{2}\subseteq I$ is either $0$ or $I$.

**Case 1: $I^{2}=0$.** For $r\in R$ write $r=i_{0}+m\cdot 1$ by (1). For any $a\
in I$,
$$ra=(i_{0}+m\cdot 1)a=i_{0}a+ma=0+ma=ma,$$
$$ar=a(i_{0}+m\cdot 1)=ai_{0}+am=0+ma=ma,$$
since $i_{0}a,ai_{0}\in I^{2}=0$. Thus $ra=ar$ for all $r\in R$, $a\in I$, so $I
\subseteq Z(R)$. Now for $r=i_{0}+m\cdot 1$ and $s=j_{0}+n\cdot 1$,
$$rs=i_{0}j_{0}+n\cdot i_{0}+m\cdot j_{0}+mn,\qquad sr=j_{0}i_{0}+m\cdot j_{0}+n
\cdot i_{0}+mn,$$
and $i_{0}j_{0}=j_{0}i_{0}=0$ (in $I^{2}=0$) while the remaining terms coincide.
 Hence $rs=sr$ for all $r,s$, so $R$ is commutative — contradicting the hypothes
is that $R$ is noncommutative.

**Case 2: $I^{2}=I\neq 0$.** A minimal two-sided ideal $I$ with $I^{2}\neq 0$ is
 a simple ring with identity: there exists a central idempotent $e\in R$ with $I
=eR=Re$, $e$ is the identity of $I$, and $R=I\oplus J$ where $J=(1-e)R$ is a two
-sided ideal. (Indeed, since $I^{2}=I\neq 0$ one finds $e\in I$ with $e^{2}=e$ a
nd $Ie=eI=I$; centrality follows because for $r\in R$, $er,re\in I$ and $e$ is t
he identity of $I$, giving $er=ere=re$.) Since $I$ is proper, $J\neq 0$.

Apply the hypothesis to the nonzero ideal $J$: $J+\mathbb{Z}\cdot 1=R$. Writing
$1=e+(1-e)$ with $e\in I$, $(1-e)\in J$, we have $\mathbb{Z}\cdot 1=\mathbb{Z}\c
dot e+\mathbb{Z}\cdot(1-e)$ with $\mathbb{Z}\cdot(1-e)\subseteq J$, so $J+\mathb
b{Z}\cdot 1=J+\mathbb{Z}\cdot e$. For this to equal $R=I\oplus J$ we need $I\sub
seteq\mathbb{Z}\cdot e$; since $e\in I$ gives $\mathbb{Z}\cdot e\subseteq I$, we
 obtain $I=\mathbb{Z}\cdot e$. In particular $I$ is commutative.

Now apply the hypothesis to $I$ itself: $I+\mathbb{Z}\cdot 1=R$. With $I=\mathbb
{Z}\cdot e$,
$$I+\mathbb{Z}\cdot 1=\mathbb{Z}\cdot e+\mathbb{Z}\cdot 1=\mathbb{Z}\cdot e+\mat
hbb{Z}\cdot(1-e)=R=\mathbb{Z}\cdot e\oplus J,$$
forcing $J=\mathbb{Z}\cdot(1-e)$. Hence $J$ is commutative. But then $R=I\oplus
J$ with $I,J$ commutative and $I\cdot J=J\cdot I=0$ is itself commutative — agai
n contradicting $R$ being noncommutative.

Both cases yield a contradiction, so the assumption that $R$ is not simple is fa
lse. Therefore $R$ is simple. $\square$
