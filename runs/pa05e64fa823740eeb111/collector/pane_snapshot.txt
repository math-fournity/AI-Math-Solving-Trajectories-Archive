Looking at this problem, I need to determine the unit group of $\mathcal{O}_d$ (
the ring of integers of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$) for square-free $d > 0$. This is
 the real quadratic case of Dirichlet's unit theorem. I'll give a self-contained
 proof.

---

## Determination of $\mathcal{O}_d^{\times}$ for square-free $d > 0$

**Answer.** For every square-free integer $d > 0$,
$$\mathcal{O}_d^{\times} = \{\,\pm\,\varepsilon^{\,n} : n \in \mathbb{Z}\,\} \co
ng \{\pm 1\} \times \mathbb{Z},$$
where $\varepsilon > 1$ is the **fundamental unit** — the smallest unit of $\mat
hcal{O}_d$ greater than $1$.

---

### Proof

**Setup.** Let $d > 0$ be square-free and $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$. T
he ring of integers is
$$\mathcal{O}_d = \begin{cases} \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{d}\,] & \text{if } d \equiv 2,3
 \pmod{4},\\[4pt] \mathbb{Z}\!\left[\frac{1+\sqrt{d}}{2}\right] & \text{if } d \
equiv 1 \pmod{4}. \end{cases}$$
The discriminant is $\Delta = 4d$ (if $d \equiv 2,3 \pmod 4$) or $\Delta = d$ (i
f $d \equiv 1 \pmod 4$). In both cases $|\Delta| > 1$ since $d > 1$ when square-
free and positive (the case $d=1$ is excluded since $1$ is not square-free in th
e usual sense for this problem; $d=1$ gives $\mathbb{Q}$, not a quadratic extens
ion).

Let $\sigma_1, \sigma_2 : \mathbb{K} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be the two embe
ddings, with $\sigma_1(\sqrt{d}) = \sqrt{d}$ and $\sigma_2(\sqrt{d}) = -\sqrt{d}
$. The **norm** is $N(\alpha) = \sigma_1(\alpha)\,\sigma_2(\alpha) \in \mathbb{Z
}$ for $\alpha \in \mathcal{O}_d$.

---

**Step 1 — Norm criterion for units.** An element $\alpha \in \mathcal{O}_d$ is
a unit if and only if $|N(\alpha)| = 1$.

*Proof.* If $\alpha\beta = 1$ with $\beta \in \mathcal{O}_d$, then $N(\alpha)N(\
beta) = N(1) = 1$, so $N(\alpha) = \pm 1$. Conversely, if $N(\alpha) = \pm 1$, t
hen $\alpha \cdot \sigma_2(\alpha) = \pm 1$ (up to identifying $\sigma_2(\alpha)
$ with the conjugate $\bar{\alpha}$), so $\bar{\alpha}/N(\alpha) \in \mathcal{O}
_d$ is an inverse of $\alpha$. $\square$

---

**Step 2 — The lattice and Minkowski's theorem.** The map $\phi: \mathcal{O}_d \
to \mathbb{R}^2$, $\alpha \mapsto (\sigma_1(\alpha), \sigma_2(\alpha))$, embeds
$\mathcal{O}_d$ as a full-rank lattice $\Lambda \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ with covol
ume $\sqrt{|\Delta|}$.

*Verification.* For $d \equiv 2,3\pmod{4}$: $\Lambda$ is generated by $(1,1)$ an
d $(\sqrt{d},-\sqrt{d})$, covolume $= 2\sqrt{d} = \sqrt{4d}$. For $d \equiv 1\pm
od{4}$: $\Lambda$ is generated by $(1,1)$ and $\bigl(\frac{1+\sqrt{d}}{2}, \frac
{1-\sqrt{d}}{2}\bigr)$, covolume $= \sqrt{d}$. $\square$

By **Minkowski's convex body theorem**: any symmetric convex body $S \subset \ma
thbb{R}^2$ with $\operatorname{vol}(S) > 4\sqrt{|\Delta|}$ contains a nonzero po
int of $\Lambda$.

---

**Step 3 — Infinitely many elements with bounded norm.** Fix a constant $C_0 > \
sqrt{|\Delta|}$. For each integer $T \geq 1$, apply Minkowski to the box $[-T,\,
 T] \times [-C_0/T,\, C_0/T]$, which has volume $4C_0 > 4\sqrt{|\Delta|}$. We ob
tain a nonzero $\alpha_T \in \mathcal{O}_d$ with
$$|\sigma_1(\alpha_T)| \leq T, \qquad |\sigma_2(\alpha_T)| \leq \frac{C_0}{T}, \
qquad |N(\alpha_T)| \leq C_0.$$

**Claim.** The $\alpha_T$ are eventually distinct, so there are infinitely many
elements of $\mathcal{O}_d$ with $|N(\alpha)| \leq C_0$.

*Proof.* As $T \to \infty$, $|\sigma_2(\alpha_T)| \leq C_0/T \to 0$. If some fix
ed $\alpha$ appeared for infinitely many $T$, then $|\sigma_2(\alpha)| \leq C_0/
T$ for arbitrarily large $T$, forcing $\sigma_2(\alpha) = 0$. Since $\sigma_2$ i
s a field embedding, $\alpha = 0$, contradicting $\alpha_T \neq 0$. $\square$

---

**Step 4 — Existence of a nontrivial unit.** Since $|N(\alpha)|$ is a positive i
nteger bounded by $\lfloor C_0 \rfloor$, by the pigeonhole principle there exist
s a fixed $m$ with $1 \leq m \leq C_0$ such that infinitely many $\alpha \in \ma
thcal{O}_d$ satisfy $|N(\alpha)| = m$.

Now $\mathcal{O}_d$ is a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module of rank $2$, so it has only fi
nitely many subgroups (hence ideals) of any given index $m$. Since $|N(\alpha)|
= |N_{\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)| = [\mathcal{O}_d : (\alpha)]$ for the prin
cipal ideal $(\alpha)$, among the infinitely many $\alpha$ with $|N(\alpha)| = m
$, there exist $\alpha \neq \pm\,\beta$ with $(\alpha) = (\beta)$.

Then $u := \alpha/\beta \in \mathcal{O}_d^{\times}$ (since $(\alpha) = (\beta)$
means $\alpha = u\beta$ for a unit $u$), and $N(u) = N(\alpha)/N(\beta) = \pm 1$
. Moreover $u \neq \pm 1$ because $\alpha \neq \pm\,\beta$ (which we ensure by c
hoosing $\alpha, \beta$ with sufficiently different $\sigma_1$-values, possible
since $|\sigma_1(\alpha)| \to \infty$ along our sequence).

**Thus a unit $u \neq \pm 1$ exists.** $\square$

---

**Step 5 — The fundamental unit.** Replacing $u$ by $-u$, $u^{-1}$, or $-u^{-1}$
 as needed, we may assume $u > 1$ (i.e., $\sigma_1(u) > 1$). Define
$$\varepsilon := \min\{\, v \in \mathcal{O}_d^{\times} : v > 1 \,\}.$$

**This minimum exists:** the set $\{v \in \mathcal{O}_d^{\times} : v > 1\}$ is n
onempty (Step 4) and has no accumulation points in $\mathbb{R}$. Indeed, if $v_n
 \to v^*$ with each $v_n$ a unit $> 1$, then $\sigma_2(v_n) = \pm 1/v_n \to \pm
1/v^*$, so $v_n \to v^*$ in both embeddings. Then $v_n - v^* \to 0$ as algebraic
 integers, forcing $v_n = v^*$ for all large $n$. So the set is discrete and bou
nded below by $1$, hence has a minimum. $\square$

---

**Step 6 — Every unit $> 1$ is a power of $\varepsilon$.** Let $v > 1$ be a unit
. Choose $n \geq 0$ with $\varepsilon^n \leq v < \varepsilon^{n+1}$. Then $w :=
v\,\varepsilon^{-n}$ is a unit with $1 \leq w < \varepsilon$. By minimality of $
\varepsilon$, we must have $w = 1$, so $v = \varepsilon^n$. $\square$

---

**Step 7 — Full structure.** Let $v \in \mathcal{O}_d^{\times}$ be any unit.

- If $v > 0$: either $v \geq 1$ (so $v = \varepsilon^n$ by Step 6) or $0 < v < 1
$ (so $v^{-1} > 1$, hence $v^{-1} = \varepsilon^m$, giving $v = \varepsilon^{-m}
$).
- If $v < 0$: then $-v > 0$ is a unit, so $-v = \varepsilon^n$, giving $v = -\va
repsilon^n$.

**Torsion.** The only roots of unity in the real field $\mathbb{K} \subset \math
bb{R}$ are $\pm 1$. So the torsion subgroup of $\mathcal{O}_d^{\times}$ is exact
ly $\{\pm 1\}$.

**Conclusion.**
$$\boxed{\mathcal{O}_d^{\times} = \{\,\pm\,\varepsilon^{\,n} : n \in \mathbb{Z}\
,\} \cong \{\pm 1\} \times \mathbb{Z},}$$
where $\varepsilon > 1$ is the fundamental unit — the smallest unit greater than
 $1$.

---

**Remark (effective computation).** The fundamental unit $\varepsilon$ can be co
mputed via the continued fraction expansion of $\sqrt{d}$ (when $d \equiv 2,3 \p
mod{4}$) or of $(1+\sqrt{d})/2$ (when $d \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$). The convergents $p
_k/q_k$ yield solutions to the Pell-type equation:

- $d \equiv 2,3 \pmod{4}$: $p_k^2 - d\,q_k^2 = \pm 1$, and $\varepsilon = p_k +
q_k\sqrt{d}$ for the appropriate $k$.
- $d \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$: $p_k^2 - d\,q_k^2 = \pm 4$, and $\varepsilon = \frac{p_
k + q_k\sqrt{d}}{2}$.

The continued fraction of $\sqrt{d}$ is eventually periodic (Lagrange's theorem)
, guaranteeing that a solution to the Pell equation is found among the convergen
ts, confirming that $\varepsilon$ always exists and is effectively computable.
