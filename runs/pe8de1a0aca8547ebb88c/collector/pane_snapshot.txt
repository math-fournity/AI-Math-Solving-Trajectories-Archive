# Carleson's Theorem on Fourier Series Convergence

## Statement

**Carleson's theorem (1966).** Let $f \in L^2([0, 2\pi])$ (equivalently, $f \in
L^2(\mathbb{T})$). Then the symmetric partial sums of its Fourier series

$$S_N f(x) \;=\; \sum_{n=-N}^{N} \hat f(n)\, e^{inx}, \qquad \hat f(n)=\frac{1}{
2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(t)\,e^{-int}\,dt,$$

converge to $f(x)$ for **almost every** $x \in [0,2\pi]$:

$$\lim_{N\to\infty} S_N f(x) = f(x) \quad \text{a.e.}$$

Hunt (1968) extended this to every $f \in L^p(\mathbb{T})$ with $1 < p \le \inft
y$.

## Application to the functions in the question

### Continuous functions

Every continuous function $f \in C([0,2\pi])$ is bounded on a finite-measure int
erval, hence $f \in L^\infty \subset L^2$. Therefore Carleson's theorem applies
directly:

> The Fourier series of a continuous function converges to it **almost everywher
e**.

The "almost everywhere" qualifier is **sharp**: du Bois-Reymond (1876) construct
ed a continuous function whose Fourier series **diverges at a single specified p
oint**, and later Kahane–Katznelson showed that for *any* measure-zero set $E$ t
here exists a continuous function whose Fourier series diverges at every point o
f $E$. So pointwise convergence *everywhere* fails in general for continuous fun
ctions — Carleson's a.e. result is the best possible.

### Riemann integrable functions

A Riemann integrable function on $[0,2\pi]$ is, by definition, bounded and conti
nuous almost everywhere (Lebesgue's criterion). Being bounded on a finite-measur
e set, it belongs to $L^\infty([0,2\pi]) \subset L^2([0,2\pi])$. Hence Carleson'
s theorem again applies:

> The Fourier series of a Riemann integrable function converges to it **almost e
verywhere**.

At points of continuity of $f$, the limit equals $f(x)$; at a jump discontinuity
 $x_0$, the partial sums converge to the average $\tfrac{1}{2}(f(x_0^+)+f(x_0^-)
)$ wherever the a.e. limit exists — consistent with Carleson's statement that th
e limit equals $f(x)$ a.e. (since modifying $f$ on a null set, e.g. redefining i
t at jumps to be the midpoint, does not change its $L^2$ class).

## Why $L^2$ (rather than $L^1$) is the natural threshold

- For $f \in L^1$, the Fourier *coefficients* are well defined and tend to $0$ (
Riemann–Lebesgue), but pointwise a.e. convergence of the partial sums **fails**:
 Kolmogorov (1923) constructed an $L^1$ function whose Fourier series diverges *
*everywhere**.
- Carleson's result shows $L^2$ is exactly the right space: a.e. convergence hol
ds for all $L^2$ (and by Hunt, all $L^p$, $p>1$) functions, while it breaks down
 at $p=1$.

## Summary

Carleson's theorem states that **the Fourier series of any $L^2$ function conver
ges to the function almost everywhere**. Since both continuous functions and (pr
operly) Riemann integrable functions on a bounded interval are bounded and hence
 in $L^2$, their Fourier series converge to them almost everywhere. The result i
s optimal: convergence cannot be strengthened to "everywhere" even for continuou
s functions (du Bois-Reymond), and it cannot be extended to all $L^1$ functions
(Kolmogorov's everywhere-divergent example).
