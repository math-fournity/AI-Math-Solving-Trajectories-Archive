## Proof

**Given:** $A^T = A^2 - I$, with $A$ non-singular and (we want) non-symmetric.

**Step 1: Derive a polynomial relation for $A$.**

Taking the transpose of both sides:
$$A = (A^T)^T = (A^2 - I)^T = (A^T)^2 - I.$$

Substituting $A^T = A^2 - I$:
$$A = (A^2 - I)^2 - I = A^4 - 2A^2 + I - I = A^4 - 2A^2.$$

So $A^4 - 2A^2 - A = 0$, i.e., $A(A^3 - 2A - I) = 0$.

Since $A$ is **non-singular**, we can left-multiply by $A^{-1}$:
$$A^3 - 2A - I = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad A^3 = 2A + I. \tag{$\star$}$$

**Step 2: Express $A^3$ using $A^T$.**

From the original equation, $A^2 = A^T + I$. Then:
$$A^3 = A \cdot A^2 = A(A^T + I) = AA^T + A.$$

Combining with $(\star)$:
$$AA^T + A = 2A + I \quad \Longrightarrow \quad AA^T = A + I. \tag{$\dagger$}$$

**Step 3: Reach the contradiction.**

The product $AA^T$ is always symmetric, since $(AA^T)^T = (A^T)^T A^T = AA^T$.

By $(\dagger)$, $AA^T = A + I$, so $A + I$ must be symmetric:
$$(A + I)^T = A + I \quad \Longrightarrow \quad A^T + I = A + I \quad \Longright
arrow \quad A^T = A.$$

**Conclusion:** Every non-singular solution of $A^T = A^2 - I$ is necessarily sy
mmetric. Therefore, there are **no** non-symmetric, non-singular matrices satisf
ying the equation.
