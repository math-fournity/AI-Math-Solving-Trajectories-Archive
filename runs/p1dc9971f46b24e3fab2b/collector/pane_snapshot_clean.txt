# Classification of $r_2 \circ r_1$ in $\mathbb{H}^2$

## Setup and general observations

Let $l_1, l_2$ be geodesics (hyperbolic lines) in $\mathbb{H}^2$, and $r_1, r_2$
 the reflections across them. Each reflection is an **orientation-reversing** is
ometry and an involution ($r_i^2 = \mathrm{id}$). Therefore $r_2 \circ r_1$ is *
*orientation-preserving**, so it belongs to the orientation-preserving subgroup
of $\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^2)$, whose elements are classified as:

- **identity**,
- **elliptic** (one fixed point in $\mathbb{H}^2$ — a rotation),
- **parabolic** (one fixed point on $\partial\mathbb{H}^2$),
- **hyperbolic / loxodromic** (two fixed points on $\partial\mathbb{H}^2$ — a tr
anslation along an axis).

The classification of $r_2 \circ r_1$ is determined entirely by the relative pos
ition of $l_1$ and $l_2$.

**Key fixed-point lemma.** A point $x \in \mathbb{H}^2$ is fixed by $r_2 \circ r
_1$ iff
$$r_2(r_1(x)) = x \iff r_1(x) = r_2(x) \quad (\text{since } r_2^{-1}=r_2).$$
Writing $y := r_1(x) = r_2(x)$: $y$ is the reflection of $x$ across $l_1$, so $l
_1$ is the perpendicular bisector of segment $xy$; likewise $l_2$ is the perpend
icular bisector of $xy$. But the perpendicular bisector of a segment in $\mathbb
{H}^2$ is **unique**. Hence either $l_1 = l_2$, or $x = y$ (meaning $x$ lies on
both $l_1$ and $l_2$). In particular:

> *If $l_1 \neq l_2$ and $l_1, l_2$ do not meet in $\mathbb{H}^2$, then $r_2 \ci
rc r_1$ has **no** fixed point in $\mathbb{H}^2$.*

Then $r_1 = r_2$, so
$$r_2 \circ r_1 = \mathrm{id}.$$

## Case 2: $l_1$ and $l_2$ intersect in $\mathbb{H}^2$

Let $P = l_1 \cap l_2$ and let $\theta \in (0, \pi/2]$ be the angle between them
. Both reflections fix $P$, so $r_2 \circ r_1$ fixes $P$. Being orientation-pres
erving with a fixed point in $\mathbb{H}^2$, it is **elliptic** (a rotation abou
t $P$).

To find the angle, work in $T_P\mathbb{H}^2$. The differential $Dr_1$ is the Euc
lidean reflection of the tangent plane across the line $\dot l_1$ (the tangent d
irection of $l_1$), and $Dr_2$ reflects across $\dot l_2$, with $\dot l_1, \dot
l_2$ meeting at angle $\theta$. The composition of two linear reflections across
 lines through the origin at angle $\theta$ is a rotation by $2\theta$. Therefor
e

$$\boxed{r_2 \circ r_1 \text{ is a rotation about } P \text{ through angle } 2\t
heta.}$$

When $\theta = \pi/2$ (perpendicular lines) this is rotation by $\pi$, i.e. the
half-turn about $P$.

## Case 3: $l_1$ and $l_2$ are asymptotically parallel

The two geodesics share a unique ideal endpoint $\xi \in \partial\mathbb{H}^2$ a
nd do not meet in $\mathbb{H}^2$. Both reflections fix $\xi$ (as a boundary poin
t), so $r_2 \circ r_1$ fixes $\xi$.

By the fixed-point lemma, $r_2 \circ r_1$ has **no fixed point in $\mathbb{H}^2$
**. It remains to rule out a second boundary fixed point. Use the upper half-pla
ne model with $\xi = \infty$, so $l_1, l_2$ are vertical geodesics $x = a$ and $
x = b$ ($a \neq b$). Reflection across $x = c$ is $(x,y) \mapsto (2c - x,\, y)$.
 Thus
$$r_2 \circ r_1 : (x,y) \mapsto (2b - (2a - x),\, y) = (x + 2(b-a),\, y),$$
a horizontal translation $(x,y) \mapsto (x + 2(b-a), y)$. This is a **parabolic*
* isometry fixing only $\infty$ on $\partial\mathbb{H}^2$ (the other boundary po
ints are all moved along horocycles centered at $\infty$).

$$\boxed{r_2 \circ r_1 \text{ is parabolic, fixing the common ideal endpoint } \
xi.}$$

Concretely it is a **horolation** (limit rotation) about $\xi$ by twice the (sig
ned) horocyclic separation of $l_1, l_2$.

## Case 4: $l_1$ and $l_2$ are ultraparallel

The geodesics have a **unique common perpendicular** geodesic $g$, meeting $l_1$
 at $A$ and $l_2$ at $B$; let $d = d_{\mathbb{H}}(A,B) > 0$.

Because $g \perp l_i$, each reflection $r_i$ maps $g$ to itself (setwise), rever
sing the orientation of $g$. Restricted to $g \cong \mathbb{R}$, the reflection
$r_1$ acts as the point-reflection about $A$ (i.e. $t \mapsto 2a - t$ with $A$ a
t coordinate $a$), and $r_2$ as the point-reflection about $B$ (coordinate $b$,
$b - a = d$). Their composition is
$$t \mapsto 2b - (2a - t) = t + 2(b-a) = t + 2d,$$
a translation of $g$ by $2d$. The two ideal endpoints of $g$ on $\partial\mathbb
{H}^2$ are therefore fixed, and every other boundary point is moved. An orientat
ion-preserving isometry with exactly two boundary fixed points is **hyperbolic**
.

$$\boxed{r_2 \circ r_1 \text{ is a hyperbolic translation along } g \text{ by di
stance } 2d,}$$
where $d$ is the distance between $l_1$ and $l_2$ along their common perpendicul
ar, and the direction is from $A = g \cap l_1$ toward $B = g \cap l_2$.
