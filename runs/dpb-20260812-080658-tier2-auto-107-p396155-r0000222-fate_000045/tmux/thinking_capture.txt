Proof.

Let $p(x) = a(x) - b(x)$. Since $a(x)$ and $b(x)$ each have degree $< n$, their
difference $p(x)$ also has degree $< n$ (the leading terms may cancel, so the
degree can only decrease, never increase).

By hypothesis, there exist $n$ distinct values $c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_n$ such that
$$a(c_i) = b(c_i), \quad i = 1, 2, \ldots, n.$$ Therefore $$p(c_i) = a(c_i) -
b(c_i) = 0, \quad i = 1, 2, \ldots, n.$$

So $p(x)$ has at least $n$ distinct roots.

Now suppose, for contradiction, that $p(x) \neq 0$. Then $p(x)$ is a nonzero
polynomial of degree $d < n$, i.e., $d \leq n - 1$. A fundamental theorem of
algebra states that a nonzero polynomial of degree $d$ over a field has at most
$d$ roots. (This follows by induction: if $r$ is a root, then $(x - r)$ divides
$p(x)$ by the factor theorem, giving $p(x) = (x-r)q(x)$ with $\deg q = d - 1$;
any other root of $p$ must be a root of $q$, so the number of roots is at most
$1 + (d-1) = d$.)

But $p(x)$ has $n$ roots and $n > d$, contradicting the fact that a degree-$d$
polynomial has at most $d$ roots.

Therefore $p(x) = 0$, i.e., $a(x) - b(x) = 0$, which gives $$a(x) = b(x). \qquad
\blacksquare$$
