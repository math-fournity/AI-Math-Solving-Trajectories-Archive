# Normal Subgroups of the Symmetric Group $S(n)$

## Answer (classification by $n$)

| $n$ | Normal subgroups of $S(n)$ | Count |
|-----|---------------------------|-------|
| $1$ | $\{e\}$ | $1$ |
| $2$ | $\{e\},\ S_2$ | $2$ |
| $3$ | $\{e\},\ A_3,\ S_3$ | $3$ |
| $4$ | $\{e\},\ V_4,\ A_4,\ S_4$ | $4$ |
| $\ge 5$ | $\{e\},\ A_n,\ S_n$ | $3$ |

Here $V_4=\{e,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}\trianglelefteq S_4$ is the Klein four
-group.

---

## Proof

A key tool throughout: **every normal subgroup is a union of conjugacy classes**
 (since $N\trianglelefteq G$ iff $N$ is fixed by every inner automorphism), and
its order must divide $|G|$ by Lagrange.

### Preliminary: conjugacy classes of $S_n$

Conjugacy classes in $S_n$ are parametrized by cycle type. We record the class s
izes for the cases we need.

---

### Case $n=1$

$S_1=\{e\}$ is trivial. The only subgroup is $\{e\}$ itself.

**Count: 1.**

---

### Case $n=2$

$S_2\cong \mathbb{Z}_2$ is abelian, so *every* subgroup is normal. The subgroups
 are $\{e\}$ and $S_2$.

**Count: 2.**

---

### Case $n=3$

$|S_3|=6$. The conjugacy classes are:

- $C_1=\{e\}$, size $1$;
- $C_2=\{\text{transpositions}\}=\{(12),(13),(23)\}$, size $3$;
- $C_3=\{\text{3-cycles}\}=\{(123),(132)\}$, size $2$.

A normal subgroup $N$ must be a union of classes containing $C_1$, with $|N|\mid
 6$. The possible unions and their orders:

| Union | Order | Divides 6? |
|-------|-------|-----------|
| $C_1$ | $1$ | yes |
| $C_1\cup C_3$ | $3$ | yes |
| $C_1\cup C_2$ | $4$ | no |
| $C_1\cup C_2\cup C_3$ | $6$ | yes |

So the normal subgroups are $\{e\}$, $A_3=C_1\cup C_3$ (the unique index-$2$ sub
group), and $S_3$.

**Count: 3.**

---

### Case $n=4$

$|S_4|=24$. The conjugacy classes are:

- $C_1=\{e\}$, size $1$;
- $C_2=\{\text{transpositions}\}$, size $\binom{4}{2}=6$;
- $C_3=\{\text{3-cycles}\}$, size $\binom{4}{3}\cdot 2=8$;
- $C_4=\{\text{4-cycles}\}$, size $\binom{4}{4}\cdot 3!=6$;
- $C_5=\{\text{double transpositions }(ij)(kl)\}$, size $3$.

Check: $1+6+8+6+3=24$. ✓

A normal subgroup is a union of classes containing $C_1$ with order dividing $24
$. We enumerate all $2^4=16$ unions containing $C_1$ and keep those whose order
divides $24$:

| Union | Order | Divides 24? |
|-------|-------|------------|
| $C_1$ | $1$ | yes |
| $C_1\cup C_5$ | $4$ | yes |
| $C_1\cup C_3$ | $9$ | no |
| $C_1\cup C_2$ | $7$ | no |
| $C_1\cup C_4$ | $7$ | no |
| $C_1\cup C_5\cup C_3$ | $12$ | yes |
| $C_1\cup C_5\cup C_2$ | $10$ | no |
| $C_1\cup C_5\cup C_4$ | $10$ | no |
| $C_1\cup C_3\cup C_2$ | $15$ | no |
| $C_1\cup C_3\cup C_4$ | $15$ | no |
| $C_1\cup C_2\cup C_4$ | $13$ | no |
| $C_1\cup C_5\cup C_3\cup C_2$ | $18$ | no |
| $C_1\cup C_5\cup C_3\cup C_4$ | $18$ | no |
| $C_1\cup C_5\cup C_2\cup C_4$ | $16$ | no |
| $C_1\cup C_3\cup C_2\cup C_4$ | $21$ | no |
| all | $24$ | yes |

The four surviving unions give exactly:

1. $\{e\}$ (order $1$);
2. $V_4:=C_1\cup C_5=\{e,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}$ (order $4$) — the Klein f
our-group, normal since it is a union of classes;
3. $A_4=C_1\cup C_5\cup C_3$ (order $12$) — the alternating group, normal as the
 unique index-$2$ subgroup;
4. $S_4$ (order $24$).

Each is indeed a subgroup (the first three are well-known subgroups; one may ver
ify closure directly, or note $V_4\cong\mathbb{Z}_2\times\mathbb{Z}_2$ and $A_4$
 is the kernel of $\operatorname{sgn}$).

**Count: 4.**

---

### Case $n\ge 5$: simplicity of $A_n$ forces only three normal subgroups

We use the classical theorem:

> **Theorem (Galois–Jordan).** *For $n\ge 5$, the alternating group $A_n$ is sim
ple.*

*(Proof sketch for completeness.)* Let $n\ge 5$ and let $H\trianglelefteq A_n$ b
e a nontrivial normal subgroup. Every nontrivial normal subgroup of $A_n$ contai
ns a $3$-cycle: one shows that $H$ must contain some element with a cycle of len
gth $\ge 3$ in its decomposition, and by taking suitable commutators with $3$-cy
cles (which exist because $n\ge 5$ gives enough room to conjugate) one extracts
a $3$-cycle into $H$. Since all $3$-cycles are conjugate in $A_n$ for $n\ge 5$ (
two $3$-cycles are conjugate in $S_n$ by a permutation $\sigma$; if $\sigma$ is
odd, conjugating by a transposition disjoint from the support — possible since $
n\ge 5$ — gives an even conjugator), $H$ contains *all* $3$-cycles. The $3$-cycl
es generate $A_n$, so $H=A_n$. ∎

Now let $N\trianglelefteq S_n$ with $n\ge 5$.

**Step 1.** $N\cap A_n\trianglelefteq A_n$ (since $N$ is normal in $S_n$, hence
in $A_n$, and $A_n$ is normal too; the intersection of two normal subgroups is n
ormal). By simplicity of $A_n$:

$$N\cap A_n \in \bigl\{\{e\},\ A_n\bigr\}.$$

**Step 2.** *If $N\cap A_n=A_n$:* then $A_n\le N$, so $N$ corresponds to a subgr
oup of $S_n/A_n\cong\mathbb{Z}_2$. Hence $N=A_n$ or $N=S_n$.

**Step 3.** *If $N\cap A_n=\{e\}$:* then the composition $N\hookrightarrow S_n\t
woheadrightarrow S_n/A_n\cong\mathbb{Z}_2$ is injective, so $|N|\le 2$.

- If $|N|=1$: $N=\{e\}$.
- If $|N|=2$: $N=\{e,\sigma\}$ with $\sigma$ an odd involution. Normality of $N$
 in $S_n$ means every conjugate of $\sigma$ lies in $N$, i.e. $\sigma$ is fixed
by every inner automorphism — equivalently $\sigma\in Z(S_n)$. But for $n\ge 3$,
 $Z(S_n)=\{e\}$ (any non-identity permutation moves some $i$; the transposition
$(ij)$ with $j\ne i$ and $j$ not moved by $\sigma$ — or a suitable transposition
 — does not commute with $\sigma$). Since $n\ge 5\ge 3$, we get $\sigma=e$, cont
radicting $|N|=2$.

So the only possibility is $N=\{e\}$.

**Conclusion for $n\ge 5$:** the normal subgroups are exactly $\{e\},\ A_n,\ S_n
$.

**Count: 3.**

---

## Summary

$$\#\{\text{normal subgroups of }S(n)\}=\begin{cases}1 & n=1,\\[2pt] 2 & n=2,\\[
2pt] 3 & n=3,\\[2pt] 4 & n=4,\\[2pt] 3 & n\ge 5.\end{cases}$$

The normal subgroups are:
- $n=1$: $\{e\}$;
- $n=2$: $\{e\},\ S_2$;
- $n=3$: $\{e\},\ A_3,\ S_3$;
- $n=4$: $\{e\},\ V_4,\ A_4,\ S_4$;
- $n\ge 5$: $\{e\},\ A_n,\ S_n$.
