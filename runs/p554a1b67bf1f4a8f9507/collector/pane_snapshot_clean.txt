## Difference between weak sequential lower semicontinuity and weak sequential c
ontinuity of the norm

**Definitions.** Let $H$ be a Hilbert space with norm $\|\cdot\|$. Recall $x_n \
rightharpoonup x$ means $x_n$ converges to $x$ *weakly*, i.e. $\langle x_n, y\ra
ngle \to \langle x, y\rangle$ for every $y \in H$.

- The norm is **weakly sequentially lower semicontinuous** (w.s.l.s.c.) if
$$x_n \rightharpoonup x \implies \|x\| \le \liminf_{n\to\infty} \|x_n\|.$$

- The norm is **weakly sequentially continuous** if
$$x_n \rightharpoonup x \implies \|x_n\| \to \|x\|, \quad\text{i.e.}\quad \lim \
|x_n\| = \|x\|.$$

**The key difference.** Lower semicontinuity is a one-sided inequality: it only
forbids the norm from *jumping up* in the weak limit — the limit norm may be str
ictly *smaller* than the limiting norms of the sequence. Continuity is the two-s
ided equality: the norm of the weak limit must equal the limit of the norms. Cle
arly weak sequential continuity $\Rightarrow$ weak sequential lower semicontinui
ty, but not conversely.

**The norm is always w.s.l.s.c.** For any $x \in H$,
$$\|x\| = \sup_{\|y\|\le 1} |\langle x, y\rangle|.$$
For each fixed $y$ with $\|y\|\le 1$, the map $x \mapsto |\langle x, y\rangle|$
is weakly continuous (weak convergence is defined exactly by testing against fix
ed vectors). A supremum of continuous functions is lower semicontinuous, so $x \
mapsto \|x\|$ is weakly sequentially lower semicontinuous. Hence whenever $x_n \
rightharpoonup x$,
$$\|x\| = \sup_{\|y\|\le 1}|\langle x,y\rangle| = \sup_{\|y\|\le 1}\lim_n|\langl
e x_n,y\rangle| \le \liminf_n \sup_{\|y\|\le 1}|\langle x_n,y\rangle| = \liminf_
n \|x_n\|.$$

**The norm is NOT weakly sequential continuous in general.** Take $H = \ell^2(\m
athbb{N})$ and let $e_n$ be the $n$-th standard basis vector ($1$ in position $n
$, $0$ elsewhere).

*Weak convergence $e_n \rightharpoonup 0$.* For any $y = (y_k) \in \ell^2$,
$$\langle e_n, y\rangle = y_n \to 0 \quad\text{as } n\to\infty,$$
because $\sum_k |y_k|^2 < \infty$ forces $y_n \to 0$. Thus $e_n \rightharpoonup
0$ weakly.

*Norms do not converge to the norm of the limit.* We have $\|e_n\| = 1$ for ever
y $n$, yet $\|0\| = 0$. Therefore
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\|e_n\| = 1 \ne 0 = \|0\|,$$
so $\|e_n\| \not\to \|0\|$. The norm is not weakly sequentially continuous.

*Consistency with lower semicontinuity.* The inequality still holds:
$$\|0\| = 0 \le 1 = \liminf_n \|e_n\|,$$
exactly as w.s.l.s.c. guarantees. The "mass" of the sequence escapes to infinity
 along the moving coordinate $n$; weak convergence cannot see it, but the norm r
ecords it. This is the canonical mechanism (concentration/escape to infinity) by
 which a norm fails weak sequential continuity while preserving lower semicontin
uity.

**Summary.** Weak sequential lower semicontinuity of the norm ($\|x\|\le\liminf\
|x_n\|$) holds in every Hilbert space and is a consequence of the norm being a s
upremum of weakly continuous linear functionals. Weak sequential continuity ($\|
x_n\|\to\|x\|$) is strictly stronger and fails whenever a weakly convergent sequ
ence loses norm by "spreading out" — the standard orthonormal basis $(e_n)$ in $
\ell^2$ weakly converges to $0$ while keeping norm $1$, giving a concrete counte
rexample.
