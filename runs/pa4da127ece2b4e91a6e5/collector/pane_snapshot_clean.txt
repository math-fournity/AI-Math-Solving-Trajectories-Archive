## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, such an $a \in I$ exists.

**Key observation.** For any $a \in I$, the set $aM = \{am : m \in M\}$ is a sub
module of $M$ (closed under addition since $am_1+am_2 = a(m_1+m_2)$, and closed
under scalar multiplication since $r\cdot(am) = (ra)m = a(rm) \in aM$). Moreover
 $RaM = aM$ because $(ra)m = a(rm) \in aM$ (taking $r=1$ gives the reverse inclu
sion). Therefore

$$\langle a, J\rangle M = (J + Ra)M = JM + RaM = JM + aM.$$

Hence $JM \subsetneq \langle a, J\rangle M$ if and only if $aM \not\subseteq JM$
.

**Reduction.** Since $J \subseteq I$, we have $JM \subseteq IM$. We also have

$$IM = \sum_{a \in I} aM,$$

because $IM$ is generated by all elements $am$ with $a \in I, m \in M$, and each
 $aM$ is a submodule.

**Suppose, for contradiction,** that $aM \subseteq JM$ for every $a \in I$. Then

$$IM = \sum_{a \in I} aM \subseteq JM.$$

Combined with $JM \subseteq IM$, this gives $IM = JM$.

But $JM$ is finitely generated by hypothesis, so $IM = JM$ would make $IM$ finit
ely generated—contradicting the hypothesis that $IM$ is **not** finitely generat
ed.

**Conclusion.** The assumption was false: there exists $a \in I$ with $aM \not\s
ubseteq JM$. For this $a$,

$$JM \subsetneq JM + aM = \langle a, J\rangle M. \qquad \blacksquare$$
