## Proof

We evaluate
$$I = \int_0^{\infty} \frac{\sqrt{x}}{(x+1)^2}\,dx$$
using the residue theorem, with the branch of $\sqrt{z}$ whose cut lies along th
e positive real axis, i.e. we take $0 \le \arg z < 2\pi$ so that
$$\sqrt{z} = |z|^{1/2}\,e^{i\arg z/2}, \qquad 0 \le \arg z < 2\pi.$$

### Contour setup

Consider
$$f(z) = \frac{\sqrt{z}}{(z+1)^2}$$
and the **keyhole contour** $\Gamma$ avoiding the positive real axis: a large ci
rcle $C_R$ of radius $R$, a small circle $C_\varepsilon$ of radius $\varepsilon$
 about the origin, and two horizontal segments $L_+$ (just above the positive re
al axis, traversed $\varepsilon\to R$) and $L_-$ (just below, traversed $R\to\va
repsilon$).

The only singularity of $f$ inside $\Gamma$ is the double pole at $z=-1$ (which
lies off the positive real axis, hence inside the contour).

### Boundary values on the cut

- **Upper edge** $L_+$ ($\arg z = 0^+$): $\sqrt{z}=\sqrt{x}$, so
$$\int_{L_+} f(z)\,dz = \int_\varepsilon^R \frac{\sqrt{x}}{(x+1)^2}\,dx.$$

- **Lower edge** $L_-$ ($\arg z = 2\pi^-$): $\sqrt{z}=\sqrt{x}\,e^{i\pi}=-\sqrt{
x}$, traversed from $R$ to $\varepsilon$, so
$$\int_{L_-} f(z)\,dz = \int_R^\varepsilon \frac{-\sqrt{x}}{(x+1)^2}\,dx = \int_
\varepsilon^R \frac{\sqrt{x}}{(x+1)^2}\,dx.$$

Adding the two edges:
$$\int_{L_+\cup L_-} f(z)\,dz = 2\int_\varepsilon^R \frac{\sqrt{x}}{(x+1)^2}\,dx
.$$

### Vanishing of the circular arcs

- **Large circle** $C_R$: $|f(z)|\sim R^{1/2}/R^2 = R^{-3/2}$, length $2\pi R$,
so $\left|\int_{C_R}\right|\lesssim R^{-1/2}\to 0$ as $R\to\infty$.
- **Small circle** $C_\varepsilon$: $|f(z)|\sim \varepsilon^{1/2}$, length $2\pi
\varepsilon$, so $\left|\int_{C_\varepsilon}\right|\lesssim \varepsilon^{3/2}\to
 0$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$.

### Residue at $z=-1$

The point $z=-1$ is a double pole. With $(z+1)^2 f(z)=\sqrt{z}$,
$$\operatorname{Res}(f,\,-1)=\lim_{z\to -1}\frac{d}{dz}\sqrt{z}=\lim_{z\to -1}\f
rac{1}{2\sqrt{z}}.$$
Since $-1=e^{i\pi}$ in our branch, $\sqrt{-1}=e^{i\pi/2}=i$, hence
$$\operatorname{Res}(f,\,-1)=\frac{1}{2i}.$$

### Applying the residue theorem

$$\oint_\Gamma f(z)\,dz = 2\pi i\cdot\operatorname{Res}(f,\,-1)=2\pi i\cdot\frac
{1}{2i}=\pi.$$

Taking $R\to\infty$ and $\varepsilon\to 0$, the circular integrals vanish and on
ly the two edges survive:
$$2\int_0^{\infty}\frac{\sqrt{x}}{(x+1)^2}\,dx = \pi.$$

Therefore
$$\boxed{\int_0^{\infty}\frac{\sqrt{x}}{(x+1)^2}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{2}.}$$
