## Proof

Let $p$ be a prime written as $p = 5a + b$ with $a \geq 0$ and $0 \leq b \leq 5$
.

**Establishing the cases.** If $b = 0$, then $p = 5a$; primality forces $a = 1$,
 so $p = 5$. If $b = 5$, then $p = 5(a+1)$, again divisible by 5, forcing $p = 5
$ (i.e., $a = 0, b = 5$, but this is the same as $a=1, b=0$). Thus for any prime
 $p \neq 5$, we must have $1 \leq b \leq 4$, confirming the problem's statement.

**Case $b = 0$ (i.e., $p = 5$).** We compute directly:
$$4p^2 + 1 = 4 \cdot 25 + 1 = 101, \qquad 6p^2 + 1 = 6 \cdot 25 + 1 = 151.$$
Both are prime (101 is not divisible by any prime $\leq \sqrt{101} \approx 10$,
namely 2, 3, 5, 7; 151 is not divisible by any prime $\leq \sqrt{151} \approx 12
$, namely 2, 3, 5, 7, 11). So both expressions are prime when $p = 5$.

**Analysis modulo 5 for $p \neq 5$.** Now suppose $p \neq 5$, so $b \in \{1, 2,
3, 4\}$. We work modulo 5.

**Expression $4p^2 + 1 \pmod{5}$:**

| $b$ | $p^2 \bmod 5$ | $4p^2+1 \bmod 5$ |
|-----|---------------|-------------------|
| 1   | 1             | $4 \cdot 1 + 1 = 5 \equiv 0$ |
| 2   | 4             | $4 \cdot 4 + 1 = 17 \equiv 2$ |
| 3   | 4             | $4 \cdot 4 + 1 = 17 \equiv 2$ |
| 4   | 1             | $4 \cdot 1 + 1 = 5 \equiv 0$ |

When $b \in \{1, 4\}$: $5 \mid 4p^2 + 1$. Since $p \geq 2$, we have $4p^2 + 1 \g
eq 17 > 5$, so $4p^2 + 1$ is **composite**.

**Expression $6p^2 + 1 \pmod{5}$:**

| $b$ | $p^2 \bmod 5$ | $6p^2+1 \bmod 5$ |
|-----|---------------|-------------------|
| 1   | 1             | $6 \cdot 1 + 1 = 7 \equiv 2$ |
| 2   | 4             | $6 \cdot 4 + 1 = 25 \equiv 0$ |
| 3   | 4             | $6 \cdot 4 + 1 = 25 \equiv 0$ |
| 4   | 1             | $6 \cdot 1 + 1 = 7 \equiv 2$ |

When $b \in \{2, 3\}$: $5 \mid 6p^2 + 1$. Since $p \geq 2$, we have $6p^2 + 1 \g
eq 25 > 5$, so $6p^2 + 1$ is **composite**.

**Combining the cases.** For every prime $p \neq 5$, the remainder $b$ falls int
o exactly one of $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$:

- If $b \in \{1, 4\}$: $4p^2 + 1$ is divisible by 5 and exceeds 5, hence composi
te.
- If $b \in \{2, 3\}$: $6p^2 + 1$ is divisible by 5 and exceeds 5, hence composi
te.

In every case, **at least one** of the two expressions is composite. Therefore,
it is impossible for both $4p^2 + 1$ and $6p^2 + 1$ to be prime simultaneously w
hen $p \neq 5$.

**Conclusion.** The only value of $p$ for which both $4p^2 + 1$ and $6p^2 + 1$ c
an be prime numbers is $\boxed{p = 5}$, yielding the primes $101$ and $151$.
