## Solution

**The answer is NO.** We cannot conclude that $Y$ is complemented in $X$.

The key tool is the **Bartle–Graves theorem** (1952):

> **Theorem (Bartle–Graves).** Let $T: E \to F$ be a surjective bounded linear m
ap between Banach spaces. Then there exists a continuous (nonlinear) map $s: F \
to E$ such that $T \circ s = \mathrm{id}_F$ and $s(0)=0$.

---

### Counterexample

Let $X = \ell^\infty$ and $Y = c_0$. Then $Y$ is a closed subspace of $X$. By **
Phillips' theorem** (1940), $c_0$ is **not** complemented in $\ell^\infty$: ther
e is no bounded linear projection from $\ell^\infty$ onto $c_0$.

Apply the Bartle–Graves theorem to the quotient map $\pi: \ell^\infty \to \ell^\
infty/c_0$, obtaining a continuous map $s: \ell^\infty/c_0 \to \ell^\infty$ with
 $\pi \circ s = \mathrm{id}$ and $s(0)=0$.

Set $Z := s(\ell^\infty/c_0) \subset \ell^\infty$.

**Claim.** $Z$ is a closed subset of $\ell^\infty$ satisfying $Z \cap c_0 = \{0\
}$ and every $x \in \ell^\infty$ has a unique decomposition $x = y + z$ with $y
\in c_0$, $z \in Z$.

**Proof of claim.**

*($Z \cap c_0 = \{0\}$)* If $z \in Z \cap c_0$, then $z = s(q)$ for some $q \in
\ell^\infty/c_0$, and $\pi(z) = 0$ (since $z \in c_0 = \ker\pi$). But $\pi(z) =
\pi(s(q)) = q$, so $q = 0$, hence $z = s(0) = 0$.

*(Unique decomposition)* For $x \in \ell^\infty$, let $q = \pi(x)$ and $z = s(q)
 \in Z$. Set $y = x - z$. Then $\pi(y) = \pi(x) - \pi(z) = q - q = 0$, so $y \in
 c_0$, and $x = y + z$. For uniqueness: if $x = y_1 + z_1 = y_2 + z_2$ with $y_i
 \in c_0$, $z_i \in Z$, then $\pi(z_1) = \pi(x) = \pi(z_2)$. Since $\pi|_Z$ is i
njective (because $Z \cap c_0 = \{0\}$), we get $z_1 = z_2$ and hence $y_1 = y_2
$.

*($Z$ is closed)* Let $(z_n)$ be a sequence in $Z$ with $z_n \to x$ in $\ell^\in
fty$. Write $z_n = s(q_n)$ where $q_n = \pi(z_n)$. By continuity of $\pi$, we ha
ve $q_n = \pi(z_n) \to \pi(x) =: q$. By continuity of $s$, we have $s(q_n) \to s
(q)$. But $s(q_n) = z_n \to x$, so $x = s(q) \in Z$. $\square$

### Conclusion

The closed subset $Z$ satisfies all the hypotheses of the problem ($Z$ closed, $
Z \cap Y = \{0\}$, unique decomposition $X = Y + Z$), yet $Y = c_0$ is **not** c
omplemented in $X = \ell^\infty$. Therefore, the given conditions do **not** suf
fice to conclude that $Y$ is complemented in $X$.

The essential point is that the hypotheses only guarantee a **nonlinear** projec
tion $P: X \to Y$ (defined by $P(x) = y$ where $x = y+z$), which has a closed gr
aph but is not linear. The closed graph theorem cannot be applied without linear
ity, and the existence of such a nonlinear "complement" (which holds for *every*
 closed subspace by Bartle–Graves) is strictly weaker than the existence of a li
near bounded projection.
