Looking at this problem, I need to prove that the arc length $L(x)$ is asymptoti

**Remark on hypotheses.** The conclusion requires $f$ to be $C^1$ on $[a,b]$ (or
 at least $f'$ continuous at $a$). Under mere rectifiability ($L(b)<\infty$) the
 statement is false: e.g., $f(t)=t^2\sin(1/t)$, $f(0)=0$, is Lipschitz (hence re
ctifiable) and differentiable at $0$, yet $L(x)/d(x)\to \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\pi\
!\sqrt{1+\cos^2\theta}\,d\theta > 1$ due to oscillation of $f'$ near $0$. The na
tural setting is $f\in C^1([a,b])$, which I now assume.

---

**Proof.** Assume $f \in C^1([a,b])$.

**Arc length integral formula.** Since $f$ is $C^1$, the function $g(t)=\sqrt{1+
f'(t)^2}$ is continuous on $[a,b]$. For any partition $P=\{a=t_0<\cdots<t_n=x\}$
, the Mean Value Theorem gives $\xi_i\in(t_{i-1},t_i)$ with $f(t_i)-f(t_{i-1})=f
'(\xi_i)\,\Delta t_i$, so

$$l(f,P)=\sum_{i=1}^n \sqrt{1+f'(\xi_i)^2}\,\Delta t_i.$$

This is a Riemann sum for $g$. Since $g$ is uniformly continuous on $[a,b]$, for
 every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that $\|P\|<\delta$ implies $|l(
f,P)-\int_a^x g(t)\,dt|\le \varepsilon(x-a)$. Refining a partition can only incr
ease $l(f,P)$ (triangle inequality), so the supremum over all partitions equals
the limit over refinements:

$$L(x)=\int_a^x \sqrt{1+f'(t)^2}\,dt. \tag{1}$$

**Step 1 — Lower bound: $L(x)/d(x)\ge 1$.** For any partition $P$ of $[a,x]$, th
e polygonal path from $(a,f(a))$ through the partition points to $(x,f(x))$ has
length $l(f,P)$, which by the triangle inequality in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is at least
the straight-line distance $d(x)=\sqrt{(x-a)^2+(f(x)-f(a))^2}$. Taking the supre
mum:

$$L(x)\ge d(x)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{L(x)}{d(x)}\ge 1. \tag{2}$$

(Note $d(x)\ge x-a>0$ for $x>a$, so the ratio is well-defined.)

**Step 2 — Upper bound: $\limsup_{x\to a^+} L(x)/d(x)\le 1$.** By the Mean Value
 Theorem, for each $x>a$ there exists $c_x\in(a,x)$ with

$$f(x)-f(a)=f'(c_x)(x-a),$$

so

$$d(x)=(x-a)\sqrt{1+f'(c_x)^2}. \tag{3}$$

Using (1),

$$\frac{L(x)}{x-a}=\frac{1}{x-a}\int_a^x g(t)\,dt.$$

Since $g$ is continuous at $a$, by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (or the m
ean value theorem for integrals),

$$\lim_{x\to a^+}\frac{1}{x-a}\int_a^x g(t)\,dt = g(a)=\sqrt{1+f'(a)^2}. \tag{4}
$$

As $x\to a^+$, we have $c_x\to a^+$, and by continuity of $f'$,

$$\sqrt{1+f'(c_x)^2}\to \sqrt{1+f'(a)^2}. \tag{5}$$

Combining (1), (3), (4), (5):

$$\frac{L(x)}{d(x)}=\frac{\frac{1}{x-a}\int_a^x g(t)\,dt}{\sqrt{1+f'(c_x)^2}}\;\
longrightarrow\;\frac{\sqrt{1+f'(a)^2}}{\sqrt{1+f'(a)^2}}=1. \tag{6}$$

**Conclusion.** From (2) we have $\liminf_{x\to a^+} L(x)/d(x)\ge 1$, and from (
6) we have $\limsup_{x\to a^+} L(x)/d(x)\le 1$. Therefore

$$\lim_{x\to a^+}\frac{L(x)}{d(x)}=1. \qquad\blacksquare$$
