## Solution

We want $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y^2)$ for
$$\begin{pmatrix} X\\ Y \end{pmatrix}\sim N_{2}\!\left(\begin{pmatrix} 0\\ 0 \en
d{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho\\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}\right), \quad -1<\r
ho<1.$$

**Step 1: Reduce the covariance.** Since $E[X]=0$,
$$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y^2) = E[XY^2] - E[X]\,E[Y^2] = E[XY^2].$$

**Step 2: Decompose $X$ into a part driven by $Y$ and an independent part.** For
 a bivariate normal with unit variances and correlation $\rho$, we may write
$$X = \rho\, Y + \sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\, Z,$$
where $Z \sim N(0,1)$ is independent of $Y$. (This is the standard regression re
presentation; the coefficient $\rho$ gives $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\rho\cdot\op
eratorname{Var}(Y)=\rho$, and $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\rho^{2}+(1-\rho^{2})=1$, w
ith $\operatorname{Cov}(Z,Y)=0$ hence independence by joint normality.)

**Step 3: Compute $E[XY^2]$.** Substituting,
$$E[XY^2] = E\!\left[\bigl(\rho Y + \sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\, Z\bigr)Y^{2}\right] = \r
ho\, E[Y^{3}] + \sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\, E[Z]\,E[Y^{2}].$$

- $Y\sim N(0,1)$ is symmetric, so $E[Y^{3}]=0$ (all odd moments of a centered no
rmal vanish).
- $Z\sim N(0,1)$ is independent of $Y$, so $E[Z]\,E[Y^{2}] = 0\cdot 1 = 0$.

Therefore
$$E[XY^{2}] = \rho\cdot 0 + \sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\cdot 0 = 0.$$

**Step 4: Conclusion.**
$$\boxed{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y^{2}) = 0.}$$

**Remark on the intuition.** The note that "$Y^{2}$ is not chi-square distribute
d due to its dependence on $X$" is a red herring for this particular quantity: t
he dependence of $Y^{2}$ on $X$ is captured entirely through the shared componen
t $\rho Y$ in $X$. That shared component contributes $\rho\, E[Y^{3}]$, which va
nishes by the symmetry of the centered normal. The residual component $\sqrt{1-\
rho^{2}}\,Z$ is independent of $Y$ (hence of $Y^{2}$) and contributes nothing. T
hus the covariance is zero for every $\rho\in(-1,1)$, even though $X$ and $Y^{2}
$ are generally **not** independent (e.g. $E[X^{2}Y^{2}]\neq E[X^{2}]\,E[Y^{2}]$
 unless $\rho=0$). Zero covariance here reflects the odd-symmetry cancellation,
not independence.
