# Proof

Let $X$ be a Banach space and $T \in \mathcal{B}(X)$ a bounded linear operator.
Recall that the **spectrum** of $T$ is

$$\sigma(T) = \{\lambda \in \mathbb{C} : \lambda I - T \text{ is not invertible
in } \mathcal{B}(X)\},$$

where "invertible in $\mathcal{B}(X)$" means $\lambda I - T$ is a bijection of $
X$ onto $X$ with bounded inverse (the boundedness of the inverse is automatic by
 the Bounded Inverse Theorem when $\lambda I - T$ is bijective).

**Claim.** Yes, $\lambda$ is necessarily in $\sigma(T)$.

**Justification.** Suppose, for some $\lambda$, that the range $\mathcal{R}(\lam
bda I - T)$ is **not dense** in $X$. Since $\mathcal{R}(\lambda I - T) \subseteq
 X$ and it is not dense, we have in particular

$$\overline{\mathcal{R}(\lambda I - T)} \subsetneq X,$$

which immediately implies

$$\mathcal{R}(\lambda I - T) \subsetneq X.$$

That is, $\lambda I - T$ is **not surjective**. An operator that is not surjecti
ve cannot be a bijection of $X$ onto $X$, hence cannot be invertible in $\mathca
l{B}(X)$. By the definition of the spectrum, it follows that

$$\lambda \in \sigma(T).$$

**Remark (finer structure).** In fact, the set of $\lambda$ for which $\mathcal{
R}(\lambda I - T)$ is not dense is precisely the union of the **point spectrum**
 and the **residual spectrum**:

- If $\lambda I - T$ is not injective, then $\lambda$ belongs to the point spect
rum $\sigma_p(T)$.
- If $\lambda I - T$ is injective but its range is not dense, then $\lambda$ bel
ongs to the residual spectrum $\sigma_r(T)$.

Both are subsets of $\sigma(T)$. The remaining part of the spectrum, the **conti
nuous spectrum** $\sigma_c(T)$, consists of those $\lambda$ for which $\lambda I
 - T$ is injective with dense but non-closed (hence proper) range — there the ra
nge *is* dense. Thus the non-density of the range is a strictly stronger conditi
on than mere membership in the spectrum, and it always forces $\lambda \in \sigm
a(T)$.
