Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether, for any four points on a s
phere, there exists a set of at most four points where the product of distances
to the four given points is equal across all elements of the set.

**Answer: Yes, such a set always exists.**

**Proof.**

Let $S$ be the sphere, and define the continuous function
$$f(y) = \prod_{i=1}^{4} |y - x_i|, \quad y \in S,$$
where $|\cdot|$ denotes Euclidean distance in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

**Step 1: $f$ takes the value $0$ and a positive maximum on $S$.**

Since each $x_i \in S$, we have $f(x_i) = 0$ for every $i$. The function $f$ is
continuous (a product of continuous functions) and $S$ is compact, so $f$ attain
s its maximum $M$ on $S$. We claim $M > 0$: since $S$ is uncountable and there a
re only four points $x_i$, there exists $y \in S$ with $y \neq x_i$ for all $i$,
 giving $f(y) > 0$, hence $M > 0$.

**Step 2: Constructing the great circle.**

Let $y^* \in S$ be a point where $f(y^*) = M$. Since $f(x_1) = 0 < M = f(y^*)$,
we have $y^* \neq x_1$. There exists a great circle $C$ on $S$ passing through b
oth $x_1$ and $y^*$ (any two distinct non-antipodal points determine a unique gr
eat circle; if they are antipodal, every great circle through $x_1$ also passes
through $y^*$).

Parametrize $C$ as $\gamma: [0, 2\pi] \to S$ with $\gamma(0) = \gamma(2\pi) = x_
1$, and let $g = f \circ \gamma$. Then $g$ is continuous on $[0, 2\pi]$ with $g(
0) = g(2\pi) = 0$, and there exists $t^* \in (0, 2\pi)$ with $g(t^*) = M$.

**Step 3: Applying the Intermediate Value Theorem.**

Choose any $c$ with $0 < c < M$. Since $g(0) = 0 < c < M = g(t^*)$, by the Inter
mediate Value Theorem there exists $t_1 \in (0, t^*)$ with $g(t_1) = c$. Since $
g(t^*) = M > c > 0 = g(2\pi)$, there exists $t_2 \in (t^*, 2\pi)$ with $g(t_2) =
 c$.

Since $t_1 < t^* < t_2$ and $\gamma$ is injective on $[0, 2\pi)$, the points $y_
1 = \gamma(t_1)$ and $y_2 = \gamma(t_2)$ are **distinct** points on $S$, and bot
h satisfy
$$\prod_{i=1}^{4} |y_j - x_i| = f(y_j) = c, \quad j = 1, 2.$$

**Conclusion.**

The set $\{y_1, y_2\}$ has $2 \leq 4$ elements, and the product of Euclidean dis
tances from each $y_j$ to the four given points $x_i$ is the same (equal to $c$)
 for all $j$. Therefore, such a set always exists, regardless of the configurati
on of the four points on the sphere. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The argument is purely topological: $f$ is a continuous function on
a compact surface that achieves both $0$ and a positive value, so any intermedia
te level set on a suitable closed curve (great circle) must be crossed at least
twice. One can obtain up to $4$ points by using two distinct great circles and s
electing a common intermediate value $c$ smaller than the maximum of $f$ on each
.
