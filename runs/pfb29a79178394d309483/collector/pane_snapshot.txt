**Answer: No.** The valuation rings of $\mathbb{Q}$ are $\mathbb{Q}$ itself toge
ther with the rings $R_p = \{a/b \in \mathbb{Q} : p \nmid b\}$ for primes $p$. T
he rings $R_p$ are exactly the *proper* valuation rings, but $\mathbb{Q}$ is a (
trivial) valuation ring not of this form.

---

## Proof

**Notation.** For a prime $p$, let $R_p := \{a/b \in \mathbb{Q} : a/b \text{ in
lowest terms},\, p \nmid b\} = \mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$.

**Step 1. Each $R_p$ is a valuation ring of $\mathbb{Q}$.**

$R_p$ is a subring (localization of $\mathbb{Z}$ at $(p)$). For any nonzero $x =
 a/b \in \mathbb{Q}$ in lowest terms: either $p \nmid b$, so $x \in R_p$; or $p
\mid b$, whence $p \nmid a$ (since $\gcd(a,b)=1$), so $x^{-1} = b/a \in R_p$. Th
us $R_p$ is a valuation ring. Clearly $R_p \neq \mathbb{Q}$ (e.g. $1/p \notin R_
p$).

**Step 2. $\mathbb{Q}$ is a valuation ring of itself.**

Trivially, every $x \in \mathbb{Q}^*$ lies in $\mathbb{Q}$. So $\mathbb{Q}$ is a
 valuation ring, and $\mathbb{Q} \neq R_p$ for any $p$ (since $1/p \in \mathbb{Q
}$ but $1/p \notin R_p$). This already shows the answer is **No**.

It remains to show these are *all* the valuation rings, i.e., every proper valua
tion ring equals some $R_p$.

**Step 3. Every valuation ring $R$ of $\mathbb{Q}$ contains $\mathbb{Z}$.**

$R$ is a subring of $\mathbb{Q}$ containing $1$, hence contains $n \cdot 1$ for
all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

**Step 4. If $R \neq \mathbb{Q}$, there exists a prime $p$ with $1/p \notin R$.*
*

If $1/p \in R$ for every prime $p$, then for any $a/b \in \mathbb{Q}$ with $b =
\prod p_i^{e_i}$, we have $1/b = \prod (1/p_i)^{e_i} \in R$, so $a/b = a \cdot (
1/b) \in R$. Hence $R = \mathbb{Q}$, contradiction.

**Step 5. At most one prime $p$ satisfies $1/p \notin R$.**

Suppose for contradiction that distinct primes $p, q$ have $1/p \notin R$ and $1
/q \notin R$. Since $\gcd(p,q)=1$, by Bézout there exist $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$ w
ith $ap + bq = 1$. Both $ap, bq \in R$ (Step 3).

By the valuation property applied to $ap, bq \in R$: either $ap/(bq) \in R$ or $
bq/(ap) \in R$.

- **Case $ap/(bq) \in R$:** let $t = ap/(bq) \in R$, so $ap = bq \cdot t$. Then
$1 = bq \cdot t + bq = bq(t+1)$, so $bq$ is a unit of $R$ with inverse $t+1 \in
R$. Hence $1/q = b \cdot (bq)^{-1} = b(t+1) \in R$ (since $b \in \mathbb{Z} \sub
seteq R$). This contradicts $1/q \notin R$.

- **Case $bq/(ap) \in R$:** by the symmetric argument (swapping $p \leftrightarr
ow q$, $a \leftrightarrow b$), we get $1/p \in R$, contradicting $1/p \notin R$.

Both cases give contradictions. Hence at most one prime has $1/p \notin R$.

**Step 6. If $R \neq \mathbb{Q}$, then $R = R_p$ for the unique prime $p$ with $
1/p \notin R$.**

By Steps 4 and 5, there is a unique prime $p$ with $1/p \notin R$, and $1/q \in
R$ for all primes $q \neq p$.

*$R_p \subseteq R$:* Take $a/b \in R_p$ (lowest terms, $p \nmid b$). Every prime
 factor $q$ of $b$ satisfies $q \neq p$, so $1/q \in R$, hence $1/b = \prod (1/q
_i)^{e_i} \in R$, and $a/b = a \cdot (1/b) \in R$.

*$R \subseteq R_p$:* Take $a/b \in \mathbb{Q}$ in lowest terms with $p \mid b$.
Then $p \nmid a$. Suppose for contradiction $a/b \in R$. Since $p \nmid a$, all
prime factors of $a$ are $\neq p$, so $1/a \in R$ (as above). Then $1/b = (1/a)(
a/b) \in R$. Write $b = pc$; since $p \nmid c$ (as $\gcd(a,b)=1$ and $p \mid b$
forces $p \nmid a$; and $c = b/p$ may or may not be divisible by $p$... actually
 $c$ could have $p$ as factor). Let me be more careful: $1/b \in R$ and $c = b/p
 \in \mathbb{Z} \subseteq R$. Then $1/p = c \cdot (1/b) \in R$, contradicting $1
/p \notin R$. So $a/b \notin R$.

Hence $R = R_p$.

**Conclusion.** The valuation rings of $\mathbb{Q}$ are precisely
$$\mathbb{Q} \quad\text{and}\quad R_p = \{a/b \in \mathbb{Q} : p \nmid b\} \text
{ for primes } p.$$
The rings $\{a/b \in \mathbb{Q} : p \nmid b\}$ are exactly the *proper* valuatio
n rings of $\mathbb{Q}$, but they are **not** all valuation rings, since $\mathb
b{Q}$ itself is a valuation ring not of this form. Therefore the answer to the q
uestion is **No**.
