## Solution

We evaluate $I = \int_0^{\infty} \frac{\tan^{-1}(\pi x) - \tan^{-1} x}{x} \, dx$
 using **Feynman's trick** (differentiation under the integral sign).

### Setup

Define a parameterized family of integrals for $a \geq 1$:

$$I(a) = \int_0^{\infty} \frac{\tan^{-1}(ax) - \tan^{-1}(x)}{x} \, dx.$$

Note that $I(1) = 0$ (the integrand vanishes identically) and our target is $I(\
pi)$.

### Differentiate under the integral sign

Differentiating with respect to $a$ (justified below):

$$I'(a) = \int_0^{\infty} \frac{1}{x} \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial a}\tan^{-1}
(ax) \, dx = \int_0^{\infty} \frac{1}{x} \cdot \frac{x}{1 + a^2 x^2} \, dx = \in
t_0^{\infty} \frac{dx}{1 + a^2 x^2}.$$

This is a standard integral:

$$I'(a) = \frac{1}{a}\int_0^{\infty} \frac{d(ax)}{1+(ax)^2} = \frac{1}{a}\cdot\f
rac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2a}.$$

### Integrate back

$$I(a) = \frac{\pi}{2}\ln a + C.$$

Applying the boundary condition $I(1) = 0$:

$$0 = \frac{\pi}{2}\ln 1 + C \implies C = 0.$$

Therefore

$$I(a) = \frac{\pi}{2}\ln a.$$

### Evaluate at $a = \pi$

$$I(\pi) = \frac{\pi}{2}\ln \pi.$$

### Justification of differentiation under the integral sign

For any $a_0 > 0$ and $a \in [a_0, A]$ (a compact subinterval of $(0,\infty)$),
the derivative of the integrand with respect to $a$ is

$$\frac{1}{1+a^2 x^2} \leq \frac{1}{1+a_0^2 x^2},$$

and $\int_0^{\infty} \frac{dx}{1+a_0^2 x^2} = \frac{\pi}{2a_0} < \infty$ is an i
ntegrable dominating function, independent of $a$. By the dominated convergence
theorem, differentiation under the integral sign is valid on every compact subin
terval of $(0,\infty)$, hence on $[1,\pi]$.

### Result

$$\boxed{\int_0^{\infty} \frac{\tan^{-1}(\pi x) - \tan^{-1} x}{x} \, dx = \frac{
\pi}{2}\ln \pi.}$$
