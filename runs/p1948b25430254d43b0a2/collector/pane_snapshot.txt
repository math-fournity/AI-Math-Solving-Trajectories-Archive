## Claim

Yes, it is possible to express $f(a)$ solely in terms of $g(x)$. In fact, the fu
nctional equation has a **unique** solution, namely $f \equiv 0$.

## Proof

Assume $g$ is continuous on $[0, \infty)$ (so that the Fundamental Theorem of Ca
lculus applies). The functional equation is

$$f(a) = \int_0^a g(x)\,f(x)\,dx. \tag{1}$$

**Step 1: Initial condition.** Setting $a = 0$ in (1):

$$f(0) = \int_0^0 g(x)\,f(x)\,dx = 0. \tag{2}$$

**Step 2: Differentiation.** Since $g$ is continuous and $f$ is continuous (bein
g an integral of a continuous integrand), the right-hand side of (1) is differen
tiable in $a$ by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. Differentiating both sides
 of (1) with respect to $a$:

$$f'(a) = g(a)\,f(a). \tag{3}$$

**Step 3: Solve the ODE.** Equation (3) is a first-order linear (separable) ODE.
 Its general solution is

$$f(a) = C\exp\!\left(\int_0^a g(x)\,dx\right) \tag{4}$$

for an arbitrary constant $C$.

**Step 4: Apply the initial condition.** Substituting $a = 0$ into (4):

$$f(0) = C\exp\!\left(\int_0^0 g(x)\,dx\right) = C \cdot e^0 = C.$$

By the initial condition (2), $f(0) = 0$, so $C = 0$.

**Step 5: Conclusion.** With $C = 0$, equation (4) gives

$$\boxed{f(a) = 0 \quad \text{for all } a.}$$

**Verification.** Substituting back into (1): $\int_0^a g(x)\cdot 0\,dx = 0 = f(
a)$. ✓

**Remark on uniqueness.** The derivation shows that *every* solution of (1) must
 satisfy the ODE (3) with initial condition (2), and the ODE with this initial c
ondition has a unique solution by the Picard–Lindelöf theorem (the right-hand si
de $g(a)f$ is Lipschitz in $f$ when $g$ is continuous). Therefore $f \equiv 0$ i
s the **only** solution.

Since the unique solution $f(a) = 0$ is expressed entirely in terms of $g(x)$ (i
ndeed, it is independent of $g$), the answer to the question is **yes**: $f(a)$
can be expressed solely in terms of $g(x)$.
