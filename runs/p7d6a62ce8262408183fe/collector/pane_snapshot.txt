## Proof

**Claim.** There exists a function $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ that is differ
entiable at exactly one point.

**Construction.** Define
$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2, & x \in \mathbb{Q}, \\ 0, & x \notin \mathbb{Q}. \end{
cases}
$$
Equivalently, $f(x) = x^2 \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)$. We show $f$ is diff
erentiable at $x = 0$ and nowhere else.

---

### Differentiability at $x = 0$

Since $0 \in \mathbb{Q}$, we have $f(0) = 0$. For any $h \neq 0$:

- If $h \in \mathbb{Q}$: $\dfrac{f(h) - f(0)}{h} = \dfrac{h^2}{h} = h$.
- If $h \notin \mathbb{Q}$: $\dfrac{f(h) - f(0)}{h} = \dfrac{0}{h} = 0$.

In both cases $\left|\dfrac{f(h)}{h}\right| \le |h| \to 0$ as $h \to 0$. Hence
$$
f'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(h) - f(0)}{h} = 0.
$$
So $f$ is differentiable at $0$.

---

### Non-differentiability at $x = a$, $a \neq 0$

We show the difference quotient $\dfrac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$ has no limit as $h \t
o 0$.

**Case 1: $a \in \mathbb{Q}$, $a \neq 0$.** Then $f(a) = a^2$.

- For $h \in \mathbb{Q}$: $a + h \in \mathbb{Q}$, so $f(a+h) = (a+h)^2$, giving
  $$
  \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} = \frac{(a+h)^2 - a^2}{h} = 2a + h \xrightarrow{h \to
0} 2a.
  $$
- For $h \notin \mathbb{Q}$: $a + h \notin \mathbb{Q}$, so $f(a+h) = 0$, giving
  $$
  \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} = \frac{-a^2}{h}.
  $$
  As $h \to 0$ through irrationals, $\dfrac{-a^2}{h}$ is unbounded (its magnitud
e $\to \infty$), so it has no finite limit.

Since the rational subsequence tends to $2a \neq 0$ while the irrational subsequ
ence is unbounded, the full limit does not exist. Thus $f$ is not differentiable
 at $a$.

**Case 2: $a \notin \mathbb{Q}$, $a \neq 0$.** Then $f(a) = 0$.

- For $h \notin \mathbb{Q}$: $a + h \notin \mathbb{Q}$, so $f(a+h) = 0$, giving
  $$
  \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} = 0 \xrightarrow{h \to 0} 0.
  $$
- For $h \in \mathbb{Q}$: $a + h \in \mathbb{Q}$, so $f(a+h) = (a+h)^2$, giving
  $$
  \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} = \frac{(a+h)^2}{h} = \frac{a^2 + 2ah + h^2}{h} = \fra
c{a^2}{h} + 2a + h.
  $$
  As $h \to 0$ through rationals, the term $\dfrac{a^2}{h}$ is unbounded (since
$a \neq 0$), so this subsequence has no finite limit.

The irrational subsequence tends to $0$ while the rational subsequence is unboun
ded, so the full limit does not exist. Thus $f$ is not differentiable at $a$.

### Conclusion

The function $f(x) = x^2 \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)$ is differentiable at
$x = 0$ (with $f'(0) = 0$) and is not differentiable at any $x \neq 0$. Therefor
e, there exists a function $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ that is differentiable at
 exactly one point. $\blacksquare$
