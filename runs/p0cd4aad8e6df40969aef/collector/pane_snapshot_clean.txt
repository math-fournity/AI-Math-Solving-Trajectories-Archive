**Setup.** Rearrange the equilibrium equation to isolate $a$:

$$a = \frac{3(x+1)}{\sqrt{x}} - \frac{x+1}{\sqrt{x^2 - x + 1}}.$$

Define $f:(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}$ by

$$f(x) = \frac{3(x+1)}{\sqrt{x}} - \frac{x+1}{\sqrt{x^2 - x + 1}}.$$

We seek all real $a$ for which $f(x) = a$ has **exactly one** solution $x > 0$.

---

**Step 1: Boundary behavior.**

- As $x \to 0^+$: $\frac{3(x+1)}{\sqrt{x}} \to +\infty$ and $\frac{x+1}{\sqrt{x^
2-x+1}} \to 1$, so $f(x) \to +\infty$.
- As $x \to +\infty$: $\frac{3(x+1)}{\sqrt{x}} = 3\!\left(\sqrt{x}+\frac{1}{\sqr
t{x}}\right) \to +\infty$ and $\frac{x+1}{\sqrt{x^2-x+1}} \to 1$, so $f(x) \to +
\infty$.

Since $f$ is continuous on $(0,+\infty)$ and tends to $+\infty$ at both ends, it
 attains a global minimum at some interior point.

---

**Step 2: Compute $f'(x)$.**

Write $f(x) = g(x) - h(x)$ where $g(x) = 3(x+1)x^{-1/2}$ and $h(x) = (x+1)(x^2-x
+1)^{-1/2}$.

**Derivative of $g$:**
$$g'(x) = 3\!\left[x^{-1/2} - \tfrac{x+1}{2}\,x^{-3/2}\right] = \frac{3(x-1)}{2\
,x^{3/2}}.$$

**Derivative of $h$:** Using the product rule with $u = x+1$, $v = (x^2-x+1)^{-1
/2}$:
$$h'(x) = (x^2-x+1)^{-1/2} + (x+1)\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)(x^2-x+1)^{-3/2}(2
x-1).$$
Factoring out $(x^2-x+1)^{-3/2}$, the bracket numerator is
$$2(x^2-x+1) - (x+1)(2x-1) = (2x^2-2x+2)-(2x^2+x-1) = -3x+3 = -3(x-1),$$
so
$$h'(x) = \frac{-3(x-1)}{2\,(x^2-x+1)^{3/2}}.$$

**Combining:**
$$f'(x) = g'(x) - h'(x) = \frac{3(x-1)}{2\,x^{3/2}} + \frac{3(x-1)}{2\,(x^2-x+1)
^{3/2}} = \frac{3(x-1)}{2}\left[\frac{1}{x^{3/2}} + \frac{1}{(x^2-x+1)^{3/2}}\ri
ght].$$

---

**Step 3: Sign analysis of $f'(x)$.**

For all $x > 0$:
- $x^{3/2} > 0$,
- $x^2 - x + 1 = \left(x - \tfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 + \tfrac{3}{4} > 0$, so $(x^2-x
+1)^{3/2} > 0$.

Hence the bracket $\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{x^{3/2}} + \frac{1}{(x^2-x+1)^{3/
2}}\right]$ is **strictly positive** for all $x > 0$. Therefore the sign of $f'(
x)$ is determined entirely by $(x-1)$:

- $f'(x) < 0$ for $0 < x < 1$ — $f$ is **strictly decreasing**.
- $f'(x) = 0$ at $x = 1$ — the **unique** critical point.
- $f'(x) > 0$ for $x > 1$ — $f$ is **strictly increasing**.

Thus $f$ has exactly one critical point (a global minimum) at $x = 1$, and no lo
cal maximum.

---

**Step 4: Value at the minimum.**

$$f(1) = \frac{3(1+1)}{\sqrt{1}} - \frac{1+1}{\sqrt{1 - 1 + 1}} = 6 - 2 = 4.$$

**Verification:** At $x=1$, $a=4$: $\text{LHS} = \frac{3\cdot2}{1} = 6$, $\text{
RHS} = 4 + \frac{2}{1} = 6$. ✓

---

**Step 5: Counting solutions.**

Since $f$ is continuous, strictly decreasing on $(0,1]$ from $+\infty$ to $4$, a
nd strictly increasing on $[1,+\infty)$ from $4$ to $+\infty$:

| Range of $a$ | Solutions |
|---|---|
| $a < 4$ | $0$ solutions (since $f(x) \geq 4$ for all $x>0$) |
| $a = 4$ | **exactly $1$ solution** ($x = 1$) |
| $a > 4$ | exactly $2$ solutions (one in $(0,1)$, one in $(1,+\infty)$, by the
Intermediate Value Theorem and strict monotonicity on each branch) |

---

**Conclusion.** The equation has exactly one unique solution if and only if $a$
equals the global minimum of $f$:

$$\boxed{a = 4}.$$
