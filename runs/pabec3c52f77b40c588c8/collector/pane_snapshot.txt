## Answer

**No.** An element of $B$ invertible in $A$ need not be invertible in $B$. Here
is a standard counterexample.

### Counterexample

Let $A = C(\mathbb{T})$, the Banach algebra of continuous complex-valued functio
ns on the unit circle $\mathbb{T} = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z|=1\}$, with the sup
norm and pointwise operations. The constant function $\mathbf{1}(z)=1$ is its id
entity.

Let $B = A(\mathbb{D})$, the **disc algebra**: the closed subalgebra of $C(\math
bb{T})$ consisting of those continuous functions on $\mathbb{T}$ that extend hol
omorphically to the open unit disc $\mathbb{D}$. (Equivalently, $B$ is the closu
re in $C(\mathbb{T})$ of the polynomials in $z$; it is a closed subalgebra conta
ining $\mathbf{1}$.)

**Claim:** $B$ is a closed subalgebra of $A$ containing $\mathbf{1}$, and there
exists $a \in B$ invertible in $A$ but not in $B$.

*Closedness.* $B$ is the uniform closure in $C(\mathbb{T})$ of the polynomials $
\sum_{k=0}^{n} c_k z^k$; uniform limits of holomorphic-on-$\mathbb{D}$ functions
 that are continuous on $\overline{\mathbb{D}}$ remain holomorphic on $\mathbb{D
}$ (by Weierstrass/Morera), so $B$ is closed. It clearly contains $\mathbf{1}$ a
nd is closed under multiplication.

*The element.* Take $a(z) = z$, i.e. $a$ is the identity function on $\mathbb{T}
$. Then $a \in B$ (it is a polynomial, and extends holomorphically to $\mathbb{D
}$).

*Invertible in $A$.* On $\mathbb{T}$ we have $|z|=1$, so $z \cdot \bar z = |z|^2
 = 1$. The function $b(z) = \bar z$ is continuous on $\mathbb{T}$, hence $b \in
A = C(\mathbb{T})$, and $a \cdot b = \mathbf{1}$. Thus $a$ is invertible in $A$.

*Not invertible in $B$.* Suppose $a$ had an inverse $g \in B$. Then $g$ would be
 continuous on $\mathbb{T}$, holomorphic on $\mathbb{D}$, and satisfy $z\,g(z) =
 1$ for all $z \in \mathbb{T}$. By the maximum principle (or uniqueness of holom
orphic extension), $z\,g(z) = 1$ for all $z \in \mathbb{D}$ as well. Evaluating
at $z = 0$ gives $0 \cdot g(0) = 1$, i.e. $0 = 1$, a contradiction. Equivalently
, $g(z) = 1/z$ has a pole at $0$ and is not holomorphic on $\mathbb{D}$, so $g \
notin B$.

### Conclusion

The element $a(z)=z$ lies in $B = A(\mathbb{D})$, is invertible in $A = C(\mathb
b{T})$ (with inverse $\bar z$), but is **not** invertible in $B$. Therefore the
answer to the question is **no**: invertibility in the larger Banach algebra $A$
 does not force invertibility in the closed subalgebra $B$.

(Remark: the obstruction is that the spectrum $\sigma_B(a)$ can be strictly larg
er than $\sigma_A(a)$; here $\sigma_A(a)=\mathbb{T}$ while $\sigma_B(a)=\overlin
e{\mathbb{D}}$, and $0 \in \sigma_B(a)\setminus\sigma_A(a)$ is exactly the point
 preventing invertibility in $B$.)
