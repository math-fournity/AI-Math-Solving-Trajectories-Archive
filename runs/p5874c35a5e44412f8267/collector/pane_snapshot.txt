**Answer: Yes.** In fact, the limit is *automatically* continuous on $C$ for any
 subsequence converging uniformly on each $C_m$. The key is that equicontinuity
holds on **all** compact subsets of $X$, not just the $C_m$.

---

**Proof.**

Let $(f_{k_n})$ be any subsequence converging to $f$ uniformly on each $C_m$ (ob
tained, e.g., by the standard diagonal argument). We show $f: C \to [0,1]$ is co
ntinuous.

Since $X$ is a Polish space (hence metrizable), $C$ with the subspace topology i
s a metric space, so it suffices to verify sequential continuity.

**Claim:** For every $x \in C$ and every sequence $(x_j)_{j \geq 1} \subseteq C$
 with $x_j \to x$ in $X$, we have $f(x_j) \to f(x)$.

*Proof of Claim.* Fix $x \in C$ and $x_j \to x$ with $x_j \in C$. Set
$$K := \{x\} \cup \{x_j : j \geq 1\}.$$
Since $(x_j)$ converges to $x$ in the metric space $X$, the set $K$ is compact:
any sequence in $K$ has either a constant subsequence or a subsequence convergin
g to $x$.

Let $\varepsilon > 0$. By the hypothesis that $\{f_n\}$ is equicontinuous on com
pact subsets of $X$, applied to $K$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that

$$\text{for all } n \in \mathbb{N} \text{ and all } y, z \in K \text{ with } d(y
,z) < \delta: \quad |f_n(y) - f_n(z)| < \varepsilon. \tag{$\star$}$$

Since $x_j \to x$, there exists $J$ such that $d(x_j, x) < \delta$ for all $j \g
eq J$.

Now fix any $j \geq J$. Both $x_j$ and $x$ belong to $C$, so $x_j \in C_{m_j}$ a
nd $x \in C_{m_0}$ for some $m_j, m_0$. Since $f_{k_n} \to f$ uniformly on each
$C_m$ (hence pointwise on $C$):

$$f_{k_n}(x_j) \to f(x_j) \quad \text{and} \quad f_{k_n}(x) \to f(x) \quad \text
{as } n \to \infty.$$

By $(\star)$, for every $n$ we have $|f_{k_n}(x_j) - f_{k_n}(x)| < \varepsilon$
(since $d(x_j, x) < \delta$ and both points lie in $K$). Taking $n \to \infty$:

$$|f(x_j) - f(x)| = \lim_{n \to \infty} |f_{k_n}(x_j) - f_{k_n}(x)| \leq \vareps
ilon.$$

This holds for every $j \geq J$. Since $\varepsilon > 0$ was arbitrary, $f(x_j)
\to f(x)$. $\square$

Since $x \in C$ and the sequence $(x_j) \subseteq C$ converging to $x$ were arbi
trary, $f$ is sequentially continuous on $C$, hence continuous (as $C$ is metriz
able). $\blacksquare$

---

**Remark on the key idea.** The crucial point is that the compact set $K = \{x\}
 \cup \{x_j\}$ need not be contained in any single $C_m$. The hypothesis of equi
continuity on *all* compact subsets of $X$—not merely on the $C_m$—is what provi
des the uniform bound $(\star)$ valid for **every** $n$ simultaneously. This uni
form-in-$n$ bound survives the passage to the limit $n \to \infty$, yielding con
tinuity of $f$ on $C$. Without equicontinuity on arbitrary compact sets (only on
 the $C_m$), the argument could fail, since a sequence converging to a point of
$C_{m_0}$ might pass through points in $C_{m'} \setminus C_{m_0}$ for arbitraril
y large $m'$, where the equicontinuity modulus could deteriorate.
