Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether a bounded solution $u$ to $
\Delta u = f$ in the first quadrant $T$ with mixed boundary conditions ($u(x,0)=
0$ Dirichlet, $u_x(0,y)=0$ Neumann) must vanish at infinity, given that $f$ is c
ontinuous and compactly supported.

**Answer: Yes, $u \to 0$ as $x^2 + y^2 \to \infty$.**

## Proof

**Step 1: Extension by reflection.**

Extend $u$ to all of $\mathbb{R}^2$ via the symmetries dictated by the boundary
conditions:
- **Even reflection in $x$** (Neumann on $x=0$): $\tilde{u}(-x, y) = \tilde{u}(x
, y)$.
- **Odd reflection in $y$** (Dirichlet on $y=0$): $\tilde{u}(x, -y) = -\tilde{u}
(x, y)$.

Explicitly, for $(x,y) \in T$: $\tilde{u}(x,y)=u(x,y)$; for $x<0, y\geq 0$: $\ti
lde{u}(x,y)=u(-x,y)$; for $x\geq 0, y<0$: $\tilde{u}(x,y)=-u(x,-y)$; for $x<0, y
<0$: $\tilde{u}(x,y)=-u(-x,-y)$.

Similarly extend $f$ to $\tilde{f}$ by the same symmetries (even in $x$, odd in
$y$).

**Regularity across the axes:** $\tilde{u}$ is continuous across $y=0$ since $\t
ilde{u}(x,0^+)=u(x,0)=0$ and $\tilde{u}(x,0^-)=-u(x,0)=0$. The $y$-derivative is
 also continuous: $\partial_y\tilde{u}(x,0^-)=u_y(x,0)=\partial_y\tilde{u}(x,0^+
)$. Across $x=0$: $\partial_x\tilde{u}(0^-,y)=-u_x(0,y)=0=\partial_x\tilde{u}(0^
+,y)$, so $\tilde{u}\in C^1$ across $x=0$. Thus $\Delta\tilde{u}=\tilde{f}$ hold
s on $\mathbb{R}^2$ in the distributional sense.

**Key properties of $\tilde{f}$:** It is bounded, compactly supported (in the di
sk of radius $R$ extended by reflection), and:
$$\iint_{\mathbb{R}^2} \tilde{f}\,d\xi\,d\eta = 0,$$
because the contributions from the four quadrants cancel: QI contributes $+\iint
_T f$, QII contributes $+\iint_T f$ (even in $x$), QIII contributes $-\iint_T f$
 (odd in $y$), QIV contributes $-\iint_T f$, giving $+1+1-1-1=0$.

**Step 2: Decomposition $\tilde{u} = w + h$.**

Let $w$ be the 2D Newtonian potential of $\tilde{f}$:
$$w(x,y) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^2} \ln\sqrt{(x-\xi)^2+(y-\eta)^2}\;\t
ilde{f}(\xi,\eta)\,d\xi\,d\eta.$$
Since $\tilde{f}$ is bounded and compactly supported, $w$ is well-defined. Since
 $\iint\tilde{f}=0$, we may write:
$$w(x,y) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\iint \bigl(\ln|x-\xi| - \ln|x|\bigr)\,\tilde{f}(\xi)\,
d\xi,$$
which shows $w$ is bounded (the integrand is $O(|\xi|/|x|)$ for large $|x|$, giv
ing $w=O(1/|x|)$; near the support, the logarithmic kernel is locally integrable
). Also $\Delta w = \tilde{f}$ distributionally.

Set $h = \tilde{u} - w$. Then $\Delta h = 0$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ (distributionally
), so by Weyl's lemma $h$ is smooth and harmonic everywhere. Since both $\tilde{
u}$ and $w$ are bounded, $h$ is a **bounded harmonic function on $\mathbb{R}^2$*
*. By Liouville's theorem, $h$ is constant.

**Determining the constant:** Both $\tilde{u}$ and $w$ are odd in $y$ (inherited
 from $\tilde{f}$'s odd symmetry in $y$), so $\tilde{u}(x,0)=0$ and $w(x,0)=0$.
Thus $h(x,0)=0$ for all $x$, giving $h\equiv 0$.

Therefore $\tilde{u} = w$.

**Step 3: Asymptotic decay of $w$.**

Since $\iint\tilde{f}=0$, the monopole ($\ln r$) term vanishes. The leading term
 is the dipole:
$$w(x,y) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{x\iint\xi\,\tilde{f} + y\iint\eta\,\tilde{f}
}{r^2} + O(r^{-2}), \qquad r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\to\infty.$$

By the even symmetry of $\tilde{f}$ in $\xi$: $\iint \xi\,\tilde{f} = 0$ (the in
tegrand is odd in $\xi$).

For the $\eta$-moment, each quadrant contributes $\iint_T \eta\, f(\xi,\eta)\,d\
xi\,d\eta$ (verified by substitution in each quadrant), so:
$$\iint \eta\,\tilde{f} = 4\iint_T \eta\, f(\xi,\eta)\,d\xi\,d\eta =: 4C_0.$$

Thus:
$$w(x,y) = \frac{2C_0\, y}{\pi\, r^2} + O(r^{-2}).$$

Since $|y| \leq r$:
$$|w(x,y)| \leq \frac{2|C_0|}{\pi\, r} + O(r^{-2}) \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \quad\t
ext{as } r\to\infty.$$

**Conclusion:** $u = \tilde{u}\big|_T = w\big|_T \to 0$ as $x^2+y^2\to\infty$, u
niformly in all directions within $T$. $\blacksquare$
