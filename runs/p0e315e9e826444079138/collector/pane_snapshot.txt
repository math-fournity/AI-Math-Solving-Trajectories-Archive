## Solution

**Problem.** Find an asymptotic expansion of $\displaystyle\frac{\ln\!\left(\int
_{x}^{x+2}e^{t^2}\,dt\right)}{x^2}$ as $x\to+\infty$.

---

### Step 1. Reduce to a Laplace-type integral at the upper endpoint

Since $e^{t^2}$ is increasing for $t>0$ and the interval $[x,x+2]$ lies in the p
ositive reals for large $x$, the integral is dominated by the neighborhood of th
e upper endpoint $t=x+2$. Set $y=x+2$ and substitute $t = y - s$, $s\in[0,2]$:

$$\int_x^{x+2} e^{t^2}\,dt = \int_0^2 e^{(y-s)^2}\,ds = e^{y^2}\int_0^2 e^{-2ys+
s^2}\,ds.$$

### Step 2. Extend to $[0,\infty)$ and expand

The tail $\int_2^\infty e^{-2ys+s^2}\,ds$ is $O(e^{-4y})$ (at $s=2$ the exponent
 equals $4-4y$, and it only decreases thereafter), so it is exponentially small
and negligible for any power-series asymptotic. Thus

$$\int_0^2 e^{-2ys+s^2}\,ds = \int_0^\infty e^{-2ys}\,e^{s^2}\,ds + O(e^{-4y}).$
$

Expanding $e^{s^2}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{s^{2n}}{n!}$ and integrating termwise
 (justified since the series converges uniformly on the relevant range for large
 $y$):

$$\int_0^\infty e^{-2ys+s^2}\,ds = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{1}{n!}\cdot\frac{(2n)
!}{(2y)^{2n+1}} = \frac{1}{2y}+\frac{1}{4y^3}+\frac{3}{8y^5}+\frac{15}{16y^7}+\c
dots$$

$$= \frac{1}{2y}\left(1+\frac{1}{2y^2}+\frac{3}{4y^4}+\cdots\right).$$

### Step 3. Take the logarithm

$$\ln\!\left(\int_x^{x+2}e^{t^2}\,dt\right) = y^2 + \ln\!\left[\frac{1}{2y}\left
(1+\frac{1}{2y^2}+\frac{3}{4y^4}+\cdots\right)\right]$$

$$= y^2 - \ln(2y) + \ln\!\left(1+\frac{1}{2y^2}+\frac{3}{4y^4}+\cdots\right).$$

Using $\ln(1+u)=u-\frac{u^2}{2}+\cdots$ with $u=\frac{1}{2y^2}+\frac{3}{4y^4}+\c
dots$:

$$\ln(1+u) = \frac{1}{2y^2}+\frac{3}{4y^4}-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{4y^4}+\cdots
 = \frac{1}{2y^2}+\frac{5}{8y^4}+O(y^{-6}).$$

Therefore

$$\ln\!\left(\int_x^{x+2}e^{t^2}\,dt\right) = y^2 - \ln(2y) + \frac{1}{2y^2} + O
(y^{-4}), \qquad y=x+2.$$

### Step 4. Divide by $x^2$ and re-expand in powers of $1/x$

Now substitute $y=x+2$ and divide by $x^2$:

$$\frac{y^2}{x^2} = \frac{(x+2)^2}{x^2} = 1+\frac{4}{x}+\frac{4}{x^2}.$$

$$\frac{\ln(2y)}{x^2} = \frac{\ln 2+\ln(x+2)}{x^2} = \frac{\ln 2+\ln x}{x^2}+\fr
ac{\ln(1+2/x)}{x^2} = \frac{\ln(2x)}{x^2}+\frac{2}{x^3}+O(x^{-4}).$$

$$\frac{1}{2x^2 y^2} = \frac{1}{2x^2(x+2)^2} = \frac{1}{2x^4}+O(x^{-5}).$$

Collecting all terms:

$$\frac{\ln\!\left(\int_x^{x+2}e^{t^2}\,dt\right)}{x^2} = 1+\frac{4}{x}+\frac{4}
{x^2}-\frac{\ln(2x)}{x^2}-\frac{2}{x^3}+O(x^{-4}).$$

### Result

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\frac{\ln\!\left(\int_{x}^{x+2}e^{t^2}\,dt\right)}{x^2} \;
=\; 1 \;+\; \frac{4}{x} \;+\; \frac{4-\ln(2x)}{x^2} \;-\; \frac{2}{x^3} \;+\; O\
!\left(\frac{1}{x^4}\right) \qquad (x\to+\infty).}$$

The leading term is $1$: the logarithmic growth of the integral is asymptotic to
 $(x+2)^2$, reflecting that $e^{t^2}$ is overwhelmingly concentrated near the up
per endpoint $t=x+2$.
