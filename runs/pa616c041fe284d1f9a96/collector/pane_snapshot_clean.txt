## Proof

We seek an extremal of the functional
$$J[y] = \int_0^1 \left[(y')^2 + 12xy\right] dx, \qquad y(0)=0,\; y(1)=1.$$

**Euler–Lagrange equation.** With $F(x,y,y') = (y')^2 + 12xy$, we compute
$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial y} = 12x, \qquad \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} = 2
y'.$$

The Euler–Lagrange equation $\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} - \frac{
\partial F}{\partial y} = 0$ gives
$$\frac{d}{dx}(2y') - 12x = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2y'' = 12x \;\Longrightarrow\;
 y'' = 6x.$$

**Integrating twice.**
$$y' = 3x^2 + c_1,$$
$$y = x^3 + c_1 x + c_2.$$

**Applying boundary conditions.**
- $y(0) = 0$: $\;0 + 0 + c_2 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; c_2 = 0$.
- $y(1) = 1$: $\;1 + c_1 + 0 = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; c_1 = 0$.

Therefore the extremal is
$$\boxed{y = x^3}.$$

**Verification that it is indeed a minimum (extremum).** The Jacobi condition /
Legendre condition: $\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial (y')^2} = 2 > 0$ everywhere, s
o the extremal yields a (weak) minimum of the functional. Conjugate points: the
Jacobi equation for the second variation is $\eta'' = 0$ with $\eta(0)=\eta(1)=0
$, giving $\eta \equiv 0$ — no conjugate point in $(0,1)$. Hence $y = x^3$ is a
genuine minimizing extremal.

**Direct check.** For $y = x^3$: $y' = 3x^2$, so
$$J = \int_0^1 \left[9x^4 + 12x\cdot x^3\right] dx = \int_0^1 \left[9x^4 + 12x^4
\right] dx = \int_0^1 21x^4\, dx = \frac{21}{5}.$$

The curve $y = x^3$ passes through $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$, satisfies the Euler–Lagr
ange equation, and realizes an extremum (minimum) of the functional.
