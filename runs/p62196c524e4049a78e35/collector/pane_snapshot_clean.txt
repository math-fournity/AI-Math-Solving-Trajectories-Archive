# Proof: Laurent Polynomials are Dense in $C(S^1, \mathbb{C})$

## Setup

Let $S^1 = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| = 1\}$ be the unit circle. A function $f \in
 C(S^1, \mathbb{C})$ is a continuous $\mathbb{C}$-valued function on $S^1$. Para
metrizing $z = e^{i\theta}$, $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$, such functions correspond t
o continuous $2\pi$-periodic functions $g(\theta) = f(e^{i\theta})$.

A polynomial of the form $P(z) = \sum_{n=-N}^{N} a_n z^n$ is a **Laurent polynom
ial**. Restricted to $S^1$ with $z = e^{i\theta}$, it becomes
$$P(e^{i\theta}) = \sum_{n=-N}^{N} a_n e^{in\theta},$$
which is a **trigonometric polynomial** (a finite linear combination of $e^{in\t
heta}$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$).

**Claim:** Laurent polynomials are dense in $C(S^1, \mathbb{C})$ with the unifor
m norm. Equivalently, trigonometric polynomials $\sum_{n=-N}^{N} a_n e^{in\theta
}$ are dense in the space of continuous $2\pi$-periodic functions.

## Proof via the Stone–Weierstrass Theorem

We apply the **complex Stone–Weierstrass theorem**:

> **Theorem (Stone–Weierstrass, complex form).** Let $X$ be a compact Hausdorff
space and $\mathcal{A} \subseteq C(X, \mathbb{C})$ a subalgebra. If $\mathcal{A}
$:
> 1. **contains the constants**,
> 2. **separates points** (for any $x \neq y \in X$, there exists $f \in \mathca
l{A}$ with $f(x) \neq f(y)$),
> 3. **is self-adjoint** (closed under complex conjugation: $f \in \mathcal{A} \
Rightarrow \overline{f} \in \mathcal{A}$),
>
> then $\mathcal{A}$ is dense in $C(X, \mathbb{C})$.

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be the algebra of Laurent polynomials restricted to $S^1$:
$$\mathcal{A} = \left\{ \sum_{n=-N}^{N} a_n z^n : N \in \mathbb{N},\ a_n \in \ma
thbb{C} \right\} \subseteq C(S^1, \mathbb{C}).$$

We verify the three hypotheses:

**(1) Contains the constants.** Taking $N = 0$ and $a_0 = c$ gives the constant
function $c \in \mathcal{A}$.

**(2) Separates points.** The function $P(z) = z$ (i.e., $N=1$, $a_1 = 1$, all o
ther $a_n = 0$) belongs to $\mathcal{A}$. For $z_1 \neq z_2 \in S^1$, $P(z_1) =
z_1 \neq z_2 = P(z_2)$, so $\mathcal{A}$ separates points.

**(3) Self-adjoint (closed under conjugation).** Let $P(z) = \sum_{n=-N}^{N} a_n
 z^n \in \mathcal{A}$. On $S^1$, $|z| = 1$ so $\overline{z} = z^{-1}$. Therefore
:
$$\overline{P(z)} = \sum_{n=-N}^{N} \overline{a_n}\, \overline{z}^{\,n} = \sum_{
n=-N}^{N} \overline{a_n}\, z^{-n} = \sum_{n=-N}^{N} \overline{a_{-n}}\, z^{n},$$
where in the last step we re-indexed $n \mapsto -n$. This is again a Laurent pol
ynomial, so $\overline{P} \in \mathcal{A}$.

**Key point:** This is where the *negative* powers are essential. The ordinary p
olynomials $\sum_{n=0}^{N} a_n z^n$ (only non-negative powers) form an algebra t
hat separates points and contains constants, but is **not** self-adjoint on $S^1
$ (since $\overline{z} = z^{-1}$ is not a polynomial in $z$). The negative power
s $z^{-n}$ are exactly what provides closure under conjugation, making the compl
ex Stone–Weierstrass theorem applicable.

All three conditions are satisfied, so by the complex Stone–Weierstrass theorem,
 $\mathcal{A}$ is dense in $C(S^1, \mathbb{C})$.

## Conclusion

For every $f \in C(S^1, \mathbb{C})$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a
 Laurent polynomial $P(z) = \sum_{n=-N}^{N} a_n z^n$ such that
$$\sup_{z \in S^1} |f(z) - P(z)| < \varepsilon.$$

Therefore, **yes**, polynomials of the form $\sum_{n=-N}^{N} a_n z^n$ (Laurent p
olynomials) can approximate any function in $C(S^1, \mathbb{C})$ uniformly. The
crucial ingredient is the inclusion of negative powers $z^{-n}$, which makes the
 algebra self-adjoint on $S^1$ and allows the complex Stone–Weierstrass theorem
to apply.
