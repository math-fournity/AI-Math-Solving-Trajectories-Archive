**Answer: Yes.**

**Construction.** Let $H = L^2([2,3], dx)$ where $dx$ is Lebesgue measure, and d
efine the multiplication operator
$$(Bf)(x) = x\,f(x), \qquad f \in L^2([2,3]).$$
Since $x \mapsto x$ is real-valued and bounded on $[2,3]$, $B$ is a bounded self
-adjoint operator on $H$.

**$B$ is self-adjoint.** For $f, g \in L^2([2,3])$,
$$\langle Bf, g\rangle = \int_2^3 x\,f(x)\,\overline{g(x)}\,dx = \int_2^3 f(x)\,
\overline{x\,g(x)}\,dx = \langle f, Bg\rangle,$$
so $B = B^*$. (Boundedness of $x$ on $[2,3]$ gives $\|Bf\| \le 3\|f\|$, so $B$ i
s everywhere-defined and bounded, hence self-adjoint.)

**$B$ has no eigenvalues.** Suppose $Bf = \lambda f$ for some $\lambda \in \math
bb{R}$ and $f \in L^2([2,3])$. Then $(x - \lambda)f(x) = 0$ for a.e. $x \in [2,3
]$. The set $\{x \in [2,3] : x = \lambda\}$ is a single point, hence has Lebesgu
e measure zero. Therefore $f(x) = 0$ a.e. on $[2,3]$, i.e. $f = 0$ in $L^2$. No
nonzero eigenvectors exist, so the point spectrum is empty.

**The spectrum of $B$ is $[2,3]$.** The spectrum of a multiplication operator $M
_\phi$ on $L^2$ equals the essential range of $\phi$. Here $\phi(x) = x$ is cont
inuous, so its essential range on $[2,3]$ is exactly $[2,3]$. Concretely:

- If $\lambda \notin [2,3]$, then $\operatorname{dist}(\lambda, [2,3]) > 0$, so
$(x-\lambda)^{-1}$ is bounded on $[2,3]$ and $B - \lambda I$ is invertible with
bounded inverse $(B-\lambda I)^{-1}g = g(x)/(x-\lambda)$. Thus $\lambda \notin \
sigma(B)$.

- If $\lambda \in [2,3]$, then $B - \lambda I$ (multiplication by $x - \lambda$)
 is injective (as shown above, $(x-\lambda)f = 0$ a.e. $\Rightarrow f = 0$). Its
 range is dense: for any $g \in L^2$ and $\varepsilon > 0$, let $g_\varepsilon =
 g \cdot \mathbf{1}_{[2,3]\setminus(\lambda-\varepsilon,\lambda+\varepsilon)}$;
then $g_\varepsilon \to g$ in $L^2$ and $g_\varepsilon(x)/(x-\lambda) \in L^2$,
so $g_\varepsilon$ lies in the range of $B - \lambda I$. But the range is not al
l of $L^2$: the constant function $\mathbf{1}$ belongs to $L^2([2,3])$, yet $\ma
thbf{1}/(x-\lambda) \notin L^2$ since $\int_2^3 |x-\lambda|^{-2}\,dx = \infty$.
Hence $B - \lambda I$ is injective with dense, non-closed (proper) range, so $\l
ambda$ belongs to the **continuous spectrum**.

**Conclusion.** The spectrum of a self-adjoint operator decomposes as $\sigma(B)
 = \sigma_p(B) \cup \sigma_c(B)$ (the residual spectrum is empty for self-adjoin
t operators). We have shown $\sigma_p(B) = \emptyset$ and $\sigma(B) = [2,3]$, s
o $\sigma_c(B) = [2,3]$.

Therefore such an operator exists: the multiplication-by-$x$ operator on $L^2([2
,3])$ is self-adjoint, has no eigenvalues, and has continuous spectrum exactly $
[2,3]$.
