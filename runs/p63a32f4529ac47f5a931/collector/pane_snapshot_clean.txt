**Yes, it is always possible.**

## Proof

Since $H$ is separable, it admits a countable dense subset $D=\{d_1,d_2,\dots\}\
subset H$. Because $A$ is dense in $H$, for each $d_n\in D$ and each $k\ge 1$ th
ere exists $a_{n,k}\in A$ with $\|a_{n,k}-d_n\|<1/k$. The collection
$$\{a_{n,k}:n\ge 1,\;k\ge 1\}\subset A$$
is countable and dense in $H$ (it approximates every $d_n$ arbitrarily well, and
 $D$ is dense). Enumerate it as a sequence $\{b_1,b_2,\dots\}\subset A$.

Apply the Gram–Schmidt process to $\{b_n\}$. Discarding any $b_n$ that lies in t
he span of its predecessors (which would produce the zero vector), we obtain a c
ountable orthonormal system $\{e_1,e_2,\dots\}$. By construction each $e_n$ is a
 finite linear combination of $b_1,\dots,b_n$. Since $A$ is a **linear subspace*
* and every $b_i\in A$, each $e_n\in A$.

It remains to show $\{e_n\}$ is complete, i.e. $\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{
e_n\}=H$. The Gram–Schmidt process preserves spans step by step:
$$\operatorname{span}\{e_1,\dots,e_n\}=\operatorname{span}\{b_1,\dots,b_n\}\quad
\text{for every }n.$$
Hence
$$\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{e_n\}=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{b_n\}=H,
$$
since $\{b_n\}$ is dense in $H$. Therefore $\{e_n\}$ is a complete orthonormal s
ystem for $H$, and every $e_n\in A$.

Thus a complete orthonormal system for $H$ contained in $A$ always exists. $\bla
cksquare$
