## Solution

**No, $f$ need not be identically zero.** Here is a counterexample and a discuss
ion of the subtlety.

### Counterexample

Let the domain be $\mathbb{C}$. Define:

$$f(z) = z, \qquad g(z) = \begin{cases} 1 & z \neq 0, \\ 2 & z = 0. \end{cases}$
$

**Verification:**

- **$f$ is holomorphic and not identically zero:** $f(z)=z$ is entire and nonzer
o for $z\neq 0$.

- **$g$ is non-holomorphic:** $g$ is not even continuous at $0$, since $\lim_{z\
to 0}g(z)=1\neq 2=g(0)$. A function that fails to be continuous cannot be holomo
rphic.

- **$fg$ is holomorphic:** For $z\neq 0$, $(fg)(z)=z\cdot 1=z$. At $z=0$, $(fg)(
0)=f(0)\cdot g(0)=0\cdot 2=0$. Thus $fg=z$ on all of $\mathbb{C}$, which is enti
re.

So we have a holomorphic $f$ (not identically zero), a non-holomorphic $g$, and
a holomorphic product $fg$. $\square$

### Why the counterexample works (and when it fails)

The mechanism is general. Suppose $f$ is holomorphic and **not** identically zer
o. Then the zeros of $f$ are **isolated**. On the open set $U=\{z:f(z)\neq 0\}$
(which is the domain minus isolated points), we have $g=(fg)/f$, a quotient of h
olomorphic functions, so **$g$ is holomorphic on $U$**. The only places where $g
$ can fail to be holomorphic are the isolated zeros of $f$. At such a zero $z_0$
, the value $g(z_0)$ is unconstrained by the product (since $f(z_0)=0$ forces $(
fg)(z_0)=0$ regardless), so we may assign $g(z_0)$ any value that breaks continu
ity — exactly as in the counterexample above.

### Remark: if $g$ is required to be continuous, then $f$ must be zero

If we add the hypothesis that $g$ is **continuous** on the whole domain, then th
e answer flips to **yes**:

Suppose $f\not\equiv 0$. As above, $g$ is holomorphic on $U$ (domain minus isola
ted zeros of $f$), and continuous everywhere. A classical consequence of **Morer
a's theorem** states: *if a function is continuous on a domain and holomorphic o
ff a discrete set of points, then it is holomorphic everywhere.* (The integral o
ver any triangle vanishes: triangles avoiding the singularities are immediate; t
riangles enclosing a singularity split into small triangles around it, whose int
egrals vanish by continuity and boundedness.) Applying this to $g$, we conclude
$g$ is holomorphic — contradicting the assumption. Hence $f\equiv 0$.

### Conclusion

Without any regularity assumption on $g$ beyond being a function, $f$ **need not
** be identically zero. The counterexample exploits the fact that at isolated ze
ros of $f$, the value of $g$ is invisible to the product $fg$. With the addition
al assumption that $g$ is continuous, Morera's theorem forces $g$ to be holomorp
hic, and so $f$ must vanish identically.
