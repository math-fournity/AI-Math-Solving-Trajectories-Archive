## Answer

**No**, $B$ does not have to be Lebesgue measurable in general. The answer depen
ds on whether the common measure is finite.

### The finite-measure case: $B$ must be measurable

Suppose $m(A)=m(C)<\infty$. Since $A\subseteq C$ and both are measurable with fi
nite measure,

$$m(C\setminus A)=m(C)-m(A)=0.$$

Now $A\subseteq B\subseteq C$ gives $B\setminus A\subseteq C\setminus A$. Since
$C\setminus A$ is a null set and Lebesgue measure is **complete** (every subset
of a null set is Lebesgue measurable), $B\setminus A$ is measurable. Therefore

$$B=A\cup(B\setminus A)$$

is a union of two measurable sets, hence measurable. So in the finite case, $B$
is necessarily measurable.

### The infinite-measure case: counterexample

Take
$$A=[0,\infty),\qquad C=\mathbb{R}.$$

Both are Lebesgue measurable, $A\subseteq C$, and $m(A)=m(C)=\infty$.

Let $V\subseteq(-\infty,0)$ be a non‑Lebesgue‑measurable set (obtained, for inst
ance, by translating a Vitali set into $(-\infty,0)$). Define

$$B=[0,\infty)\cup V.$$

Then $A=[0,\infty)\subseteq B\subseteq\mathbb{R}=C$, and $m(A)=m(C)=\infty$.

**$B$ is not measurable.** Suppose for contradiction that $B$ were Lebesgue meas
urable. Then

$$B\cap(-\infty,0)=V$$

would be measurable, being the intersection of two measurable sets. But $V$ was
chosen to be non‑measurable — contradiction. Hence $B$ is not Lebesgue measurabl
e.

### Conclusion

The hypothesis $A\subseteq B\subseteq C$ with $A,C$ measurable and $m(A)=m(C)$ d
oes **not** force $B$ to be measurable. It does force $B$ to be measurable when
the common measure is finite (by completeness of Lebesgue measure), but it fails
 in the infinite case, as the counterexample above shows.
