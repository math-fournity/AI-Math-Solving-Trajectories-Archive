## Solution

**Interpreting the problem.** $H(l,d,k)$ is the Hales–Jewett number: the least $
n$ such that every $k$-coloring of $[l]^n$ contains a monochromatic $d$-dimensio
nal combinatorial subspace. We compute $H(2,1,3)$.

**Step 1: Reduce combinatorial lines to comparable pairs.** For $l=2$, write $[2
]^n=\{0,1\}^n$. A $1$-dimensional combinatorial subspace (combinatorial line) is
 determined by a nonempty set of wildcard coordinates $W\subseteq[n]$ and fixed
values on $[n]\setminus W$. It consists of exactly two points:
$$p_0 = (\text{fixed values},\; 0 \text{ on } W), \qquad p_1 = (\text{fixed valu
es},\; 1 \text{ on } W).$$
Since $p_0$ and $p_1$ agree off $W$ and $p_0=0,\; p_1=1$ on $W$, we have $p_0 \l
e p_1$ componentwise and $p_0\neq p_1$. Conversely, any comparable pair $x\le y$
, $x\neq y$, in $\{0,1\}^n$ arises this way by taking $W=\{i: x_i\neq y_i\}$ (wh
ich is nonempty, and necessarily $x_i=0,y_i=1$ there). Thus:

> A monochromatic combinatorial line in $\{0,1\}^n$ is exactly a monochromatic *
*comparable pair** in the Boolean lattice $2^{[n]}$.

So $H(2,1,3)$ is the least $n$ such that every $3$-coloring of $2^{[n]}$ contain
s a monochromatic comparable pair, i.e. the least $n$ such that $2^{[n]}$ **cann
ot** be partitioned into $3$ antichains.

**Step 2: Apply Mirsky's theorem.** By Mirsky's theorem, the minimum number of a
ntichains needed to partition a finite poset equals the length of its longest ch
ain. The Boolean lattice $2^{[n]}$ has a longest chain
$$\varnothing \subset \{1\} \subset \{1,2\} \subset \cdots \subset [n],$$
of length $n+1$. Hence $2^{[n]}$ can be partitioned into $3$ antichains iff $n+1
\le 3$, i.e. $n\le 2$.

**Step 3: Lower bound $H(2,1,3)>2$.** For $n=2$, partition $2^{[2]}$ by rank int
o three antichains:
$$\{\varnothing\},\quad \bigl\{\{1\},\{2\}\bigr\},\quad \bigl\{\{1,2\}\bigr\}.$$
Color these three levels with three distinct colors. No two comparable elements
share a color, so there is no monochromatic line. Concretely in $\{0,1\}^2$: col
or $00$ red, $01$ and $10$ blue, $11$ green. The five lines $\{00,10\},\{00,01\}
,\{00,11\},\{01,11\},\{10,11\}$ are all bichromatic. Hence $H(2,1,3)>2$.

**Step 4: Upper bound $H(2,1,3)\le 3$.** For $n=3$, the chain
$$000 \;\le\; 100 \;\le\; 110 \;\le\; 111$$
has $4$ points. In any $3$-coloring, by the pigeonhole principle two of these fo
ur points receive the same color; they are comparable, hence form a monochromati
c combinatorial line. Therefore every $3$-coloring of $\{0,1\}^3$ contains a mon
ochromatic line, giving $H(2,1,3)\le 3$.

**Conclusion.** Combining the lower and upper bounds,

$$\boxed{H(2,1,3)=3}.$$
