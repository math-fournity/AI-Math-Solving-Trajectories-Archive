**Claim:** Yes, $T$ is necessarily a bounded operator.

**Key fact (Hilbert space norm duality).** In any Hilbert space $V$, for every $
x \in V$,
$$\|x\| = \sup_{\|w\| \le 1} |\langle x, w\rangle|.$$

*Proof of the fact.* The inequality $\le$ is Cauchy–Schwarz: $|\langle x, w\rang
le| \le \|x\|\|w\| \le \|x\|$ when $\|w\|\le 1$. For the reverse inequality, if
$x \neq 0$, take $w = x/\|x\|$ (which has $\|w\|=1$); then $|\langle x, w\rangle
| = \|x\|$. If $x=0$ both sides are $0$. $\square$

**Main argument.** Fix an arbitrary $v \in V$. The hypothesis gives, for all $w
\in V$,
$$|\langle T(v), w\rangle| \le C\|v\|\,\|w\|.$$
Restricting to unit vectors ($\|w\| = 1$),
$$|\langle T(v), w\rangle| \le C\|v\| \quad \text{for all } w \text{ with } \|w\
|=1.$$
Taking the supremum over all such $w$ and applying the key fact with $x = T(v)$:
$$\|T(v)\| = \sup_{\|w\|=1} |\langle T(v), w\rangle| \le C\|v\|.$$

This holds for every $v \in V$, so
$$\|T\| = \sup_{v \neq 0} \frac{\|T(v)\|}{\|v\|} \le C < \infty.$$

Hence $T$ is a bounded linear operator (with operator norm at most $C$). $\black
square$

**Remark.** The hypothesis is precisely the statement that the bilinear form $(v
,w) \mapsto \langle T(v), w\rangle$ is bounded. The argument shows that in a Hil
bert space, boundedness of this form is equivalent to boundedness of $T$ itself
— the reverse direction being immediate from Cauchy–Schwarz. The crucial ingredi
ent is the self-dual nature of Hilbert space: the norm of a vector is recoverabl
e from its inner products against unit vectors, so a uniform bound on all such i
nner products yields a bound on the vector's norm.
