## Proof

We need to count the number of ordered triples $(a, b, c)$ of non-negative integ
ers satisfying the constraint
$$a + b + c = n.$$

**Claim:** The number of such triples is
$$d_n(\{1, x, y\}) = \binom{n+2}{2} = \frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{2}.$$

**Proof by the Stars and Bars method.**

We count the number of ways to distribute $n$ identical units ("stars") into 3 d
istinct bins (corresponding to the exponents $a$, $b$, $c$ of the seeds $1$, $x$
, $y$ respectively), where each bin receives a non-negative number of units.

**Construction.** Represent any solution $(a, b, c)$ as a sequence of $n$ stars
($\star$) and $2$ bars ($|$), where:
- $a$ stars appear before the first bar,
- $b$ stars appear between the two bars,
- $c$ stars appear after the second bar.

For example, with $n = 4$ and $(a,b,c) = (1, 2, 1)$:
$$\star \mid \star\star \mid \star$$

**Bijection.** Every arrangement of $n$ stars and $2$ bars in a line corresponds
 to exactly one valid triple $(a, b, c)$ with $a+b+c=n$, and conversely every va
lid triple produces exactly one such arrangement. This is a bijection between:
- the set of solutions $\{(a,b,c) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^3 : a+b+c=n\}$, and
- the set of sequences of length $n+2$ consisting of $n$ stars and $2$ bars.

**Counting.** The number of such sequences is the number of ways to choose the p
ositions of the 2 bars (equivalently, the $n$ stars) among $n+2$ total positions
:
$$\binom{n+2}{2} = \binom{n+2}{n}.$$

**Verification for small cases:**
- $n=0$: only $(0,0,0)$, giving $1$ structure. Formula: $\binom{2}{2}=1$. ✓
- $n=1$: $(1,0,0),(0,1,0),(0,0,1)$, giving $3$ structures. Formula: $\binom{3}{2
}=3$. ✓
- $n=2$: $(2,0,0),(0,2,0),(0,0,2),(1,1,0),(1,0,1),(0,1,1)$, giving $6$ structure
s. Formula: $\binom{4}{2}=6$. ✓

**Conclusion.** The closed-form formula is:

$$\boxed{d_n(\{1, x, y\}) = \binom{n+2}{2} = \frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{2}}.$$
