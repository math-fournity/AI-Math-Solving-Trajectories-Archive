## Proof

**Claim:** For every integer $n \ge 2$ and every linear operator $T: \mathbb{R}^
n \to \mathbb{R}^n$, there exists a $T$-invariant subspace of dimension exactly
2.

---

### Case $n = 2$

$\mathbb{R}^2$ itself is a $T$-invariant subspace of dimension 2. ✓

---

### Case $n \ge 3$

Let $p(x) = \det(xI - T)$ be the characteristic polynomial (degree $n \ge 3$). O
ver $\mathbb{R}$, $p$ factors into linear factors and irreducible quadratic fact
ors.

#### Subcase A: $p$ has an irreducible quadratic factor $q(x)$

Write $q(x) = x^2 + bx + c$ (irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$, so $b^2 - 4c < 0$).

By the **primary decomposition theorem**, $V_q = \ker(q(T)^m)$ (where $m$ is the
 multiplicity of $q$ in $p$) is a nonzero $T$-invariant subspace. Let $S = T|_{V
_q}$; the minimal polynomial of $S$ is $q(x)^k$ for some $k \ge 1$.

Set $W = \ker(q(S))$. Then $W$ is $S$-invariant (since $S$ and $q(S)$ commute) a
nd $W \neq \{0\}$:
- If $k = 1$: $q(S) = 0$, so $W = V_q \neq \{0\}$.
- If $k \ge 2$: Since $q^k$ is the minimal polynomial, $q(S)^{k-1} \neq 0$, so t
here exists $v$ with $q(S)^{k-1}v \neq 0$. This vector lies in $\ker(q(S)) = W$,
 so $W \neq \{0\}$.

On $W$, we have $q(S|_W) = 0$, so the minimal polynomial of $S|_W$ divides $q$.
Since $W \neq \{0\}$ and $q$ is irreducible, the minimal polynomial is exactly $
q$. In particular, **$S|_W$ has no real eigenvalue** (any real eigenvalue $\alph
a$ would make $(x-\alpha)$ a factor of $q$, contradicting irreducibility).

Now pick any nonzero $w \in W$ and set $U = \operatorname{span}\{w, Sw\}$.

- **$U$ is $S$-invariant:** From $q(S)w = 0$ we get $S^2 w = -b\,Sw - c\,w \in U
$, so $S$ maps both generators of $U$ into $U$.
- **$\dim U = 2$:** If $Sw = \alpha w$ for some $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$, then $w
$ is an eigenvector of $S|_W$ with real eigenvalue $\alpha$, which is impossible
. Hence $w$ and $Sw$ are linearly independent.

Thus $U$ is a 2-dimensional $T$-invariant subspace. ✓

#### Subcase B: All eigenvalues of $T$ are real

**B1: $T$ has at least two distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$.**

Let $v_1, v_2$ be corresponding eigenvectors. Then $Tv_i = \lambda_i v_i \in \op
eratorname{span}\{v_1, v_2\}$, so $\operatorname{span}\{v_1, v_2\}$ is a 2-dimen
sional $T$-invariant subspace. ✓

**B2: $T$ has a single eigenvalue $\lambda$ (i.e., $T = \lambda I + N$ with $N$
nilpotent).**

- If $N = 0$: $T = \lambda I$, and every subspace is $T$-invariant. Since $n \ge
 3 \ge 2$, any 2-dimensional subspace works. ✓
- If $N \neq 0$: The Jordan canonical form of $T$ (over $\mathbb{R}$, valid sinc
e all eigenvalues are real) contains at least one Jordan block $J_k(\lambda)$ wi
th $k \ge 2$. Let $e_1, e_2$ be the first two basis vectors of this block. Then:
$$Te_1 = \lambda e_1, \qquad Te_2 = e_1 + \lambda e_2.$$
Both images lie in $\operatorname{span}\{e_1, e_2\}$, which is therefore a 2-dim
ensional $T$-invariant subspace. ✓

---

### Conclusion

In every case — $n = 2$ trivially, and $n \ge 3$ via either an irreducible quadr
atic factor (Subcase A) or all-real eigenvalues (Subcase B) — the operator $T$ a
dmits a 2-dimensional invariant subspace.

**Therefore, yes: every linear operator on $\mathbb{R}^n$ has an invariant subsp
ace of dimension 2 for all $n \ge 2$.** $\blacksquare$
