## Answer

**No, such a function does not exist.** The limit is always zero by the Riemann–
Lebesgue lemma, which applies to every $L^1$ function (the additional Riemann-in
tegrability hypothesis is redundant and does not escape the conclusion).

---

## Proof

We prove the **Riemann–Lebesgue lemma**: if $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, then
$$
\widehat{f}(\omega) := \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} f(x)\,e^{-i\omega x}\,dx \;\long
rightarrow\; 0 \quad\text{as } \omega\to\infty.
$$
Since the problem requires $f\in L^1$, this immediately forces the limit to be $
0$, hence it can never be non-zero.

### Step 1. Indicator of an interval

Let $f = \mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}$. Then
$$
\widehat{f}(\omega) = \int_a^b e^{-i\omega x}\,dx = \frac{e^{-i\omega b}-e^{-i\o
mega a}}{-i\omega},
$$
so $|\widehat{f}(\omega)| \le \frac{2}{|\omega|}\to 0$.

### Step 2. Step functions

A step function $g = \sum_{k=1}^n c_k\,\mathbf{1}_{[a_k,b_k]}$ has, by linearity
,
$$
|\widehat{g}(\omega)| \le \sum_{k=1}^n |c_k|\,\frac{2}{|\omega|}\to 0.
$$

### Step 3. Density of step functions in $L^1$

Step functions are dense in $L^1(\mathbb{R})$: for every $f\in L^1$ and every $\
varepsilon>0$ there exists a step function $g$ with $\|f-g\|_1 < \varepsilon$.

(This is standard: approximate $f$ by a compactly supported $L^1$ function, then
 by a bounded one, then by a simple function, then by a step function using regu
larity of Lebesgue measure.)

### Step 4. General $f\in L^1$

Given $\varepsilon>0$, pick a step function $g$ with $\|f-g\|_1<\varepsilon$. Th
en
$$
|\widehat{f}(\omega)| \le |\widehat{g}(\omega)| + |\widehat{f-g}(\omega)| \le |\
widehat{g}(\omega)| + \|f-g\|_1 < |\widehat{g}(\omega)| + \varepsilon.
$$
By Step 2, $\widehat{g}(\omega)\to 0$, so
$$
\limsup_{\omega\to\infty}|\widehat{f}(\omega)| \le \varepsilon.
$$
Since $\varepsilon>0$ is arbitrary,
$$
\lim_{\omega\to\infty}\widehat{f}(\omega)=0.
$$

### Conclusion

The hypothesis "$f$ is Riemann-integrable" only restricts the class of functions
 further (it is a subset of $L^1$ on bounded intervals, and combined with the gl
obal $L^1$ condition it changes nothing about the argument above). For **every**
 $f\in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ — Riemann-integrable or not — the Fourier transform vani
shes at infinity. Therefore the limit is necessarily $0$, and consequently **no*
* function that is both $L^1$ and Riemann-integrable can make the limit exist an
d be non-zero.

$$
\boxed{\text{No such function exists.}}
$$
