## Proof

The functional is $I[y]=\int_{a}^{b}(y'^2+y^4)\,dx$ with the Lagrangian $L(x,y,y
')=y'^2+y^4$.

**Step 1: Derive the Euler–Lagrange equation.**

The Euler–Lagrange equation is
$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial y}-\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}=0.$$

Computing the partial derivatives:
- $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial y}=4y^3$,
- $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial y'}=2y'$, so $\dfrac{d}{dx}\!\left(\dfrac{\partia
l L}{\partial y'}\right)=2y''$.

Hence the Euler–Lagrange equation is
$$4y^3-2y''=0,\qquad\text{ i.e., }\qquad y''=2y^3,$$
with boundary conditions $y(a)=0$ and $y(b)=0$.

**Step 2: Show $y\equiv 0$ is the only solution.**

Suppose $y$ is any $C^2$ solution of $y''=2y^3$ on $[a,b]$ satisfying $y(a)=y(b)
=0$. Multiply the equation by $y$ and integrate over $[a,b]$:

$$\int_a^b y\,y''\,dx = 2\int_a^b y^4\,dx.$$

Integrate the left-hand side by parts:

$$\int_a^b y\,y''\,dx = \Big[y\,y'\Big]_a^b - \int_a^b (y')^2\,dx.$$

The boundary term vanishes because $y(a)=0$ and $y(b)=0$:

$$\Big[y\,y'\Big]_a^b = y(b)\,y'(b)-y(a)\,y'(a)=0.$$

Therefore

$$-\int_a^b (y')^2\,dx = 2\int_a^b y^4\,dx.$$

The left-hand side is non-positive (since $(y')^2\geq 0$), while the right-hand
side is non-negative (since $y^4\geq 0$). The only way a non-positive number equ
als a non-negative number is if both are zero:

$$\int_a^b (y')^2\,dx = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \int_a^b y^4\,dx = 0.$$

From $\int_a^b (y')^2\,dx=0$ and the continuity of $y'$, it follows that $y'\equ
iv 0$ on $[a,b]$, so $y$ is constant on $[a,b]$. The boundary condition $y(a)=0$
 then forces $y\equiv 0$ on $[a,b]$.

**Conclusion.** The zero function is the only solution to the Euler–Lagrange equ
ation $y''=2y^3$ with boundary conditions $y(a)=0$ and $y(b)=0$.
