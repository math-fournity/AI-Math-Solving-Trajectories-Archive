Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether an "approach independent de
rivative" at a point implies continuity there.

**Answer: Yes, $f$ is necessarily continuous at $\mathbf{c}$.**

## Proof

**Definition.** A function $f: S \to \mathbb{R}^m$ (with $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}
^n$) has an *approach independent derivative* at $\mathbf{c} \in S$ if there exi
sts a linear map $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ such that

$$\lim_{\mathbf{h} \to \mathbf{0}} \frac{\left\| f(\mathbf{c} + \mathbf{h}) - f(
\mathbf{c}) - L(\mathbf{h}) \right\|}{\|\mathbf{h}\|} = 0,$$

where the limit is independent of the path along which $\mathbf{h}$ approaches $
\mathbf{0}$. (This is the Fréchet derivative; "approach independent" emphasizes
that the limit holds for *every* manner of approach, not merely along lines or s
elected curves.)

---

**Step 1: Rewrite the defining limit.**

By the definition above, for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ s
uch that whenever $0 < \|\mathbf{h}\| < \delta$ (and $\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{h} \in
S$),

$$\left\| f(\mathbf{c} + \mathbf{h}) - f(\mathbf{c}) - L(\mathbf{h}) \right\| \l
eq \varepsilon \|\mathbf{h}\|.$$

Equivalently, setting $\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{h}) = f(\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{h}) - f(\ma
thbf{c}) - L(\mathbf{h})$, we have $\|\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{h})\| / \|\mathbf{h}\|
\to 0$ as $\mathbf{h} \to \mathbf{0}$, so

$$f(\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{h}) - f(\mathbf{c}) = L(\mathbf{h}) + \mathbf{r}(\mathbf{
h}), \qquad \text{with } \frac{\|\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{h})\|}{\|\mathbf{h}\|} \to 0
. \tag{1}$$

**Step 2: Use continuity of the linear map $L$.**

Since $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is a linear map between *finite-dimensi
onal* normed spaces, $L$ is bounded: there exists a constant $M \geq 0$ such tha
t

$$\|L(\mathbf{h})\| \leq M\|\mathbf{h}\| \quad \text{for all } \mathbf{h} \in \m
athbb{R}^n. \tag{2}$$

In particular, $L(\mathbf{h}) \to \mathbf{0}$ as $\mathbf{h} \to \mathbf{0}$.

**Step 3: Combine to show $f(\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{h}) \to f(\mathbf{c})$.**

Taking norms in (1) and applying the triangle inequality:

$$\left\| f(\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{h}) - f(\mathbf{c}) \right\| \leq \|L(\mathbf{h})
\| + \|\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{h})\|.$$

Using (2) and the fact that $\|\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{h})\| = \|\mathbf{h}\| \cdot \
frac{\|\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{h})\|}{\|\mathbf{h}\|}$:

$$\left\| f(\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{h}) - f(\mathbf{c}) \right\| \leq M\|\mathbf{h}\|
 + \|\mathbf{h}\|\cdot\frac{\|\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{h})\|}{\|\mathbf{h}\|} = \|\mat
hbf{h}\|\!\left(M + \frac{\|\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{h})\|}{\|\mathbf{h}\|}\right).$$

As $\mathbf{h} \to \mathbf{0}$:
- $\|\mathbf{h}\| \to 0$,
- $\frac{\|\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{h})\|}{\|\mathbf{h}\|} \to 0$ (by the derivative d
efinition),
- $M$ is a fixed constant.

Therefore the right-hand side tends to $0 \cdot (M + 0) = 0$, giving

$$\lim_{\mathbf{h} \to \mathbf{0}} \left\| f(\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{h}) - f(\mathbf{
c}) \right\| = 0,$$

which is precisely the statement that $f$ is continuous at $\mathbf{c}$. $\black
square$

---

**Remark on why "approach independent" matters.** If one only required the exist
ence of directional derivatives (or even that all directional derivatives agree)
, continuity would *not* follow—the classic counterexample $f(x,y) = \frac{x^2 y
}{x^4+y^2}$ (with $f(0,0)=0$) has all directional derivatives equal to $0$ at th
e origin yet is discontinuous there (along $y=x^2$ the limit is $1/2$). The stre
ngth of the *approach independent* (Fréchet) condition—requiring the linear appr
oximation to hold uniformly over **all** paths—is exactly what forces the remain
der $\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{h})$ to be $o(\|\mathbf{h}\|)$, which in turn forces con
tinuity.
