# Solution

**Claim:** It is **not possible** for the Taylor series of a function to diverge
 at a point when the remainder term $R_n$ tends to $0$ at that point.

## Proof

Let $f$ be a function that is infinitely differentiable at a point $a$, and fix
a point $x$. By Taylor's theorem, for every $n \geq 0$ we have the exact identit
y

$$f(x) = T_n(x) + R_n(x),$$

where

$$T_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k$$

is the $n$-th Taylor polynomial (the $(n+1)$-st partial sum of the Taylor series
) and $R_n(x)$ is the remainder term.

The Taylor series of $f$ about $a$, evaluated at $x$, is

$$\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k.$$

By definition, this series **converges** if and only if the sequence of partial
sums $\{T_n(x)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}$ converges (to a finite limit), and it **diverge
s** if and only if $\{T_n(x)\}$ fails to converge.

Now suppose $R_n(x) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$. Rearranging the Taylor identity:

$$T_n(x) = f(x) - R_n(x).$$

Since $f(x)$ is a fixed finite constant (independent of $n$) and $R_n(x) \to 0$,
 we obtain

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} T_n(x) = f(x) - \lim_{n \to \infty} R_n(x) = f(x) - 0 = f(
x).$$

Therefore the sequence of partial sums $\{T_n(x)\}$ converges to $f(x)$, which m
eans the Taylor series **converges** (and in fact converges to $f(x)$).

Since convergence of $\{T_n(x)\}$ is equivalent to convergence of the Taylor ser
ies, and we have shown $\{T_n(x)\}$ converges whenever $R_n(x) \to 0$, it follow
s that **the Taylor series cannot diverge when $R_n(x) \to 0$**.

## Remark

The converse direction is the more interesting one and is where genuine subtlety
 arises:

- **$R_n \to 0 \Rightarrow$ Taylor series converges (to $f$):** Always true, as
proven above. This is an elementary consequence of the identity $T_n = f - R_n$.

- **Taylor series converges $\not\Rightarrow R_n \to 0$:** This can fail. The Ta
ylor series may converge, but to a value different from $f(x)$, in which case $R
_n(x) \to f(x) - S \neq 0$. The classic example is the Cauchy function

$$g(x) = \begin{cases} e^{-1/x^2}, & x \neq 0, \\ 0, & x = 0, \end{cases}$$

whose Taylor series about $0$ is identically $0$ (all derivatives at $0$ vanish)
, so it converges everywhere to $0$. But $g(x) > 0$ for $x \neq 0$, so $R_n(x) =
 g(x) - 0 = g(x) \not\to 0$ for any $x \neq 0$. Here the series converges but $R
_n \not\to 0$.

This asymmetry confirms that the condition $R_n \to 0$ is **strictly stronger**
than convergence of the Taylor series: it forces not merely convergence, but con
vergence *to the correct value* $f(x)$. In particular, $R_n \to 0$ makes diverge
nce of the Taylor series impossible.

## Conclusion

No. If $R_n(x) \to 0$, then $T_n(x) = f(x) - R_n(x) \to f(x)$, so the partial su
ms of the Taylor series converge, and the Taylor series cannot diverge.
