## Solution

The 1-form $\alpha = \frac{x\,dy - y\,dx}{x^2+y^2}$ is the standard angular form
 on $M = \mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$.

**Step 1: Express $\alpha$ in polar coordinates.**

Set $x = r\cos\phi$, $y = r\sin\phi$. Then
$$dx = \cos\phi\,dr - r\sin\phi\,d\phi, \qquad dy = \sin\phi\,dr + r\cos\phi\,d\
phi.$$

Computing the numerator:
$$x\,dy - y\,dx = r\cos\phi(\sin\phi\,dr + r\cos\phi\,d\phi) - r\sin\phi(\cos\ph
i\,dr - r\sin\phi\,d\phi)$$
$$= r^2(\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi)\,d\phi = r^2\,d\phi.$$

Since $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$, we obtain
$$\alpha = \frac{r^2\,d\phi}{r^2} = d\phi.$$

**Step 2: Pull back to the curve.**

The curve $\mathcal{C}$ is parametrized by $\theta \in \mathbb{S}^1 \cong [0,2\p
i]$ via
$$\gamma(\theta) = \bigl(f(e^{i\theta})\cos\theta,\; f(e^{i\theta})\sin\theta\bi
gr).$$

In polar coordinates this is $r(\theta) = f(e^{i\theta})$ and $\phi(\theta) = \t
heta$. Since $f:\mathbb{S}^1 \to (0,1)$ is smooth and strictly positive, $r(\the
ta) > 0$ for all $\theta$, so the curve never passes through the origin and wind
s once counterclockwise around it as $\theta$ runs from $0$ to $2\pi$.

Pulling back $\alpha = d\phi$ along $j\circ\gamma$:
$$j^*\alpha\big|_\gamma = d\phi = d\theta.$$

**Step 3: Compute the integral.**

$$\int_\mathcal{C} j^*\alpha = \int_0^{2\pi} d\theta = 2\pi.$$

**Remark.** The form $\alpha$ is closed ($d\alpha = 0$) but not exact on $M$, an
d its integral over any closed curve equals $2\pi$ times the winding number of t
hat curve around the origin. Since $f > 0$, the curve $\mathcal{C}$ has winding
number $+1$, confirming the result independent of the specific choice of $f$.

$$\boxed{\int_\mathcal{C} j^*\alpha = 2\pi}$$
