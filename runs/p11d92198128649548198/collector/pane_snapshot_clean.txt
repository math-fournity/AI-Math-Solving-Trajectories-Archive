## Solution

**Yes**, such an automorphism exists.

**Proof.**

View $(\mathbb{R}, +)$ as a vector space over $\mathbb{Q}$. This vector space is
 infinite-dimensional, since $1$ and $\sqrt{2}$ are $\mathbb{Q}$-linearly indepe
ndent. By Zorn's Lemma (Axiom of Choice), every vector space admits a basis, so
let $B$ be a $\mathbb{Q}$-basis of $\mathbb{R}$ with $1, \sqrt{2} \in B$.

Define $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ by specifying its action on the basis $B$
:

- $f(\sqrt{2}) = -\sqrt{2}$,
- $f(b) = b$ for every $b \in B \setminus \{\sqrt{2}\}$,

and extend $\mathbb{Q}$-linearly to all of $\mathbb{R}$.

**$f$ is a group automorphism.** The set $B' := \bigl(B \setminus \{\sqrt{2}\}\b
igr) \cup \{-\sqrt{2}\}$ is again a $\mathbb{Q}$-basis of $\mathbb{R}$, since re
placing a basis vector by a nonzero scalar multiple of itself preserves linear i
ndependence and spanning. Thus $f$ sends a basis to a basis, so it is a $\mathbb
{Q}$-linear bijection — equivalently, a group automorphism of $(\mathbb{R}, +)$.

**$f$ is not the identity.** We have $f(\sqrt{2}) = -\sqrt{2} \neq \sqrt{2}$.

**$f$ fixes an infinite set.** Since $f(1) = 1$, for every $q \in \mathbb{Q}$:
$$f(q) = f(q \cdot 1) = q \cdot f(1) = q \cdot 1 = q.$$
Hence $\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \operatorname{Fix}(f)$, and $\mathbb{Q}$ is infinite
.

Therefore, there exists a group automorphism of $(\mathbb{R}, +)$ that fixes an
infinite subset of $\mathbb{R}$ (namely $\mathbb{Q}$) but is not the identity. $
\blacksquare$
