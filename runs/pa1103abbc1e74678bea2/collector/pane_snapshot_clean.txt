## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. If $R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{c}]$ is a UFD (with $c$ a non-square in
teger), then $R$ is a PID.

The key insight is that the UFD hypothesis forces $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{c}]$ to be t
he full ring of integers of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{c})$, hence a Dedekind domain, whe

### Step 1: The UFD hypothesis forces $c$ to be squarefree.

Write $c = d k^2$ where $d$ is squarefree and $k \geq 1$. Then $\sqrt{c} = k\sqr
t{d}$, so $R = \mathbb{Z}[k\sqrt{d}]$.

If $k > 1$, the element $\sqrt{d}$ is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$ (root of $x^2 -
 d = 0$), hence integral over $R$. But $\sqrt{d} \notin R$, since elements of $R
$ have the form $a + bk\sqrt{d}$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$, and $\sqrt{d} = \fr
ac{1}{k}(k\sqrt{d})$ is not of this form when $k > 1$. Thus $R$ is not integrall
y closed.

Every UFD is integrally closed (this is a standard theorem: in a UFD, any elemen
t of the fraction field integral over $R$ must lie in $R$, by clearing denominat
ors and using unique factorization to show the denominator is a unit). Therefore
, if $R$ is a UFD, we must have $k = 1$, i.e., **$c$ is squarefree**.

### Step 2: The UFD hypothesis forces $c \not\equiv 1 \pmod{4}$.

Assume $c$ is squarefree and $c \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$. Consider the element $\alpha
 = \frac{1+\sqrt{c}}{2}$. It satisfies:
$$\alpha^2 - \alpha + \frac{1-c}{4} = 0.$$
Since $c \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, the value $\frac{1-c}{4}$ is an integer, so $\alpha
$ is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$, hence integral over $R$.

But $\alpha \notin R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{c}]$, since elements of $R$ are $a + b\s
qrt{c}$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$, and $\alpha = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\sqrt
{c}$ has non-integer coefficients. Thus $R$ is not integrally closed, contradict
ing the UFD hypothesis.

Therefore, if $R$ is a UFD, **$c \not\equiv 1 \pmod{4}$**.

### Step 3: When $c$ is squarefree and $c \not\equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, $R$ is the rin
g of integers of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{c})$.

By the standard classification of rings of integers in quadratic number fields:
- If $c$ is squarefree and $c \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, the ring of integers is $\math
bb{Z}\!\left[\frac{1+\sqrt{c}}{2}\right]$.
- If $c$ is squarefree and $c \not\equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, the ring of integers is $\
mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{c}]$.

So under our hypotheses, $R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{c}]$ is the full ring of integers
 $\mathcal{O}_K$ of $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{c})$.

### Step 4: $R$ is a Dedekind domain.

The ring of integers of any number field is a Dedekind domain: it is Noetherian
(finitely generated as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module), integrally closed (by definition)
, and every nonzero prime ideal is maximal (Krull dimension 1, since $\mathcal{O
}_K / P$ is a finite integral domain, hence a field, for any nonzero prime $P$).

### Step 5: In a Dedekind domain, UFD $\Rightarrow$ PID.

In a Dedekind domain, every nonzero ideal factors uniquely as a product of nonze
ro prime ideals. It suffices to show every nonzero prime ideal is principal.

Let $P$ be a nonzero prime ideal of $R$. Since $R$ is a UFD, every nonzero eleme
nt of $P$ factors into irreducibles (which are prime in a UFD). Pick any nonzero

s prime, some $p_i \in P$. Then $(p_i) \subseteq P$.

Now $(p_i)$ is a nonzero prime ideal (since $p_i$ is a prime element). In a Dede
kind domain, every nonzero prime ideal is maximal, so $(p_i) = P$. Thus $P$ is p
rincipal.

Since every prime ideal is principal and every ideal is a product of prime ideal
s, every ideal is principal. Therefore $R$ is a PID. $\blacksquare$

### Conclusion

Combining Steps 1–5: If $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{c}]$ is a UFD, then $c$ is squarefree
and $c \not\equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, making $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{c}]$ the ring of integer
s of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{c})$, hence a Dedekind domain. In a Dedekind domain, UFD
implies PID. Therefore **yes**, $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{c}]$ being a UFD necessarily i
