**Answer: Yes, it is possible.**

## Construction

Define $h: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$ by
$$
h(x,y,z) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{xy}{x^2+y^2} & \text{if } (x,y) \neq (0,0), \\[6
pt] 0 & \text{if } (x,y) = (0,0). \end{cases}
$$
(Note $h$ does not depend on $z$.)

Define $k: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^3$ by $k(t) = (t,\, t,\, 0)$.

## $h$ has all partial derivatives at the origin

Since $h(0,0,z) = 0$ for every $z$, and $h(t,0,0) = 0$, $h(0,t,0) = 0$ for every
 $t$:

$$
\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}(0,0,0) = \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{h(t,0,0)-h(0,0,0)}{t}
= \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{0}{t} = 0,
$$

$$
\frac{\partial h}{\partial y}(0,0,0) = \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{h(0,t,0)-h(0,0,0)}{t}
= \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{0}{t} = 0,
$$

$$
\frac{\partial h}{\partial z}(0,0,0) = \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{h(0,0,t)-h(0,0,0)}{t}
= \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{0}{t} = 0.
$$

All three partial derivatives exist at the origin (and equal $0$). ✓

## $k$ has all partial derivatives at the origin

The domain of $k$ is $\mathbb{R}$, so "all partial derivatives at the origin" me
ans the derivative $k'(0)$ exists. Since $k(t)=(t,t,0)$ is a linear (hence smoot
h) map, $k'(0) = (1,1,0)$ exists. ✓

## $h \circ k$ does not have partial derivatives at the origin

We have $k(0) = (0,0,0)$, so $(h\circ k)(0) = h(0,0,0) = 0$. For $t \neq 0$:
$$
(h \circ k)(t) = h(t,t,0) = \frac{t\cdot t}{t^2+t^2} = \frac{t^2}{2t^2} = \frac{
1}{2}.
$$

Therefore
$$
(h\circ k)(t) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{2} & t \neq 0, \\[4pt] 0 & t = 0. \end{c
ases}
$$

This function is **not even continuous** at $t=0$ (since $\lim_{t\to 0}(h\circ k
)(t) = 1/2 \neq 0 = (h\circ k)(0)$), and consequently **not differentiable** at
$t=0$. In particular, $(h\circ k)$ does not have a (partial) derivative at the o
rigin. ✓

## Why the chain rule does not apply

The classical chain rule requires $h$ to be *differentiable* at $k(0)$, not mere
ly to have partial derivatives. Existence of all partial derivatives does not im
ply differentiability (nor even continuity, as this example shows: $h$ is discon
tinuous at the origin since $h(t,t,0)=1/2 \not\to 0 = h(0,0,0)$). By choosing th
e curve $k(t)=(t,t,0)$ to approach the origin along the "bad" direction $y=x$ wh
ere $h$ is discontinuous, the composition inherits a discontinuity and hence fai
ls to be differentiable.

## Conclusion

Such functions $h$ and $k$ exist. The explicit example above shows $h$ and $k$ b
oth possess all partial derivatives at the origin, yet $h\circ k$ fails to have
a derivative there.
