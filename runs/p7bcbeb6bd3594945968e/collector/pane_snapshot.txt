\infty)$). So $\phi_n$ must transition from $1$ to $0$, and we need $|\phi_n'|
_{L^2}^2 \to 0$. Spreading the transition over an interval of length $T_n \to
\infty$ makes $\phi_n' \sim 1/T_n$, giving $I_n \sim 1/T_n \to 0$.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Proof

Construction. Choose $f_n(r) = r^{1/n}$, so $\psi_n(r) = \psi(r^{1/n})$.

Verification of conditions.

  1. Smoothness: $r \mapsto r^{1/n}$ is $C^\infty$ on $(0,\infty)$, and $\psi
  \in C^\infty((0,\infty))$, so $\psi_n \in C^\infty((0,\infty);[0,1])$.
  2. Supported away from zero: Since $\psi$ is supported away from zero, there
  exists $\varepsilon > 0$ with $\psi(s) = 0$ for $s \in (0,\varepsilon]$. For
  $r \leq \varepsilon^n$, we have $r^{1/n} \leq \varepsilon$, so $\psi_n(r) =
  0$. Thus $\psi_n$ is supported on $[\varepsilon^n, \infty) \subset (0,
  \infty)$. ✓
  3. Identically one near $[1,\infty)$: Since $\psi \equiv 1$ near $[1,\infty)$,
  there exists $\delta \in (0,1]$ with $\psi(s) = 1$ for $s \geq \delta$. For $r
  \geq 1$, $r^{1/n} \geq 1 \geq \delta$, so $\psi_n(r) = 1$ for all $r \geq 1$.
  ✓

Computation of the integral. We have $f_n'(r) = \frac{1}{n} r^{1/n - 1}$, so:

$$\psi_n'(r) = \psi'(r^{1/n}) \cdot \frac{1}{n}, r^{1/n - 1}.$$

Therefore:

$$I_n = \int_0^\infty \left(\psi'(r^{1/n})\right)^2 \frac{1}{n^2}, r^{2/n - 2}
\cdot r, dr = \frac{1}{n^2} \int_0^\infty \left(\psi'(r^{1/n})\right)^2 r^{2/n -
1}, dr.$$

Substitution: Let $s = r^{1/n}$, so $r = s^n$, $dr = n, s^{n-1}, ds$, and $r^{2/
n-1} = s^{2 - n}$. Then:

$$I_n = \frac{1}{n^2} \int_0^\infty (\psi'(s))^2, s^{2-n} \cdot n, s^{n-1}, ds =
\frac{1}{n} \int_0^\infty (\psi'(s))^2, s, ds.$$

Finiteness of the constant. Since $\psi$ is constant ($=0$) near $0$ and
constant ($=1$) near $[1,\infty)$, the derivative $\psi'$ is smooth and
supported on a compact subset $K \subset [\varepsilon, 1] \subset (0,\infty)$.
Hence:

$$C := \int_0^\infty (\psi'(s))^2, s, ds = \int_K (\psi'(s))^2, s, ds < \infty.$
$

Conclusion. We obtain:

$$I_n = \frac{C}{n} \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} 0.$$

Thus the answer is yes — such a sequence exists, with the explicit choice
$f_n(r) = r^{1/n}$.

Remark on why this works while $\psi(2^n r)$ does not. The scaling $f_n(r) = 2^n
r$ compresses the transition region geometrically (by a constant factor), which
scales the derivative and the measure in exactly compensating ways, leaving
$I_n$ invariant. The power-law choice $f_n(r) = r^{1/n}$ instead spreads the
transition region across $[\varepsilon^n, 1]$, which grows exponentially in $n$-
space (equivalently, linearly in $t = -\ln r$ space with length $\sim n$). The
derivative $\psi_n' \sim 1/n$ over a region of $t$-length $\sim n$, giving $I_n
\sim n \cdot (1/n)^2 = 1/n \to 0$.
