## Proof

We work in the framework of continuous semimartingales, where $\langle \cdot, \c
dot \rangle$ denotes the quadratic covariation (sharp bracket) process.

### Setup and notation

Let $r_t$ be a (real-valued, adapted, integrable) stochastic process, and consid
er the process
$$X_t := \int_0^t r_s\, ds.$$
Its differential is $dX_t = r_t\, dt$. The expression $\langle r_t\, dt,\, r_t\,
 dt\rangle$ is shorthand for the quadratic variation $\langle X, X\rangle_t$.

### Key fact: finite variation ⟹ zero quadratic variation

A classical result in stochastic calculus states:

> **Theorem.** If $A = (A_t)_{t\ge 0}$ is a continuous adapted process of finite
 variation on compact intervals, then its quadratic variation is identically zer
o: $\langle A, A\rangle_t = 0$ for all $t \ge 0$.

*Proof of the theorem (sketch).* For any partition $\pi = \{0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdo
ts < t_n = t\}$ with mesh $\|\pi\| \to 0$,
$$\sum_{i} (A_{t_{i+1}} - A_{t_i})^2 \le \Big(\sup_i |A_{t_{i+1}} - A_{t_i}|\Big
) \sum_i |A_{t_{i+1}} - A_{t_i}| \le \Big(\sup_i |A_{t_{i+1}} - A_{t_i}|\Big) \c
dot V_t(A).$$
Since $A$ is continuous, $\sup_i |A_{t_{i+1}} - A_{t_i}| \to 0$ as $\|\pi\|\to 0
$, while $V_t(A) < \infty$ by finite variation. Hence the sum converges to $0$,
giving $\langle A, A\rangle_t = 0$. $\square$

### Applying it to $X_t = \int_0^t r_s\, ds$

The process $X_t = \int_0^t r_s\, ds$ is an integral with respect to Lebesgue me
asure $ds$. Provided $r$ is (pathwise) integrable on compacts, $X$ is a continuo
us process of finite variation, with total variation
$$V_t(X) \le \int_0^t |r_s|\, ds < \infty.$$
By the theorem above,
$$\boxed{\langle X, X\rangle_t = \langle r_t\, dt,\, r_t\, dt\rangle = 0.}$$

### Does $\langle dt, dt\rangle = 0$ *imply* $\langle r_t\, dt,\, r_t\, dt\rangl
e = 0$?

**No — not as a matter of implication.** The conclusion $\langle r_t\, dt,\, r_t
\, dt\rangle = 0$ is **true**, but it does **not** follow from $\langle dt, dt\r
angle = 0$ by any algebraic "factoring out" rule. The reasons are distinct:

1. **$\langle dt, dt\rangle = 0$ is true** because $t \mapsto t$ is a finite-var
iation (deterministic) process.

2. **$\langle r_t\, dt,\, r_t\, dt\rangle = 0$ is true** because $X_t = \int_0^t
 r_s\, ds$ is *also* a finite-variation process — the integrand $r_t$ being stoc
hastic does not change the fact that integration is against $ds$ (Lebesgue measu
re), which produces a finite-variation path.

3. **The would-be "factoring" identity $\langle r_t\, dt,\, r_t\, dt\rangle = r_
t^2\,\langle dt, dt\rangle$ is not a valid rule of the quadratic covariation cal
culus.** That identity *is* valid for stochastic integrals against a *martingale
* $M$:
$$\text{if } dX_t = H_t\, dM_t, \text{ then } \langle X, X\rangle_t = \int_0^t H
_s^2\, d\langle M, M\rangle_s.$$
But $dt$ is **not** a local martingale; it is a finite-variation differential. T
he martingale-integral formula simply does not apply, so one cannot "pull $r_t^2
$ out" and then invoke $\langle dt, dt\rangle = 0$.

4. **A counterexample to the implication as a logical principle.** Consider repl
acing $dt$ by a martingale differential $dM_t$ (with $\langle M, M\rangle \ne 0$
) and $r_t$ by a non-trivial adapted process. Then $\langle M, M\rangle \ne 0$ d
oes not by itself determine $\langle \int r\, dM, \int r\, dM\rangle$; the latte
r equals $\int r^2\, d\langle M, M\rangle$, which depends on the *integrand*, no
t just the integrator's bracket. This shows that the value of $\langle \text{int
egrator}, \text{integrator}\rangle$ alone never determines $\langle \text{integr
and}\cdot\text{integrator}, \text{integrand}\cdot\text{integrator}\rangle$ in ge
neral — the conclusion here is a coincidence of *both* factors being finite-vari
ation, not a consequence of $\langle dt,dt\rangle=0$ alone.

### Conclusion

$$\langle r_t\, dt,\, r_t\, dt\rangle = 0.$$

The result holds because $r_t\, dt$ is the differential of the finite-variation
process $\int_0^t r_s\, ds$, and every continuous finite-variation process has z
ero quadratic variation. The premise $\langle dt, dt\rangle = 0$ is *consistent*
 with this conclusion but does **not** imply it: there is no general rule allowi
ng one to extract $r_t^2$ from a quadratic covariation against a non-martingale
differential. The two zeros arise from the *same underlying reason* (finite vari
ation), not from one implying the other.
