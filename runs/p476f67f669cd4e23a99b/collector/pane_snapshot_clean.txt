Looking at this problem, I need to count triangulations of a convex $n$-gon wher
e every triangle has at least one boundary edge.

**Setting up the dual tree characterization.** A triangulation of a convex $n$-g
on produces $n-2$ triangles and $n-3$ diagonals. The dual graph has one node per
 triangle, with two nodes adjacent when the corresponding triangles share a diag
onal. This dual is always a tree on $n-2$ nodes.

A triangle with $k$ boundary edges has $3-k$ diagonal sides, so its degree in th
e dual tree is $3-k$. The condition "every triangle has at least one boundary ed
ge" excludes triangles with 0 boundary edges (degree 3 in the dual). So the dual
 tree must have maximum degree $\leq 2$, meaning it must be a **path**.

I'll count triangulations whose dual tree is a path (called *zigzag triangulatio
ns*).

**Defining an auxiliary count.** Let $g(n)$ = number of zigzag triangulations of
 a convex $n$-gon where the triangle containing a specific boundary edge (say ed
ge $(1,n)$) is a **leaf** of the dual path.

A leaf has degree 1, meaning 2 boundary edges. The triangle at edge $(1,n)$ is $
(1,k,n)$; it has 2 boundary edges only if $k=2$ (making $(1,2)$ a boundary edge)
 or $k=n-1$ (making $(n-1,n)$ a boundary edge).

**Recurrence for $g(n)$.**
- If $k=2$: the ear $(1,2,n)$ is removed, leaving an $(n-1)$-gon $\{2,3,\ldots,n
\}$. The ear connects in the dual to the triangle containing edge $(2,n)$. For t
he overall dual to remain a path, that triangle must be a leaf of the $(n-1)$-go
n's dual — giving $g(n-1)$ possibilities.
- If $k=n-1$: by symmetry, also $g(n-1)$.

These cases are disjoint for $n \geq 4$, so $g(n) = 2\,g(n-1)$ with $g(3)=1$, gi
ving $g(n) = 2^{n-3}$.

**From $g(n)$ to $f(n)$.** Let $f(n)$ be the total number of zigzag triangulatio
ns. Summing $g(n)$ over all $n$ boundary edges gives $n \cdot g(n)$. Each zigzag
 triangulation has exactly **2 ears** (the two endpoints of the dual path). Each
 ear has exactly 2 boundary edges, so it is a leaf for exactly 2 edges. Since ea
ch boundary edge belongs to a unique triangle, the 4 edges associated with the 2
 ears are distinct. Thus each triangulation is counted exactly 4 times:

$$n \cdot g(n) = 4 \cdot f(n) \implies f(n) = \frac{n \cdot 2^{n-3}}{4} = n \cdo
t 2^{n-5}.$$

**Verification.**
- $n=4$: $4 \cdot 2^{-1} = 2$. Both triangulations of a quadrilateral have all t
riangles as ears. ✓
- $n=5$: $5 \cdot 2^0 = 5 = C_3$. Every triangulation of a pentagon has a dual t
hat is a path on 3 nodes (the only tree on 3 nodes). ✓
- $n=6$: $6 \cdot 2^1 = 12$. Total triangulations $C_4=14$; exactly 2 have a sta
r dual (central triangles $\{1,3,5\}$ and $\{2,4,6\}$), so $14-2=12$. ✓
- $n=7$: $7 \cdot 2^2 = 28$. Total $C_5=42$; there are 7 triangles with 0 bounda
ry edges (3 non-consecutive vertices from 7), each forcing sub-polygons of sizes
 $\{3,3,4\}$, contributing $1\cdot1\cdot2=2$ triangulations each, giving $42 - 7
\cdot2 = 28$. ✓

**Answer:** The number of ways is $\boxed{n \cdot 2^{n-5}}$.
