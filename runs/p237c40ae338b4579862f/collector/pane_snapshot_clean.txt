# Landen's Transformation for Computing $F(k,\phi)$

## Setup and notation

The incomplete elliptic integral of the first kind is

$$F(k,\phi)=\int_{0}^{\phi}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}},\qquad 0
\le k\le 1.$$

The complete integral is $K(k)=F(k,\pi/2)$. Landen's transformation reduces the
modulus $k$ to a smaller one $k_1<k$, at the cost of a rescaled amplitude, so th
at iterating drives $k\to 0$, where the integrand becomes $1$ and the integral i
s trivial.

## The descending (Gauss) Landen transformation

Define the **descending Landen modulus**

$$k_1=\frac{1-k'}{1+k'},\qquad k':=\sqrt{1-k^{2}},$$

and the **descending amplitude**

$$\tan\phi_1=\frac{\sin 2\phi}{k'+\cos 2\phi}\;=\;\frac{1-k_1}{1+k_1}\tan\phi\;\
cdot\;\frac{1}{\;1+\frac{1-k_1}{1+k_1}\tan^{2}\phi\;}\;\;(\text{equivalent forms
}).$$

A cleaner statement uses the half-angle substitution. With

$$\tan\phi=\frac{\sin 2\phi_{1}}{k_1+\cos 2\phi_{1}}$$

one obtains the **Landen identity**

$$\boxed{\;F(k,\phi)=(1+k_1)\,F(k_1,\phi_1)\;}$$

where $k_1=(1-k')/(1+k')<k$ and $\phi_1$ is the rescaled amplitude above.

*Derivation sketch.* Substitute $\sin\theta=\dfrac{(1+k_1)\sin\theta_1}{1+k_1\si
n^{2}\theta_1}$ (the Landen angle map). A direct differentiation gives

$$d\theta=\frac{(1+k_1)\cos\theta_1}{1+k_1\sin^{2}\theta_1}\,d\theta_1,$$

and the identity $1-k^{2}\sin^{2}\theta=\left(\dfrac{1-k_1\sin^{2}\theta_1}{1+k_
1\sin^{2}\theta_1}\right)^{2}$ turns the square root into a perfect square, yiel
ding exactly $(1+k_1)\,d\theta_1/\sqrt{1-k_1^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_1}$. The amplitud
e correspondence $\theta=0\Rightarrow\theta_1=0$, $\theta=\phi\Rightarrow\theta_
1=\phi_1$ fixes the limits. ∎

## Iteration (the general method)

Set $k^{(0)}=k$, $\phi^{(0)}=\phi$, and iterate

$$k^{(n+1)}=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-(k^{(n)})^{2}}}{1+\sqrt{1-(k^{(n)})^{2}}},\qquad
\tan\phi^{(n+1)}=\frac{\sin 2\phi^{(n)}}{\sqrt{1-(k^{(n)})^{2}}+\cos 2\phi^{(n)}
}.$$

Each step satisfies

$$F\!\bigl(k^{(n)},\phi^{(n)}\bigr)=\bigl(1+k^{(n+1)}\bigr)\,F\!\bigl(k^{(n+1)},
\phi^{(n+1)}\bigr).$$

Telescoping,

$$F(k,\phi)=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\bigl(1+k^{(n)}\bigr)\;\cdot\;F\!\bigl(k^{(N)},\phi^{
(N)}\bigr).$$

**Quadratic convergence of the modulus.** Since $k^{(n+1)}\sim \tfrac14(k^{(n)})
^{2}$ for small $k^{(n)}$, the modulus vanishes quadratically: after $N\approx \
log_2\log_2(1/k)$ steps, $k^{(N)}$ is below any prescribed tolerance $\varepsilo
n$.

**Termination.** Once $k^{(N)}<\varepsilon$, the integrand is $1+O(\varepsilon)$
, so

$$F\!\bigl(k^{(N)},\phi^{(N)}\bigr)=\phi^{(N)}+O(\varepsilon),$$

and therefore

$$\boxed{\;F(k,\phi)\;\approx\;\phi^{(N)}\prod_{n=1}^{N}\bigl(1+k^{(n)}\bigr)\;}
$$

to accuracy $O(\varepsilon)$. This is the general Landen method.

## Specializations

**Complete integral $K(k)$.** With $\phi=\pi/2$, the amplitude stays $\pi/2$ at
every step (since $\tan\phi^{(n+1)}=\sin\pi/(\cdots)=\infty$), giving the classi
cal descending relation

$$K(k)=(1+k_1)\,K(k_1)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(1+k^{(n)}\bigr)\cdot\frac{\pi}{
2},$$

since $F(0,\pi/2)=\pi/2$.

**Ascending form.** The inverse $k=(2\sqrt{k_1})/(1+k_1)$ gives $F(k_1,\phi_1)=\
frac{1}{1+k}F(k,\phi)$, useful when one wants to *raise* the modulus (e.g. towar
d $k\to1$, where $K\to\infty$ logarithmically).

**Arithmetic-geometric mean (AGM) form.** Writing $a_0=1$, $b_0=k'$, and $a_{n+1
}=(a_n+b_n)/2$, $b_{n+1}=\sqrt{a_nb_n}$, one has $k^{(n)}=a_n/b_n-1$ and

$$K(k)=\frac{\pi}{2\,\operatorname{AGM}(1,k')},$$

so the Landen product is equivalently evaluated by the quadratically-convergent
AGM iteration — the form used in practice (and in `scipy.special.ellipk`).

## Summary of the algorithm

1. **Initialize** $k^{(0)}=k$, $\phi^{(0)}=\phi$, $P=1$.
2. **Iterate** while $k^{(n)}>\varepsilon$:
   - $k'\leftarrow\sqrt{1-(k^{(n)})^{2}}$
   - $k^{(n+1)}\leftarrow(1-k')/(1+k')$
   - $\tan\phi^{(n+1)}\leftarrow\sin 2\phi^{(n)}/(k'+\cos 2\phi^{(n)})$
   - $P\leftarrow P\cdot(1+k^{(n+1)})$
3. **Terminate**: $F(k,\phi)\approx P\cdot\phi^{(N)}$.

The method converges quadratically in the modulus, requires only square roots an
d arithmetic per step, and reduces the elliptic integral to a plain angle after
finitely many (logarithmically few) transformations.
