## Proof

**Yes**, such a function exists for every $n \geq 1$.

### Construction

For each permutation $\sigma \in S_n$, define $\phi_\sigma : \mathbb{R}^n \to \m
athbb{R}$ by
$$\phi_\sigma(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{k=1}^{n-1} \frac{x_{\sigma(k)}}{x_{\sigm
a(k)} + x_{\sigma(k+1)}}$$
when all denominators $x_{\sigma(k)} + x_{\sigma(k+1)} \neq 0$, and $\phi_\sigma
 = 0$ otherwise. (For $n=1$, the empty product is $1$.)

Choose **distinct** real numbers $\{c_\sigma : \sigma \in S_n\}$ and set
$$f(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} c_\sigma\, \phi_\sigma(x_1, \dots,
x_n).$$

### Key Lemma

For any $\sigma, \tau \in S_n$, the iterated limit of $\phi_\sigma$ taken in ord
er $\tau$ satisfies:
$$\lim_{x_{\tau(1)}\to 0}\lim_{x_{\tau(2)}\to 0}\cdots\lim_{x_{\tau(n)}\to 0} \p
hi_\sigma = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \sigma = \tau, \\ 0 & \text{if } \sigma
 \neq \tau. \end{cases}$$

### Proof of Key Lemma

The iterated limit in order $\tau$ sends variables to zero from the **inside out
**: $x_{\tau(n)}$ first, then $x_{\tau(n-1)}$, …, $x_{\tau(1)}$ last. Thus $x_i$
 is sent to zero *before* $x_j$ iff $\tau^{-1}(i) > \tau^{-1}(j)$.

Each factor $\frac{x_{\sigma(k)}}{x_{\sigma(k)} + x_{\sigma(k+1)}}$ **resolves**
 (becomes $0$ or $1$) when the first of $x_{\sigma(k)}, x_{\sigma(k+1)}$ is sent
 to zero:

- **If $x_{\sigma(k+1)}$ is sent first** (i.e., $\tau^{-1}(\sigma(k+1)) > \tau^{
-1}(\sigma(k))$): the factor tends to $\frac{x_{\sigma(k)}}{x_{\sigma(k)}} = 1$.
- **If $x_{\sigma(k)}$ is sent first** (i.e., $\tau^{-1}(\sigma(k)) > \tau^{-1}(
\sigma(k+1))$): the factor tends to $\frac{0}{x_{\sigma(k+1)}} = 0$.

Once any factor resolves to $0$, the entire product becomes $0$ and **remains**
$0$ through all subsequent limits. The product equals $1$ iff **every** factor r
esolves to $1$, which requires
$$\tau^{-1}(\sigma(k+1)) > \tau^{-1}(\sigma(k)) \quad \text{for all } k = 1, \do
ts, n-1,$$
i.e., $\tau^{-1}(\sigma(1)) < \tau^{-1}(\sigma(2)) < \cdots < \tau^{-1}(\sigma(n
))$, which means $\tau^{-1} \circ \sigma = \mathrm{id}$, i.e., $\tau = \sigma$.
∎

### Intermediate Limits Exist

At each stage of the iterated limit, the unresolved factors are of the form $\fr
ac{x_a}{x_a + x_b}$ with $x_a, x_b \neq 0$ (both still active). When sending $x_
c \to 0$:

- **Factors involving $x_c$** resolve to $0$ or $1$ as above. The singularity oc
curs at $x_c = -x_a$ or $x_c = -x_b$, but since $x_a, x_b \neq 0$, this is at $|
x_c| = |x_a| \neq 0$ or $|x_c| = |x_b| \neq 0$ — **away from** $0$. So the limit
 as $x_c \to 0$ exists.
- **Factors not involving $x_c$** are constant in $x_c$.

A variable $x_c$ appears in at most two factors (once in a numerator, once in a
denominator), and both resolve independently. Thus every intermediate limit exis
ts and is well-defined.

### Conclusion

By linearity of iterated limits (each exists, and a finite sum of existing limit
s is the limit of the sum, applied inductively over the nested limits):
$$L_\tau := \lim_{x_{\tau(1)}\to 0}\cdots\lim_{x_{\tau(n)}\to 0} f = \sum_{\sigm
a \in S_n} c_\sigma \cdot \mathbf{1}[\sigma = \tau] = c_\tau.$$

Since the $c_\sigma$ are chosen to be **distinct real numbers**, all $n!$ iterat
ed limits $L_\tau = c_\tau$ are finite and pairwise different. $\blacksquare$
