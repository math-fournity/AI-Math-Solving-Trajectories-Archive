## Proof

**Claim.** There exists an uncountable set of algebraically independent transcen
dental numbers (in fact, a subset of $\mathbb{R}$).

We prove this by showing that $\mathbb{R}$ admits a *transcendence basis* over $
\mathbb{Q}$ (a maximal algebraically independent set), that every element of suc
h a basis is transcendental, and that the basis must be uncountable.

---

### Step 1. Existence of a maximal algebraically independent set (Zorn's lemma)

Let $\mathcal{F}$ be the family of all algebraically independent subsets of $\ma
thbb{R}$, partially ordered by inclusion. $\mathcal{F}$ is nonempty (e.g. $\{\pi
\} \in \mathcal{F}$, since $\pi$ is transcendental).

Let $\{T_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}$ be a totally ordered chain in $\mathcal{F}$, a
nd set $T = \bigcup_\alpha T_\alpha$. We claim $T \in \mathcal{F}$, i.e. $T$ is
algebraically independent. Take any finite subset $\{x_1, \dots, x_n\} \subseteq
 T$ and any nonzero polynomial $f \in \mathbb{Q}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$. Each $x_i$ l
ies in some $T_{\alpha_i}$; since the chain is totally ordered, one of the indic
es $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n$ is largest, say $\alpha_j$, so all $x_1, \dots, x
_n \in T_{\alpha_j}$. Algebraic independence of $T_{\alpha_j}$ gives $f(x_1, \do
ts, x_n) \neq 0$. Hence $T$ is algebraically independent, so $T \in \mathcal{F}$
 is an upper bound for the chain.

By Zorn's lemma, $\mathcal{F}$ has a maximal element $T$. This $T$ is a **transc
endence basis** of $\mathbb{R}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.

---

### Step 2. Every element of $T$ is transcendental

Suppose $t \in T$ were algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$. Then its minimal polynomial
$m \in \mathbb{Q}[X]$, $m \neq 0$, satisfies $m(t) = 0$. But then the singleton
$\{t\} \subseteq T$ is algebraically dependent (witnessed by the nonzero univari
ate polynomial $m$), contradicting the algebraic independence of $T$. Therefore
every $t \in T$ is transcendental.

---

### Step 3. $T$ is uncountable

By maximality, for every $r \in \mathbb{R}$, the set $T \cup \{r\}$ is algebraic
ally dependent, which means $r$ is algebraic over the field $\mathbb{Q}(T)$. Hen
ce $\mathbb{R}$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}(T)$.

Suppose for contradiction that $T$ is countable. Then $\mathbb{Q}(T)$ — the fiel
d generated over $\mathbb{Q}$ by countably many elements — is countable (it is a
 countable union of finitely generated subfields, each countable). The set of el
ements of $\mathbb{R}$ algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}(T)$ is then also countable: th
ere are only countably many nonzero polynomials over the countable field $\mathb
b{Q}(T)$, and each has only finitely many roots in $\mathbb{R}$. But this set eq
uals all of $\mathbb{R}$, which is uncountable — a contradiction.

Therefore $T$ is uncountable.

---

### Conclusion

$T$ is an uncountable set (Step 3) of algebraically independent (by definition o
f $\mathcal{F}$) transcendental numbers (Step 2). Hence such a set exists. $\bla
cksquare$
