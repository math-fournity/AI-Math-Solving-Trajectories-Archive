## Proof

**Step 1: General solution is an entire function.**

Writing $u = p + iq$ and setting $z = x + it$, the PDE $u_t = iu_x$ yields:
$$p_t + iq_t = i(p_x + iq_x) = ip_x - q_x,$$
so $p_t = -q_x$ and $q_t = p_x$. These are precisely the Cauchy–Riemann equation
s for $f(z) = p(x,t) + iq(x,t)$ with $z = x+it$. Since $u$ is $C^1$, the functio
n $f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$ is holomorphic, hence entire. Thus every $C^1$ sol
ution takes the form
$$u(x,t) = f(x+it)$$
for some entire function $f$.

**Step 2: Fourier transform representation.**

Assume $u(\cdot,t)\in L^2(\mathbb{R})$ for all $t$ in an open interval $I$ (cont
aining $0$), and that $g(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u(x,t)|^2\,dx$ satisfies $g'(t)=0
$ on $I$, so $g$ is constant.

Since $f$ is entire and $f(\cdot+it)\in L^2$ for $t\in I$, standard Cauchy-estim
ate arguments (integrating the Cauchy integral formula over circles of radius $r
$ within the strip, then over $x$, applying Cauchy–Schwarz and Fubini) show that
 $f'(\cdot+it)\in L^2$ for $t$ in any compact subinterval of $I$. Consequently $
u_x = f'(\cdot+it)$ and $u_t = if'(\cdot+it)$ are both in $L^2$ locally in $t$,
and we may take the Fourier transform in $x$ of the PDE:
$$\partial_t \hat{u}(\xi,t) = i(i\xi)\hat{u}(\xi,t) = -\xi\,\hat{u}(\xi,t).$$
Solving this ODE in $t$ for each $\xi$:
$$\hat{u}(\xi,t) = \hat{u}(\xi,0)\,e^{-\xi t}.$$

By Plancherel's theorem:
$$g(t) = \int_{\mathbb{R}}|u(x,t)|^2\,dx = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{
u}(\xi,0)|^2 e^{-2\xi t}\,d\xi.$$

**Step 3: The key argument — non-negative integrand with zero integral.**

Set $\phi(\xi) = \frac{1}{2\pi}|\hat{u}(\xi,0)|^2 \geq 0$. Since $u(\cdot,0)\in
L^2$, we have $\phi\in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, and
$$g(t) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \phi(\xi)\,e^{-2\xi t}\,d\xi = C \quad\text{(constant
) for all } t\in I.$$

Fix $t_0\in I$ and pick $h>0$ with $t_0\pm h\in I$. Since $g$ is constant:
$$0 = g(t_0+h)+g(t_0-h)-2g(t_0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(\xi)\,e^{-2\xi t_0}\bigl(e
^{-2\xi h}+e^{2\xi h}-2\bigr)\,d\xi.$$

The term in parentheses is $2(\cosh(2\xi h)-1)\geq 0$, with equality **only** at
 $\xi=0$ (for $h>0$). The full integrand
$$\phi(\xi)\,e^{-2\xi t_0}\cdot 2(\cosh(2\xi h)-1)$$
is a product of non-negative factors, hence non-negative.

**Well-definedness:** The integral is not of the form $\infty-\infty$, since
$$\int \phi(\xi)\,e^{-2\xi t_0}\cosh(2\xi h)\,d\xi = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(g(t_0-h)+
g(t_0+h)\bigr)<\infty,$$
and $\int \phi(\xi)\,e^{-2\xi t_0}\,d\xi = g(t_0)<\infty$. So the integral of th
e non-negative integrand equals $0$.

A non-negative measurable function with zero integral vanishes a.e. Therefore:
$$\phi(\xi)\,e^{-2\xi t_0}\bigl(\cosh(2\xi h)-1\bigr)=0 \quad\text{a.e.}$$

For every $\xi\neq 0$, $\cosh(2\xi h)-1>0$ and $e^{-2\xi t_0}>0$, so $\phi(\xi)=
0$ for a.e. $\xi\neq 0$. Since $\phi\in L^1$, this gives $\phi=0$ a.e. on $\math
bb{R}$.

**Step 4: Conclusion.**

$\phi=0$ a.e. implies $|\hat{u}(\xi,0)|^2=0$ a.e., hence $\hat{u}(\cdot,0)=0$ in
 $L^2$, so $u(\cdot,0)=0$ in $L^2$. But $u(\cdot,0)=f|_{\mathbb{R}}$ is the rest
riction of an entire function to the real line, hence continuous, so $f(x)=0$ fo
r all $x\in\mathbb{R}$. By the **identity theorem** for holomorphic functions, $
f\equiv 0$ on $\mathbb{C}$, giving $u(x,t)=f(x+it)=0$ for all $(x,t)\in\mathbb{R
}^2$.

Therefore, the only $C^1$ solution of $u_t=iu_x$ with $\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u(x,t)|
^2\,dx<\infty$ and $\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u(x,t)|^2\,dx=0$ is the trivia
l solution $u\equiv 0$. **No non-trivial solutions exist.**
