Yes, such a function exists. Here is the proof.

**Construction.** Consider the standard smooth bump function
$$
\psi(x)=\begin{cases}\exp\!\Big(\!-\dfrac{1}{1-x^{2}}\Big), & |x|<1,\\[4pt] 0, &
 |x|\ge 1.\end{cases}
$$
It is well known (and follows by induction on the order of the derivative, since
 every derivative of $\psi$ is a rational function of $x$ times $\exp(-1/(1-x^{2
}))$ inside $(-1,1)$, and all one-sided limits of all derivatives at $x=\pm1$ eq
ual $0$) that $\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})$, with $\operatorname{supp}\psi=[-
1,1]$ and $\psi>0$ on $(-1,1)$.

Define
$$
f(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi\!\big(4(x-n)\big),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.
$$

**$f$ is well defined and $C^{\infty}$.** The support of the $n$-th summand is
$$
\operatorname{supp}\psi(4(\cdot-n))=\Big[n-\tfrac14,\,n+\tfrac14\Big].
$$
These closed intervals are pairwise disjoint and separated by gaps of length $\t
frac12$. Hence for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$ there is a neighborhood on which **at
most one** summand is nonzero; the sum is locally finite. Consequently $f$ is $C
^{\infty}$, with $f$ agreeing on each interval $(n-\tfrac14,n+\tfrac14)$ with th
e single smooth function $\psi(4(x-n))$.

**$f$ is periodic.** For every $x$,
$$
f(x+1)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi\!\big(4(x+1-n)\big)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi\!
\big(4(x-(n-1))\big)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi\!\big(4(x-m)\big)=f(x),
$$
so $f$ is $1$-periodic (and nonconstant, since $f(0)=\psi(0)=e^{-1}>0$).

**$f$ is not analytic.** On the open interval $I=(\tfrac14,\tfrac34)$ every summ
and $\psi(4(x-n))$ has $|4(x-n)|>1$ for all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$ (the closest intege
rs are $0$ and $1$, giving $|4(x-n)|\ge 4\cdot\tfrac14=1$ with strict inequality
 in the interior), so $f\equiv 0$ on $I$. On the other hand $f(0)=\psi(0)=e^{-1}
\neq 0$, so $f$ is not identically zero on $\mathbb{R}$.

If $f$ were real-analytic on $\mathbb{R}$, then by the identity theorem for real
-analytic functions, vanishing on the nonempty open interval $I$ would force $f\
equiv 0$ on all of $\mathbb{R}$ — contradicting $f(0)=e^{-1}$. Hence $f$ is not
analytic.

**Conclusion.** The function $f$ constructed above is periodic, infinitely diffe
rentiable, and not analytic. Therefore a periodic $C^{\infty}$ function that is
not analytic **does exist**.
