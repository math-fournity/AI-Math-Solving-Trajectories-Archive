### Evaluation of the integral

Let
$$I = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-(\ln x)^2/2}\,dx.$$

**Substitution.** Set $u = \ln x$, so $x = e^u$ and $dx = e^u\,du$. As $x \to 0^
+$, $u \to -\infty$; as $x \to \infty$, $u \to +\infty$. Hence
$$I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-u^2/2}\,e^u\,du = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-
u^2/2 + u}\,du.$$

**Completing the square.** Rewrite the exponent:
$$-\frac{u^2}{2} + u = -\frac{1}{2}(u^2 - 2u) = -\frac{1}{2}(u-1)^2 + \frac{1}{2
}.$$

Therefore
$$I = e^{1/2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-(u-1)^2/2}\,du.$$

**Gaussian integral.** The standard identity $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-t^2/2}
\,dt = \sqrt{2\pi}$ (obtained by shifting $t = u-1$) gives
$$I = e^{1/2}\cdot\sqrt{2\pi} = \sqrt{2\pi e}.$$

### Expression in terms of the Gamma function

Recall the classical value
$$\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right) = \int_{0}^{\infty} t^{-1/2}e^{-t}\,dt = \sq
rt{\pi},$$
which follows from the substitution $t = s^2$ reducing it to the Gaussian integr
al $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-s^2}ds = \sqrt{\pi}$.

Since $\sqrt{\pi} = \Gamma(1/2)$, we may write
$$I = \sqrt{2\pi e} = \sqrt{2e}\cdot\sqrt{\pi} = \sqrt{2e}\;\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac
{1}{2}\right).$$

Thus **yes**, the integral can be expressed in terms of the Gamma function:
$$\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-(\ln x)^2/2}\,dx = \sqrt{2\pi e} = \
sqrt{2e}\;\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right).}$$

**Remark.** The factor $\sqrt{2e}$ is not itself a Gamma value at a "nice" point
, so the integral is not *equal* to a single Gamma value $\Gamma(s)$ for some si
mple $s$; rather, it is a Gamma value ($\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt{\pi}$) multiplied by t
he elementary constant $\sqrt{2e}$. The appearance of $e^{1/2}$ comes from the l
inear term $u$ introduced by the Jacobian $dx = e^u\,du$ when converting the log
-domain Gaussian back to the $x$-domain — geometrically, the shift $u \mapsto u-
1$ that centers the Gaussian at $u=1$ (i.e. $x = e$), which is exactly the mode
of the integrand viewed as a function of $x$.
