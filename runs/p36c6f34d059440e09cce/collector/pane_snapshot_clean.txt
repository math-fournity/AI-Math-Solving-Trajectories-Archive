**Answer: Yes.** If $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ has Lebesgue outer measure zero, th
en $A \times B$ has Lebesgue outer measure zero in $\mathbb{R}^2$ for *every* su
bset $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.

---

### Proof

Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ with $m^*(A) = 0$, and let $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}$
 be arbitrary. We show $m_2^*(A \times B) = 0$.

**Step 1: Truncation.** For each $k \in \mathbb{N}$, set $B_k := B \cap [-k, k]$
. Then
$$A \times B = \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} (A \times B_k).$$
By countable subadditivity of outer measure, it suffices to show $m_2^*(A \times
 B_k) = 0$ for every fixed $k$.

**Step 2: Covering argument for fixed $k$.** Fix $k$ and fix $\varepsilon > 0$.
Since $B_k \subseteq [-k,k]$, its outer measure satisfies $m^*(B_k) \le 2k$. Hen
ce there exists a countable collection of open intervals $\{L_m\}_{m}$ covering
$B_k$ with
$$\sum_m |L_m| < 2k + 1.$$
(For instance, take a covering with total length $< 2k + \delta$ for small $\del
ta > 0$.)

Since $m^*(A) = 0$, there exists a countable collection of open intervals $\{I_n
\}_{n}$ covering $A$ with
$$\sum_n |I_n| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2k+1}.$$

**Step 3: Product covering.** The collection of rectangles $\{I_n \times L_m\}_{
n,m}$ covers $A \times B_k$, since every $(a,b) \in A \times B_k$ has $a \in I_n
$ for some $n$ and $b \in L_m$ for some $m$. The total area is
$$\sum_{n,m} |I_n \times L_m| = \sum_{n,m} |I_n|\,|L_m| = \left(\sum_n |I_n|\rig
ht)\!\left(\sum_m |L_m|\right) < \frac{\varepsilon}{2k+1}\cdot(2k+1) = \varepsil
on.$$

(All terms are non-negative, so Tonelli/Fubini for sums justifies the factorizat
ion even though the sums may be infinite — here both sums are finite, so it is j
ust the product of two convergent series of non-negative terms.)

**Step 4: Conclusion.** Since $\varepsilon > 0$ was arbitrary, $m_2^*(A \times B
_k) = 0$ for each $k$. Therefore
$$m_2^*(A \times B) \le \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} m_2^*(A \times B_k) = 0,$$
so $m_2^*(A \times B) = 0$. $\blacksquare$

---

**Remark.** The truncation step is essential: $B$ itself may be unbounded (e.g.,
 $B = \mathbb{R}$), in which case $m^*(B) = \infty$ and a direct single-covering
 argument would yield an infinite total area. Restricting to $B_k \subseteq [-k,
k]$ gives finite outer measure $m^*(B_k) \le 2k$, which is then traded off again
st the smallness of the covering of $A$ via the factor $\varepsilon/(2k+1)$.
