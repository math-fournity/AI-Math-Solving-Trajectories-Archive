Looking at this problem, I need to determine when sequences $\{x_n\} \subset [0,
1]$ with $x_n \to 1$ can satisfy $x_n^{a_n} - x_n^{b_n} \to 1$, where $\{a_n\},
\{b_n\}$ are given sequences of nonnegative reals with $b_n > 0$.

**Answer:** Such sequences $\{x_n\}$ exist if and only if $b_n \to \infty$ and $
\dfrac{a_n}{b_n} \to 0$.

---

### Proof

**($\Longrightarrow$) Necessity.**

Suppose $\{x_n\}$ satisfies $0 \le x_n \le 1$, $x_n \to 1$, and $x_n^{a_n} - x_n
^{b_n} \to 1$.

Since $0 \le x_n \le 1$ and $a_n, b_n \ge 0$, both $x_n^{a_n}$ and $x_n^{b_n}$ l
ie in $[0,1]$. Their difference tending to $1$ forces:
$$x_n^{a_n} \to 1 \quad \text{and} \quad x_n^{b_n} \to 0.$$

Since $x_n^{b_n} \to 0 \ne 1^{b_n}$, we must have $x_n < 1$ for all sufficiently
 large $n$. Write $x_n = e^{-\delta_n}$ where $\delta_n = -\ln x_n > 0$ and $\de
lta_n \to 0^+$.

Then:
- $x_n^{a_n} = e^{-a_n \delta_n} \to 1$ implies $a_n \delta_n \to 0$.
- $x_n^{b_n} = e^{-b_n \delta_n} \to 0$ implies $b_n \delta_n \to +\infty$.

From $b_n \delta_n \to +\infty$ with $\delta_n \to 0$, we conclude $b_n \to +\in
fty$.

From $a_n \delta_n \to 0$ and $b_n \delta_n \to +\infty$:
$$\frac{a_n}{b_n} = \frac{a_n \delta_n}{b_n \delta_n} \longrightarrow \frac{0}{\
infty} = 0. \qquad \checkmark$$

**($\Longleftarrow$) Sufficiency.**

Suppose $b_n \to \infty$ and $a_n/b_n \to 0$. Define:
$$\delta_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\,b_n \cdot \max(a_n,\, 1)\,}}, \qquad x_n = e^{-\de
lta_n}.$$

Then $0 < x_n < 1$ and $x_n \to 1$, since $b_n \cdot \max(a_n,1) \ge b_n \to \in
fty$ gives $\delta_n \to 0$.

**$a_n \delta_n \to 0$:**
- If $a_n \ge 1$: $\;a_n \delta_n = \dfrac{a_n}{\sqrt{a_n b_n}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{a
_n}{b_n}} \to 0.$
- If $a_n < 1$: $\;a_n \delta_n = \dfrac{a_n}{\sqrt{b_n}} \le \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{b_
n}} \to 0.$

Hence $x_n^{a_n} = e^{-a_n \delta_n} \to e^0 = 1$.

**$b_n \delta_n \to \infty$:**
- If $a_n \ge 1$: $\;b_n \delta_n = \sqrt{\dfrac{b_n}{a_n}} \to \infty$ (since $
a_n/b_n \to 0$).
- If $a_n < 1$: $\;b_n \delta_n = \sqrt{b_n} \to \infty.$

Hence $x_n^{b_n} = e^{-b_n \delta_n} \to e^{-\infty} = 0$.

Therefore:
$$x_n^{a_n} - x_n^{b_n} \;\longrightarrow\; 1 - 0 = 1. \qquad \checkmark$$

---

**Conclusion.** The existence of such $\{x_n\}$ is completely characterized: it
is possible precisely when $b_n \to \infty$ and $a_n/b_n \to 0$ (equivalently, $
b_n/a_n \to \infty$ when $a_n > 0$).

- **Yes** when $b_n/a_n \to \infty$: e.g., $a_n = n,\; b_n = n^2$ works with $x_
n = e^{-1/n^{3/2}}$.
- **No** when $b_n/a_n \not\to \infty$: e.g., $a_n = n,\; b_n = 2n$ is impossibl
e, since $a_n \delta_n \to 0$ forces $b_n \delta_n = 2(a_n \delta_n) \to 0$, con
tradicting $x_n^{b_n} \to 0$.
