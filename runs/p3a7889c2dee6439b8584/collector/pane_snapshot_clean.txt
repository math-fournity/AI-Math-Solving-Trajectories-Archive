## Solution

**Claim.** $H_n(M;\mathbb{R})$ is **not** always zero. It vanishes precisely whe
n $\partial M\neq\varnothing$, and is isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}$ (per connected
component) when $\partial M=\varnothing$.

We treat the standard convention that "manifold with boundary $\partial M$" perm
its $\partial M=\varnothing$; the boundary is merely *named* $\partial M$.

---

### Tool: Poincaré–Lefschetz duality

Let $M$ be a compact orientable $n$-manifold with boundary. Poincaré–Lefschetz d
uality gives a cap-product isomorphism
$$
D\colon H^k(M,\partial M;\mathbb{R})\;\xrightarrow{\;\cong\;}\;H_{n-k}(M;\mathbb
{R}),\qquad 0\le k\le n,
$$
and likewise $H^k(M;\mathbb{R})\cong H_{n-k}(M,\partial M;\mathbb{R})$.

Setting $k=0$ yields
$$
H_n(M;\mathbb{R})\;\cong\;H^0(M,\partial M;\mathbb{R}). \tag{1}
$$

So the question reduces to computing $H^0(M,\partial M;\mathbb{R})$.

---

### Relative $H^0$ via the long exact sequence of the pair

The long exact sequence of the pair $(M,\partial M)$ in cohomology begins
$$
0\;\longrightarrow\;H^0(M,\partial M;\mathbb{R})\;\longrightarrow\;H^0(M;\mathbb
{R})\;\xrightarrow{\;r\;}\;H^0(\partial M;\mathbb{R})\;\longrightarrow\;H^1(M,\p
artial M;\mathbb{R})\;\longrightarrow\;\cdots
$$
so
$$
H^0(M,\partial M;\mathbb{R})\;=\;\ker r. \tag{2}
$$

Now $H^0(M;\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{c(M)}$ and $H^0(\partial M;\mathbb{R})\co
ng\mathbb{R}^{c(\partial M)}$, where $c(\cdot)$ denotes the number of connected
components. The restriction map $r$ sends a locally constant function on $M$ to
its restriction on $\partial M$.

- **If $\partial M\neq\varnothing$:** every component of $M$ meets $\partial M$
(a boundary point of a manifold-with-boundary lies in a component that also cont
ains interior points, and conversely each component of $M$ contains boundary poi
nts — indeed each component of a compact manifold with nonempty boundary has non
empty boundary). Hence $r$ is **injective**: a constant on a component of $M$ th
at vanishes on the boundary portion of that component must vanish identically on
 that component. By (2),
  $$
  H^0(M,\partial M;\mathbb{R})=0.
  $$
  Combining with (1):
  $$\boxed{H_n(M;\mathbb{R})=0\quad\text{whenever }\partial M\neq\varnothing.}$$

- **If $\partial M=\varnothing$ (closed case):** then $(M,\partial M)=(M,\varnot
hing)$, so $H^0(M,\partial M;\mathbb{R})=H^0(M;\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{c(M)}
$. By (1),
  $$
  H_n(M;\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{c(M)}.
  $$
  For a connected closed orientable $n$-manifold this is $\mathbb{R}\neq 0$ — th
e classical top fundamental class.

---

### Counterexample showing "always zero" is false

Take $M=S^n$, the $n$-sphere. It is compact, orientable, and $\partial M=\varnot
hing$. By the closed case above,
$$
H_n(S^n;\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}\neq 0.
$$
Thus $H_n(M;\mathbb{R})$ is **not** always zero.

---

### Conclusion

$H_n(M;\mathbb{R})$ is **not always zero**. Precisely:

- $\partial M\neq\varnothing\;\Longrightarrow\;H_n(M;\mathbb{R})=0$ (by Poincaré
–Lefschetz duality + injectivity of $H^0(M)\to H^0(\partial M)$);
- $\partial M=\varnothing\;\Longrightarrow\;H_n(M;\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{c(
M)}$, which is nonzero whenever $M\neq\varnothing$.

The compact orientable closed manifold $S^n$ furnishes an explicit counterexampl
e to the assertion that $H_n(M;\mathbb{R})$ is always zero.
