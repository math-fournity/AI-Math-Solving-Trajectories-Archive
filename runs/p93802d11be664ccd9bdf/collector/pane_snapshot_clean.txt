The limit **holds**. Here is the proof.

---

**Theorem.** Let $(a_n)_{n\ge 0}$ be a sequence of complex numbers with $\lim_{n
\to\infty} a_n = L \neq 0$. Then
$$\lim_{x\to 1^-} (1-x)\sum_{n\ge 0} a_n x^n = L.$$

**Proof.**

Set $b_n := a_n - L$, so that $b_n \to 0$ as $n\to\infty$. Using the geometric s
eries identity
$$\sum_{n\ge 0} x^n = \frac{1}{1-x}, \qquad |x|<1,$$
we have $(1-x)\sum_{n\ge 0} x^n = 1$, and therefore
$$(1-x)\sum_{n\ge 0} a_n x^n - L \;=\; (1-x)\sum_{n\ge 0} (a_n - L)x^n \;=\; (1-
x)\sum_{n\ge 0} b_n x^n.$$

It suffices to show
$$(1-x)\sum_{n\ge 0} b_n x^n \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \quad\text{as } x\to 1^-.$$

Let $\varepsilon > 0$. Since $b_n \to 0$, choose $N\in\mathbb{N}$ such that $|b_
n| < \varepsilon/2$ for all $n \ge N$. Split the sum at $N$:

$$(1-x)\sum_{n\ge 0} b_n x^n \;=\; \underbrace{(1-x)\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} b_n x^n}_{\
text{(I)}} \;+\; \underbrace{(1-x)\sum_{n\ge N} b_n x^n}_{\text{(II)}}.$$

**Term (I).** This is a *finite* sum, so
$$|\text{(I)}| \;\le\; (1-x)\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|b_n| \;\xrightarrow[x\to 1^-]{}\; 0
.$$
Hence there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $|\text{(I)}| < \varepsilon/2$ wheneve
r $1-\delta < x < 1$.

**Term (II).** Using $|b_n| < \varepsilon/2$ for $n\ge N$ and $x^n \ge 0$ for $x
\in(0,1)$,
$$|\text{(II)}| \;\le\; (1-x)\sum_{n\ge N}|b_n|\,x^n \;\le\; \frac{\varepsilon}{
2}(1-x)\sum_{n\ge N}x^n \;\le\; \frac{\varepsilon}{2}(1-x)\sum_{n\ge 0}x^n \;=\;
 \frac{\varepsilon}{2}.$$

**Combining.** For $1-\delta < x < 1$,
$$\left|(1-x)\sum_{n\ge 0} a_n x^n - L\right| \;=\; |\text{(I)}| + |\text{(II)}|
 \;<\; \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \;=\; \varepsilon.$$

Since $\varepsilon > 0$ was arbitrary,
$$\lim_{x\to 1^-} (1-x)\sum_{n\ge 0} a_n x^n = L. \qquad\blacksquare$$

---

**Remark.** The hypothesis $L\neq 0$ is not needed for the proof; the argument w
orks for $L=0$ as well. The result is the "easy" (Abelian) direction of the Frob
enius theorem: convergence of $a_n$ to $L$ implies Abel-summability to $L$. The
argument uses only the triangle inequality on $|b_n|$, so it applies verbatim to
 complex-valued sequences.
