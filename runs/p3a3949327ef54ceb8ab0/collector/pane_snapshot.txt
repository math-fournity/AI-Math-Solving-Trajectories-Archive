## Solution

### Part (a)

**The parametrization.** $\mathbf{r}(u,v) = (\sin u\cos v,\;\sin u\sin v,\;\cos
u)$ with $0\le u\le \pi/2$, $0\le v\le 2\pi$ is the standard spherical-coordinat
e parametrization of the **upper hemisphere** of the unit sphere ($x^2+y^2+z^2=1
$, $z\ge 0$).

**Boundary of the parameter domain.** The domain $[0,\pi/2]\times[0,2\pi]$ has f
our edges:

| Edge | Image on surface | Contribution |
|------|-----------------|--------------|
| $u=0$ | $\mathbf{r}(0,v)=(0,0,1)$ — collapses to the **north pole** (single po
int) | Degenerate; no boundary curve |
| $u=\pi/2$ | $\mathbf{r}(\pi/2,v)=(\cos v,\sin v,0)$ — the **equator** | Non-de
generate boundary |
| $v=0$ | Meridian from north pole to $(1,0,0)$ | Cancelled by $v=2\pi$ edge |
| $v=2\pi$ | Same meridian, opposite direction | Cancelled by $v=0$ edge |

Since $v=0$ and $v=2\pi$ trace the **same meridian** in **opposite directions**,
 they cancel. The $u=0$ edge collapses to a point. Therefore:

> **The boundary is the equator**: the circle $x^2+y^2=1,\; z=0$.

**Induced orientation.** Compute the normal:

$$\mathbf{r}_u = (\cos u\cos v,\;\cos u\sin v,\;-\sin u),\qquad \mathbf{r}_v = (
-\sin u\sin v,\;\sin u\cos v,\;0)$$

$$\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v = \bigl(\sin^2\!u\cos v,\;\sin^2\!u\sin v,\;\si
n u\cos u\bigr) = \sin u\;\mathbf{r}(u,v).$$

Since $\sin u\ge 0$ on the domain, $\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v$ points in th
e **same direction as $\mathbf{r}$**, i.e., **radially outward**. The induced or
ientation is the **outward-normal** orientation.

**Boundary direction.** At the $u=\pi/2$ edge, the outward normal of the paramet
er domain is $+\partial/\partial u$, so the boundary-orientation rule (outward n
ormal first, then tangent, matching the domain orientation $du\wedge dv$) gives
the tangent as $+\partial/\partial v$. Thus the equator is traversed as $v$ incr
eases from $0$ to $2\pi$:

$$\mathbf{r}(\pi/2,v)=(\cos v,\sin v,0),\quad v:0\to 2\pi.$$

This goes $(1,0,0)\to(0,1,0)\to(-1,0,0)\to(0,-1,0)\to(1,0,0)$, which is **counte
rclockwise when viewed from above** (from $+z$ looking down).

*Right-hand rule check:* At the equator the outward normal is horizontal (radial
). Standing on the boundary with head pointing outward, the upper hemisphere lie
s on the left — this forces counterclockwise motion as seen from above. ✓

> **Part (a) summary:** Boundary = equator $x^2+y^2=1, z=0$, oriented **counterc
lockwise as viewed from above** ($+z$). Surface normal = **outward** (radially a
way from origin).

### Part (b)

**The parametrization.** $\mathbf{R}(u,v)=(\sin u\sin v,\;\sin u\cos v,\;\cos u)
$ with the same domain. This is still the **upper hemisphere**, but the roles of
 $\sin v$ and $\cos v$ in the $x,y$ components are **swapped** relative to part
(a) — effectively reversing the $v$-orientation.

**Boundary.** By the identical topological argument (same parameter domain, same
 collapse at $u=0$, same cancellation of $v=0$ and $v=2\pi$ meridians):

> **The boundary is the equator**: the circle $x^2+y^2=1,\; z=0$.

**Induced orientation.** Compute the normal:

$$\mathbf{R}_u = (\cos u\sin v,\;\cos u\cos v,\;-\sin u),\qquad \mathbf{R}_v = (
\sin u\cos v,\;-\sin u\sin v,\;0)$$

$$\mathbf{R}_u\times\mathbf{R}_v = \bigl(-\sin^2\!u\sin v,\;-\sin^2\!u\cos v,\;-
\sin u\cos u\bigr) = -\sin u\;\mathbf{R}(u,v).$$

Since $\sin u\ge 0$, the normal $\mathbf{R}_u\times\mathbf{R}_v$ points in the *
*opposite direction of $\mathbf{R}$**, i.e., **radially inward**. The induced or
ientation is the **inward-normal** orientation — **opposite** to part (a).

**Boundary direction.** The same boundary-orientation analysis gives the $u=\pi/
2$ edge traversed in $+v$ direction:

$$\mathbf{R}(\pi/2,v)=(\sin v,\cos v,0),\quad v:0\to 2\pi.$$

This goes $(0,1,0)\to(1,0,0)\to(0,-1,0)\to(-1,0,0)\to(0,1,0)$, which is **clockw
ise when viewed from above** (from $+z$ looking down) — the **reverse** of part
(a).

*Right-hand rule check:* At the equator the inward normal points toward the orig
in. Standing on the boundary with head pointing inward, the upper hemisphere on
the left forces clockwise motion as seen from above. ✓

> **Part (b) summary:** Boundary = equator $x^2+y^2=1, z=0$, oriented **clockwis
e as viewed from above** ($+z$). Surface normal = **inward** (radially toward or
igin).

### Comparison

Both surfaces are the **same upper hemisphere**, but the parametrizations induce
 **opposite orientations**:

| | Part (a) | Part (b) |
|---|---|---|
| Surface | Upper hemisphere | Upper hemisphere |
| Normal $\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v$ | **Outward** (radially out) | **Inwar
d** (radially in) |
| Boundary | Equator $x^2+y^2=1, z=0$ | Equator $x^2+y^2=1, z=0$ |
| Boundary direction (viewed from $+z$) | **Counterclockwise** | **Clockwise** |

The swap of $\sin v$ and $\cos v$ between the $\mathbf{i}$ and $\mathbf{j}$ comp
onents reverses the sign of $\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v$ (equivalently, reve
rses the $v$-orientation), which flips both the surface normal and the induced b
oundary direction.
