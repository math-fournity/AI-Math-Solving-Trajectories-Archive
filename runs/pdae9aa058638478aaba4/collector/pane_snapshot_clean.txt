## Proof

**Answer: Yes, $E$ can have zero measure.**

The given property
$$\forall x \in X,\ \forall \delta > 0,\quad E \cap B(x,\delta) \neq \emptyset$$
says precisely that $E$ is **dense** in $X$: every open ball centered at any poi
nt of $X$ meets $E$, i.e., $\overline{E} = X$.

Density and measure are independent notions. A set can be dense and yet have zer
o measure.

**Example.** Take $X = \mathbb{R}$ with the standard metric $d(x,y)=|x-y|$, and
let $E = \mathbb{Q}$, the set of rational numbers.

*Denseness.* For every $x \in \mathbb{R}$ and every $\delta > 0$, the interval $
(x-\delta, x+\delta)$ contains a rational number (by the Archimedean property of
 $\mathbb{R}$). Hence $\mathbb{Q} \cap B(x,\delta) \neq \emptyset$, so $E$ satis
fies the stated property.

*Zero measure.* $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable. Every countable subset of $\mathbb{R}
$ has Lebesgue measure zero: enumerate $E = \{q_1, q_2, \dots\}$ and cover each
$q_n$ by an interval of length $\varepsilon/2^n$; the total measure of the cover
 is $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \varepsilon/2^n = \varepsilon$. Since $\varepsilon > 0$
 is arbitrary, $m(E) = 0$.

Thus $E = \mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$ (satisfying the given property) a
nd has Lebesgue measure zero. $\blacksquare$
