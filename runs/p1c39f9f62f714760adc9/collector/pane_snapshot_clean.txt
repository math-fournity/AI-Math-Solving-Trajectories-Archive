## Solution

The statement is **TRUE**.

**Setup and notation.** Let $b_n = a_n - a_{n-1}$ (with $a_{-1}=0$), so $a_n = \
sum_{k=0}^{n} b_k$ is the $n$-th partial sum of the series $\sum b_k$. Since $0\
le a_n\le 1$, we have $|b_n|\le 1$, hence $\sum b_n s^n$ converges absolutely fo
r every $0<s<1$.

---

**Step 1. Rewrite the left-hand side as an Abel mean of $(a_n)$.**

Using $a_{-1}=0$ and a shift of index,
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} b_n s^n
= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n s^n - \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_{n-1} s^n
= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n s^n - s\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n s^n
= (1-s)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n s^n.
$$
(The term $a_{-1}s^0=0$ causes no issue, and $|a_n|\le 1$ justifies passing to t
he limit.) Thus
$$
\text{LHS} = (1-s)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n s^n,
$$
the **Abel (Poisson) mean** of the bounded sequence $(a_n)$.

The right-hand side
$$
\text{RHS} = \lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m+1}\sum_{n=0}^{m} a_n =: L
$$
is the **Cesàro $(C,1)$ mean** of $(a_n)$, assumed to exist.

So the claim reduces to the classical fact: *for a bounded sequence, Cesàro summ
ability implies Abel summability to the same limit.*

---

**Step 2. Prove Cesàro $\Rightarrow$ Abel for bounded sequences.**

Set $c_n = a_n - L$ and $\tau_m = \frac{1}{m+1}\sum_{n=0}^{m} c_n = \frac{1}{m+1
}\sum_{n=0}^m a_n - L \to 0$. Since $|a_n|\le 1$, $|c_n|\le 1+|L|$, so $|\tau_m|
\le 1+|L|$ (bounded). Let $C_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} c_k = (n+1)\tau_n$. By summation
 by parts (using $C_n s^n\to 0$ since $|C_n|\le (n+1)(1+|L|)$ and $|s|<1$),
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n s^n = (1-s)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} C_n s^n = (1-s)\sum_{n=0}
^{\infty}(n+1)\tau_n s^n.
$$
Therefore
$$
(1-s)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n s^n - L = (1-s)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n s^n = (1-s)^
{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+1)\tau_n s^n.
$$
The kernel $(1-s)^2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+1)s^n = 1$ is a probability distributio
n. Given $\varepsilon>0$, pick $N$ with $|\tau_n|<\varepsilon/2$ for $n>N$. Spli
t the sum at $N$:
- **Tail:** $\displaystyle (1-s)^2\sum_{n>N}(n+1)|\tau_n|s^n \le \frac{\varepsil
on}{2}(1-s)^2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+1)s^n = \frac{\varepsilon}{2}$.
- **Head:** $\displaystyle (1-s)^2\sum_{n=0}^{N}(n+1)|\tau_n|s^n \le (1-s)^2\cdo
t M\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{N}(n+1) \xrightarrow[s\to 1^-]{} 0$, where $M=\max_{0\le n\l
e N}|\tau_n|$; hence this is $<\varepsilon/2$ for $s$ sufficiently close to $1$.

Combining, $\left|(1-s)\sum a_n s^n - L\right| < \varepsilon$ for $s$ close enou
gh to $1^-$. Since $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary,
$$
\lim_{s\to 1^{-}}(1-s)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n s^n = L.
$$

---

**Step 3. Conclude.** Combining Steps 1 and 2,
$$
\lim_{s\to 1^{-}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(a_n-a_{n-1})s^n
= \lim_{s\to 1^{-}}(1-s)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n s^n
= L
= \lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m+1}\sum_{n=0}^{m} a_n,
$$
whenever the right-hand side limit $L$ exists. The hypotheses $0\le a_n\le 1$ (b
oundedness) and convergence of $\sum a_n s^n$ for $0<s<1$ are exactly what is ne
eded; the divergence of $\sum a_n$ is consistent context but not required for th
e equality itself.

Hence the asserted identity **holds**.
