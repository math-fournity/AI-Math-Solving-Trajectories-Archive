## Proof

**Claim:** The condition does **not** imply that $f$ can be extended to $0$.

**Counterexample.** Let $n = 1$ and define
$$f: D^\times = D\setminus\{0\} \;\longrightarrow\; \mathbb{P}^1, \qquad f(z_1,
z_2) = [z_1 : z_2],$$
where $D \subset \mathbb{C}^2$ is the open unit disk (ball) and $0 = (0,0)$.

This map is well-defined and holomorphic on $D^\times$, since $(z_1,z_2) \neq (0
,0)$ there and $[z_1:z_2]$ gives a holomorphic map into $\mathbb{P}^1$ (it is co
vered by the holomorphic map $\mathbb{C}^2\setminus\{0\} \to \mathbb{C}^2\setmin
us\{0\}$, $(z_1,z_2)\mapsto (z_1,z_2)$, followed by the quotient $\mathbb{C}^2\s
etminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{P}^1$).

**The sequence condition is satisfied.** Take $p_i = (1/i,\, 0) \in D^\times$ fo
r $i$ large. Then $p_i \to 0$ and
$$f(p_i) = [1/i : 0] = [1 : 0] =: x \quad \text{for all } i.$$
So there exists a sequence converging to $0$ along which $f$ is constantly $x =
[1:0]$.

**$f$ does not extend to $0$.** Suppose for contradiction that $f$ extends to a
holomorphic (hence continuous) map $\tilde{f}: D \to \mathbb{P}^1$ with $\tilde{
f}|_{D^\times} = f$.

- Along the $z_1$-axis: for $z_1 \neq 0$, $\tilde{f}(z_1, 0) = [z_1:0] = [1:0]$,
 so by continuity $\tilde{f}(0,0) = [1:0]$.
- Along the $z_2$-axis: for $z_2 \neq 0$, $\tilde{f}(0, z_2) = [0:z_2] = [0:1]$,
 so by continuity $\tilde{f}(0,0) = [0:1]$.

This gives $[1:0] = [0:1]$, a contradiction since $[1:0] \neq [0:1]$ in $\mathbb
{P}^1$.

**Why the condition is too weak.** The hypothesis only constrains $f$ along a *s
ingle* sequence approaching $0$. In several complex variables, a point is a codi
mension-$2$ singularity, and the Hartogs extension theorem guarantees automatic
extension for **$\mathbb{C}$-valued** holomorphic functions. However, $\mathbb{P
}^n$-valued maps are not covered by Hartogs: the target is compact and not a vec
tor space, so there is no analogue of the Hartogs phenomenon. The map $[z_1:z_2]
$ has different limiting values along different directions, and knowing the limi
t along one sequence tells us nothing about the behavior along other directions.
 The existence of a single sequence with a constant value is far weaker than req
uiring a unique limit along *all* approaches to $0$.

Therefore, the given condition does **not** imply that $f$ extends to $0$.
