Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether a real analytic function eq
uals its Taylor series at the endpoints of the interval of convergence (given th
e series converges there), and prove the result.

## Proof

**Setup.** Let $f$ be a real analytic function on an open interval $I$, let $a \
in I$, and let
$$T(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n (x-a)^n$$
be the Taylor series of $f$ at $a$, with radius of convergence $R > 0$. Since $f
$ is analytic at $a$, we have $f(x) = T(x)$ for all $x \in (a-R, a+R)$.

**Claim.** If the series converges at an endpoint $x_0 = a+R$ (or $a-R$) and $f$
 is defined and continuous at $x_0$, then $f(x_0) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n R^n$
 (resp. $\sum c_n(-R)^n$).

The key tool is Abel's theorem for power series.

---

**Abel's Theorem.** *If $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n R^n$ converges to $S$, then $\l
im_{t \to 1^-} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n (tR)^n = S$.*

*Proof.* Let $S_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} c_k R^k$ and $S = \lim S_n$. By Abel's summat
ion by parts, for $0 \le t < 1$:
$$\sum_{n=0}^{N} c_n(tR)^n = S_N t^N + \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} S_n\bigl(t^n - t^{n+1}\b
igr).$$
As $N \to \infty$, $S_N t^N \to 0$ (since $S_N$ is bounded and $t^N \to 0$), giv
ing
$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n(tR)^n = (1-t)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} S_n\, t^n.$$
Since $S_n \to S$, for any $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N_0$ with $|S_n - S|
< \varepsilon$ for $n \ge N_0$. Then
$$\left|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n(tR)^n - S\right| = \left|(1-t)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty
}(S_n - S)t^n\right| \le (1-t)\sum_{n=0}^{N_0-1}|S_n - S|\,t^n + \varepsilon\,(1
-t)\sum_{n=N_0}^{\infty} t^n.$$
The second term is $\le \varepsilon$. The first term is a fixed finite sum multi
plied by $(1-t) \to 0$ as $t \to 1^-$. Hence $\lim_{t\to 1^-} \sum c_n(tR)^n = S
$. $\square$

---

**Applying to $f$.** For $x \in (a,\, a+R)$, write $x = a + tR$ with $t \in (0,1
)$. Then
$$f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n(x-a)^n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n(tR)^n.$$
By Abel's theorem,
$$\lim_{x \to (a+R)^-} f(x) = \lim_{t \to 1^-} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n(tR)^n = \
sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n R^n.$$

If $f$ is continuous at $x_0 = a+R$ (from the left), then
$$f(a+R) = \lim_{x \to (a+R)^-} f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n R^n.$$

The identical argument at the left endpoint gives: if $\sum c_n(-R)^n$ converges
 and $f$ is continuous at $a - R$, then $f(a-R) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n(-R)^n$
.

---

**Two important remarks.**

1. **Convergence at the endpoint forces a continuous extension.** The convergenc
e of $\sum c_n R^n$ alone (via Abel's theorem) guarantees that $\lim_{x \to (a+R
)^-} f(x)$ exists and equals $\sum c_n R^n$. Thus $f$ *automatically* admits a c
ontinuous extension to the endpoint with value equal to the series sum. If $f$ i
s already defined at the endpoint consistently with this limit, the equality hol
ds. This is the natural setting whenever we speak of "$f$ at the endpoint."

2. **The endpoint cannot be a point of analyticity (when $R$ is the exact radius
).** If $f$ were real analytic at $a+R$ — meaning $f$ admits a convergent power
series in a neighborhood of $a+R$ — then $f$ would extend to a complex analytic
function near $a+R \in \mathbb{C}$, placing the nearest complex singularity at d
istance strictly greater than $R$ from $a$. This would force the radius of conve
rgence to exceed $R$, contradicting the definition of $R$. Therefore the endpoin
t necessarily lies on the boundary of the domain of analyticity, and the equalit
y $f(a+R) = \sum c_n R^n$ holds by **continuity via Abel's theorem**, not by ana
lyticity at the endpoint itself.

**Concrete illustration.** The function $f(x) = \log(1+x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} x^n$ is real analytic on $(-1, \infty)$, with Taylor series
 at $0$ having radius $R=1$ (singularity at $x = -1$). At the right endpoint $x
= 1$: the series $\sum \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} = \log 2$ converges, and indeed $f(1
) = \log 2$, confirming the theorem.

---

**Conclusion.** Yes — a real analytic function is equal to its Taylor series at
the endpoints of the interval of convergence, provided the series converges ther
e and the function is defined continuously at the endpoint. Abel's theorem ensur
es that convergence of the series at the endpoint implies the function's one-sid
ed limit at the endpoint equals the series sum, so continuity of $f$ at the endp
oint yields the equality $f = T$ there.
