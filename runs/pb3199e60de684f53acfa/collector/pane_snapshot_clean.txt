## Answer

**No.** Weak convergence is not preserved under the (nonlinear) absolute value m
ap. Here is a counterexample.

---

### Counterexample

Let $\Omega = (0, 2\pi)$ and set
$$u_n(x) = \sin(nx), \qquad u(x) = 0.$$

**Step 1: $u_n \rightharpoonup 0$ weakly in $L^2(0,2\pi)$.**

For every $g \in L^2(0,2\pi) \subset L^1(0,2\pi)$, by the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma
$$\int_0^{2\pi} \sin(nx)\, g(x)\,dx \;\longrightarrow\; 0.$$
Hence $u_n \rightharpoonup 0 = u$ weakly in $L^2(0,2\pi)$. Clearly $|u| = 0$.

**Step 2: $|u_n| = |\sin(nx)|$ does NOT converge weakly to $0$.**

The function $|\sin t|$ is $\pi$-periodic, and its Fourier expansion is
$$|\sin t| = \frac{2}{\pi} - \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(2kt)}{4k
^2-1}.$$
Therefore
$$|u_n(x)| = |\sin(nx)| = \frac{2}{\pi} - \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{
\cos(2knx)}{4k^2-1}.$$

For any $g \in L^2(0,2\pi)$, each oscillatory term satisfies
$$\int_0^{2\pi} \cos(2knx)\, g(x)\,dx \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \quad (n\to\infty)$$
again by Riemann–Lebesgue. The series $\sum_{k\ge 1}\frac{1}{4k^2-1}$ is absolut
ely convergent, so we may pass the limit through the sum, giving
$$\int_0^{2\pi} |\sin(nx)|\, g(x)\,dx \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{2
\pi} g(x)\,dx.$$

Thus
$$|u_n| \rightharpoonup \frac{2}{\pi} \quad\text{weakly in } L^2(0,2\pi).$$

**Step 3: Conclusion.**

We have $|u| = 0$, but $|u_n| \rightharpoonup \tfrac{2}{\pi} \neq 0$. Testing ag
ainst $g \equiv 1$ already shows the discrepancy:
$$\int_0^{2\pi}|\sin(nx)|\,dx = 4 \;\longrightarrow\; 4 \neq 0 = \int_0^{2\pi}|u
|\,dx.$$

So $|u_n|$ does **not** converge weakly to $|u|$.

---

### Remark

The obstruction is that the map $u \mapsto |u|$ is nonlinear. Weak convergence o
nly controls the action of linear functionals, and nonlinear operations generall
y destroy it. (The map $u\mapsto|u|$ *is* continuous from $L^2$ strong to $L^2$
strong, but weak convergence is too weak to pass through it.)
