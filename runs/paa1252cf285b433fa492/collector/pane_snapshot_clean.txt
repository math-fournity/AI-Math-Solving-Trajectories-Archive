## Proof

**Statement:** The statement is **TRUE**: under the given hypotheses, $c = a \cd
ot b$.

**Setup.** We work on a $\sigma$-finite measure space $(\Omega, \mu)$. We are gi
ven:
- $a_n, b_n \in L^2(\Omega)$, with $a_n \rightharpoonup a$ weakly in $L^2$,
- $b_n \to b$ strongly in $L^2$,
- $a_n b_n \in L^2(\Omega)$, the sequence $\{a_n b_n\}$ is bounded in $L^2$, and
 $a_n b_n \rightharpoonup c$ weakly in $L^2$ for some $c \in L^2$.

We prove $c = a \cdot b$ a.e.

---

**Step 1: $a_n b_n \rightharpoonup ab$ weakly in $L^1$ (i.e., in $\sigma(L^1, L^
\infty)$).**

Let $\phi \in L^\infty(\Omega)$. We show $\int a_n b_n \phi \to \int ab\,\phi$.

Decompose:
$$\int a_n b_n \phi = \int a_n (b\,\phi)\,dx + \int a_n (b_n - b)\phi\,dx.$$

*First term.* Since $b \in L^2$ and $\phi \in L^\infty$, we have $b\phi \in L^2$
 (as $|b\phi| \leq \|\phi\|_\infty |b|$). By weak convergence $a_n \rightharpoon
up a$ in $L^2$:
$$\int a_n (b\phi)\,dx \to \int a(b\phi)\,dx = \int ab\,\phi\,dx.$$

*Second term.* By Cauchy–Schwarz:
$$\left|\int a_n(b_n - b)\phi\,dx\right| \leq \|a_n\|_{L^2}\,\|b_n - b\|_{L^2}\,
\|\phi\|_{L^\infty}.$$
Weak convergence $a_n \rightharpoonup a$ in $L^2$ implies $\sup_n \|a_n\|_{L^2}
< \infty$ (Uniform Boundedness Principle). Strong convergence gives $\|b_n - b\|
_{L^2} \to 0$. Hence the second term $\to 0$.

Combining: $\int a_n b_n \phi \to \int ab\,\phi$ for every $\phi \in L^\infty$.
$\checkmark$

---

**Step 2: Identify $c$ using characteristic functions of finite-measure sets.**

Since $a_n b_n \rightharpoonup c$ in $L^2$, for every $\psi \in L^2$:
$$\int a_n b_n \psi \to \int c\,\psi.$$

Let $E \subset \Omega$ be any measurable set with $\mu(E) < \infty$. Then $\chi_
E \in L^2 \cap L^\infty$ (since $\|\chi_E\|_{L^2}^2 = \mu(E) < \infty$ and $\|\c
hi_E\|_\infty = 1$).

- From $L^2$ weak convergence (test $\psi = \chi_E \in L^2$): $\displaystyle\int
 a_n b_n \chi_E \to \int c\,\chi_E$.
- From Step 1 (test $\phi = \chi_E \in L^\infty$): $\displaystyle\int a_n b_n \c
hi_E \to \int ab\,\chi_E$.

By uniqueness of limits:
$$\int_E c\,dx = \int_E ab\,dx \quad \text{for every } E \text{ with } \mu(E) <
\infty. \tag{$\ast$}$$

---

**Step 3: Conclude $c = ab$ a.e.**

Both $c$ and $ab$ are integrable on every finite-measure set $E$: $c \in L^2 \su
bset L^1(E)$ (by Cauchy–Schwarz, $\int_E |c| \leq \|c\|_{L^2}\mu(E)^{1/2}$), and
 $ab \in L^1(\Omega) \subset L^1(E)$ (by Hölder, $ab \in L^1$ since $a,b \in L^2
$).

Equation $(\ast)$ says $\int_E (c - ab) = 0$ for every finite-measure set $E$. S
ince the measure space is $\sigma$-finite, we can write $\Omega = \bigcup_{k=1}^
\infty \Omega_k$ with $\mu(\Omega_k) < \infty$. On each $\Omega_k$, the function
 $c - ab \in L^1(\Omega_k)$ has zero integral over every measurable subset, whic
h forces $c = ab$ a.e. on $\Omega_k$. Taking the countable union, $c = ab$ a.e.
on $\Omega$.

Since $c \in L^2$, this also shows $ab \in L^2$.

---

**Conclusion.** The weak $L^2$ limit of $a_n b_n$ is necessarily $a \cdot b$. Th
e statement is **true**: $a \cdot b = c$ holds.

The key mechanism is that strong convergence of $b_n$ allows us to "transfer" th
e weak convergence of $a_n$ through the product (Step 1), yielding weak-$L^1$ co
nvergence to $ab$. The assumed weak-$L^2$ convergence to $c$ is then compatible
with this only if $c = ab$, which we pin down by testing against characteristic
functions of finite-measure sets (Step 2–3).
