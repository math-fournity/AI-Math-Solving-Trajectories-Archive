## Proof

**Step 1: Identify the distribution of the transformed variables.**

Let $X_i = -\log(1 - U_i)$. Since $U_i \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ and the map $
u \mapsto 1-u$ preserves uniformity, $1-U_i \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ as well.
 For $x > 0$,

$$
P(X_i \le x) = P(-\log(1-U_i) \le x) = P(1-U_i \ge e^{-x}) = P(U_i \le 1 - e^{-x
}) = 1 - e^{-x}.
$$

Hence each $X_i$ has CDF $F(x) = 1 - e^{-x}$ on $(0,\infty)$, i.e. $X_i \sim \ma
thrm{Exponential}(1)$. Independence is preserved under deterministic transformat
ion, so $\{X_i\}$ are i.i.d. $\mathrm{Exponential}(1)$.

**Step 2: Express $N((0,s])$ in terms of partial sums.**

Let $S_k = \sum_{i=1}^k X_i$ with $S_0 = 0$. Then

$$
N((0,s]) = \sup\{k \in \mathbb{N} : S_k \le s\} = \max\{k \ge 0 : S_k \le s\}.
$$

Equivalently, $\{N((0,s]) \ge n\} = \{S_n \le s\}$, so

$$
\{N((0,s]) = n\} = \{S_n \le s\} \cap \{S_{n+1} > s\} = \{S_n \le s < S_{n+1}\}.
$$

Since $S_{n+1} = S_n + X_{n+1}$ and the event $\{S_n \le s\} \cap \{S_{n+1} > s\
}$ is exactly $\{S_n \le s\} \setminus \{S_{n+1} \le s\}$ (because $\{S_{n+1} \l
e s\} \subseteq \{S_n \le s\}$),

$$
P\bigl(N((0,s]) = n\bigr) = P(S_n \le s) - P(S_{n+1} \le s).
$$

**Step 3: Distribution of the partial sums.**

The sum of $n$ i.i.d. $\mathrm{Exponential}(1)$ variables is $\mathrm{Gamma}(n,1
)$, with CDF

$$
P(S_n \le s) = 1 - e^{-s}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \frac{s^j}{j!}, \qquad s \ge 0,
$$

(and $P(S_0 \le s) = 1$ for $s \ge 0$, consistent with the empty sum convention)
.

**Step 4: Compute the law of $N((0,s])$.**

For $n \ge 0$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P\bigl(N((0,s]) = n\bigr)
&= P(S_n \le s) - P(S_{n+1} \le s) \\
&= \left(1 - e^{-s}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{s^j}{j!}\right) - \left(1 - e^{-s}\sum_
{j=0}^{n}\frac{s^j}{j!}\right) \\
&= e^{-s}\cdot \frac{s^n}{n!}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This holds for every $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, and these probabilities sum to $e^{-s
}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} s^n/n! = e^{-s} e^s = 1$, confirming a valid distribution.

**Step 5: Conclusion.**

The probability mass function $P(N((0,s])=n) = e^{-s} s^n / n!$ for $n \in \{0,1
,2,\dots\}$ is precisely the Poisson distribution with parameter $s$.

**Yes, $N((0,s])$ is a Poisson random variable with parameter $s$.**

*Remark.* This is the standard construction of a Poisson process: i.i.d. $\mathr
m{Exponential}(1)$ inter-arrival times $X_i$ produce a counting process $N((0,s]
)$ that is a Poisson process of rate $1$, so its increment over an interval of l
ength $s$ is $\mathrm{Poisson}(s)$.
