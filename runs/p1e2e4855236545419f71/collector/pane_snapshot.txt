## Proof of Erdős Problem 499

**Theorem.** Let $M = (m_{ij})$ be an $n \times n$ doubly stochastic matrix (i.e
., $m_{ij} \ge 0$, $\sum_j m_{ij} = 1$ for all $i$, and $\sum_i m_{ij} = 1$ for
all $j$). Then there exists a permutation $\sigma \in S_n$ such that
$$\prod_{i=1}^{n} m_{i,\sigma(i)} \ge n^{-n}.$$

**Proof.**

We first handle the case where all entries of $M$ are strictly positive, then ex
tend to the general case by approximation.

**Step 1 (Reduction to an assignment problem).** Assume all $m_{ij} > 0$. Define
 $a_{ij} = -\log m_{ij}$. Then the desired inequality is equivalent to:
$$\min_{\sigma \in S_n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i,\sigma(i)} \le n \log n.$$

**Step 2 (Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem).** By the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem,
every doubly stochastic matrix is a convex combination of permutation matrices:
any doubly stochastic $X = (x_{ij})$ can be written as $X = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n
} \lambda_\sigma P_\sigma$ where $\lambda_\sigma \ge 0$, $\sum_\sigma \lambda_\s
igma = 1$, and $P_\sigma$ is the permutation matrix of $\sigma$.

Therefore, for any doubly stochastic matrix $X$:
$$\sum_{i,j} a_{ij}\, x_{ij} = \sum_{\sigma} \lambda_\sigma \sum_{i} a_{i,\sigma
(i)} \ge \sum_{\sigma} \lambda_\sigma \cdot \min_{\tau \in S_n} \sum_i a_{i,\tau
(i)} = \min_{\tau \in S_n} \sum_i a_{i,\tau(i)}.$$

Applying this with $X = M$ (which is doubly stochastic):
$$\sum_{i,j} a_{ij}\, m_{ij} \ge \min_{\sigma \in S_n} \sum_{i=1}^n a_{i,\sigma(
i)}. \tag{$\star$}$$

**Step 3 (Entropy bound).** We now upper-bound the left-hand side of $(\star)$:
$$\sum_{i,j} a_{ij}\, m_{ij} = \sum_{i,j} (-\log m_{ij})\, m_{ij} = \sum_{i=1}^n
 \left(\sum_{j=1}^n -m_{ij} \log m_{ij}\right).$$

For each row $i$, the inner sum $H_i = -\sum_j m_{ij} \log m_{ij}$ is the Shanno
n entropy of the probability distribution $(m_{i1}, \dots, m_{in})$ (since $m_{i
j} \ge 0$ and $\sum_j m_{ij} = 1$).

By **Jensen's inequality** applied to the concave function $\log$, with weights
$m_{ij}$ and values $1/m_{ij}$:
$$H_i = \sum_j m_{ij} \log\!\left(\frac{1}{m_{ij}}\right) \le \log\!\left(\sum_j
 m_{ij} \cdot \frac{1}{m_{ij}}\right) = \log\!\left(\sum_j 1\right) = \log n.$$

Therefore:
$$\sum_{i,j} a_{ij}\, m_{ij} = \sum_{i=1}^n H_i \le n \log n. \tag{$\star\star$}
$$

**Step 4 (Combining).** From $(\star)$ and $(\star\star)$:
$$\min_{\sigma \in S_n} \sum_{i=1}^n (-\log m_{i,\sigma(i)}) \le n \log n.$$

Exponentiating:
$$\max_{\sigma \in S_n} \prod_{i=1}^n m_{i,\sigma(i)} \ge e^{-n \log n} = n^{-n}
.$$

This establishes the result for doubly stochastic matrices with all positive ent
ries.

**Step 5 (Handling zero entries by approximation).** Now let $M$ be an arbitrary
 doubly stochastic matrix (possibly with zero entries). For $\varepsilon \in (0,
 1)$, define:
$$M_\varepsilon = (1 - \varepsilon)\, M + \varepsilon \cdot \frac{J}{n},$$
where $J$ is the all-ones matrix. Then $M_\varepsilon$ is doubly stochastic (as
a convex combination of doubly stochastic matrices) and has all entries strictly
 positive (since $J/n$ has all entries $1/n > 0$).

By Steps 1–4, for each $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $\sigma_\varepsilon \in S
_n$ with:
$$\prod_{i=1}^n (M_\varepsilon)_{i, \sigma_\varepsilon(i)} \ge n^{-n}.$$

Since $S_n$ is finite, there exists a single permutation $\sigma^* \in S_n$ and
a sequence $\varepsilon_k \to 0$ such that $\sigma_{\varepsilon_k} = \sigma^*$ f
or all $k$. By continuity of $M_\varepsilon \to M$ as $\varepsilon \to 0$:
$$\prod_{i=1}^n m_{i, \sigma^*(i)} = \lim_{k \to \infty} \prod_{i=1}^n (M_{\vare
psilon_k})_{i, \sigma^*(i)} \ge n^{-n}.$$

**Conclusion.** For every $n \times n$ doubly stochastic matrix $M$, there exist
s $\sigma \in S_n$ such that $\prod_{i=1}^n m_{i,\sigma(i)} \ge n^{-n}$. $\black
square$

---

**Remark on tightness.** The bound $n^{-n}$ is achieved with equality when $M =
\frac{1}{n}J$ (the matrix with all entries $1/n$), since every permutation gives
 product $(1/n)^n = n^{-n}$. The proof also shows that the result is a consequen
ce of two classical facts: the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem (geometry of the dou
bly stochastic polytope) and the maximum entropy principle ($H \le \log n$).
