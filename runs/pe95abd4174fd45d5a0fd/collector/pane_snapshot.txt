## Proof

**Claim.** For $n \ge 2$, there exists a function $f:S^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}$, defi
ned on the unit sphere $S^{n-1}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^n:\|x\|=1\}$, that is finite at
 every point of $S^{n-1}$ yet is unbounded. For $n=1$ no such function exists.

---

### Case $n\ge 2$: an unbounded, everywhere-finite function exists

Since $n\ge 2$, the sphere $S^{n-1}$ is infinite, so we may choose a point $p\in
 S^{n-1}$ and a sequence of **distinct** points $\{x_k\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset S
^{n-1}\setminus\{p\}$ with $x_k\to p$. (For instance, fix any unit vector $v\per
p p$—possible because $n\ge 2$—and set $x_k=\cos(1/k)\,p+\sin(1/k)\,v$; then $x_
k\in S^{n-1}$, the $x_k$ are distinct, and $x_k\to p$.)

Define $f:S^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}$ by

$$
f(x)=
\begin{cases}
k, & x=x_k\ \text{for some}\ k\ge 1,\\[4pt]
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

**Finiteness at every point.** Every $x\in S^{n-1}$ receives a value: either som
e positive integer $k$ (if $x=x_k$) or $0$ (otherwise). In particular $f(p)=0$ s
ince $p\notin\{x_k\}$. Hence $f$ is finite at every point.

**Unboundedness.** For every $M>0$ choose an integer $k>M$; then $f(x_k)=k>M$. T
hus $\sup_{x\in S^{n-1}} f(x)=+\infty$, so $f$ is unbounded.

No continuity, measurability, or any regularity hypothesis was assumed, so nothi
ng prevents this construction. $\square$

---

### Case $n=1$: no unbounded everywhere-finite function exists

For $n=1$ the unit sphere is $S^{0}=\{-1,+1\}$, a two-point set. Any function $f
:S^{0}\to\mathbb{R}$ that is finite at every point takes only the two finite val
ues $f(-1)$ and $f(+1)$, so

$$
\sup_{x\in S^{0}}|f(x)|=\max\bigl(|f(-1)|,|f(+1)|\bigr)<\infty.
$$

Hence every everywhere-finite function on $S^{0}$ is automatically bounded. $\sq
uare$

---

### Remark (the role of continuity)

The obstruction in the case $n\ge 2$ is purely the **lack of regularity**. The s
phere $S^{n-1}$ is compact (closed and bounded in $\mathbb{R}^n$), so by the ext
reme value theorem every **continuous** real-valued function on it is bounded an
d attains its bounds. The example above is necessarily discontinuous: it has a s
equence $x_k\to p$ along which $f(x_k)=k\to\infty$ while $f(p)=0$.

---

**Conclusion.** A function on the unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^n$ that is finite a
t every point **can** be unbounded when $n\ge 2$ (explicit construction given ab
ove); it **cannot** be unbounded when $n=1$, since the sphere is then finite.
