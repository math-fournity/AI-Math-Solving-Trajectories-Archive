## Preliminaries

A sequence $(\lambda_n)_{n\geq 1}$ is a **multiplier** from class $\mathcal{A}$
to class $\mathcal{B}$ if for every $f(z)=\sum a_n z^n \in \mathcal{A}$, the fun
ction $\sum \lambda_n a_n z^n \in \mathcal{B}$. This is equivalent to requiring
that the **Hadamard (convolution) product** $f * g \in \mathcal{B}$ for all $f \
in \mathcal{A}$, where $g(z) = \sum \lambda_n z^n$.

The classes:
- $S$: univalent functions $f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n z^n$ in $\mathbb{D}$, (a)
 with $a_1=1$, (b) generally.
- $C$: convex univalent functions ($\operatorname{Re}(1+zf''/f')>0$).
- $S^*$: starlike univalent functions.
- $K$: close-to-convex functions ($f$ is close-to-convex if $\exists\, g\in C$ w
ith $\operatorname{Re}(f'/g')>0$).
- $N$: Nevanlinna class (bounded characteristic); $N^+$: Smirnov class.

The **Ruscheweyh–Sheil-Small theorem** (1973) establishes:
$$C * C \subseteq C, \qquad S^* * C \subseteq S^*, \qquad K * C \subseteq K,$$
and the multiplier characterization: $(\lambda_n)$ is a multiplier from $C$ to $
C$ **iff** $\sum \lambda_n z^n \in C$.

## Part (a): Multipliers from $S$ to $S$ with $a_1=1$

**Necessary conditions.** Let $(\lambda_n)$ be a multiplier from $S$ to $S$ (nor
malized).

1. **$\lambda_1 = 1$:** Take $f(z)=z \in S$. Then $\sum \lambda_n a_n z^n = \lam
bda_1 z$. For this to lie in $S$ with $a_1=1$, we need $\lambda_1=1$.

2. **$g(z):=\sum \lambda_n z^n \in S$:** Take $f(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}=\sum_{n=1}^\in
fty z^n \in S$ (it is convex, hence in $S$). Then $\sum \lambda_n a_n z^n = \sum
 \lambda_n z^n = g(z)$, so $g \in S$.

3. **$\sum n\lambda_n z^n \in S$:** Take the Koebe function $k(z)=\frac{z}{(1-z)
^2}=\sum n z^n \in S$. Then $\sum n\lambda_n z^n \in S$.

4. **Rotational invariance:** For every $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}$, $e^{-i\alpha}k(e^
{i\alpha}z)=\frac{z}{(1-e^{i\alpha}z)^2}=\sum n\,e^{i(n-1)\alpha}z^n\in S$, so $
\sum n\lambda_n e^{i(n-1)\alpha}z^n\in S$ for all $\alpha$.

**The RSS-type characterization fails.** The natural conjecture (by analogy with
 the $C\to C$ case) would be: *$(\lambda_n)$ is a multiplier from $S$ to $S$ iff
 $g(z)=\sum\lambda_n z^n \in S$.* **This is false.**

*Counterexample.* Set $\lambda_n = (-1)^{n-1}n$. Then
$$g(z)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty (-1)^{n-1}n\,z^n = \frac{z}{(1+z)^2} = -k(-z) \in S$$
(since $k\in S$ and $f(z)\mapsto -f(-z)$ preserves univalence and normalization)
. However, applying this multiplier to the Koebe function $k(z)=z/(1-z)^2=\sum n
\,z^n$:
$$\sum \lambda_n\cdot n\, z^n = \sum (-1)^{n-1}n^2\,z^n = \frac{z(1-z)}{(1+z)^3}
=:h(z).$$
We compute $h'(z)=\frac{1-4z+z^2}{(1+z)^4}$, which vanishes at $z=2-\sqrt{3}\in(
0,1)\subset\mathbb{D}$. Since a univalent function has non-vanishing derivative
in $\mathbb{D}$, $h\notin S$. Thus $g\in S$ but $(\lambda_n)$ is **not** a multi
plier from $S$ to $S$.

**Known sufficient conditions.**
- **Dilation:** $(t^{n-1})$ for $0<t\leq 1$ is a multiplier, since $\frac{1}{t}f
(tz)\in S$ whenever $f\in S$.
- **Convex multipliers:** If $g(z)=\sum\lambda_n z^n\in C$, then by the RSS theo
rem $C*C\subseteq C\subseteq S$, so $(\lambda_n)$ maps $C$ to $C\subset S$. Howe
ver, this only guarantees the multiplier property on $C$, not on all of $S$. Whe
ther $C*S\subseteq S$ holds is itself doubtful.

**Conclusion for (a).** The necessary condition $g=\sum\lambda_n z^n\in S$ (with
 $\lambda_1=1$) is **not sufficient**. The complete characterization of multipli
ers from $S$ to $S$ remains an **open problem**. The gap between the necessary c
ondition ($g\in S$) and the best known sufficient conditions (e.g., $g\in C$, or
 dilation sequences) has not been closed.

## Part (b): Multipliers from $S$ to $S$ (general, no normalization)

Here $S$ denotes all univalent functions $\sum a_n z^n$ in $\mathbb{D}$ (with $a
_1\neq 0$, but no fixed normalization).

**Necessary conditions.**
1. **$\lambda_1\neq 0$:** $f(z)=z\in S$ gives $\lambda_1 z\in S$, requiring $\la
mbda_1\neq 0$.
2. **$g(z)=\sum\lambda_n z^n\in S$:** From $f(z)=z/(1-z)\in S$ as before.
3. **$\sum n\lambda_n z^n\in S$ and all rotations thereof:** From the Koebe func
tion and its rotations.

**The same counterexample applies.** The sequence $\lambda_n=(-1)^{n-1}n$ gives
$g(z)=z/(1+z)^2\in S$, but maps $k(z)=z/(1-z)^2\in S$ to $z(1-z)/(1+z)^3\notin S
$.

**Conclusion for (b).** The characterization is the same open problem as in (a),
 with the only difference being $\lambda_1\neq 0$ instead of $\lambda_1=1$. The
RSS-type characterization ($g\in S$ iff multiplier) **fails**, and no complete d
escription is known.

## Part (c): Multipliers of close-to-convex functions into themselves

Let $K$ denote the class of close-to-convex functions.

**Necessary condition.** If $(\lambda_n)$ is a multiplier from $K$ to $K$, then
since $z/(1-z)\in C\subseteq K$, we need $g(z)=\sum\lambda_n z^n\in K$.

**Sufficient condition (RSS).** By the Ruscheweyh–Sheil-Small theorem, $K*C\subs
eteq K$. Therefore, if $g(z)=\sum\lambda_n z^n\in C$, then $f*g\in K$ for every
$f\in K$, so $(\lambda_n)$ is a multiplier from $K$ to $K$.

**The natural conjecture fails.** The conjecture "$g\in K$ iff $(\lambda_n)$ is
a multiplier from $K$ to $K$" would require $K*K\subseteq K$. This is **false**:
 take $f(z)=z/(1-z)^2\in S^*\subseteq K$ and $g(z)=z/(1+z)^2\in S^*\subseteq K$.
 Then
$$f*g = \frac{z(1-z)}{(1+z)^3}$$
has $h'(2-\sqrt{3})=0$, so $h\notin S$, hence $h\notin K$. Thus $K*K\not\subsete
q K$.

**Conclusion for (c).** A **sufficient** condition is $g=\sum\lambda_n z^n\in C$
; a **necessary** condition is $g\in K$. The strict inclusion $C\subsetneq K$ le
aves a gap, and the naive conjecture ($g\in K$) is refuted by the counterexample
 above. The complete characterization is **open**.

## Part (d): Multipliers from $S$ to $C$

**Necessary condition.** If $(\lambda_n)$ is a multiplier from $S$ to $C$, then
from $f(z)=z/(1-z)\in S$ we get $g(z)=\sum\lambda_n z^n\in C$.

**The necessary condition is not sufficient.** Take $\lambda_n=1$ for all $n$, s
o $g(z)=z/(1-z)\in C$. Apply to the Koebe function $k(z)=z/(1-z)^2\in S$:
$$\sum \lambda_n\cdot n\, z^n = \sum n\,z^n = \frac{z}{(1-z)^2}=k(z)\notin C$$
(the Koebe function is starlike but not convex). So $(\lambda_n)\equiv 1$ satisf
ies $g\in C$ but is not a multiplier from $S$ to $C$.

**A sharp result for dilation sequences.** The **radius of convexity** of $S$ is
 $r_0 = 2-\sqrt{3}$. This means every $f\in S$ is convex in $|z|<r_0$, and the K
oebe function shows this is sharp.

For the dilation multiplier $\lambda_n = t^{n-1}$: the function $\frac{1}{t}f(tz
)$ is convex in $|z|<1$ iff $f$ is convex in $|z|<t$. Therefore:

> **$(t^{n-1})$ is a multiplier from $S$ to $C$ if and only if $t\leq 2-\sqrt{3}
$.**

*Proof.* If $t\leq 2-\sqrt{3}$, then for every $f\in S$, $f$ is convex in $|z|<t
$ (by definition of radius of convexity), so $\frac{1}{t}f(tz)$ is convex in $|z
|<1$. Conversely, if $t>2-\sqrt{3}$, the Koebe function $k(z)=z/(1-z)^2$ is not
convex in $|z|<t$ (its derivative $k'(z)=(1+z)/(1-z)^3$ yields $\operatorname{Re
}(1+zk''/k')=\operatorname{Re}\frac{1+4tz+t^2z^2}{1-t^2z^2}$, which is negative
at $z=-1$ when $t>2-\sqrt{3}$, since $1-4t+t^2<0$). $\square$

**Conclusion for (d).** The necessary condition $g\in C$ is **not sufficient**.
The class of multipliers from $S$ to $C$ is strictly smaller than $C$ and strict
ly larger than just $\{0\}$ (it contains all dilations with $t\leq 2-\sqrt{3}$).
 A complete characterization is **open**. The multipliers must be "small enough"
 to force convexity even on the most extremal univalent functions.

## Part (e): Multipliers from $N$ to $N$ (and $N^+$ to $N^+$)

**The class $N$ (Nevanlinna class).** $f\in N$ iff $\sup_{0<r<1}\frac{1}{2\pi}\i
nt_0^{2\pi}\log^+|f(re^{i\theta})|\,d\theta<\infty$. Key facts:
- If $\sum|a_n|<\infty$, then $f\in N$ (indeed $f\in H^p$ for all $p>0$).
- If $f\in N$, then $|a_n|\leq e^{c\sqrt{n}}$ for some $c>0$.

**The class $N^+$ (Smirnov class).** $N^+ = \{f\in N : f=g/h,\; g,h\in H^\infty,
\; |h(e^{i\theta})|=1 \text{ a.e.}\}$. Equivalently, $f\in N^+$ iff $f\in N$ and
 $f$ admits a factorization $f = I\cdot O$ (inner times outer) without an additi
onal "denominator" that is not inner. The crucial property (Smirnov's theorem):
if $f\in N^+$ and $|f(e^{i\theta})|\in L^p$ for some $p>0$, then $f\in H^p$.

**Known results for $N$.**
- **Dilation is a multiplier:** $(t^{n-1})$ for $0<t<1$ is a multiplier from $N$
 to $N$, since $\frac{1}{t}f(tz)\in N$ whenever $f\in N$ (the Nevanlinna charact
eristic is monotone under dilation).
- **$(n)$ is NOT a multiplier (Frostman):** There exists $f\in N$ such that $\su
m n\,a_n z^n\notin N$. Intuitively, differentiation (which corresponds to multip
lying coefficients by $n$) can destroy bounded characteristic.
- **$(1/(n+1))$ is NOT a multiplier (Duren):** There exists $f\in N$ such that $
\sum a_n z^n/(n+1)\notin N$. This is more subtle; integration (which corresponds
 to dividing by $n+1$) can also destroy the Nevanlinna property, because the Smi
rnov theorem can fail: if $f\in N$ but $f\notin N^+$, then integrating can produ
ce a function whose boundary values are too large.

**Necessary condition.** If $(\lambda_n)$ is a multiplier from $N$ to $N$, then
from $f(z)=1/(1-z)=\sum z^n\in N$ (in fact $\in H^\infty$... well, $1/(1-z)\noti
n H^\infty$ but $\in N$ since $\log^+|1/(1-re^{i\theta})|$ is integrable), we ne
ed $g(z)=\sum\lambda_n z^n\in N$.

**Sufficient condition.** If $\sum|\lambda_n|<\infty$ (i.e., $g\in$ Wiener algeb
ra), then for $f\in N$ with $|a_n|\leq e^{c\sqrt{n}}$, we have $|\lambda_n a_n|\
leq |\lambda_n| e^{c\sqrt{n}}$, and $\sum|\lambda_n|e^{c\sqrt{n}}<\infty$ (since
 $\lambda_n$ decays faster than any exponential by summability), so $\sum\lambda
_n a_n z^n$ has absolutely summable coefficients, hence is in $N$.

**The $N^+$ case.** The problem suggests $N^+$ is more tractable. The key advant
age: $N^+$ is closed under division by inner functions and satisfies Smirnov's t
heorem. For $N^+$:
- Dilation $(t^{n-1})$ is still a multiplier.
- The negative results for $(n)$ and $(1/(n+1))$ may or may not extend to $N^+$;
 the structure of $N^+$ (particularly the Smirnov theorem) provides additional c
ontrol.

**Conclusion for (e).** The multiplier problem for $N$ (and $N^+$) is **open**.
Known multipliers include dilation sequences $(t^{n-1})$ and sequences with $\su
m|\lambda_n|<\infty$. Known non-multipliers include $(n)$ (Frostman) and $(1/(n+
1))$ (Duren). The $N^+$ case, while suggested as more tractable due to the bette
r structural properties of the Smirnov class, has not been completely resolved.
