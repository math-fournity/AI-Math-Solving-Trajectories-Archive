# Solution

**Answer: Yes, it is always possible.**

## Proof

Let $R$ be a principal ideal domain, and let $A$ be a $k \times n$ matrix over $
R$ with $1 \le k < n$, such that the greatest common divisor of all $k \times k$
 minors of $A$ is $1$.

### Step 1: Invariance of the minor-gcd under unimodular equivalence

**Claim:** If $U \in \mathrm{GL}_k(R)$ and $V \in \mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ are unimodul
ar, then the gcd of the $k \times k$ minors of $A$ equals the gcd of the $k \tim
es k$ minors of $UAV$.

*Proof of claim.* By the Cauchy–Binet formula, each $k \times k$ minor of $UA$ i
s an $R$-linear combination of $k \times k$ minors of $A$ (the coefficients are
$k \times k$ minors of $U$). Hence the ideal $I_k(UA) \subseteq I_k(A)$, where $
I_k(\cdot)$ denotes the ideal generated by all $k \times k$ minors. Since $U$ is
 invertible, $A = U^{-1}(UA)$ gives the reverse inclusion, so $I_k(UA) = I_k(A)$
. The same argument with $V$ (using Cauchy–Binet on the right) shows $I_k(AV) =
I_k(A)$. Combining, $I_k(UAV) = I_k(A)$. Since $R$ is a PID, equality of ideals
means equality of gcds (up to units). $\square$

### Step 2: Reduction to Smith Normal Form

Over a PID, every matrix admits a Smith Normal Form: there exist $U \in \mathrm{
GL}_k(R)$ and $V \in \mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ such that

$$D := UAV = \begin{pmatrix} d_1 & & & & & \\ & d_2 & & & & \\ & & \ddots & & &
\\ & & & d_k & 0 \cdots 0 \end{pmatrix},$$

where $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_k$ and all off-diagonal entries are zero
(the diagonal entries $d_i$ occupy positions $(i,i)$ for $i = 1, \ldots, k$, wit
h the remaining $n - k$ columns being zero).

The $k \times k$ minors of $D$: to get a nonzero $k \times k$ minor, we must sel
ect $k$ columns. Since $D$ has nonzero entries only in columns $1, \ldots, k$, t
he only nonzero $k \times k$ minor is obtained by selecting columns $1, \ldots,
k$, yielding

$$\det(D_{[:,\,1..k]}) = d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k.$$

All other $k \times k$ minors are zero. Hence the gcd of all $k \times k$ minors
 of $D$ is $d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k$ (up to a unit).

By Step 1, this equals the gcd of $k \times k$ minors of $A$, which is $1$. Ther
efore $d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k$ is a unit in $R$. Since $R$ is an integral domain and
 $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_k$, each $d_i$ must be a unit.

### Step 3: Normalizing to the standard form

Since each $d_i$ is a unit, let $U' = \mathrm{diag}(d_1^{-1}, \ldots, d_k^{-1})
\in \mathrm{GL}_k(R)$. Then

$$U' D = U' U A V = \begin{pmatrix} I_k & 0_{k \times (n-k)} \end{pmatrix}.$$

Set $\tilde{U} = U'U \in \mathrm{GL}_k(R)$. We have

$$\tilde{U} A V = \begin{pmatrix} I_k & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

### Step 4: Constructing $B$

We seek an $(n-k) \times n$ matrix $B$ such that $M := \begin{pmatrix} A \\ B \e
nd{pmatrix} \in \mathrm{GL}_n(R)$, i.e., $\det(M)$ is a unit.

From $\tilde{U} A V = \begin{pmatrix} I_k & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, we get $A = \tilde
{U}^{-1} \begin{pmatrix} I_k & 0 \end{pmatrix} V^{-1}$.

**Choose** $B := \begin{pmatrix} 0_{(n-k)\times k} & I_{n-k} \end{pmatrix} V^{-1
}$.

Then:

$$M = \begin{pmatrix} A \\ B \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \tilde{U}^{-1} \beg
in{pmatrix} I_k & 0 \end{pmatrix} V^{-1} \\[4pt] \begin{pmatrix} 0 & I_{n-k} \en
d{pmatrix} V^{-1} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \tilde{U}^{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & I_{n
-k} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} I_k & 0 \\ 0 & I_{n-k} \end{pmatrix} V^{-1}.$$

Simplifying:

$$M = \begin{pmatrix} \tilde{U}^{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & I_{n-k} \end{pmatrix} \cdot I_n
\cdot V^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} \tilde{U}^{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & I_{n-k} \end{pmatrix} V
^{-1}.$$

Now:
- $\begin{pmatrix} \tilde{U}^{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & I_{n-k} \end{pmatrix}$ is unimodula
r because $\tilde{U}^{-1} \in \mathrm{GL}_k(R)$, so its determinant is $\det(\ti
lde{U}^{-1}) \cdot 1 = \det(\tilde{U})^{-1}$, a unit.
- $V^{-1} \in \mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ is unimodular.

The product of two unimodular matrices is unimodular. Therefore $M = \begin{pmat
rix} A \\ B \end{pmatrix}$ is unimodular.

### Conclusion

We have explicitly constructed

$$B = \begin{pmatrix} 0_{(n-k)\times k} & I_{n-k} \end{pmatrix} V^{-1},$$

where $V$ is the unimodular matrix from the Smith Normal Form decomposition $\ti
lde{U}AV = \begin{pmatrix} I_k & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. This $B$ makes $\begin{pmatri
x} A \\ B \end{pmatrix}$ unimodular.

The only hypothesis used was that the gcd of $k \times k$ minors of $A$ is $1$,
which forced all Smith Normal Form diagonal entries to be units, reducing $A$ to
 the standard form $\begin{pmatrix} I_k & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which is trivially c
ompletable to $I_n$.

**Therefore, it is always possible to find such a matrix $B$.** $\blacksquare$
