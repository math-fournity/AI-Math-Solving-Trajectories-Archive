## Solution

We evaluate $I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\cos x - 1}{x^2}\,dx$.

**Setup.** Since $\cos x = \operatorname{Re}(e^{ix})$, we introduce the complex
function
$$f(z) = \frac{e^{iz}-1}{z^2},$$
which is meromorphic on $\mathbb{C}$ with a single pole at $z=0$. Near the origi
n,
$$e^{iz}-1 = iz - \frac{z^2}{2} - \frac{iz^3}{6}+\cdots \implies f(z) = \frac{i}
{z} - \frac{1}{2} - \frac{iz}{6}+\cdots,$$
so $z=0$ is a **simple pole** with residue $\operatorname{Res}(f,0)=i$.

**Contour.** Let $\mathcal{C}$ be the positively oriented contour consisting of:
- the real segment $[-R,-\varepsilon]$,
- the small upper semicircle $C_\varepsilon: z=\varepsilon e^{i\theta}$, $\theta
:\pi\to 0$ (clockwise, indenting above the pole),
- the real segment $[\varepsilon, R]$,
- the large upper semicircle $C_R: z=Re^{i\theta}$, $\theta:0\to\pi$.

Since $f$ is analytic on and inside $\mathcal{C}$ (the pole at $0$ is excluded b
y the indentation), Cauchy's theorem gives
$$\int_{-R}^{-\varepsilon} f(x)\,dx + \int_{C_\varepsilon} f(z)\,dz + \int_{\var
epsilon}^{R} f(x)\,dx + \int_{C_R} f(z)\,dz = 0. \tag{1}$$

**Large arc $C_R$.** Split $f = e^{iz}/z^2 - 1/z^2$.

- $\displaystyle\int_{C_R}\frac{dz}{z^2} = \frac{i}{R}\int_0^\pi e^{-i\theta}\,d
\theta = \frac{2}{R}\xrightarrow[R\to\infty]{}0.$
- For $\displaystyle\int_{C_R}\frac{e^{iz}}{z^2}\,dz$: on $C_R$, $|e^{iz}|=e^{-R
\sin\theta}$, so by Jordan's lemma
$$\left|\int_{C_R}\frac{e^{iz}}{z^2}\,dz\right| \le \frac{1}{R}\int_0^\pi e^{-R\
sin\theta}\,d\theta \xrightarrow[R\to\infty]{}0.$$

Hence $\int_{C_R} f(z)\,dz \to 0$ as $R\to\infty$.

**Small arc $C_\varepsilon$.** Using the Laurent expansion $f(z)=\frac{i}{z}-\fr
ac12+O(z)$:
$$\int_{C_\varepsilon} f(z)\,dz = \int_{C_\varepsilon}\frac{i}{z}\,dz + \int_{C_
\varepsilon}\!\left(-\tfrac12+O(z)\right)dz.$$
The second integral is $O(\varepsilon)\to 0$. For the first, with $z=\varepsilon
 e^{i\theta}$, $\theta:\pi\to 0$:
$$\int_{C_\varepsilon}\frac{i}{z}\,dz = i\int_\pi^0 \frac{i\varepsilon e^{i\thet
a}}{\varepsilon e^{i\theta}}\,d\theta = i^2\int_\pi^0 d\theta = -(-\pi) = \pi.$$
Thus $\int_{C_\varepsilon} f(z)\,dz \to \pi$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$.

**Taking the limit.** Letting $R\to\infty$ and $\varepsilon\to 0$ in (1):
$$\operatorname{PV}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ix}-1}{x^2}\,dx + \pi + 0 =
 0 \implies \operatorname{PV}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ix}-1}{x^2}\,dx =
 -\pi. \tag{2}$$

**Extracting the answer.** Taking real parts of (2):
$$\operatorname{PV}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos x - 1}{x^2}\,dx = -\pi.$$
(The imaginary part $\operatorname{PV}\!\int \frac{\sin x}{x^2}\,dx = 0$ is cons
istent with it being the principal value of an odd integrand.)

Finally, $\frac{\cos x - 1}{x^2}$ has a **removable** singularity at $x=0$ (its
limit is $-\tfrac12$), so the principal value coincides with the ordinary conver
gent integral. Therefore

$$\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos x - 1}{x^2}\,dx = -\pi.}$$
