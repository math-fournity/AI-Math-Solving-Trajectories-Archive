Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether pairwise independence of $X
$, $Y$, and $X\oplus Y$ from $B$ implies joint independence of $(X,Y)$ from $B$.

**Answer: Yes, $I(X,Y:B)=0$.**

**Proof.**

Since $X$ and $Y$ are independent uniform bits, the joint distribution $P(X,Y)$
is uniform on $\{0,1\}^2$, i.e., $P(X=x,Y=y)=\tfrac{1}{4}$ for all $(x,y)$.

The conditions $I(X:B)=0$, $I(Y:B)=0$, $I(X\oplus Y:B)=0$ mean that $X$, $Y$, an
d $X\oplus Y$ are each (marginally) independent of $B$.

Fix any value $b$ in the support of $B$. Define the conditional probabilities:
$$p_{xy} := P(X=x,\; Y=y \mid B=b), \qquad x,y \in \{0,1\}.$$

These satisfy $\sum_{x,y} p_{xy} = 1$. The three independence conditions impose:

1. **$X \perp B$:** $P(X=0\mid B=b)=\tfrac12$, so $p_{00}+p_{01}=\tfrac12$ (and
$p_{10}+p_{11}=\tfrac12$).
2. **$Y \perp B$:** $P(Y=0\mid B=b)=\tfrac12$, so $p_{00}+p_{10}=\tfrac12$ (and
$p_{01}+p_{11}=\tfrac12$).
3. **$X\oplus Y \perp B$:** $P(X\oplus Y=0\mid B=b)=\tfrac12$, so $p_{00}+p_{11}
=\tfrac12$ (and $p_{01}+p_{10}=\tfrac12$).

From (1) and (2): $p_{00}+p_{01}=\tfrac12$ and $p_{00}+p_{10}=\tfrac12$, giving
$p_{01}=p_{10}$.

From (1) and (3): $p_{00}+p_{01}=\tfrac12$ and $p_{00}+p_{11}=\tfrac12$, giving
$p_{01}=p_{11}$.

So $p_{01}=p_{10}=p_{11}=:q$. Then $p_{00}=\tfrac12 - q$. Using the normalizatio
n $\sum p_{xy}=1$:
$$(\tfrac12 - q) + q + q + q = 1 \implies \tfrac12 + 2q = 1 \implies q = \tfrac1
4.$$

Therefore:
$$p_{00}=p_{01}=p_{10}=p_{11}=\tfrac14.$$

This holds for **every** $b$. The conditional distribution $P(X,Y\mid B=b)$ is t
he uniform distribution on $\{0,1\}^2$, identical to the marginal $P(X,Y)$, rega
rdless of $b$. Hence:

$$P(X=x, Y=y \mid B=b) = P(X=x, Y=y) \quad \text{for all } x,y,b,$$

which is precisely $(X,Y) \perp B$, i.e., $I(X,Y:B)=0$. $\blacksquare$

**Key insight:** For two binary variables, the three constraints $X\perp B$, $Y\
perp B$, $X\oplus Y\perp B$ provide enough equations (together with normalizatio
n) to pin down all four entries of the conditional joint distribution to $\tfrac
14$ each. The system is exactly determined—there is no remaining degree of freed
om that could depend on $b$, so no counterexample exists.
