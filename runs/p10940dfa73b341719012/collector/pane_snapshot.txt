## Substitution Rule

Define the digit substitution $\sigma : \{0,1,\dots,9\} \to \{1,2\}$ by

$$
\sigma(d) = \begin{cases} 2 & \text{if } d = 1, \\ 1 & \text{if } d \neq 1. \end
{cases}
$$

**Key properties:**

1. **No fixed point:** For every $d \in \{0,\dots,9\}$, $\sigma(d) \neq d$. (If
$d=1$, $\sigma(d)=2\neq 1$; if $d\neq 1$, $\sigma(d)=1\neq d$ since $d\in\{0,2,3
,\dots,9\}$.)
2. **Range avoids $0$ and $9$:** $\sigma(d)\in\{1,2\}$ for all $d$, so the const
ructed number never contains the digit $0$ or $9$.

Property (2) is the crucial extra feature: a decimal whose digits all lie in $\{
1,2\}$ has a **unique** decimal representation, since the non-uniqueness of deci
mal expansion arises *only* from trailing strings of $9$s (equivalently trailing
 $0$s). A number with no $0$ or $9$ digit cannot be a "trailing $9$s" or "traili
ng $0$s" expansion of any other decimal, so its representation is unambiguous.

## Proof (Uncountability of $(0,1)$)

**Theorem.** The open interval $(0,1)$ is uncountable.

**Proof.** Suppose, for contradiction, that $(0,1)$ is countable. Then there exi
sts a sequence (list) $\{x_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ enumerating every real number in
$(0,1)$. Write each $x_n$ in decimal notation, choosing the representation that
does **not** end in trailing $9$s (this is the unique non-terminating-in-$9$s re
presentation, which exists for every real in $(0,1)$):

$$
x_n = 0.d_{n,1}\, d_{n,2}\, d_{n,3}\, \cdots, \qquad d_{n,k}\in\{0,1,\dots,9\}.
$$

Construct a new number $y = 0.e_1\, e_2\, e_3\, \cdots$ by applying the substitu
tion rule to the diagonal digits:

$$
e_n = \sigma(d_{n,n}) \quad \text{for each } n\ge 1.
$$

**Claim 1: $y \in (0,1)$.** Since every $e_n \in \{1,2\}$, we have $y \ge 0.111\
ldots > 0$ and $y \le 0.222\ldots < 1$. Hence $y\in(0,1)$.

**Claim 2: $y \neq x_n$ for every $n$.** By construction, $e_n = \sigma(d_{n,n})
 \neq d_{n,n}$ (no fixed point). So $y$ and $x_n$ differ in the $n$-th decimal d
igit.

We must verify this digit-difference implies genuine numerical difference, not m
erely a notational artifact. Two distinct decimal strings represent the same rea
l number *only if* one ends in trailing $9$s and the other in trailing $0$s (e.g
., $0.12999\ldots = 0.13000\ldots$). But:

- $y$ contains no digit $0$ or $9$ (all digits are $1$ or $2$), so $y$'s decimal
 expansion is neither a trailing-$9$s form nor a trailing-$0$s form.
- $x_n$ was chosen in its non-trailing-$9$s representation.

The only way two decimal strings differing at some position $n$ could still be e
qual is the trailing-$9$s/$0$s ambiguity. Since $y$ has no $0$ or $9$ digit at a
ll, this ambiguity cannot arise. Therefore $y \neq x_n$ as real numbers.

**Claim 3: Contradiction.** $y\in(0,1)$ but $y\neq x_n$ for every $n$, contradic
ting the assumption that $\{x_n\}$ enumerates all of $(0,1)$.

Hence no countable list can enumerate $(0,1)$; the interval is uncountable. $\bl
acksquare$

**Remark on why the standard rule fails and this one succeeds.** The naive rule
"$e_n = (d_{n,n}+1)\bmod 10$" has no fixed point but can produce $0$ or $9$, lea
ving the diagonal number vulnerable to the $0.999\ldots = 1.000\ldots$ ambiguity
 — one cannot conclude $y\neq x_n$ from a single digit difference when that diff
erence is exactly $9$-vs-$0$ at the start of a tail. Restricting the range to $\
{1,2\}$ (or any subset of $\{1,\dots,8\}$) eliminates this ambiguity entirely, m
aking the diagonal argument rigorous without any case analysis on the list entri
es.
