## Proof

**Claim:** It is **not** necessarily true that $R = S$.

**Counterexample.** Let $R = \mathbb{Q}$ (the field of rational numbers) and $S
= \mathbb{Z}$ (the ring of integers).

- $R = \mathbb{Q}$ is an integral domain (in fact, a field).
- $S = \mathbb{Z}$ is a unital subring of $R$: it is closed under addition, addi
tive inverses, and multiplication, and it contains the same multiplicative ident
ity $1 \in \mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}$.
- The field of fractions of $R = \mathbb{Q}$ is $F(\mathbb{Q}) = \mathbb{Q}$ its
elf, since $\mathbb{Q}$ is already a field.
- The field of fractions of $S = \mathbb{Z}$ is $F(\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Q}$, si
nce every rational number $\frac{a}{b}$ (with $a \in \mathbb{Z}$, $b \in \mathbb
{Z}\setminus\{0\}$) is a ratio of two integers.

Therefore $F(R) = F(S) = \mathbb{Q}$, yet $R = \mathbb{Q} \neq \mathbb{Z} = S$ (
for instance, $\frac{1}{2} \in R$ but $\frac{1}{2} \notin S$).

This provides an explicit counterexample, so the implication "$F(R) = F(S) \impl
ies R = S$" is **false** in general. $\blacksquare$
