# Comparing the Risk of the MLE and the James-Stein Estimator

## Setup

Let $X \sim N(\theta, I_n)$ with $X, \theta \in \mathbb{R}^n$. We estimate $\the
ta$ under squared-error loss
$$L(\theta, \delta) = \|\delta(X) - \theta\|^2, \qquad R(\theta,\delta) = \mathb
b{E}_\theta\|\delta(X)-\theta\|^2.$$

Two estimators:

- **MLE**: $\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = X$.
- **James-Stein (JS)**: $\hat\theta_{\text{JS}} = \left(1 - \dfrac{n-2}{\|X\|^2}
\right)X$, valid (dominating) for $n \ge 3$.
- **Positive-part JS**: $\hat\theta_{\text{JS}^+} = \left(1 - \dfrac{n-2}{\|X\|^
2}\right)^+ X$.

## Theoretical Risk

**MLE.** Since $X-\theta \sim N(0,I_n)$, $\|X-\theta\|^2 \sim \chi^2_n$, so
$$R(\theta, \hat\theta_{\text{MLE}}) = n \quad \text{for all } \theta.$$
The MLE risk is constant and equal to the dimension.

**James-Stein.** A standard application of Stein's lemma gives the exact risk
$$R(\theta, \hat\theta_{\text{JS}}) = n - (n-2)^2\,\mathbb{E}_\theta\!\left[\fra
c{1}{\|X\|^2}\right].$$

Writing $\|X\|^2 \sim \chi^2_n(\lambda)$ with noncentrality $\lambda = \|\theta\
|^2/2$, one obtains
$$\mathbb{E}_\theta\!\left[\frac{1}{\|X\|^2}\right] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}
{\chi'^2_n(\lambda)}\right],$$
which is strictly positive and decreasing in $\lambda$. Consequently:

- **At $\theta = 0$**: $\|X\|^2 \sim \chi^2_n$, $\mathbb{E}[1/\chi^2_n] = 1/(n-2
)$, so
  $$R(0, \hat\theta_{\text{JS}}) = n - (n-2)^2\cdot\frac{1}{n-2} = 2.$$
  The JS risk at the origin is exactly **2**, independent of $n$ (for $n\ge 3$).

- **As $\|\theta\|\to\infty$**: $\mathbb{E}[1/\|X\|^2]\to 0$, so $R(\theta,\hat\
theta_{\text{JS}})\to n$; the two estimators become equivalent.

- **For all $\theta$ and $n\ge 3$**: $R(\theta,\hat\theta_{\text{JS}}) < n = R(\
theta,\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}})$ — the JS estimator **strictly dominates** the ML
E. This is the celebrated inadmissibility result of James & Stein (1961).

The positive-part version satisfies $R(\theta,\hat\theta_{\text{JS}^+}) \le R(\t
heta,\hat\theta_{\text{JS}})$, with strict improvement near the origin and on th
e region where the shrinkage factor would be negative.

## Observed Trends

1. **MLE risk is constant and equals $n$.** This is confirmed empirically: regar
dless of $\theta$, the average squared error of $X$ hovers around $n$ (e.g. $\ap
prox 3.00, 5.00, 10.00$), matching $\mathbb{E}\chi^2_n = n$.

2. **JS strictly dominates MLE for every $\theta$ when $n\ge 3$.** In every row
the JS (and JS$^+$) risk is below $n$. This is the empirical manifestation of Ja
mes-Stein domination.

3. **Largest gains at $\theta = 0$.** At the origin the JS risk collapses to exa
ctly $2$ regardless of dimension, while the MLE risk grows linearly with $n$. Th
e percentage reduction therefore grows with $n$: $33\% \to 60\% \to 80\%$ for $n
=3,5,10$. Shrinkage toward $0$ is maximally beneficial when the truth really is
near $0$.

4. **Gains increase with dimension.** Holding $\|\theta\|$ roughly fixed, the ga
p $n - R(\theta,\hat\theta_{\text{JS}})$ grows with $n$ because the shrinkage fa
ctor $(n-2)/\|X\|^2$ has more "room" to act. Higher-dimensional estimation benef
its more from borrowing strength across coordinates.

5. **Gains shrink as $\|\theta\|$ grows.** For sparse $\theta=(3,0,\dots,0)$ the
 noncentrality $\lambda = 9/2$ is moderate, so $\mathbb{E}[1/\|X\|^2]$ is small
and the JS risk approaches $n$. As $\|\theta\|\to\infty$ the shrinkage factor te
nds to $1$ and JS $\to$ MLE; the domination becomes negligible. This is the "con
sistency" of JS — it never does much worse than MLE, and in the worst case it is
 asymptotically equal.

6. **Positive-part JS is uniformly no better-than and often better than ordinary
 JS.** The improvement is most visible near $\theta=0$ and in low dimensions whe
re the unshrunk factor $1-(n-2)/\|X\|^2$ is frequently negative; truncating it t
o $0$ removes sign-flip errors and strictly lowers risk on that region. The gap
between JS and JS$^+$ narrows as $\|\theta\|$ or $n$ grows.

7. **Sparse vs dense $\theta$ at equal $\|\theta\|$.** With the same $\|\theta\|
^2$, the risk depends only on $\lambda = \|\theta\|^2/2$ (it is spherically symm
etric in $\theta$), so sparse and dense configurations with equal norm yield ide
ntical JS risk. What changes between "sparse" and "dense" rows above is the norm
, not the sparsity per se. The practical takeaway: JS helps whenever the *total*
 signal energy $\|\theta\|^2$ is not vastly larger than $n$; the internal layout
 of $\theta$ is irrelevant to the risk.

## Conclusion

The simulation confirms the theory: for $n \ge 3$ the James-Stein estimator has
strictly lower squared-error risk than the MLE for **every** value of $\theta$,
with the advantage being

- largest when $\theta$ is near $0$ (risk drops from $n$ to $2$),
- increasing in dimension $n$,
- vanishing as $\|\theta\|\to\infty$ (JS becomes equivalent to MLE), and
- slightly further improved by taking the positive part.

The MLE, despite being unbiased and minimax with constant risk $n$, is **inadmis
sible** in dimension $3$ and higher — a striking illustration that admissibility
 and unbiasedness are not the same, and that shrinking toward a common point can
 simultaneously reduce risk in every direction.
