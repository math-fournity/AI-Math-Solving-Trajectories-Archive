## Proof

**Yes**, such non-identity matrices exist. The key observation is that the ident
ity $e^{X+Y} = e^X e^Y$ holds whenever $X$ and $Y$ commute (i.e., $XY = YX$), si
nce the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff series then collapses to $Z = X + Y$. We need o
nly exhibit two **non-identity** commuting matrices.

### Explicit example

Take the following $2 \times 2$ real matrices:

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
Y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Both are non-identity.** $X \neq I$ (its $(2,2)$ entry is $0$, not $1$), and $
Y \neq I$ (its $(1,1)$ entry is $0$, not $1$).

**They commute.** Both are diagonal, so

$$
XY = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = YX.
$$

**Compute each side.** Since $X$ and $Y$ are diagonal, exponentiation is entrywi
se:

$$
e^X = \begin{pmatrix} e & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
e^Y = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Hence

$$
e^X e^Y = \begin{pmatrix} e & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0
 & e \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} e & 0 \\ 0 & e \end{pmatrix} = e \cdot I.
$$

On the other hand,

$$
X + Y = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = I,
$$

so

$$
e^{X+Y} = e^I = e \cdot I = \begin{pmatrix} e & 0 \\ 0 & e \end{pmatrix}.
$$

### Conclusion

$$
e^{X+Y} = e \cdot I = e^X e^Y,
$$

with both $X$ and $Y$ non-identity matrices. Therefore such matrices exist.

(More generally, *any* pair of commuting non-identity matrices works, since $e^{
X+Y} = e^X e^Y$ is guaranteed whenever $[X,Y] = 0$.)
