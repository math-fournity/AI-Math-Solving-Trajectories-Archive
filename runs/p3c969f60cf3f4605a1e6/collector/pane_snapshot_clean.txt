## Proof

**Claim:** $X = \{f \in C^\infty[0,1] : f^{(n)}(0)=f^{(n)}(1)=0 \ \forall\, n\ge
q 0\}$ is dense in $L^2[0,1]$.

**Key observation.** Let $C_c^\infty(0,1)$ denote the space of smooth functions
on $[0,1]$ whose support is a compact subset of the open interval $(0,1)$. We cl
aim
$$
C_c^\infty(0,1) \subseteq X.
$$

Indeed, if $f \in C_c^\infty(0,1)$, then $\operatorname{supp}(f) \subset (0,1)$
is compact, so there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ with $\operatorname{supp}(f) \subs
eteq [\varepsilon, 1-\varepsilon]$. Consequently $f \equiv 0$ on the neighborhoo
ds $[0,\varepsilon)$ and $(1-\varepsilon, 1]$ of the endpoints. Viewing $f$ as a
 $C^\infty$ function on $[0,1]$ (it is smooth across the points $\varepsilon$ an
d $1-\varepsilon$ since it is identically $0$ on one side), every derivative $f^
{(n)}$ also vanishes identically near $0$ and near $1$. Hence
$$
f^{(n)}(0) = 0 = f^{(n)}(1) \quad \text{for all } n \geq 0,
$$
so $f \in X$.

**Density of $C_c^\infty(0,1)$ in $L^2[0,1]$.** This is a standard fact. For com
pleteness, here is the argument:

- The set of continuous functions on $[0,1]$ is dense in $L^2[0,1]$ (e.g. by Lus
in's theorem / dominated convergence, or by approximating simple functions).
- Given any $g \in L^2[0,1]$ and $\varepsilon > 0$, pick continuous $h$ on $[0,1
]$ with $\|g - h\|_{L^2} < \varepsilon/2$.
- Define $h_\delta(x) = h(x) \cdot \varphi_\delta(x)$, where $\varphi_\delta \in
 C_c^\infty(0,1)$ is a smooth cutoff with $\varphi_\delta \equiv 1$ on $[\delta,
 1-\delta]$, $0 \leq \varphi_\delta \leq 1$, and $\varphi_\delta \equiv 0$ on $[
0,\delta/2]\cup[1-\delta/2,1]$. Then $h_\delta \in C_c^\infty(0,1)$, and as $\de
lta \to 0$, $\|h - h_\delta\|_{L^2} \to 0$ by dominated convergence (since $h$ i
s bounded). Choose $\delta$ small enough that $\|h - h_\delta\|_{L^2} < \varepsi
lon/2$.
- Then $\|g - h_\delta\|_{L^2} < \varepsilon$ with $h_\delta \in C_c^\infty(0,1)
$.

Thus $C_c^\infty(0,1)$ is dense in $L^2[0,1]$.

**Conclusion.** We have the chain of inclusions
$$
C_c^\infty(0,1) \subseteq X \subseteq L^2[0,1].
$$
Since $C_c^\infty(0,1)$ is dense in $L^2[0,1]$ and is contained in $X$, the clos
ure of $X$ in $L^2[0,1]$ contains the closure of $C_c^\infty(0,1)$, which is all
 of $L^2[0,1]$. Therefore
$$
\overline{X}^{\,L^2} = L^2[0,1],
$$
i.e., $X$ is dense in $L^2[0,1]$.

**Remark.** The functions in $X$ are precisely the smooth functions that are "fl
at" at both endpoints. The standard bump function $e^{-1/x}$ (extended by $0$) a
nd its translates/scaled versions generate many nontrivial elements of $X$, but
the argument above shows that even the smaller, more familiar class $C_c^\infty(
0,1)$ already suffices for density. $\blacksquare$
