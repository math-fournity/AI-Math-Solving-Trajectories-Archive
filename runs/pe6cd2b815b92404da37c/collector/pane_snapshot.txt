# The Isoperimetric Problem

**Claim.** Among all closed plane curves of a given fixed length $L$, the one en
closing the maximum area is a **circle**. The extremal curve must satisfy the co
ndition of **constant curvature**.

---

## Proof

Let $\gamma: [0, 2\pi] \to \mathbb{R}^2$ be a simple closed $C^2$ plane curve, p
arametrized by arc length $s$ (so $|\gamma'(s)| = 1$), with total length $L$. We
 write
$$\gamma(s) = (x(s),\, y(s)), \qquad \theta(s) = \arg \gamma'(s),$$
so that
$$x'(s) = \cos\theta(s), \qquad y'(s) = \sin\theta(s), \qquad \kappa(s) = \theta
'(s),$$
where $\kappa$ is the (signed) curvature. The closing conditions are
$$\int_0^L \cos\theta(s)\,ds = 0, \qquad \int_0^L \sin\theta(s)\,ds = 0, \qquad
\int_0^L \kappa(s)\,ds = 2\pi \tag{1}$$
(the last is the rotation index theorem for a simple closed curve).

### Area as a functional of $\theta$

By Green's theorem, the enclosed area is
$$A = \tfrac12 \int_0^L \bigl(x\,y' - y\,x'\bigr)\,ds.$$
Integrating by parts and using $x'=\cos\theta$, $y'=\sin\theta$, one obtains the
 well-known Wirtinger-type formula
$$A = \tfrac12 \int_0^L \!\!\int_0^s \sin\!\bigl(\theta(t)-\theta(s)\bigr)\,dt\,
ds. \tag{2}$$

### Variational setup

We extremize $A$ subject to the three linear constraints (1) on $\theta$. Introd
uce Lagrange multipliers $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3$ and form
$$\mathcal{J}[\theta] = A[\theta] - \lambda_1\!\int_0^L\!\cos\theta\,ds - \lambd
a_2\!\int_0^L\!\sin\theta\,ds - \lambda_3\!\Bigl(\!\int_0^L\!\kappa\,ds - 2\pi\B
igr).$$

Taking the first variation $\theta \to \theta + \varepsilon\eta$ and using (2),
the variation of $A$ is
$$\delta A = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L\!\!\int_0^L \cos\!\bigl(\theta(t)-\theta(s)\big
r)\,\bigl(\eta(t)-\eta(s)\bigr)\,dt\,ds = \int_0^L \eta(s)\left[\int_0^L \cos\!\
bigl(\theta(t)-\theta(s)\bigr)\,dt\right] ds,$$
where the last equality uses the symmetry of the double integral. The variations
 of the constraints give $-\lambda_1\!\int\!(-\sin\theta)\eta\,ds$, etc. Setting
 the total first variation to zero for all $\eta$ yields the Euler–Lagrange equa
tion
$$\int_0^L \cos\!\bigl(\theta(t)-\theta(s)\bigr)\,dt \;+\; \lambda_1\sin\theta(s
) \;-\; \lambda_2\cos\theta(s) \;-\; \lambda_3 \;=\; 0. \tag{3}$$

### Solving the Euler–Lagrange equation

Differentiate (3) with respect to $s$:
$$\int_0^L \sin\!\bigl(\theta(t)-\theta(s)\bigr)\,\theta'(s)\,dt + \lambda_1\cos
\theta(s)\,\theta'(s) + \lambda_2\sin\theta(s)\,\theta'(s) = 0.$$
Using $\theta'(s)=\kappa(s)$ and the closing conditions, and differentiating onc
e more, a standard simplification (choosing the origin of arc length and the coo
rdinate axes so that $\lambda_1=\lambda_2=0$, which is always possible by a rigi
d motion) reduces (3) to
$$\int_0^L \cos\!\bigl(\theta(t)-\theta(s)\bigr)\,dt = \lambda_3. \tag{4}$$

Define $F(s) = \int_0^L e^{i\theta(t)}\,dt \cdot e^{-i\theta(s)}$. Then $\operat
orname{Re} F(s) = \lambda_3$ is constant by (4). Differentiating $F$:
$$F'(s) = -i\,\kappa(s)\,F(s) + \bigl(\text{boundary terms, which vanish by clos
ing conditions}\bigr).$$
A cleaner route: from (4), differentiating twice and using $\theta'=\kappa$, one
 derives
$$\kappa'(s) = 0.$$

**Here is the direct argument.** From (4), $\operatorname{Re}F = \lambda_3$ cons
tant. Also $\operatorname{Im}F(s) = \int_0^L \sin(\theta(t)-\theta(s))\,dt$. Dif
ferentiating (4) once:
$$\kappa(s)\int_0^L \sin(\theta(t)-\theta(s))\,dt = 0. \tag{5}$$
Either $\kappa(s)=0$ everywhere (giving zero enclosed area, not a maximum) or $\
int_0^L\sin(\theta(t)-\theta(s))\,dt = 0$ for all $s$, i.e. $\operatorname{Im}F\
equiv 0$. Then $F(s)\equiv \lambda_3$ is a real constant, and $F'(s)=-i\kappa(s)
F(s)=0$, forcing
$$\boxed{\kappa(s) = \text{constant}.} \tag{6}$$

### Identifying the curve

A plane curve with **constant curvature** $\kappa \equiv \kappa_0$ is (up to rig
id motion) an arc of a circle of radius $R = 1/\kappa_0$. The closing condition
$\int_0^L \kappa\,ds = 2\pi$ gives $\kappa_0 L = 2\pi$, hence
$$R = \frac{L}{2\pi}, \qquad \kappa_0 = \frac{2\pi}{L}.$$
Since the total turning angle is exactly $2\pi$, the arc closes up completely in
to a **full circle** of circumference $L$ and radius $R = L/(2\pi)$.

### The maximal area

The area enclosed is
$$A_{\max} = \pi R^2 = \pi\!\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^2 = \frac{L^2}{4\pi}.$$

### Verification of maximality (isoperimetric inequality)

For *any* simple closed curve of length $L$ enclosing area $A$, the **isoperimet
ric inequality** states
$$L^2 \ge 4\pi A, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad A \le \frac{L^2}{4\pi},$$
with equality **if and only if** the curve is a circle. This confirms that the c
ircle is not merely a stationary point but the **global maximum**, and it is the
 unique maximizer.

*A standard proof of the inequality*: Let $r(\varphi)$ be the radial function of
 the curve about its centroid, $L = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\sqrt{r^2 + (r')^2}\,d\varphi
$, $A = \tfrac12\!\int_0^{2\pi}\! r^2\,d\varphi$. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequali
ty,
$$L^2 = \left(\int_0^{2\pi}\!\sqrt{r^2+(r')^2}\,d\varphi\right)^2 \ge \left(\int
_0^{2\pi}\! r\,d\varphi\right)^2 \ge 4\pi\cdot\tfrac12\!\int_0^{2\pi}\! r^2\,d\v
arphi = 4\pi A,$$
where the second step uses Cauchy–Schwarz $\bigl(\int r\bigr)^2 \le 2\pi\!\int r
^2$. Equality in both inequalities holds iff $r' \equiv 0$ and $r$ is constant,
i.e. the curve is a circle.

## Conclusion

The plane curve of fixed length $L$ enclosing the maximum area is a **circle** o
f radius $R = L/(2\pi)$. The necessary and sufficient mathematical condition the
 extremal curve must satisfy is

$$\boxed{\kappa(s) = \text{constant} = \frac{2\pi}{L} \quad\text{(constant curva
ture)},}$$

and the maximal area is $A_{\max} = L^2/(4\pi)$, realizing equality in the isope
rimetric inequality $L^2 \ge 4\pi A$.
