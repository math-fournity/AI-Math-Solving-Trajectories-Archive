## Proof

**Answer: Yes.** In fact, a weak-* convergent net in $\mathfrak{X}^*$ is not mer
ely eventually bounded — it is **globally bounded** (bounded over the entire ind
ex set). The key tool is the Uniform Boundedness Principle in its form for **arb
itrary** families, which requires no countability of the index set.

---

**Setup.** Let $\mathfrak{X}$ be a Banach space and let $(f_\alpha)_{\alpha \in
A}$ be a net in $\mathfrak{X}^*$ that is weak-* convergent to some $f \in \mathf
rak{X}^*$. By definition of weak-* convergence, for every $x \in \mathfrak{X}$,
$$
f_\alpha(x) \longrightarrow f(x) \quad \text{in the scalar field.}
$$

**Step 1: Pointwise boundedness.** A convergent net of scalars is bounded: for e
ach fixed $x \in \mathfrak{X}$, the net $(f_\alpha(x))_{\alpha \in A}$ converges
 to $f(x)$, so
$$
\sup_{\alpha \in A} |f_\alpha(x)| < \infty.
$$
Thus the family $\{f_\alpha : \alpha \in A\} \subseteq \mathfrak{X}^*$ is **poin
twise bounded** on $\mathfrak{X}$.

**Step 2: Apply the Uniform Boundedness Principle.** The Uniform Boundedness Pri
nciple (Banach–Steinhaus) in its general form states:

> *Let $\mathfrak{X}$ be a **complete** normed space and let $\mathcal{F} \subse
teq \mathfrak{X}^*$ be a family (indexed by an arbitrary set) that is pointwise
bounded, i.e. $\sup_{g \in \mathcal{F}} |g(x)| < \infty$ for every $x \in \mathf
rak{X}$. Then $\mathcal{F}$ is uniformly bounded: $\sup_{g \in \mathcal{F}} \|g\
| < \infty$.*

The completeness of $\mathfrak{X}$ (it is a Banach space) is exactly the hypothe
sis needed; the proof via the Baire Category Theorem works for **any** index set
, with no countability requirement. Applying it to $\mathcal{F} = \{f_\alpha : \
alpha \in A\}$ yields
$$
\sup_{\alpha \in A} \|f_\alpha\| \;<\; \infty.
$$

**Step 3: Conclusion.** Since the entire family is uniformly bounded, in particu
lar there exists $M < \infty$ with $\|f_\alpha\| \le M$ for **all** $\alpha \in
A$. Hence for any choice of $\alpha_0 \in A$,
$$
\sup_{\alpha \ge \alpha_0} \|f_\alpha\| \le M < \infty,
$$
so the net is eventually bounded (indeed, globally bounded).

**Remark on why completeness matters and why nets are not a counterexample.** Th
e completeness of $\mathfrak{X}$ is essential: on a non-complete normed space on
e can construct pointwise-bounded (even pointwise-convergent) families of functi
onals that are not uniformly bounded. The distinction between sequences and nets
 is irrelevant here because the Uniform Boundedness Principle does not require t
he index set to be countable — it is a statement about arbitrary families. The c
ommon intuition that "nets can be wilder than sequences" does not defeat UBP, si
nce the principle already handles uncountable/unindexed families. Thus weak-* co
nvergence of a net, by forcing pointwise convergence (hence pointwise boundednes
s) on the complete space $\mathfrak{X}$, automatically grants uniform boundednes
s of the whole net.
