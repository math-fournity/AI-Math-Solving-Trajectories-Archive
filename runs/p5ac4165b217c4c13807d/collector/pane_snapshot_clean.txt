## Proof

**Setup.** We have $N = 10^9$ people. Each person makes 200 purchases per year,
and each purchase is a selection of 15 items from 2000. Two people are *identifi
ed* if they share at least one identical purchase (same 15-item subset). We want
 $P(\text{malicious} \mid \text{identified})$.

**Step 1: Number of distinct possible purchases.**

The number of possible 15-item subsets from 2000 items is:
$$S = \binom{2000}{15} = \frac{2000 \cdot 1999 \cdots 1986}{15!}$$

The numerator is approximately $2000^{15} \cdot e^{-105/2000} \approx 2000^{15}
\cdot 0.949$.

$$2000^{15} = (2 \times 10^3)^{15} = 2^{15} \times 10^{45} = 32768 \times 10^{45
} \approx 3.28 \times 10^{49}$$

$$\text{Numerator} \approx 3.28 \times 10^{49} \times 0.949 \approx 3.11 \times
10^{49}$$

$$15! = 1{,}307{,}674{,}368{,}000 \approx 1.31 \times 10^{12}$$

$$S = \binom{2000}{15} \approx \frac{3.11 \times 10^{49}}{1.31 \times 10^{12}} \
approx 2.38 \times 10^{37}$$

**Step 2: False positive rate for a single pair of people.**

Consider two innocent people $A$ and $B$, each making 200 random purchases. A *c
oincidental match* occurs if any of $A$'s 200 purchases equals any of $B$'s 200
purchases.

For a single pair of purchases (one from each person):
$$P(\text{same purchase}) = \frac{1}{S} \approx \frac{1}{2.38 \times 10^{37}}$$

There are $200 \times 200 = 40{,}000$ pairs of purchases. Assuming independence:
$$P(\text{no match}) = \left(1 - \frac{1}{S}\right)^{40000}$$

Since $S \gg 40000$:
$$P(\text{match between } A \text{ and } B) = 1 - \left(1 - \frac{1}{S}\right)^{
40000} \approx \frac{40000}{S} \approx \frac{4 \times 10^4}{2.38 \times 10^{37}}
 \approx 1.68 \times 10^{-33}$$

**Step 3: Expected number of false positives across all pairs.**

The number of pairs of people is:
$$\binom{10^9}{2} \approx \frac{(10^9)^2}{2} = 5 \times 10^{17}$$

The expected number of coincidental (false positive) matches:
$$\mathbb{E}[\text{FP}] = 5 \times 10^{17} \times 1.68 \times 10^{-33} \approx 8
.4 \times 10^{-16}$$

This is **effectively zero** — we expect fewer than $10^{-15}$ false positive pa
irs in the entire dataset.

**Step 4: Bayes' theorem.**

Let $k$ = number of truly malicious pairs (who deliberately purchase identical i
tems). A malicious pair is identified with probability $\approx 1$.

$$P(\text{malicious} \mid \text{identified}) = \frac{k}{k + \mathbb{E}[\text{FP}
]} = \frac{k}{k + 8.4 \times 10^{-16}}$$

For any $k \geq 1$ (even a single malicious pair):
$$P(\text{malicious} \mid \text{identified}) = \frac{1}{1 + 8.4 \times 10^{-16}}
 \approx 1 - 8.4 \times 10^{-16} \approx 1$$

**Step 5: Conclusion.**

The space of possible 15-item purchases, $\binom{2000}{15} \approx 2.38 \times 1
0^{37}$, is so astronomically large compared to the total number of purchases ($
2 \times 10^{11}$) that the expected number of coincidental matches across all $
\binom{10^9}{2} \approx 5 \times 10^{17}$ pairs of people is approximately $8.4
\times 10^{-16}$ — effectively zero.

By Bayes' theorem, the probability that an identified pair is actually malicious
 is:

$$\boxed{P(\text{malicious} \mid \text{identified}) \approx 1 - 8.4 \times 10^{-
16} \approx 1}$$

The false positive rate is negligible ($\sim 10^{-16}$), so any pair identified
as having purchased the same set of 15 items is malicious with probability effec
tively equal to **1** (certainty). The combinatorial space $\binom{2000}{15} \ap
prox 2.38 \times 10^{37}$ dwarfs the number of observations so completely that a
 coincidental match is practically impossible.
