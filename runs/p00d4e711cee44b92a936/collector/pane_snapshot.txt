Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether there exists a $C^1$ period
ic function whose Fourier coefficients violate $\sum_n n|c_n| < \infty$.

**Answer: Yes, such a function exists.**

## Proof

We work on $\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}$ with Fourier coefficients $\
hat{f}(n) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)e^{-inx}\,dx$.

### Step 1: The Wiener algebra $A(\mathbb{T})$ is a proper subset of $C(\mathbb{
T})$

The Wiener algebra $A(\mathbb{T}) = \{f \in C(\mathbb{T}) : \sum_{n} |\hat{f}(n)
| < \infty\}$ is a Banach space under $\|f\|_A = \sum_n |\hat{f}(n)|$ (isometric
 to $\ell^1(\mathbb{Z})$). The space $C(\mathbb{T})$ is Banach under $\|f\|_\inf
ty$. The inclusion $A(\mathbb{T}) \hookrightarrow C(\mathbb{T})$ is continuous s
ince $\|f\|_\infty \leq \|f\|_A$.

**Suppose for contradiction** that $A(\mathbb{T}) = C(\mathbb{T})$ as sets. By t
he Bounded Inverse Theorem (Open Mapping Theorem), there exists $C > 0$ with
$$\|f\|_A \leq C\|f\|_\infty \quad \text{for all } f \in C(\mathbb{T}).$$

Consider the trigonometric polynomials $f_N(x) = \sum_{n=1}^N \frac{\sin(nx)}{n}
$.

**Claim:** $\|f_N\|_\infty \leq \pi$ for all $N \geq 1$.

*Proof of claim.* For $x \in [0, \pi/N]$: $|\sin(nx)| \leq nx$, so $|f_N(x)| \le
q \sum_{n=1}^N \frac{nx}{n} = Nx \leq \pi$.

For $x \in [\pi/N, \pi]$: Using $\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{e^{inx}}{n} = -\log(1-e
^{ix})$ and $\operatorname{Im}(-\log(1-e^{ix})) = \frac{\pi-x}{2}$, we write
$$f_N(x) = \frac{\pi - x}{2} - \operatorname{Im}(R_N(x)), \quad R_N(x) = \sum_{n
=N+1}^\infty \frac{e^{inx}}{n}.$$
By Dirichlet's test, $|R_N(x)| \leq \frac{1}{(N+1)|\sin(x/2)|}$. For $x \in [\pi
/N, \pi]$, the inequality $\sin(x/2) \geq x/\pi \geq 1/N$ gives $|R_N(x)| \leq \
frac{N}{N+1} \leq 1$. Thus $|f_N(x)| \leq \frac{\pi}{2} + 1 \leq \pi$.

For $x \in [\pi, 2\pi]$: the identity $f_N(2\pi - x) = -f_N(x)$ reduces to the p
revious case. $\square$

On the other hand, $\|f_N\|_A = 2\sum_{n=1}^N \frac{1}{n} \geq 2\log N \to \inft
y$.

This contradicts $\|f_N\|_A \leq C\|f_N\|_\infty \leq C\pi$ for large $N$. There
fore $A(\mathbb{T}) \neq C(\mathbb{T})$.

### Step 2: Construction of the $C^1$ function

Since $A(\mathbb{T}) \subsetneq C(\mathbb{T})$, there exists $g \in C(\mathbb{T}
)$ with $\sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} |\hat{g}(n)| = \infty$. Replacing $g$ by $g - \
hat{g}(0)$ (which does not affect $\sum_{n \neq 0} |\hat{g}(n)|$), we may assume
 $\hat{g}(0) = 0$, i.e., $\int_0^{2\pi} g(x)\,dx = 0$.

Define $f(x) = \int_0^x g(t)\,dt$. Since $g$ is continuous and has zero mean, $f
$ is $2\pi$-periodic and $C^1$, with $f' = g$.

By integration by parts (using periodicity of $f$):
$$\hat{g}(n) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f'(x)\,e^{-inx}\,dx = in\,\hat{f}(n)
\quad (n \neq 0).$$

Therefore $\hat{f}(n) = \frac{\hat{g}(n)}{in}$ for $n \neq 0$, and
$$\sum_{n \neq 0} n\,|\hat{f}(n)| = \sum_{n \neq 0} |\hat{g}(n)| = \infty.$$

### Conclusion

The function $f$ is continuously differentiable and $2\pi$-periodic, yet $\sum_n
 n|c_n| = \infty$. The key mechanism is that $C^1$ only forces $nc_n \to 0$ (Rie
mann–Lebesgue applied to $f'$), which is far weaker than $\sum n|c_n| < \infty$.
 The latter is equivalent to $f'$ belonging to the Wiener algebra $A(\mathbb{T})
$, which is a proper subset of $C(\mathbb{T})$.
