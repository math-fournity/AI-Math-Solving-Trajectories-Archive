## Proof

**Step 1: Every vertex in a triangulation of the sphere has degree ≥ 3.**

A triangulation of the sphere is a 2-dimensional simplicial complex homeomorphic
 to $S^2$. For any vertex $v$, its link $\operatorname{lk}(v)$ is a 1-dimensiona
l simplicial complex homeomorphic to $S^1$ (a cycle). Since a simplicial complex
 has at most one edge between any pair of vertices, the shortest possible cycle
has 3 vertices. The degree of $v$ equals the number of vertices in $\operatornam
e{lk}(v)$, so $\deg(v) \geq 3$.

**Step 2: Every vertex has degree exactly 3.**

The condition "only vertices of degree 1, 2, and 3" restricts $\deg(v) \in \{1,
2, 3\}$. Combined with Step 1, every vertex satisfies $\deg(v) = 3$.

**Step 3: The face-size condition is automatically satisfied.**

In a triangulation (simplicial complex), every 2-dimensional face is a triangle
with exactly 3 vertices. Since $3 \leq 6$, the condition "all faces have at most
 6 vertices" is trivially satisfied.

**Step 4: Compute $V$, $E$, $F$ via Euler's formula.**

Let $V, E, F$ be the numbers of vertices, edges, and faces. Since every vertex h
as degree 3:
$$3V = 2E.$$
Since every face is a triangle:
$$3F = 2E.$$
Thus $V = F$ and $E = \frac{3V}{2}$.

By Euler's formula for the sphere:
$$V - E + F = 2 \implies V - \frac{3V}{2} + V = 2 \implies \frac{V}{2} = 2 \impl
ies V = 4.$$

So $V = 4$, $F = 4$, $E = 6$.

**Step 5: Uniqueness — this is the tetrahedron.**

With 4 vertices, there are exactly $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ possible edges and $\binom
{4}{3} = 4$ possible triangles. Since $E = 6$ and $F = 4$, all possible edges an
d all possible triangles must be present. This is precisely the boundary of the
tetrahedron (the complete graph $K_4$ with its four triangular faces), which is
the unique triangulation of the sphere on 4 vertices.
