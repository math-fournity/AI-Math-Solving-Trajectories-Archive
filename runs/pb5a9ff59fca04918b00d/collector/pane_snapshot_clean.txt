# Solution

**Yes**, such a Lie algebra exists. The key distinction is that "reductive" is a
n *intrinsic* property of $\mathfrak{g}$, while "reductive in $\mathfrak{h}$" is
 an *extrinsic* property depending on the embedding. The failure can occur preci
sely when $\mathfrak{g}$ is reductive but **not semisimple** (i.e., has a nontri
vial center).

## Definitions

- $\mathfrak{g}$ is **reductive** (intrinsically) if $\operatorname{rad}(\mathfr
ak{g}) = \mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{g})$, equivalently if the adjoint representation
 of $\mathfrak{g}$ on itself is completely reducible.
- $\mathfrak{g} \subseteq \mathfrak{h}$ is **reductive in $\mathfrak{h}$** if th
e adjoint action of $\mathfrak{g}$ on $\mathfrak{h}$ (i.e., the representation $
\operatorname{ad}\colon \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{h})$) is comple
tely reducible.

## The Example

Work over $\mathbb{C}$. Let

$$\mathfrak{g} = \mathbb{C} \quad\text{(1-dimensional abelian Lie algebra)}, \qq
uad \mathfrak{h} = \mathfrak{gl}_2(\mathbb{C}),$$

and embed $\mathfrak{g} \hookrightarrow \mathfrak{h}$ by sending the generator $
t$ to

$$x = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

### $\mathfrak{g}$ is reductive as an abstract Lie algebra

Since $\mathfrak{g}$ is abelian, every subspace is an ideal, so $\operatorname{r
ad}(\mathfrak{g}) = \mathfrak{g}$. Likewise $\mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{g}) = \mathf
rak{g}$. Hence $\operatorname{rad}(\mathfrak{g}) = \mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{g})$,
which is the definition of reductive. (Equivalently, $\operatorname{ad}_{\mathfr
ak{g}}$ is the zero map, hence trivially completely reducible.)

### $\mathfrak{g}$ is **not** reductive in $\mathfrak{h}$

The action of $\mathfrak{g}$ on $\mathfrak{h}$ is $t \mapsto t\cdot\operatorname
{ad}(x)$. For this to be completely reducible, the linear operator $\operatornam
e{ad}(x)$ on the vector space $\mathfrak{h} = \mathfrak{gl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ must
be semisimple (diagonalizable).

Write the Jordan–Chevalley decomposition $x = s + n$ with

$$s = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = I, \qquad n = \begin{pmatri
x} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Since $s = I$ is central in $\mathfrak{gl}_2$, we have $\operatorname{ad}(s) = 0
$, and therefore

$$\operatorname{ad}(x) = \operatorname{ad}(n).$$

Now $n$ is nilpotent ($n^2 = 0$), so $\operatorname{ad}(n)$ is nilpotent (standa
rd fact: $\operatorname{ad}(n)$ is nilpotent whenever $n$ is; here $\operatornam
e{ad}(n)^3 = 0$ on $\mathfrak{gl}_2$). Moreover $\operatorname{ad}(n) \neq 0$, s
ince for instance

$$\operatorname{ad}(n)\!\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = n\begin{p
matrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}n = 0 - \begi
n{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix} = -n \neq 0.$$

A nonzero nilpotent linear map cannot be semisimple (a diagonalizable nilpotent
map is zero). Therefore $\operatorname{ad}(x)$ is **not** semisimple, so the rep
resentation of $\mathfrak{g}$ on $\mathfrak{h}$ is **not** completely reducible.
 Hence $\mathfrak{g}$ is not reductive in $\mathfrak{h}$.

## Remark on why semisimple $\mathfrak{g}$ cannot serve as a counterexample

If $\mathfrak{g}$ is semisimple over a field of characteristic $0$, then by **We
yl's theorem** every finite-dimensional representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ is comp
letely reducible. The adjoint action of $\mathfrak{g}$ on any containing algebra
 $\mathfrak{h}$ is such a representation, so a semisimple $\mathfrak{g}$ is *alw
ays* reductive in $\mathfrak{h}$. The counterexample must therefore exploit a no
ntrivial center: the central (abelian) part of $\mathfrak{g}$ acts via $\operato
rname{ad}$, and whether that action is semisimple depends on the choice of embed
ding. Embedding the generator as a non-semisimple element (a Jordan block) force
s the action to be non-semisimple, breaking reductivity in $\mathfrak{h}$ while
preserving intrinsic reductivity.

## Conclusion

The 1-dimensional abelian Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g} = \mathbb{C}$ is reductive,
but embedded in $\mathfrak{h} = \mathfrak{gl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ via $t \mapsto \beg
in{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, it is **not** reductive in $\mathfrak{h}$. $\
blacksquare$
