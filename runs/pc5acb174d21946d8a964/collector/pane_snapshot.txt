### Proof

We show that $f(x) = 1/x$ is **not Lebesgue integrable** on $[0,1]$, and that it
s Lebesgue integral equals $+\infty$.

**Step 1: Measurability and non-negativity.**

The function $f(x)=1/x$ is defined and continuous (hence Borel measurable) on $(
0,1]$. At $x=0$ it is undefined, but $\{0\}$ is a Lebesgue-null set, so we may e
xtend $f$ arbitrarily at $0$ (e.g. $f(0)=0$) without affecting any Lebesgue inte
gral. The extended function is non-negative and measurable on $[0,1]$.

**Step 2: Definition of the integral of a non-negative measurable function.**

For a non-negative measurable function, the Lebesgue integral is defined as
$$
\int_{[0,1]} f\,d\mu \;=\; \sup\left\{\int_{[0,1]} s\,d\mu \;:\; 0\le s\le f,\;
s \text{ simple}\right\} \;\in\; [0,+\infty],
$$
where the supremum is allowed to be $+\infty$.

**Step 3: Comparison with the Riemann integral via truncation.**

For each $n\ge 1$, define the simple (in fact step) function
$$
s_n(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1}{k/n}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\left(\frac{k-1}{n},\,\fra
c{k}{n}\right]}(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{n}{k}\,\mathbf{1}_{\left(\frac{k-1}{n},
\,\frac{k}{n}\right]}(x).
$$
On each subinterval $\big(\tfrac{k-1}{n},\tfrac{k}{n}\big]$ we have $x\le \tfrac
{k}{n}$, hence $\tfrac{1}{x}\ge \tfrac{n}{k}=s_n(x)$. Thus $0\le s_n\le f$, so $
s_n$ is an admissible simple minorant. Its integral is the lower Darboux sum of
$1/x$:
$$
\int_{[0,1]} s_n\,d\mu = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{n}{k}\cdot\frac{1}{n} = \sum_{k=1}^
{n}\frac{1}{k} = H_n,
$$
the $n$-th harmonic number.

**Step 4: The harmonic series diverges.**

It is classical that $H_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}\to+\infty$ as $n\to\infty$
 (e.g. by grouping terms: $1+\tfrac12+\big(\tfrac13+\tfrac14\big)+\big(\tfrac15+
\cdots+\tfrac18\big)+\cdots \ge 1+\tfrac12+\tfrac12+\tfrac12+\cdots$).

**Step 5: Conclusion.**

Since each $s_n\le f$,
$$
\int_{[0,1]} f\,d\mu \;\ge\; \int_{[0,1]} s_n\,d\mu \;=\; H_n \;\xrightarrow[n\t
o\infty]{}\; +\infty.
$$
Therefore
$$
\int_{[0,1]} \frac{1}{x}\,d\mu \;=\; +\infty.
$$

**Remark (consistency with the improper Riemann integral).** The improper Rieman
n integral $\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\fr
ac{dx}{x}=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}(-\ln\varepsilon)=+\infty$ agrees with t
he Lebesgue integral. This agreement is guaranteed because $f\ge 0$: for non-neg
ative measurable functions, the Lebesgue integral coincides with the improper Ri
emann integral whenever the latter is defined (allowing the value $+\infty$). Th
e divergence is not an artifact of the definition; it reflects the genuine singu
larity of $1/x$ at the origin.

Hence $f(x)=1/x$ is **not Lebesgue integrable** on $[0,1]$, and its Lebesgue int
egral is

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]}\frac{1}{x}\,d\mu = +\infty.}
$$
