Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether $\lim_{t\to 0^+} \theta(t)
e^{-\int_t^1 \theta(s)/s\,ds} = 0$ necessarily holds under the given conditions.

**Answer: The limit does NOT hold in general.** I construct a counterexample.

---

## Setup and Reformulation

Let $\Phi(t) = \int_t^1 \frac{\theta(s)}{s}\,ds$, so $\Phi(t) \to \infty$ as $t
\to 0^+$. Define $h(t) = e^{-\Phi(t)}$, so $h:(0,1]\to(0,1]$ is $C^1$, increasin
g (since $\Phi$ is decreasing), $h(0^+)=0$, $h(1)=1$.

Differentiating: $h'(t) = -\Phi'(t)\,e^{-\Phi(t)} = \frac{\theta(t)}{t}\,h(t)$,
so $\theta(t) = \frac{t\,h'(t)}{h(t)}$.

The key identity is:
$$\theta(t)\,e^{-\Phi(t)} = \theta(t)\,h(t) = t\,h'(t).$$

So the question reduces to: **must $t\,h'(t) \to 0$ as $t\to 0^+$?**

## Why $t\,h'(t)$ Need Not Vanish

If $t\,h'(t) \geq \varepsilon > 0$ near $0$, then $h'(t) \geq \varepsilon/t$, gi
ving $h(t) \geq \varepsilon \ln(1/t) + C \to \infty$, contradicting $h(0^+)=0$.
So $t\,h'(t)$ cannot stay bounded away from $0$. **But it can have persistent sp
ikes.**

## Explicit Counterexample

Let $t_n = 2^{-n}$ for $n \geq 1$, and $\varepsilon_n = t_n^2/4 = 2^{-2n-2}$ (ha
lf-width of spike $n$). One checks the intervals $[t_n - \varepsilon_n,\; t_n +
\varepsilon_n]$ are pairwise disjoint for $n \geq 1$ (since $t_n - t_{n+1} = 2^{
-(n+1)} \gg 2\varepsilon_n$).

**Define $g:(0,1]\to[0,1]$** continuous by:
- $g(t_n) = 1$ for all $n \geq 1$,
- $g(t) = 0$ for $t \notin \bigcup_n [t_n - \varepsilon_n,\; t_n+\varepsilon_n]$
,
- On each $[t_n-\varepsilon_n,\; t_n+\varepsilon_n]$: $g(t) = 1 - |t - t_n|/\var
epsilon_n$ (triangular bump).

Then $g$ is continuous on $(0,1]$ (piecewise linear, zero at all bump boundaries
, zero near $t=1$).

**Define** $h(t) = \int_0^t \frac{g(s)}{s}\,ds$.

**Convergence of the integral:** The contribution from spike $n$ is
$$\int_{t_n-\varepsilon_n}^{t_n+\varepsilon_n} \frac{g(s)}{s}\,ds \leq \frac{1 \
cdot 2\varepsilon_n}{t_n/2} = \frac{4\varepsilon_n}{t_n} = \frac{t_n^2}{t_n} = t
_n = 2^{-n}.$$
Since $\sum 2^{-n} < \infty$, the integral converges, so $h(0^+) = 0$.

**Positivity:** For any $t > 0$, there exists $n$ with $t_n < t$, so $h(t) \geq
\int_{\text{spike }n} g(s)/s\,ds > 0$. Thus $h(t) > 0$ on $(0,1]$.

**Monotonicity:** $h'(t) = g(t)/t \geq 0$, so $h$ is non-decreasing. Let $C = h(
1) > 0$.

**Define** $\theta(t) = \frac{g(t)}{h(t)}$ for $t \in (0,1]$.

### Verification of all hypotheses

1. **$\theta$ is continuous on $(0,1]$:** $g$ is continuous, $h$ is $C^1$ and st
rictly positive on $(0,1]$. ✓

2. **$\theta:(0,1]\to[0,\infty)$:** $g \geq 0$ and $h > 0$, so $\theta \geq 0$.
✓

3. **$\int_0^1 \frac{\theta(s)}{s}\,ds = \infty$:** Since $\frac{d}{ds}\ln h(s)
= \frac{h'(s)}{h(s)} = \frac{g(s)}{s\,h(s)} = \frac{\theta(s)}{s}$, we get
$$\int_t^1 \frac{\theta(s)}{s}\,ds = \ln h(1) - \ln h(t) = \ln\frac{C}{h(t)} \xr
ightarrow{t\to 0^+} \ln\frac{C}{0} = +\infty. \quad\checkmark$$

### The limit fails

We have $\Phi(t) = \int_t^1 \theta(s)/s\,ds = \ln(C/h(t))$, so $e^{-\Phi(t)} = h
(t)/C$. Therefore:
$$\theta(t)\,e^{-\Phi(t)} = \frac{g(t)}{h(t)} \cdot \frac{h(t)}{C} = \frac{g(t)}
{C}.$$

At $t = t_n = 2^{-n}$:
$$\theta(t_n)\,e^{-\Phi(t_n)} = \frac{g(t_n)}{C} = \frac{1}{C} > 0 \quad \text{f
or all } n.$$

Since $t_n \to 0^+$ but $\theta(t_n)\,e^{-\Phi(t_n)} = 1/C \not\to 0$, the limit
 **does not hold**:

$$\boxed{\lim_{t\to 0^+}\theta(t)\,e^{-\int_t^1 \theta(s)/s\,ds} \neq 0 \text{ i
n general.}}$$

The expression equals $g(t)/C$, which has persistent spikes of height $1/C$ at $
t = 2^{-n} \to 0$, so the limit does not even exist (the $\limsup$ is $1/C > 0$
while the $\liminf$ is $0$).
