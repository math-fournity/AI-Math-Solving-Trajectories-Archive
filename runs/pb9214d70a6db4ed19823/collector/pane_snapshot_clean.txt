## Proof

We work in the **upper half-plane model** $\mathbb{H} = \{z = x+iy : y > 0\}$ wi
th metric $ds^2 = (dx^2+dy^2)/y^2$. Geodesics are precisely vertical lines and s
emicircles with centers on the real axis, orthogonal to $\partial\mathbb{H} = \m
athbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}$.

Two distinct non-intersecting geodesics $L, L'$ (no common point in $\mathbb{H}$
) fall into two classes:
- **(A) Asymptotically parallel**: sharing exactly one point on $\partial\mathbb
{H}$.
- **(B) Ultra-parallel**: sharing no point on $\partial\mathbb{H}$.

**Claim.** A geodesic meeting both $L$ and $L'$ orthogonally at finite points ex
ists **if and only if** $L$ and $L'$ are ultra-parallel. When it exists, it is *
*unique**.

---

### Case (A): Asymptotically parallel — no common perpendicular exists

Apply an isometry sending the common ideal point to $\infty$. Then both geodesic
s become vertical lines $L = \{x=a\}$, $L' = \{x=b\}$, $a \neq b$.

A geodesic perpendicular to $L$ at a finite point $(a, y_0)$ must be a semicircl
e centered at $(a,0)$ with radius $y_0$:
$$(x-a)^2 + y^2 = y_0^2.$$
At any intersection with $L' = \{x=b\}$, the tangent to this semicircle has dire
ction perpendicular to the radius vector $(b-a,\, y_1)$. For orthogonality with
the vertical line $x=b$ (vertical tangent), the semicircle's tangent must be hor
izontal, requiring the radius vector to be vertical, i.e. $b - a = 0$. This cont
radicts $a \neq b$.

Hence **no** geodesic is perpendicular to both at finite points. $\checkmark$

---

### Case (B): Ultra-parallel — a unique common perpendicular exists

We treat the sub-cases.

**Sub-case (i): $L = \{x=a\}$ vertical, $L'$ a semicircle with center $c$, radiu
s $r$.**
Ultra-parallel means $|a-c| > r$ (the vertical line lies outside the semicircle'
s diameter, no shared endpoint).

A semicircle centered at $(a,0)$ with radius $y_0$ is perpendicular to $L$ at $(
a,y_0)$. For it to be perpendicular to $L'$, the circle-orthogonality condition
gives
$$(a-c)^2 = y_0^2 + r^2 \implies y_0 = \sqrt{(a-c)^2 - r^2}.$$
Since $|a-c|>r$, we get $y_0 > 0$: a **unique** valid semicircle. The two circle
s intersect (orthogonality condition guarantees $|y_0 - r| \le \sqrt{y_0^2+r^2}
\le y_0+r$), and intersection points lie in $y>0$. So the common perpendicular e
xists and is unique.

**Sub-case (ii): Both $L, L'$ semicircles, centers $c_1 \neq c_2$, radii $r_1, r
_2$.**
A common perpendicular semicircle with center $c$, radius $R$ must satisfy:
$$(c-c_1)^2 = R^2 + r_1^2, \qquad (c-c_2)^2 = R^2 + r_2^2.$$
Subtracting:
$$2c(c_2 - c_1) = r_1^2 - r_2^2 - c_1^2 + c_2^2 \implies c = \frac{r_1^2 - r_2^2
 - c_1^2 + c_2^2}{2(c_2-c_1)},$$
giving a **unique** $c$, then $R^2 = (c-c_1)^2 - r_1^2$. The ultra-parallel cond
ition (no intersection, no shared endpoint: $|c_1-c_2| > r_1+r_2$ or $|c_1-c_2|
< |r_1-r_2|$) ensures $R^2 > 0$, yielding a unique common perpendicular.

**Sub-case (iii): Concentric semicircles, $c_1 = c_2$, $r_1 \neq r_2$.**
The vertical line $x = c_1$ passes through the common center. At each intersecti
on with a semicircle centered at $c_1$, the semicircle's radius is horizontal wh
ile the vertical line's tangent is vertical — they are orthogonal. So $x = c_1$
is perpendicular to both, and it is the unique such geodesic (any semicircular c
ommon perpendicular would require $c_1 = c_2$ in the orthogonality equations, fo
rcing $r_1 = r_2$, contradiction).

---

### Conclusion

A geodesic intersecting two distinct non-intersecting geodesics $L$ and $L'$ ort
hogonally at points **other than infinity** exists **if and only if** $L$ and $L
'$ are **ultra-parallel** (sharing no point even on the boundary at infinity). I
n that case the common perpendicular is **unique**. If $L$ and $L'$ are merely *
*asymptotically parallel** (sharing one ideal point), no such geodesic exists at
 finite points.
