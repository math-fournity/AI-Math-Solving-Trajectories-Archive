## Counterexample

Let $\Omega = (0,1) \subset \mathbb{R}$, and let $\{e_n\}_{n \geq 1}$ be the sta
ndard orthonormal basis of $L^2(0,1)$, e.g. the Fourier basis
$$e_n(t) = \sqrt{2}\sin(n\pi t), \qquad n = 1, 2, \dots$$

Every $x \in L^2(0,1)$ has a unique expansion $x = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n e_n$
with $a_n = \langle x, e_n\rangle$ and $\|x\|^2 = \sum_{n} |a_n|^2$.

Define $T: L^2(0,1) \to L^2(0,1)$ by
$$Tx = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n}\, a_n\, e_n.$$

### $T$ is well-defined and bounded (continuous)

For any $x = \sum a_n e_n$,
$$\|Tx\|^2 = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{|a_n|^2}{n^2} \leq \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |a
_n|^2 = \|x\|^2,$$
since $1/n^2 \leq 1$ for all $n \geq 1$. Hence $\|T\| \leq 1$, so $T$ is a bound
ed (therefore continuous) linear operator. (In fact $\|T\| = 1$, attained in the
 limit along $e_1$.)

### The nullspace of $T$ is $\{0\}$

Suppose $Tx = 0$. Then $\sum_{n} \frac{1}{n} a_n e_n = 0$, so by uniqueness of F
ourier coefficients, $\frac{1}{n} a_n = 0$ for every $n$. Since $1/n \neq 0$, we
 get $a_n = 0$ for all $n$, hence $x = 0$. Thus $\ker T = \{0\}$.

### No constant $C > 0$ satisfies $\|Tx\| \geq C\|x\|$

Consider the unit vectors $x = e_n$. Then $\|e_n\| = 1$ and
$$\|Te_n\| = \left\|\frac{1}{n} e_n\right\| = \frac{1}{n} \xrightarrow{n \to \in
fty} 0.$$

If a constant $C > 0$ with $\|Tx\| \geq C\|x\|$ for all $x \neq 0$ existed, then
 in particular $\|Te_n\| \geq C\|e_n\| = C$ for every $n$, i.e. $\frac{1}{n} \ge
q C$ for every $n$. Letting $n \to \infty$ gives $0 \geq C$, contradicting $C >
0$.

## Conclusion

The claim is **disproved**. A continuous (bounded) linear operator $T: L^2(\Omeg
a) \to L^2(\Omega)$ with trivial nullspace need **not** be bounded below. The es
sential obstruction is that $L^2(\Omega)$ is infinite-dimensional: injectivity o
f a bounded linear operator does not force its inverse (on the range) to be boun
ded. The diagonal operator above is injective and bounded, yet its singular valu
es $1/n$ accumulate at $0$, so no uniform lower bound $C > 0$ can exist.
