# Projective Transformations in the Proof of Bézout's Theorem over an Arbitrary
Algebraically Closed Field

## Answer

**Yes.** The projective transformations and all their properties used in the (al
gebraic) proof of Bézout's theorem for plane projective curves hold over an arbi
trary algebraically closed field $k$. The proof goes through verbatim.

Below I identify every property of projective transformations that the proof inv
okes, and verify each one over an arbitrary algebraically closed field. The sing
le field-specific fact required — that $k$ be infinite — is guaranteed by algebr
aic closure itself.

---

## The Key Lemma: Every Algebraically Closed Field Is Infinite

**Lemma.** If $k$ is algebraically closed, then $k$ is infinite.

*Proof.* Suppose for contradiction that $k$ is finite, say $|k| = q$. Consider t
he polynomial
$$
f(X) = 1 + \prod_{a \in k}(X - a) = 1 + X^q - X \in k[X].
$$
For every $a \in k$, we have $\prod_{b \in k}(a - b) = 0$, so $f(a) = 1 \neq 0$.
 Thus $f$ has no root in $k$, contradicting the assumption that $k$ is algebraic
ally closed. $\square$

This lemma is the linchpin: every "general position" or "generic choice" argumen
t in the proof reduces to finding an element (a line, a point, a transformation)
 outside a finite union of proper closed subsets, which is possible precisely be
cause $k$ is infinite.

## Properties of Projective Transformations Used in the Proof

The standard algebraic proof of Bézout's theorem (as in Fulton's *Algebraic Curv
es* or van der Waerden) uses the following properties of projective transformati
ons. I verify each over an arbitrary algebraically closed field $k$.

### Property 1: $\mathrm{PGL}_3(k)$ acts on $\mathbb{P}^2(k)$

The projective linear group $\mathrm{PGL}_3(k) = \mathrm{GL}_3(k) / k^\times$ ac
ts on $\mathbb{P}^2(k)$ by $[M] \cdot [v] = [Mv]$. This is defined over **any**
field $k$: it requires only the ring structure of $k$ and the existence of inver
tible $3 \times 3$ matrices (e.g., the identity). No field-specific property is
needed. ✓

### Property 2: $\mathrm{PGL}_3(k)$ acts transitively on points and on lines

**On points:** Given $P = [a:b:c]$ and $Q = [a':b':c']$ in $\mathbb{P}^2(k)$, th
e nonzero vector $(a,b,c)$ can be extended to a basis $\{(a,b,c), v_2, v_3\}$ of
 $k^3$ (this uses only that $k$ is a field — any linearly independent set extend
s to a basis). Similarly $(a',b',c')$ extends to a basis. The change-of-basis ma
trix $M \in \mathrm{GL}_3(k)$ sends $(a,b,c) \mapsto \lambda(a',b',c')$ for some
 $\lambda \in k^\times$, hence $[M] \cdot P = Q$. ✓

**On lines:** Lines in $\mathbb{P}^2$ are parametrized by the dual projective pl
ane $(\mathbb{P}^2)^\vee$, and $\mathrm{PGL}_3(k)$ acts transitively on it by th
e dual (contragredient) representation. Equivalently, a line is the kernel of a
nonzero linear form $\ell \in (k^3)^\vee$, and the same basis-extension argument
 applies. ✓

These work over **any** field, algebraically closed or not.

### Property 3: Projective transformations preserve the degree of a curve

A plane projective curve of degree $d$ is defined by a homogeneous polynomial $F
 \in k[X,Y,Z]$ of degree $d$. A projective transformation $[M] \in \mathrm{PGL}_
3(k)$ sends $F$ to $F \circ M^{-1}$, which is the composition of $F$ with an **i
nvertible** linear substitution. Since $M^{-1}$ is invertible:

- $F \circ M^{-1}$ is still homogeneous of degree $d$ (linearity of the substitu
tion preserves homogeneity and degree).
- The degree cannot drop: if $\deg(F \circ M^{-1}) < d$, then applying $(M^{-1})
^{-1} = M$ would give $F$ back with degree $< d$, a contradiction.

This is purely algebraic and holds over **any** field. ✓

### Property 4: Projective transformations preserve "no common component"

Two curves $F, G$ have no common component iff $F$ and $G$ are coprime in the UF
D $k[X,Y,Z]$. A projective transformation $[M]$ induces a $k$-algebra automorphi
sm $\varphi_M : k[X,Y,Z] \to k[X,Y,Z]$ (linear change of variables). Automorphis
ms preserve the ideal structure: $\gcd(F, G) = 1 \iff \gcd(\varphi_M(F), \varphi
_M(G)) = 1$. This holds over **any** field. ✓

### Property 5: Projective transformations preserve intersection multiplicities

The intersection multiplicity of $F$ and $G$ at a point $P \in \mathbb{P}^2(k)$
is defined algebraically as
$$
I(P, F \cap G) = \dim_k \left( \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^2, P} \,/\, (F, G) \right
),
$$
where $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^2, P}$ is the local ring of $\mathbb{P}^2$ at $P$
. A projective transformation $[M]$ sending $P$ to $Q$ induces a $k$-algebra iso
morphism $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^2, P} \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{
P}^2, Q}$ carrying the ideal $(F, G)$ to $(\varphi_M(F), \varphi_M(G))$. Therefo
re
$$
\dim_k\!\left(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^2, P}/(F,G)\right) = \dim_k\!\left(\mathca
l{O}_{\mathbb{P}^2, Q}/(\varphi_M(F),\varphi_M(G))\right),
$$
i.e., $I(P, F \cap G) = I(Q, \varphi_M(F) \cap \varphi_M(G))$.

This definition and argument are entirely algebraic — they use only the $k$-alge
bra structure of local rings and the fact that $[M]$ induces an automorphism. Th
ey hold over **any** algebraically closed field (algebraic closure ensures that
all intersection points are $k$-rational, so the local rings are $k$-algebras wi
th $k$-rational closed points). ✓

### Property 6: A projective transformation can move the curves into "general po
sition"

This is the property that genuinely requires $k$ to be infinite, and it is the o
nly place where the field hypothesis enters in a nontrivial way.

**What is needed:** Given two curves $F, G$ with finitely many intersection poin
ts $P_1, \ldots, P_s$, we need a line $L \subset \mathbb{P}^2(k)$ that contains
none of the $P_i$. Then a projective transformation sending $L$ to the line at i
nfinity $\{Z = 0\}$ ensures that every intersection point has $Z \neq 0$, so tha
t all intersections are visible in the affine chart $Z \neq 0$ and are captured
by the resultant $\mathrm{Res}_Z(F, G)$.

**Why such a line exists:** The set of lines in $\mathbb{P}^2(k)$ is parametrize
d by $(\mathbb{P}^2)^\vee(k) \cong \mathbb{P}^2(k)$. For each point $P_i$, the s
et of lines through $P_i$ is a line $\ell_i^\vee$ in the dual plane — a proper c
losed subset. The union $\bigcup_{i=1}^s \ell_i^\vee$ is a finite union of prope
r closed subsets of $(\mathbb{P}^2)^\vee(k)$. Since $k$ is infinite (by the Lemm
a), a finite union of proper closed subsets cannot cover $\mathbb{P}^2(k)$: a no
nzero homogeneous polynomial (or a product of finitely many such) cannot vanish
on all of $\mathbb{P}^2(k)$ when $k$ is infinite. Hence there exists a line $L$
avoiding all $P_i$.

**Why we can send $L$ to $\{Z=0\}$:** By Property 2, $\mathrm{PGL}_3(k)$ acts tr
ansitively on lines, so there exists $[M] \in \mathrm{PGL}_3(k)$ with $[M](L) =
\{Z = 0\}$.

This step uses: (a) $k$ is infinite (from the Lemma, guaranteed by algebraic clo
sure), and (b) transitivity of $\mathrm{PGL}_3(k)$ on lines (Property 2, valid o
ver any field). ✓

### Property 7: The resultant factors completely, giving exactly $mn$ roots coun
ted with multiplicity

After the change of coordinates, the resultant $R(X,Y) = \mathrm{Res}_Z(F, G)$ i
s a nonzero homogeneous polynomial of degree $mn$ in $X, Y$ (the degree formula
for the resultant of homogeneous polynomials of degrees $m, n$ is purely algebra
ic and field-independent). Since $k$ is **algebraically closed**, $R$ splits com
pletely:
$$
R(X,Y) = c \prod_{i=1}^{mn} (\alpha_i X - \beta_i Y),
$$
giving exactly $mn$ roots in $\mathbb{P}^1(k)$ counted with multiplicity. The st
andard theorem (see Fulton, *Algebraic Curves*, §5) states that the multiplicity
 of $[\beta:\alpha]$ as a root of $R$ equals the sum of intersection multiplicit
ies $\sum_{P \mapsto [\beta:\alpha]} I(P, F \cap G)$. This theorem is proved by
purely algebraic means (properties of the resultant and local rings) and holds o
ver any algebraically closed field.

Summing over all roots:
$$
\sum_{P \in F \cap G} I(P, F \cap G) = \sum_{\text{roots of } R} (\text{multipli
city}) = mn.
$$

This requires algebraic closure (for $R$ to split) — which is given. ✓

Every property of projective transformations used in the proof of Bézout's theor
em either holds over an arbitrary field (Properties 1–5) or holds because algebr
aically closed fields are necessarily infinite (Property 6, via the Lemma). The
final counting step (Property 7) uses algebraic closure directly, which is the h
ypothesis.

**No property used in the proof requires $k = \mathbb{C}$, characteristic zero,
or any restriction beyond algebraic closure.** Topological proofs of Bézout's th
eorem (using winding numbers, etc.) are specific to $\mathbb{C}$ or $\mathbb{R}$
, but the algebraic proof — which is the one that uses projective transformation
s in the sense described above — is completely field-independent given algebraic
 closure.
