## Solution

**Claim:** There are no natural numbers $n$ such that the sum of the digits of $
n^2$ equals 2006.

**Proof.**

We use the well-known congruence between a number and its digit sum modulo 9.

**Lemma.** For every natural number $m$ with decimal representation $m = \sum_{i
} d_i \cdot 10^i$ (each $d_i \in \{0,1,\dots,9\}$), the digit sum $s(m) = \sum_i
 d_i$ satisfies
$$s(m) \equiv m \pmod{9}.$$
*Proof of lemma.* Since $10 \equiv 1 \pmod{9}$, we have $10^i \equiv 1 \pmod{9}$
 for all $i \ge 0$, hence
$$m = \sum_i d_i \cdot 10^i \equiv \sum_i d_i = s(m) \pmod{9}. \qquad \square$$

**Main argument.** Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists a natural numbe
r $n$ with $s(n^2) = 2006$. Applying the lemma to $m = n^2$,
$$n^2 \equiv s(n^2) = 2006 \pmod{9}.$$

Computing $2006 \bmod 9$: the digit sum of $2006$ is $2+0+0+6 = 8$, so
$$2006 \equiv 8 \pmod{9}.$$

Thus we would need
$$n^2 \equiv 8 \pmod{9}.$$

However, the quadratic residues modulo 9 are obtained by squaring each residue c
lass:
$$0^2 \equiv 0,\quad 1^2 \equiv 1,\quad 2^2 \equiv 4,\quad 3^2 \equiv 0,\quad 4^
2 \equiv 7,\quad 5^2 \equiv 7,\quad 6^2 \equiv 0,\quad 7^2 \equiv 4,\quad 8^2 \e
quiv 1 \pmod{9}.$$

The set of quadratic residues modulo 9 is therefore
$$\{0,\; 1,\; 4,\; 7\}.$$

Since $8 \notin \{0, 1, 4, 7\}$, the congruence $n^2 \equiv 8 \pmod{9}$ has **no
 solution**. This contradicts the requirement derived above.

**Conclusion.** No natural number $n$ satisfies $s(n^2) = 2006$. The set of such
 natural numbers is empty:
$$\boxed{\varnothing}.$$
