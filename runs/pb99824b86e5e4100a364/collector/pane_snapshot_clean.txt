## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, such a continuous function **r** always exists.

**Construction.** Pick any point $c \in (a,b)$ and define
$$\mathbf{r}(t) = \int_c^t \mathbf{v}(s)\,ds, \qquad t \in (a,b),$$
where the integral is taken component-wise. Since $\mathbf{v}$ is continuous, $\
mathbf{r}$ is well-defined and continuously differentiable on $(a,b)$, with
$$\mathbf{r}'(t) = \mathbf{v}(t), \qquad \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| = \|\mathbf{v}(t)\|
= 1 \neq 0.$$

**Verification of the limit.** Fix $t_0 \in (a,b)$. For $t \searrow t_0$ (i.e.,
$t > t_0$, $t \to t_0$),
$$\mathbf{r}(t) - \mathbf{r}(t_0) = \int_{t_0}^{t} \mathbf{v}(s)\,ds = \mathbf{v
}(t_0)(t - t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t}\bigl(\mathbf{v}(s) - \mathbf{v}(t_0)\bigr)\,ds.
$$

Since $\mathbf{v}$ is continuous at $t_0$, for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exi
sts $\delta > 0$ such that $\|\mathbf{v}(s) - \mathbf{v}(t_0)\| < \varepsilon$ w
henever $|s - t_0| < \delta$. Hence for $0 < t - t_0 < \delta$,
$$\left\|\int_{t_0}^{t}\bigl(\mathbf{v}(s)-\mathbf{v}(t_0)\bigr)\,ds\right\| \le
 \varepsilon\,(t-t_0).$$

athbf{v}(t_0))\,ds$, we have $\|\boldsymbol{\eta}(t)\| = o(t-t_0)$, so
$$\mathbf{r}(t)-\mathbf{r}(t_0) = (t-t_0)\bigl[\mathbf{v}(t_0) + \boldsymbol{\et
a}(t)/(t-t_0)\bigr],$$
where $\boldsymbol{\eta}(t)/(t-t_0) \to \mathbf{0}$ as $t \searrow t_0$.

Therefore
$$\|\mathbf{r}(t)-\mathbf{r}(t_0)\| = (t-t_0)\bigl\|\mathbf{v}(t_0) + \boldsymbo
l{\eta}(t)/(t-t_0)\bigr\|.$$
