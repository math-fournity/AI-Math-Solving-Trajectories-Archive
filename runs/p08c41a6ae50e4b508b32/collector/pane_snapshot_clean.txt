## Example: The Derivative Operator on Polynomials

Let $V = \mathbb{R}[x]$, the vector space of all polynomials with real coefficie
nts. This space is infinite-dimensional, with basis $\{1, x, x^2, x^3, \dots\}$.

Define the linear transformation $T: V \to V$ by
$$T(p) = p'$$
i.e., $T$ is the formal derivative operator. Linearity follows from the linearit
y of differentiation.

### $T$ has only one eigenvalue, namely $0$

Suppose $p \in V$ is an eigenvector of $T$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, so $p' = \
lambda p$ with $p \neq 0$.

- **If $\lambda = 0$:** then $p' = 0$, so $p$ is a nonzero constant polynomial.
This is valid, and every nonzero constant is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $0$.

- **If $\lambda \neq 0$:** the differential equation $p' = \lambda p$ has genera
l solution $p(x) = Ce^{\lambda x}$. But $e^{\lambda x}$ is not a polynomial (it
has infinitely many nonzero terms in its Taylor expansion, and no nonzero polyno
mial equals it). Hence $C = 0$, contradicting $p \neq 0$.

Therefore the only eigenvalue of $T$ is $\boxed{0}$.

### $T$ is not nilpotent

Recall $T$ is nilpotent if $T^n = 0$ for some positive integer $n$. But for ever
y $n \geq 1$,
$$T^n(x^n) = \frac{d^n}{dx^n}(x^n) = n! \neq 0.$$
Since $x^n \in V$ and $T^n(x^n) \neq 0$, we have $T^n \neq 0$ for every $n$. Hen
ce $T$ is **not** nilpotent.

### Conclusion

The derivative operator $T = d/dx$ on $V = \mathbb{R}[x]$ is a linear transforma
tion on an infinite-dimensional vector space whose only eigenvalue is $0$, yet $
T$ is not nilpotent. This contrasts with the finite-dimensional case, where an o
perator whose only eigenvalue is $0$ must be nilpotent (since its characteristic
 polynomial is $t^n$, and by Cayley–Hamilton $T^n = 0$). The infinite-dimensiona
lity is essential: there is no characteristic polynomial to force nilpotence.
