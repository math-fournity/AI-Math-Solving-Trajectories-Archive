## Proof

We work throughout in $\mathbb{F}_2$. "Odd determinant" means the determinant eq
uals $1$ in $\mathbb{F}_2$. Set $M = A \bmod 2$, an $n \times n$ matrix over $\m
athbb{F}_2$. The condition "$A$ is very odd" translates to:

> For every nonempty $S \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}$, $\det(M[S,S]) = 1$ in $\mathbb
{F}_2$.

Since reduction mod $2$ is a ring homomorphism, $A^k \bmod 2 = M^k$, and $\det((
A^k)[S,S])$ is odd iff $\det(M^k[S,S]) = 1$ in $\mathbb{F}_2$. Thus it suffices
to prove:

> **If $M$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$ has all principal minors equal to $1$, then so do
es $M^k$ for every $k \geq 1$.**

### Key Lemma

**Lemma.** *An $n \times n$ matrix $M$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$ has all principal min
ors equal to $1$ if and only if $M = I + N$ where $N$ is a $(0,1)$-matrix with z
ero diagonal and $N$ is nilpotent.*

**Proof of Lemma.**

**(⟹)** Suppose all principal minors of $M$ are $1$.

- The $1 \times 1$ principal minors give $M_{ii} = 1$ for all $i$. Set $N := M -
 I$ (equivalently $M + I$ in $\mathbb{F}_2$). Then $N$ is a $(0,1)$-matrix with
zero diagonal.

- The characteristic polynomial of $M$ is $p(x) = \det(xI - M)$. The coefficient
 of $x^{n-k}$ is $(-1)^k e_k$ where $e_k$ is the sum of all $k \times k$ princip
al minors. Over $\mathbb{F}_2$ the signs vanish, so this coefficient is $e_k = \
binom{n}{k} \cdot 1 = \binom{n}{k} \bmod 2$. By the binomial theorem:
$$p(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} x^{n-k} = (x+1)^n.$$

- By Cayley–Hamilton, $p(M) = (M + I)^n = 0$. Since $M + I = M - I = N$ in $\mat
hbb{F}_2$, we get $N^n = 0$, so $N$ is nilpotent.

**(⟸)** Suppose $M = I + N$ where $N$ is a $(0,1)$-matrix with zero diagonal and
 $N$ is nilpotent.

Consider the directed graph $G$ of $N$: vertices $\{1,\dots,n\}$, with edge $i \
to j$ iff $N_{ij} = 1$ (and $i \neq j$, since $N$ has zero diagonal). We claim $
G$ is acyclic. Indeed, if $G$ contained a directed cycle $i_1 \to i_2 \to \cdots
 \to i_m \to i_1$ of length $m \geq 2$, then $(N^m)_{i_1, i_1} \geq 1$ (the prod
uct along the cycle contributes), so $N^m \neq 0$. Moreover, traversing the cycl
e $j$ times shows $(N^{jm})_{i_1,i_1} \geq 1$ for all $j \geq 1$, so $N$ is not
nilpotent—contradiction.

Since $G$ is a DAG, there exists a permutation $\pi$ such that $\pi N \pi^{-1}$
is strictly upper triangular. Then $\pi M \pi^{-1} = I + \pi N \pi^{-1}$ is uppe
r triangular with all diagonal entries equal to $1$. For any nonempty $S$, the p
rincipal submatrix $(\pi M \pi^{-1})[\pi(S), \pi(S)]$ is upper triangular with a
ll diagonal entries $1$, hence has determinant $1$. Since simultaneous row/colum
n permutation merely relabels the principal submatrices, $\det(M[S,S]) = 1$ for
all nonempty $S$. $\square$

### Main Argument

Suppose $M$ has all principal minors $1$. By the Lemma, $M = I + N$ where $N$ is
 a $(0,1)$-matrix with zero diagonal and $N$ is nilpotent (with $N^n = 0$).

For $k \geq 1$, expand over $\mathbb{F}_2$:
$$M^k = (I + N)^k = \sum_{j=0}^{n} \binom{k}{j} N^j = I + N', \qquad N' := \sum_
{j=1}^{n} \binom{k}{j} N^j,$$
where $\binom{k}{j}$ is computed in $\mathbb{F}_2$ and $N^j = 0$ for $j \geq n$.

We verify that $N'$ satisfies the three conditions of the Lemma:

1. **$(0,1)$-matrix:** $N'$ is a matrix over $\mathbb{F}_2$, hence has entries i
n $\{0,1\}$.

2. **Zero diagonal:** Since $N$ is nilpotent with zero diagonal and $(0,1)$-entr
ies, its digraph is a DAG (as shown in the Lemma). In a DAG there are no closed
walks, so $(N^j)_{ii} = 0$ for every $j \geq 1$ and every $i$ (the entry counts
walks of length $j$ from $i$ to $i$). Therefore:
$$(N')_{ii} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \binom{k}{j} (N^j)_{ii} = 0.$$

3. **Nilpotent:** Every term in $N'$ contains $N^j$ with $j \geq 1$, so we can f
actor $N' = N \cdot h(N)$ for some polynomial $h$. Then:
$$(N')^n = \bigl(N \cdot h(N)\bigr)^n = N^n \cdot h(N)^n = 0 \cdot h(N)^n = 0.$$

By the Lemma (⟸ direction) applied to $N'$, the matrix $M^k = I + N'$ has all pr
incipal minors equal to $1$.

### Conclusion

We have shown that $M^k = (A \bmod 2)^k = A^k \bmod 2$ has every principal minor
 equal to $1$ in $\mathbb{F}_2$. Equivalently, every principal minor of $A^k$ is
 odd. Therefore $A^k$ is very odd for every $k \geq 1$. $\blacksquare$
