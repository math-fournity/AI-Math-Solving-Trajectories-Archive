## Proof

**Answer: 1** (for a nonzero ideal; the zero ideal is the degenerate case genera
ted by 0 polynomials).

### Lower bound

An ideal generated by 0 polynomials is, by definition, the zero ideal $\{0\}$. I
f we require a **nonzero** ideal (the standard interpretation of "generate an id
eal" in this context, since the zero ideal is trivially generated by the empty s
et and the question asks for a minimum number of *polynomials*), then at least 1
 polynomial is necessary. So the minimum is $\geq 1$.

### Upper bound (construction with 1 polynomial)

Consider the principal ideal
$$I = (x_1^2) \subset \mathbb{C}[x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n].$$

This ideal is generated by the single polynomial $x_1^2$.

**Claim:** $I$ contains no linear monomials.

A linear monomial is a monomial of total degree 1, i.e., one of $x_1, x_2, \ldot
s, x_n$.

Every element of $I$ has the form $x_1^2 \cdot f$ for some $f \in \mathbb{C}[x_1
, \ldots, x_n]$.

- If $f = 0$, then $x_1^2 f = 0$, which is not a linear monomial.
- If $f \neq 0$, then $x_1^2 f$ has total degree $\geq 2$ (since $x_1^2$ already
 contributes degree 2 and multiplication by a nonzero polynomial cannot reduce t
he degree). Hence $x_1^2 f$ has degree $\geq 2$ and cannot equal any linear mono
mial $x_j$ (which has degree 1).

Therefore no element of $I$ is a linear monomial; equivalently, $I$ contains no
linear monomials. Yet $I$ is nonzero (it contains $x_1^2$) and proper (it does n
ot contain $1$, since every element is divisible by $x_1^2$).

### Conclusion

- 0 generators yield only the zero ideal — a degenerate case.
- 1 generator suffices: the ideal $(x_1^2)$ is nonzero, proper, and contains no
linear monomials.
- No nonzero ideal can be generated by fewer than 1 polynomial.

Hence the minimum number of polynomials required is $\boxed{1}$.
