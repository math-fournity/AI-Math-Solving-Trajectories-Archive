The answer is **No**. Here is a counterexample.

---

**Claim.** There exists a function $f$, analytic on $\mathbb D$ and continuous o
n $\overline{\mathbb D}$, not identically zero, with infinitely many distinct ze
ros in $\mathbb D$ accumulating to $\partial\mathbb D$.

**Construction.** Set $a_n = 1 - \frac{1}{n^2}$ for $n \ge 2$, so $a_n \in (0,1)
 \subset \mathbb D$ and $a_n \to 1 \in \partial\mathbb D$. Since
$$\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}(1 - a_n) = \sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^2} < \infty,$$
the **Blaschke condition** is satisfied, so the Blaschke product
$$B(z) = \prod_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{a_n - z}{1 - a_n\, z}$$
converges uniformly on compact subsets of $\mathbb D$ to a bounded analytic func
tion on $\mathbb D$, with $|B(z)|\le 1$ for all $z\in\mathbb D$, and whose zero
set in $\mathbb D$ is exactly $\{a_n : n\ge 2\}$.

Now define
$$f(z) = (z-1)\,B(z).$$

**$f$ is analytic on $\mathbb D$:** Both $z\mapsto z-1$ and $B$ are analytic on
$\mathbb D$. ✓

**$f$ is continuous on $\overline{\mathbb D}$:** We check continuity at each bou
ndary point $\zeta\in\partial\mathbb D$.

- *At $\zeta = 1$:* Since $|B(z)|\le 1$ for all $z\in\mathbb D$,
  $$|f(z)| = |z-1|\cdot|B(z)| \le |z-1| \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \quad\text{as } z\
to 1.$$
  Defining $f(1):=0$, $f$ is continuous at $z=1$.

- *At $\zeta \neq 1$:* The zeros $a_n$ accumulate only at $1$, so $|\zeta - a_n|
$ is bounded below for all $n$. The Blaschke product $B$ extends continuously to
 $\zeta$ whenever $\sum_n \frac{1-|a_n|}{|\zeta - a_n|}<\infty$; since $|\zeta-a
_n|\ge c_\zeta>0$, this sum is bounded by $c_\zeta^{-1}\sum(1-a_n)<\infty$. Henc
e $B$ (and thus $f$) is continuous at $\zeta$.

**$f$ has infinitely many zeros accumulating to the boundary:** Since $z-1\neq 0
$ in $\mathbb D$, the zeros of $f$ in $\mathbb D$ are exactly the zeros of $B$,
namely $\{a_n\}_{n\ge 2} = \{\tfrac{3}{4},\tfrac{8}{9},\tfrac{15}{16},\dots\}$,
which are infinitely many distinct points in $\mathbb D$ with $a_n\to 1\in\parti
al\mathbb D$. ✓

**$f$ is not identically zero:** We compute $B(0) = \prod_{n=2}^{\infty} a_n = \
prod_{n=2}^{\infty}\!\left(1-\tfrac{1}{n^2}\right)$. The partial products telesc
ope:
$$\prod_{n=2}^{N}\frac{(n-1)(n+1)}{n^2} = \frac{1}{N}\cdot\frac{N+1}{2} = \frac{
N+1}{2N}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{1}{2}.$$
So $B(0)=\tfrac{1}{2}$, and
$$f(0) = (0-1)\cdot\tfrac{1}{2} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\neq 0.$$
Hence $f\not\equiv 0$. ✓

---

**Conclusion.** The function $f(z)=(z-1)\prod_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{(1-1/n^2)-z}{1
-(1-1/n^2)\,z}$ is analytic on $\mathbb D$, continuous on $\overline{\mathbb D}$
, has infinitely many zeros in $\mathbb D$ accumulating to the boundary point $1
$, and is not identically zero. Therefore, the given hypotheses do **not** force
 $f\equiv 0$.

The key point is that the identity theorem requires zeros to accumulate at a poi
nt **inside** the domain of analyticity. Accumulation at the boundary is insuffi
cient: the Blaschke condition $\sum(1-|z_n|)<\infty$ (which any nonzero $H^\inft
y$ function's zeros must satisfy) is compatible with $|z_n|\to 1$, and multiplyi
ng by $(z-1)$ enforces continuity at the accumulation point without introducing
interior zeros.
