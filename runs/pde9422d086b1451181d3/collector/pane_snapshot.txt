## Proof

**Yes**, such a prime ideal always exists. We prove that if $S^{-1}R$ is local,
then setting $\mathfrak{p}$ to be the contraction of its maximal ideal yields $S
^{-1}R \cong R_{\mathfrak{p}}$ as $R$-algebras.

### Setup

Let $\mathfrak{m}$ be the unique maximal ideal of $S^{-1}R$, and let
$$\mathfrak{p} := \mathfrak{m}^c = \{r \in R : r/1 \in \mathfrak{m}\}$$
be its contraction along the canonical map $\iota: R \to S^{-1}R$.

**$\mathfrak{p}$ is prime and disjoint from $S$.** Since $\mathfrak{m}$ is prime
 (maximal), its contraction $\mathfrak{p}$ is prime. Every $s \in S$ maps to a u
nit $s/1$ in $S^{-1}R$, hence $s/1 \notin \mathfrak{m}$, so $s \notin \mathfrak{
p}$. Thus $S \cap \mathfrak{p} = \varnothing$.

**$\mathfrak{p}$ is the unique maximal element among primes disjoint from $S$.**
 The primes of $S^{-1}R$ biject (via contraction/extension) with primes of $R$ d
isjoint from $S$. Since $S^{-1}R$ is local with maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$, ev
ery prime of $S^{-1}R$ is contained in $\mathfrak{m}$, so every prime of $R$ dis
joint from $S$ is contained in $\mathfrak{p}$. Hence $\mathfrak{p}$ is the large
st prime disjoint from $S$.

### Constructing the $R$-algebra map

Since $S \subseteq R \setminus \mathfrak{p}$, every element of $S$ becomes a uni
t in $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$. By the universal property of localization, there is a u
nique $R$-algebra homomorphism
$$\varphi: S^{-1}R \longrightarrow R_{\mathfrak{p}}, \qquad r/s \longmapsto r/s.
$$

### Injectivity

Suppose $\varphi(r/s) = 0$, i.e., $r/s = 0$ in $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$. Then there ex
ists $u \notin \mathfrak{p}$ with $ur = 0$. Since $R$ is an integral domain and
$u \neq 0$ (as $0 \in \mathfrak{p}$), we get $r = 0$, hence $r/s = 0$ in $S^{-1}
R$. So $\ker\varphi = 0$; $\varphi$ is injective.

### Surjectivity (the key step)

We claim: **for every $t \in R \setminus \mathfrak{p}$, the ideal $(t)$ meets $S
$.**

Indeed, $t \notin \mathfrak{p}$ means $t/1 \notin \mathfrak{m}$. Since $S^{-1}R$
 is local, every element outside $\mathfrak{m}$ is a unit, so $t/1$ is a unit in
 $S^{-1}R$. By the unit criterion for localization, $t/1$ is a unit iff there ex
ists $b \in R$ with $tb \in S$, i.e., $(t) \cap S \neq \varnothing$.

Now take any element $r/t \in R_{\mathfrak{p}}$ (with $t \notin \mathfrak{p}$).
By the claim, pick $a \in R$ with $s := at \in S$. Then in $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$:
$$\frac{r}{t} = \frac{ra}{at} = \frac{ra}{s} = \varphi\!\left(\frac{ra}{s}\right
).$$
So $\varphi$ is surjective.

### Conclusion

$\varphi: S^{-1}R \to R_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is a bijective $R$-algebra homomorphism,
 hence an $R$-algebra isomorphism. The desired prime is $\mathfrak{p} = \mathfra
k{m}^c$, the contraction of the maximal ideal of $S^{-1}R$.

**Remark on the role of the hypotheses.** Injectivity uses that $R$ is a domain.
 Surjectivity uses locality crucially: it is exactly the locality of $S^{-1}R$ t
hat forces every element outside $\mathfrak{p}$ to have a multiple in $S$, which
 is what makes the natural map $S^{-1}R \to R_{\mathfrak{p}}$ hit every fraction
 $r/t$ with $t \notin \mathfrak{p}$.
