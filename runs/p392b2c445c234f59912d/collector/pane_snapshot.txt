## Solution

We evaluate $I = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{-1/x^{2}}\,dx$.

**Key observation on the behavior at infinity.** For $x \neq 0$, the integrand i
s $f(x)=e^{-1/x^{2}}$. As $|x|\to\infty$,

$$
\frac{1}{x^{2}}\to 0^{+},\qquad -\frac{1}{x^{2}}\to 0^{-},\qquad e^{-1/x^{2}}\to
 e^{0}=1.
$$

So the integrand does **not** decay at infinity; it tends to the positive consta
nt $1$. This immediately suggests divergence.

**Rigorous proof of divergence.** Consider the tail on $[1,+\infty)$. For every
$x$ with $|x|\ge 1$, we have $x^{2}\ge 1$, hence $\frac{1}{x^{2}}\le 1$, hence $
-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\ge -1$, and therefore

$$
e^{-1/x^{2}}\ge e^{-1}>0.
$$

Integrating over $[1,+\infty)$,

$$
\int_{1}^{+\infty} e^{-1/x^{2}}\,dx \;\ge\; \int_{1}^{+\infty} e^{-1}\,dx \;=\;
e^{-1}\cdot(+\infty)\;=\;+\infty.
$$

Thus the improper integral over $[1,+\infty)$ already diverges to $+\infty$.

**Behavior near the origin (no cancellation possible).** Near $x=0$, as $x\to 0$
, $\frac{1}{x^{2}}\to+\\infty$, so $e^{-1/x^{2}}\to 0$. Defining $f(0)=0$ makes
$f$ continuous (indeed $C^{\infty}$) on $\mathbb{R}$, and $f$ is bounded by $1$.
 Hence $f$ is integrable on every bounded interval, e.g. on $[-1,1]$. In particu
lar $f(x)\ge 0$ everywhere, so there is no cancellation between the singularity
region and the tails — the integral is a sum of nonnegative contributions.

**Conclusion.** Since $f\ge 0$ on $\mathbb{R}$ and

$$
\int_{1}^{+\infty} e^{-1/x^{2}}\,dx = +\infty,
$$

the full improper integral diverges:

$$
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{-1/x^{2}}\,dx \;=\; +\infty \quad\text{(the i
ntegral diverges)}.}
$$

The divergence is caused by the integrand tending to $1$ (rather than to $0$) as
 $|x|\to\infty$, which makes the tails contribute infinite area. The point $x=0$
, despite being where $e^{-1/x^{2}}$ has its famous non-analytic smooth "flat" b
ehavior, is completely harmless for integration.
