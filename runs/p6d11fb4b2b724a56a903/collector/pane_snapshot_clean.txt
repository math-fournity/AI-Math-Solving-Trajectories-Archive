## Proof

We evaluate
$$I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\sin^2 x}{x^2}\,dx.$$

We use the **Plancherel (Parseval) theorem** for the Fourier transform. Adopt th
e convention
$$\widehat{f}(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,e^{-i\omega x}\,dx, \qquad
 f(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{f}(\omega)\,e^{i\omega x}\,d
\omega.$$
Plancherel states $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x)|^2\,dx = \frac{1}{2
\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\widehat{f}(\omega)|^2\,d\omega$.

**Step 1. Fourier transform of the rectangular function.**

Let $\mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}$ be the indicator of $[-1,1]$. Its Fourier transform is
$$\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}}(\omega)=\int_{-1}^{1}e^{-i\omega x}\,dx=\frac{e^
{-i\omega}-e^{i\omega}}{-i\omega}=\frac{2\sin\omega}{\omega},$$
with the value at $\omega=0$ understood as the limit $2$.

**Step 2. Invert to get the sinc function.**

By the inversion formula,
$$\mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{2\sin\omega}
{\omega}\,e^{i\omega x}\,d\omega.$$
Equivalently, taking the Fourier transform of $f(x)=\dfrac{\sin x}{x}$ (with $f(
0)=1$) gives
$$\widehat{f}(\omega)=\pi\,\mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}(\omega).$$
Indeed, this is the symmetric statement of Step 1: the Fourier transform is (up
to the $2\pi$ normalization) an involution, and $\frac{2\sin\omega}{\omega}$ is
the transform of $\mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}$, so $\frac{\sin x}{x}$ transforms to $\pi
\,\mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}$.

**Step 3. Apply Plancherel.**

With $f(x)=\dfrac{\sin x}{x}$, we have $|f(x)|^2=\dfrac{\sin^2 x}{x^2}$, so
$$I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x)|^2\,dx=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\w
idehat{f}(\omega)|^2\,d\omega=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\pi^2\,\mathb
f{1}_{[-1,1]}(\omega)\,d\omega.$$
The integrand is $\pi^2$ on $[-1,1]$ (length $2$) and $0$ elsewhere, hence
$$I=\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\pi^2\cdot 2=\pi.$$

**Step 4. Justification of Plancherel's applicability.**

The function $f(x)=\frac{\sin x}{x}$ (with $f(0)=1$) is continuous, bounded, and
 $f\in L^2(\mathbb{R})$ since $|f(x)|^2\sim 1/x^2$ is integrable at infinity and
 $f$ is bounded near $0$. Its Fourier transform $\pi\,\mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}\in L^1
\cap L^2$. Thus $f\in L^1\cap L^2$ is not strictly needed; Plancherel extends th
e $L^1\cap L^2$ isometry to all of $L^2$, and here both $f$ and $\widehat{f}$ ar
e in $L^2$, so the identity is valid. (One may also approximate $f$ by $f_R(x)=\
frac{\sin x}{x}\mathbf{1}_{[-R,R]}(x)\in L^1\cap L^2$ and let $R\to\infty$; $\wi
dehat{f_R}\to\pi\,\mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}$ in $L^2$ by dominated convergence, giving
 the same result.)

**Conclusion.**

$$\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^2 x}{x^2}\,dx=\pi.}$$
