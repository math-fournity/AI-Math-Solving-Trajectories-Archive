Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether there exists an algebraic m
odular lattice with elements $a \le b$ such that $f(x) = (x \vee a) \wedge b$ fa
ils to be a $\vee$-homomorphism.

**Answer: Yes, such a lattice exists.**

## Proof

Consider $X = L(\mathbb{R}^2)$, the lattice of linear subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^2
$, ordered by inclusion, with $\vee = $ subspace sum and $\wedge = $ subspace in
tersection.

**$X$ is a modular lattice.** The lattice of subspaces of any vector space is mo
dular: if $U \subseteq W$, then $U + (V \cap W) = (U + V) \cap W$ for all subspa
ces $V$. This is the classical modular law for subspaces.

**$X$ is algebraic.** $X$ is complete (arbitrary intersections and sums of subsp
aces exist). Since $\mathbb{R}^2$ is finite-dimensional, every subspace of $\mat
hbb{R}^2$ is finite-dimensional, hence compact in the lattice-theoretic sense (i
f $W \le \bigvee_i W_i$, then $W$ is spanned by finitely many vectors, each lyin
g in a finite sub-join). Therefore every element is compact, and trivially every
 element is the join of compact elements (namely itself). So $X$ is algebraic.

**Choice of elements.** Let $a = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ (the zero subspace) and $b = \o
peratorname{span}(e_1 + e_2)$ (a line). Clearly $a \le b$.

With this choice, $f(x) = (x \vee a) \wedge b = (x + \{\mathbf{0}\}) \cap b = x
\cap b$, i.e., $f$ is simply "meet with $b$."

**Counterexample to $\vee$-homomorphism.** Set $x = \operatorname{span}(e_1)$ an
d $y = \operatorname{span}(e_2)$.

- $x \vee y = \operatorname{span}(e_1) + \operatorname{span}(e_2) = \mathbb{R}^2
$.
- $f(x \vee y) = \mathbb{R}^2 \cap b = b = \operatorname{span}(e_1 + e_2) \neq \
{\mathbf{0}\}$.
- $f(x) = \operatorname{span}(e_1) \cap \operatorname{span}(e_1+e_2) = \{\mathbf
{0}\}$ (the lines are distinct).
- $f(y) = \operatorname{span}(e_2) \cap \operatorname{span}(e_1+e_2) = \{\mathbf
{0}\}$ (the lines are distinct).
- $f(x) \vee f(y) = \{\mathbf{0}\} \vee \{\mathbf{0}\} = \{\mathbf{0}\}$.

Therefore:
$$f(x \vee y) = \operatorname{span}(e_1+e_2) \neq \{\mathbf{0}\} = f(x) \vee f(y
).$$

This shows $f$ is not a $\vee$-homomorphism.

**Remark on why modularity does not suffice.** The modular law gives us, for $a
\le b$:
$$(x \vee a) \wedge b = a \vee (x \wedge b),$$
so $f(x) = a \vee (x \wedge b)$. The failure of join-preservation reduces to the
 failure of $\wedge$ to distribute over $\vee$: we need $(x \vee y) \wedge b = (
x \wedge b) \vee (y \wedge b)$, which is exactly the distributive law — a proper
ty stronger than modularity. The subspace lattice of $\mathbb{R}^2$ is modular b
ut not distributive (it contains the diamond $M_3$), providing the required coun
terexample.
