## Solution

We claim **C = 1** works, and that it is sharp (optimal).

### Step 1 — Reduction to non-negative functions

For general $f \in L^1(0,+\infty)$,
$$|Tf(x)| = \frac{1}{x}\left|\int_0^x f(t)\,dt\right| \le \frac{1}{x}\int_0^x |f
(t)|\,dt = T(|f|)(x).$$
Hence $\{|Tf|>\lambda\}\subseteq \{T(|f|)>\lambda\}$, so it suffices to prove
$$\lambda\, m\big(\{x>0 : Tg(x)>\lambda\}\big) \le \|g\|_1$$
for every $g\ge 0$, $g\in L^1(0,+\infty)$.

### Step 2 — Proof for $g\ge 0$

Let $g\in L^1(0,+\infty)$, $g\ge 0$, and define the primitive
$$H(x)=\int_0^x g(t)\,dt.$$
Then $H$ is absolutely continuous, non-decreasing, $H(0)=0$, and $H(x)\to \|g\|_
1=:M<\infty$ as $x\to\infty$. We have $Tg(x)=H(x)/x$.

Fix $\lambda>0$ and set
$$E=\{x>0 : H(x)>\lambda x\}=\{x>0 : Tg(x)>\lambda\}.$$

**$E$ is bounded.** Since $H(x)\to M$, we have $H(x)/x\to 0$ as $x\to\infty$, so
 for all sufficiently large $x$, $H(x)/x<\lambda$; thus $E\subset(0,R)$ for some
 $R<\infty$.

**$E$ is open.** The map $x\mapsto H(x)-\lambda x$ is continuous, so $E=\{H(x)-\
lambda x>0\}$ is open in $(0,+\infty)$. Consequently
$$E=\bigcup_{i}(a_i,b_i)$$
is a countable union of disjoint open intervals with $0\le a_i<b_i<\infty$.

**Endpoint identity.** If $a_i>0$, then $a_i$ is a boundary point of $E$, so by
continuity $H(a_i)=\lambda a_i$; if $a_i=0$, then $H(0)=0=\lambda\cdot 0$. Since
 $E$ is bounded, every $b_i<\infty$, and $b_i$ being a boundary point gives $H(b
_i)=\lambda b_i$. (The alternative $b_i=\infty$ is excluded by $H(x)/x\to 0$.)

Therefore, on each component,
$$\int_{a_i}^{b_i} g(t)\,dt = H(b_i)-H(a_i)=\lambda b_i-\lambda a_i=\lambda(b_i-
a_i).$$

**Summing over components.** Since $g\ge 0$ and the intervals are disjoint, mono
tone convergence (equivalently, countable additivity of the integral of a non-ne
gative function over an open set) gives
$$\int_E g(t)\,dt = \sum_i \int_{a_i}^{b_i} g(t)\,dt = \sum_i \lambda(b_i-a_i)=\
lambda\, m(E).$$

Since $g\ge 0$,
$$\int_E g(t)\,dt \le \int_0^\infty g(t)\,dt = \|g\|_1.$$

Combining,
$$\boxed{\lambda\, m\big(\{Tg>\lambda\}\big) = \int_E g \le \|g\|_1.}$$

Applying this to $g=|f|$ and using Step 1 yields, for all $f\in L^1(0,+\infty)$
and all $\lambda>0$,
$$\lambda\, m\big(\{|Tf|>\lambda\}\big)\le \|f\|_1.$$

So **$C=1$** is a valid constant.

### Step 3 — Sharpness ($C=1$ is optimal)

For $\varepsilon>0$ let $f_\varepsilon = \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\mathbf{1}_{(0,\
varepsilon)}$, so $\|f_\varepsilon\|_1=1$. Then
$$Tf_\varepsilon(x)=\frac{1}{x}\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\min(x,\varepsilon)=\be
gin{cases}1/\varepsilon,& 0<x\le\varepsilon,\\[2pt]1/x,& x>\varepsilon.\end{case
s}$$
For any $0<\lambda<1/\varepsilon$,
$$\{Tf_\varepsilon>\lambda\}=(0,\,1/\lambda),\qquad m\big(\{Tf_\varepsilon>\lamb
da\}\big)=1/\lambda,$$
and therefore
$$\lambda\, m\big(\{Tf_\varepsilon>\lambda\}\big)=1=\|f_\varepsilon\|_1.$$
Thus any admissible constant must satisfy $C\ge 1$. Since $C=1$ is admissible, i
t is the best (smallest) possible constant.

**Conclusion.** The constant $C=1$ satisfies
$$\lambda\, m\big(\{|Tf|>\lambda\}\big)\le \|f\|_1\qquad\forall\,\lambda>0,\;\fo
rall\,f\in L^1(0,+\infty),$$
and no smaller constant is valid.
