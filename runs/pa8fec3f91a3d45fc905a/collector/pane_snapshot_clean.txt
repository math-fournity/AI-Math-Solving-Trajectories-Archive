Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the inclusion $L^p(\mathbb{
R}) \cap L^q(\mathbb{R}) \subseteq L^r(\mathbb{R})$ (with $p < q$) forces $r \in
 [p, q]$.

**Answer: Yes.** I'll prove both directions — the classical interpolation (suffi
ciency) and counterexamples (necessity).

---

## Proof

### Part 1: If $r \in [p, q]$, then $L^p \cap L^q \subseteq L^r$ (classical inte
rpolation)

Let $f \in L^p \cap L^q$ with $p < q$ and $r \in [p, q]$. Write $r = \theta p +
(1-\theta)q$ for some $\theta \in [0, 1]$. By Hölder's inequality (or the log-co
nvexity of $L^s$ norms):

$$\|f\|_r^r = \int |f|^r = \int |f|^{\theta p} |f|^{(1-\theta)q} \leq \left(\int
 |f|^p\right)^\theta \left(\int |f|^q\right)^{1-\theta} = \|f\|_p^{\theta p} \|f
\|_q^{(1-\theta)q} < \infty.$$

So $f \in L^r$. (The case $\theta \in \{0,1\}$ is trivial.)

### Part 2: If $r > q$, then $L^p \cap L^q \not\subseteq L^r$

**Counterexample.** Define $f(x) = |x|^{-1/r} \cdot \mathbf{1}_{(0,1)}(x)$.

- **$f \in L^p$:** $\int_0^1 x^{-p/r}\,dx = \frac{1}{1 - p/r} < \infty$, since $
r > q > p$ gives $p/r < 1$. ✓
- **$f \in L^q$:** $\int_0^1 x^{-q/r}\,dx = \frac{1}{1 - q/r} < \infty$, since $
r > q$ gives $q/r < 1$. ✓
- **$f \notin L^r$:** $\int_0^1 x^{-1}\,dx = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0^+} \ln(1/\v
arepsilon) = \infty$. ✗

Thus $f \in L^p \cap L^q$ but $f \notin L^r$, so the inclusion fails for $r > q$
.

### Part 3: If $r < p$, then $L^p \cap L^q \not\subseteq L^r$

**Counterexample.** Define $f(x) = |x|^{-1/r} \cdot \mathbf{1}_{(1,\infty)}(x)$.

- **$f \in L^p$:** $\int_1^\infty x^{-p/r}\,dx = \frac{1}{p/r - 1} < \infty$, si
nce $r < p$ gives $p/r > 1$. ✓
- **$f \in L^q$:** $\int_1^\infty x^{-q/r}\,dx = \frac{1}{q/r - 1} < \infty$, si
nce $r < p < q$ gives $q/r > 1$. ✓
- **$f \notin L^r$:** $\int_1^\infty x^{-1}\,dx = \lim_{R \to \infty} \ln R = \i
nfty$. ✗

Thus $f \in L^p \cap L^q$ but $f \notin L^r$, so the inclusion fails for $r < p$
.

### Conclusion

Combining all three parts:

- $r \in [p, q] \Longrightarrow L^p \cap L^q \subseteq L^r$ (Part 1).
- $r > q \Longrightarrow L^p \cap L^q \not\subseteq L^r$ (Part 2).
- $r < p \Longrightarrow L^p \cap L^q \not\subseteq L^r$ (Part 3).

Therefore $L^p(\mathbb{R}) \cap L^q(\mathbb{R}) \subseteq L^r(\mathbb{R})$ **if
and only if** $r \in [p, q]$. In particular, the answer to the question is **yes
**: the inclusion forces $r \in [p, q]$.
