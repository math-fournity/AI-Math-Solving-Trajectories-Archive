Looking at this problem, I need to find sets in 3D space that are non-planar, no
t all of space, and contain the circle through any three of their points.

**Answer: A sphere.**

## Proof

### A sphere satisfies all three conditions

Let $S$ be a sphere. It is not contained in any plane, and $S \neq \mathbb{R}^3$
. For any three non-collinear points $A, B, C \in S$, they determine a plane $P$
. Since $P$ contains three points of $S$, it is a secant plane, and $P \cap S$ i
s a circle passing through $A, B, C$ and contained in $S$. ✓

### A sphere is the only such set

Let $M \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$ satisfy: (i) $M$ is not planar, (ii) $M \neq \mat
hbb{R}^3$, (iii) the circle through any three non-collinear points of $M$ lies i
n $M$.

**Step 1: $M$ contains a circle.** Since $M$ is not planar, it contains four non
-coplanar points, hence three non-collinear points $A, B, C$. The circle $C_0$ t
hrough them lies in $M$ (by (iii)). Let $\pi_0$ be its plane.

**Step 2: $M$ contains a point outside $\pi_0$.** Since $M$ is not planar, there
 exists $D \in M$, $D \notin \pi_0$. The circle $C_0$ and point $D$ determine a
unique sphere $S$.

**Step 3: $S \subseteq M$.** Take any $P \in S$. The plane through $D$ and $P$ m
eets $\pi_0$ in a line $\ell$. If $\ell$ cuts $C_0$ in two points $A, B$, then $
P$ lies on the circle through $A, B, D$ (the intersection of this plane with $S$
), which is in $M$ since $A, B, D \in M$. This covers an open dense subset of $S
$.

For any remaining $P$ (where $\ell$ is tangent to $C_0$): pick $E \in M \cap S$
with $E \neq P, D$ and $E \notin \pi_0$, chosen so the plane through $E, P$ meet
s $C_0$ in two points $F, G$ (the bad choices of $E$ form a curve on $S$, so thi
s is possible since $M \cap S$ is dense). Then $P$ lies on the circle through $E
, F, G \in M$, so $P \in M$. Thus $S \subseteq M$.

**Step 4: If $M$ contains any point $X \notin S$, then $M$ contains a second sph
ere $S' \neq S$ through $C_0$.**

*Case $X \notin \pi_0$:* Take $A, B \in C_0$. The circle $C_1$ through $X, A, B$
 lies in $M$ in a plane $\pi_1 \neq \pi_0$. Since $X \notin S$, the circle $C_1$
 is not contained in $S$; pick $E \in C_1$, $E \neq A, B$, so $E \notin S$ and $
E \notin \pi_0$. By the argument of Step 3 (using $E$ instead of $D$), the spher
e $S'$ through $C_0$ and $E$ satisfies $S' \subseteq M$. Since $E \notin S$, we
have $S' \neq S$, and $S \cap S' = C_0$.

*Case $X \in \pi_0 \setminus S$:* Then $X \notin C_0$. Take $A, B \in C_0$ with
$A, B, X$ non-collinear; the circle through them lies in $\pi_0 \cap M$ and diff
ers from $C_0$. Pick $Y$ on it with $Y \notin C_0$. Using $Y, D$, and a point of
 $C_0$, form a circle in $M$ not lying in $\pi_0$; a point $E$ on it satisfies $
E \notin \pi_0$, $E \notin S$, reducing to the first case.

**Step 5: Two distinct spheres through $C_0$ force $M = \mathbb{R}^3$.**

We have $S, S' \subseteq M$ with $S \cap S' = C_0$ in plane $\pi_0$. The centers
 of $S$ and $S'$ both lie on the line $\ell$ perpendicular to $\pi_0$ through th
e center of $C_0$.

Take any point $Z \in \mathbb{R}^3$. Let $\pi$ be the plane through $Z$ and $\el
l$. Then:
- $\pi \cap S = C_S$ (a great circle of $S$), so $C_S \subseteq M$.
- $\pi \cap S' = C_{S'}$ (a great circle of $S'$), so $C_{S'} \subseteq M$.
- $\pi \cap \pi_0$ is a line through the center of $C_0$, meeting $C_0$ in two p
oints $R_1, R_2 \in M$.
- $C_S \cap C_{S'} = \{R_1, R_2\}$ (two distinct circles in $\pi$).

**2D Lemma:** *In a plane, if $M$ contains two distinct circles meeting at $R_1,
 R_2$, and has the circle-closure property, then $M$ contains the entire plane.*

*Proof of Lemma:* For any $Z$ in the plane with $Z \neq R_1, R_2$: pick $P$ on t
he first circle ($P \neq R_1, R_2$, $P, R_1, Z$ non-collinear). The circle throu
gh $P, R_1, Z$ meets the second circle at $R_1$ and generically one other point
$Q$. For all but finitely many choices of $P$, $Q$ exists and $Q \neq R_1$. Then
 $P, Q, R_1 \in M$ and $Z$ lies on their circle, so $Z \in M$. Since $R_1, R_2 \
in M$ already, the entire plane lies in $M$. $\square$

Applying the lemma in $\pi$: $\pi \subseteq M$. Since $Z$ was arbitrary, $M = \m
athbb{R}^3$, contradicting condition (ii).

**Conclusion:** $M$ cannot contain any point outside $S$, so $M \subseteq S$. Co
mbined with Step 3, $M = S$: **a sphere**. $\blacksquare$
