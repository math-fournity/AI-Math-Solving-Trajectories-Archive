$$u(x) = \sum_{|\alpha| = m} a_\alpha\, x^\alpha, \qquad a_\alpha \in \mathbb{R}

where the sum ranges over all multi-indices $\alpha = (\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$

### Sufficiency (every homogeneous polynomial of degree $m$ works)

A polynomial is real-analytic on $\mathbb{R}^n$. If $u(x) = \sum_{|\alpha|=m} a_
\alpha x^\alpha$, then for any $t \in \mathbb{R}$:
$$u(tx) = \sum_{|\alpha|=m} a_\alpha (tx)^\alpha = \sum_{|\alpha|=m} a_\alpha\,
t^{|\alpha|}\, x^\alpha = t^m \sum_{|\alpha|=m} a_\alpha\, x^\alpha = t^m u(x).$
$
So the homogeneity condition is satisfied. $\checkmark$

### Necessity (every solution is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $m$)

Since $u$ is real-analytic on $\mathbb{R}^n$, it admits a convergent power serie

$$u(x) = \sum_{\alpha} a_\alpha\, x^\alpha = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \sum_{|\alpha|=
k} a_\alpha\, x^\alpha,$$
valid in a neighborhood of $0$ (and in fact the function is real-analytic everyw
here, but we only need the expansion near the origin).

**Step 1: Evaluate at the origin.** Setting $x = 0$ and $t = 0$ in $u(tx) = t^m
u(x)$ gives $u(0) = 0 \cdot u(0) = 0$ (since $m \geq 1$). Thus $a_0 = u(0) = 0$.

**Step 2: Compare power series in $t$.** Fix any $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Define $f

The power series of $f$ at $t = 0$ is obtained by substituting:
$$f(t) = u(tx) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \left(\sum_{|\alpha|=k} a_\alpha\, x^\alpha
\right) t^k.$$

The power series of $g$ at $t = 0$ is:
$$g(t) = t^m u(x) = t^m \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \sum_{|\alpha|=k} a_\alpha\, x^\alph
a = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \left(\sum_{|\alpha|=k} a_\alpha\, x^\alpha\right) t^{k+
m} = \sum_{j=m}^{\infty} \left(\sum_{|\alpha|=j-m} a_\alpha\, x^\alpha\right) t^
{j}.$$

By **uniqueness of the power series expansion** of a real-analytic function (the
 coefficients of $t^j$ must agree for every $j \geq 0$), we equate coefficients
of $t^j$:

- **For $j < m$:** The coefficient of $t^j$ in $g$ is $0$, so $\sum_{|\alpha|=j}
 a_\alpha\, x^\alpha = 0$ for all $x$ in a neighborhood of the origin. A polynom

$|\alpha| = j$.

- **For $j = m$:** The coefficient of $t^m$ in $f$ is $\sum_{|\alpha|=m} a_\alph
a\, x^\alpha$, and in $g$ it is $\sum_{|\alpha|=0} a_\alpha\, x^\alpha = a_0 = 0
$. Wait—let me recompute. The coefficient of $t^m$ in $g$ is $\sum_{|\alpha|=0}
a_\alpha x^\alpha = a_0 \cdot 1 = a_0 = 0$ (from Step 1). And the coefficient of

That can't be right—this would force $u \equiv 0$. Let me re-examine.

Actually, I need to be more careful. $g(t) = t^m u(x)$ where $u(x)$ is a **fixed

So the power series of $g$ in $t$ is:
$$g(t) = u(x)\, t^m = \left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \sum_{|\alpha|=k} a_\alpha x^\al
pha\right) t^m.$$

But $u(x)$ is just a constant (with respect to $t$), so the coefficient of $t^j$

- $u(x)$ if $j = m$,
- $0$ if $j \neq m$.

The coefficient of $t^j$ in $f(t) = u(tx)$ is $\sum_{|\alpha|=j} a_\alpha x^\alp
ha$.

By uniqueness of power series in $t$:

- **For $j \neq m$:** $\sum_{|\alpha|=j} a_\alpha x^\alpha = 0$ for all $x$ in a
 neighborhood of $0$. Since this is a polynomial in $x$ vanishing on an open set

- **For $j = m$:** $\sum_{|\alpha|=m} a_\alpha x^\alpha = u(x)$. This is automat

**Step 3: Conclusion.** From the case $j \neq m$, all coefficients $a_\alpha$ wi

$$u(x) = \sum_{|\alpha| = m} a_\alpha\, x^\alpha,$$
which is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $m$. $\blacksquare$
