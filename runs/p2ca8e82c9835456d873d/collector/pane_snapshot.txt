## Solution

**Answer: No.** A function whose partial derivatives are of bounded variation ne
ed not belong to $W^{1,\infty}$.

### Key Obstruction

The Sobolev space $W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)$ requires $f \in L^\infty(\Omega)$ **and
** every weak partial derivative $\partial_i f \in L^\infty(\Omega)$. The space
$BV(\Omega)$ of functions of bounded variation only guarantees $L^1$ membership
and, via the Sobolev–BV embedding, $L^{n/(n-1)}(\Omega)$ on a bounded Lipschitz
domain. For $n \geq 2$, $n/(n-1) < \infty$, so **$BV(\Omega) \not\subset L^\inft
y(\Omega)$**. Hence a partial derivative can be of bounded variation yet fail to
 be essentially bounded, immediately excluding $f$ from $W^{1,\infty}$.

### Counterexample ($n = 2$)

Let $\Omega = B(0,1) \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ and define

$$
f(x_1, x_2) \;=\; \int_{0}^{x_1} \log\!\sqrt{t^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}\;dt .
$$

**Step 1 — The partial derivative $\partial_1 f$ is of bounded variation.** Diff
erentiating under the integral,

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}(x_1,x_2) \;=\; \log\!\sqrt{x_1^{2}+x_2^{2}} \;=\
; \log|x|.
$$

We verify $\log|x| \in BV(B(0,1))$. Its distributional gradient is $\nabla\log|x
| = x/|x|^{2}$ (for $x\neq 0$), so

$$
|D(\log|x|)|(B(0,1)) \;\leq\; \int_{B(0,1)} \frac{1}{|x|}\,dx \;=\; \int_{0}^{1}
\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{r}\,r\,d\theta\,dr \;=\; 2\pi \;<\;\infty .
$$

Since $\log|x|$ is locally integrable on $B(0,1)$ (as $\int_{0}^{1}|\log r|\,r\,
dr < \infty$) and its total variation is finite, $\log|x| \in BV(B(0,1))$.

**Step 2 — $\partial_1 f \notin L^\infty$.** As $x \to 0$, $\log|x| \to -\infty$
, so $\log|x|$ is unbounded and $\partial_1 f \notin L^\infty(\Omega)$.

**Step 3 — Conclusion.** Since $W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)$ requires every first-order
 partial derivative to lie in $L^\infty(\Omega)$, and $\partial_1 f = \log|x| \n
otin L^\infty(\Omega)$, we have

$$
f \notin W^{1,\infty}(\Omega),
$$

even though $\partial_1 f$ is of bounded variation.

### Remark on dimension

The failure is intrinsic to $n \geq 2$: the BV embedding $BV \hookrightarrow L^{
n/(n-1)}$ is strictly weaker than $L^\infty$. (In $n=1$ on a **bounded** interva
l, $BV \hookrightarrow L^\infty$ does hold, so the implication can survive there
; but on unbounded domains even $n=1$ fails—e.g., $f(x)=x$ on $\mathbb{R}$ has $
f'=1\in BV$ yet $f\notin L^\infty(\mathbb{R})$.)
