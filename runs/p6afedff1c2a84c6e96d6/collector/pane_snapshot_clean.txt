## Proof

**Answer: Yes.** The minimal polynomial of any constructible number over $\mathb
b{Q}$ is solvable by radicals.

### Setup and key definitions

A number $t$ is **constructible** if and only if it lies in a field obtained fro
m $\mathbb{Q}$ by a finite tower of quadratic extensions:

$$\mathbb{Q} = K_0 \subset K_1 \subset \cdots \subset K_n, \qquad _{i+1}:K_i]
= 2, \qquad t \in K_n.$$

Equivalently, each step adjoins a square root: $K_{i+1} = K_i(\sqrt{a_i})$ for s
ome $a_i \in K_i$. By multiplicativity of degree in towers,

$$_n : \mathbb{Q}] = 2^n.$$

Since $\mathbb{Q}(t) \subseteq K_n$, we have $[\mathbb{Q}(t):\mathbb{Q}] \mid 2^
n$, so $\deg f = [\mathbb{Q}(t):\mathbb{Q}] = 2^m$ for some $m \le n$.

### The splitting field of $f$ has degree a power of $2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$

Let $L$ be the **normal closure** of $K_n$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. We prove by induct
ion on $n$ that $L/\mathbb{Q}$ is Galois with $[L:\mathbb{Q}]$ a power of $2$.

**Base case** ($n=0$): $L = \mathbb{Q}$, degree $1 = 2^0$. ✓

**Inductive step**: Assume the normal closure $M$ of $K_{n-1}/\mathbb{Q}$ is Gal
ois over $\mathbb{Q}$ with $[M:\mathbb{Q}] = 2^r$. Write $K_n = K_{n-1}(\sqrt{a}
)$ with $a \in K_{n-1}$.

Since $M/\mathbb{Q}$ is normal and contains $K_{n-1}$, every $\mathbb{Q}$-embedd
ing $\sigma_j : K_{n-1} \hookrightarrow \overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ has image in $M$
and extends to an automorphism of $M$. Let $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_s$ be these
embeddings (where $s = _{n-1}:\mathbb{Q}] = 2^{n-1}$). The normal closure of $
K_n/\mathbb{Q}$ is

$$L = M\bigl(\sqrt{\sigma_1(a)},\; \sqrt{\sigma_2(a)},\; \dots,\; \sqrt{\sigma_s
(a)}\bigr).$$

Each adjunction $\sqrt{\sigma_j(a)}$ produces an extension of degree $1$ or $2$
over the field generated by the preceding ones (it is the root of $x^2 - \sigma_
j(a)$). Therefore

$$[L : M] \mid 2^s = 2^{2^{n-1}},$$

and so

$$[L:\mathbb{Q}] = [L:M]\cdot[M:\mathbb{Q}] \mid 2^{2^{n-1}} \cdot 2^r = 2^{2^{n
-1}+r},$$

which is a power of $2$. Moreover $L/\mathbb{Q}$ is Galois (it is a normal closu
re in characteristic $0$). This completes the induction.

### The Galois group of $f$ is a $2$-group

Let $E$ be the splitting field of $f(x)$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, i.e., the normal clo
sure of $\mathbb{Q}(t)/\mathbb{Q}$. Since $\mathbb{Q}(t) \subseteq K_n \subseteq
 L$ and $L/\mathbb{Q}$ is normal, $L$ contains every conjugate of $t$, hence $E
\subseteq L$.

Because $L/\mathbb{Q}$ is Galois and $E$ is an intermediate Galois extension (be
ing a splitting field, $E/\mathbb{Q}$ is normal and separable), the Galois corre
spondence gives

$$\operatorname{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}) \cong \operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q})\,/\,\o
peratorname{Gal}(L/E).$$

Thus $\operatorname{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q})$ is a **quotient of a $2$-group**, hence
itself a $2$-group (its order divides $[L:\mathbb{Q}]$, a power of $2$).

### Conclusion via Galois's theorem

Every finite $p$-group is solvable (this follows from the fact that the center o
f a nontrivial $p$-group is nontrivial, giving a central series with cyclic fact
ors of order $p$). In particular, $\operatorname{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q})$ is a $2$-gr
oup and therefore **solvable**.

By the Galois solvability criterion (a polynomial over a field of characteristic
 $0$ is solvable by radicals if and only if the Galois group of its splitting fi
eld is solvable), the minimal polynomial $f(x)$ is **solvable by radicals** over
 $\mathbb{Q}$.

$\blacksquare$
