## Proof

**Part 1: A holomorphic square root of $f$ exists on a neighborhood of $z_0$.**

Since $w = f(z)\,dz^2$ is a 2-differential, $f$ is holomorphic on $U$. We are gi
ven $f(z_0) \neq 0$. By continuity of $f$, there is a neighborhood $W \subseteq
U$ of $z_0$ on which $f$ never vanishes. Write
$$f(z) = f(z_0)\,g(z), \qquad g(z) := \frac{f(z)}{f(z_0)}.$$
Then $g$ is holomorphic on $W$ with $g(z_0) = 1$. By continuity we may shrink $W
$ to a neighborhood $V \subseteq W$ of $z_0$ such that $|g(z) - 1| < 1$ for all
$z \in V$. In particular $g(V)$ lies in the disk $D(1,1)$, which is simply conne
cted and does not contain $0$.

On a simply connected domain avoiding $0$, a holomorphic logarithm of $g$ exists
: there is a holomorphic function $h: V \to \mathbb{C}$ with $e^{h(z)} = g(z)$.
(This follows because $g'/g$ is holomorphic on $V$, so $h(z) = \int_{z_0}^{z} g'
(\zeta)/g(\zeta)\,d\zeta$ is a well-defined primitive, and $g\,e^{-h}$ has zero
derivative with value $1$ at $z_0$.) Setting $g(z)^{1/2} := e^{h(z)/2}$ gives a
holomorphic square root of $g$ on $V$.

Choosing either square root $c$ of $f(z_0)$, we obtain a well-defined holomorphi
c function
$$f(z)^{1/2} := c\,g(z)^{1/2}, \qquad z \in V,$$
satisfying $\bigl(f(z)^{1/2}\bigr)^2 = f(z)$. Note also $f(z)^{1/2} \neq 0$ on $
V$ since $f \neq 0$ on $V$.

---

**Part 2: $q$ defines a local coordinate on $V$.**

Define
$$q(v) := \int_{z_0}^{v} f(z)^{1/2}\,dz, \qquad v \in V.$$
After shrinking $V$ to be simply connected (e.g. a small disk), the integrand $f
(z)^{1/2}$ is holomorphic on $V$, so the integral is path-independent and $q$ is
 a well-defined holomorphic function on $V$ with $q(z_0) = 0$.

Its differential is
$$dq = f(z)^{1/2}\,dz.$$
Since $f(z)^{1/2} \neq 0$ on $V$, we have $dq \neq 0$ at every point of $V$; equ
ivalently $q'(z) = f(z)^{1/2}$ never vanishes. By the holomorphic inverse functi
on theorem, $q: V \to q(V)$ is a biholomorphism onto its image. Hence $q$ is a v
alid local coordinate on $V$.

---

**Verification via the hint.** In the $q$-coordinate,
$$(dq)^2 = \bigl(f(z)^{1/2}\,dz\bigr)^2 = f(z)\,dz^2 = w.$$
Thus the 2-differential $w$ takes the canonical form $w = (dq)^2$ in the coordin
ate $q$, confirming that $q$ is the natural local coordinate adapted to $w$. $\b
lacksquare$
