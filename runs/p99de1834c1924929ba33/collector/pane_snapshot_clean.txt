## Proof

**Claim.** There exists a bijection $f:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}$ such that $f\bigl(\ma
thbb{Q}\cap(0,1)\bigr)=\mathbb{Q}$ and $f\bigl((0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}\bigr)=\m
athbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$.

The construction splits cleanly into a "rational part" and an "irrational part."

---

### Step 1. A bijection between the rationals

The set $\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)$ is countably infinite (it is a subset of the count
able set $\mathbb{Q}$ and is clearly infinite, e.g. it contains $\{1/n:n\ge 2\}$
). The set $\mathbb{Q}$ is also countably infinite. Therefore there exists a bij
ection
$$\varphi:\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)\;\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}\;\mathbb{Q}.$$
(For instance, enumerate both sets and match the $n$-th element of one to the $n
$-th element of the other.)

---

### Step 2. A bijection between the irrationals

We show that both $(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$
 have cardinality $\mathfrak{c}=|\mathbb{R}|$.

- **Upper bound.** $(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}\subseteq(0,1)$, so $|(0,1)\setminus
\mathbb{Q}|\le|(0,1)|=\mathfrak{c}$. Likewise $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}\sub
seteq\mathbb{R}$ gives $|\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}|\le\mathfrak{c}$.

- **Lower bound / exact value.** We have the disjoint union
$$(0,1)=\bigl((0,1)\cap\mathbb{Q}\bigr)\;\sqcup\;\bigl((0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}\
bigr),$$
so, writing $\kappa=|(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}|$,
$$\mathfrak{c}=|(0,1)|=\kappa+\aleph_{0}=\max(\kappa,\aleph_{0}).$$
Since $\aleph_{0}<\mathfrak{c}$, we must have $\kappa=\mathfrak{c}$. (If $\kappa
$ were countable, then $(0,1)$ would be a union of two countable sets, hence cou
ntable — contradiction.) The identical argument with $\mathbb{R}$ in place of $(
0,1)$ gives $|\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}|=\mathfrak{c}$.

Since both sets have cardinality $\mathfrak{c}$, there exists a bijection
$$\psi:(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}\;\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}\;\mathbb{R}\setminus\mat
hbb{Q}.$$

---

### Step 3. Combine

Define $f:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}$ by
$$f(x)=\begin{cases}\varphi(x), & x\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1),\\[4pt] \psi(x), & x\i
n(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}.\end{cases}$$

- **Well-defined and total:** Every $x\in(0,1)$ is either rational or irrational
, so exactly one case applies.
- **Injective:** The two pieces have disjoint domains and disjoint images ($\var
phi$ lands in $\mathbb{Q}$, $\psi$ lands in $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$), an
d each of $\varphi,\psi$ is injective.
- **Surjective:** $\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{Q}\sqcup(\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q})=\o
peratorname{im}\varphi\sqcup\operatorname{im}\psi=\operatorname{im}f$.
- **Rationality preservation:** By construction $f(\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1))=\mathbb{
Q}$ and $f((0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q})=\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$.

Hence $f$ is a bijection with the required property. $\blacksquare$

---

**Remark.** The argument uses only that (i) the rationals on both sides are coun
tably infinite, and (ii) the irrationals on both sides have cardinality $\mathfr
ak{c}$. The same reasoning shows more generally that for any two uncountable Bor
el subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ of the same cardinality, one can find a rationality-p
reserving bijection whenever the rational parts match in cardinality.
