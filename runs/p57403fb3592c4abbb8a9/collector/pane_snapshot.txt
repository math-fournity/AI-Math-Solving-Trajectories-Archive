## Analysis

The key objects:
- $X_2^c$ is the **continuous part** of the semimartingale $X_2$, hence $X_2^c$
is itself a continuous semimartingale.
- $X_1$ is a càdlàg semimartingale (the integrand), which may have jumps.
- $X_1(s-)$ is the left-limit process (predictable), while $X_1(s)$ is the value
 process (optional).

The difference between the two integrals is:
$$\int_0^t X_1(s)\,dX_2^c(s) - \int_0^t X_1(s-)\,dX_2^c(s) = \int_0^t \Delta X_1
(s)\,dX_2^c(s)$$
where $\Delta X_1(s) = X_1(s) - X_1(s-)$.

## Proof

**The statement is TRUE.**

**Step 1: The jump set of $X_1$ is countable.**

Since $X_1$ is càdlàg, its jumps $\Delta X_1(s) \neq 0$ occur on at most a count
able set $J = \{s_1, s_2, \ldots\} \subset [0,t]$. (For each $n$, there are only
 finitely many jumps with $|\Delta X_1| > 1/n$ on $[0,t]$, and the jump set is t
he countable union over $n$.)

**Step 2: Decompose the continuous integrator.**

Since $X_2^c$ is a continuous semimartingale, it decomposes as $X_2^c = M + A$,
where:
- $M$ is a **continuous** local martingale,
- $A$ is a **continuous** finite-variation process.

Both $M$ and $A$ are continuous, so the measures they induce are **atomless**.

**Step 3: The finite-variation part vanishes.**

The integral $\int_0^t \Delta X_1(s)\,dA(s)$ is a Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral. S
ince $A$ is continuous, the signed measure $dA$ has no atoms. The integrand $\De
lta X_1$ is supported on the countable set $J$. An atomless measure assigns zero
 to every singleton, hence to every countable set:
$$\int_0^t \Delta X_1(s)\,dA(s) = \sum_{s_i \in J} \Delta X_1(s_i) \cdot \Delta
A(s_i) = \sum_{s_i \in J} \Delta X_1(s_i) \cdot 0 = 0.$$

**Step 4: The martingale part vanishes.**

The stochastic integral $\int_0^t \Delta X_1(s)\,dM(s)$ is governed by the quadr
atic variation $\langle M \rangle$, which is a **continuous** increasing process
 (hence atomless). The isometry property gives:
$$\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_0^t \Delta X_1(s)\,dM(s)\right)^2\right] = \mathbb{
E}\left[\int_0^t (\Delta X_1(s))^2\,d\langle M\rangle_s\right].$$

Since $\langle M \rangle$ is continuous (atomless) and $\Delta X_1$ is supported
 on the countable set $J$, we have $d\langle M\rangle(J) = 0$, so:
$$\int_0^t (\Delta X_1(s))^2\,d\langle M\rangle_s = 0,$$
which implies $\int_0^t \Delta X_1(s)\,dM(s) = 0$ a.s. (The local case follows b
y localization.)

**Step 5: Conclusion.**

Combining Steps 3 and 4:
$$\int_0^t \Delta X_1(s)\,dX_2^c(s) = \int_0^t \Delta X_1(s)\,dM(s) + \int_0^t \
Delta X_1(s)\,dA(s) = 0 + 0 = 0.$$

Therefore:
$$\int_0^t X_1(s)\,dX_2^c(s) = \int_0^t X_1(s-)\,dX_2^c(s).$$

**The reasoning is correct:** The equality holds precisely because the integrato
r $X_2^c$ is continuous, making its associated measures ($dA$ and $d\langle M\ra
ngle$) atomless. The jumps of the integrand $X_1$ live on a countable set, and a
n atomless measure assigns zero mass to any countable set. Hence "the integratio
n on the jumps of the integrand $X_1$ is zero," as stated.

**Remark on well-definedness:** The integral $\int X_1(s-)\,dX_2^c(s)$ uses the
predictable integrand $X_1(s-)$ and is defined in the standard Itô theory. The i
ntegral $\int X_1(s)\,dX_2^c(s)$ uses the optional (not necessarily predictable)
 integrand $X_1(s)$; for a **continuous** integrator, this is well-defined (e.g.
, via Riemann sums or the optional stochastic integral), and the above argument
shows it coincides with the predictable version. This coincidence is a special p
roperty of continuous integrators — it fails for general semimartingale integrat
ors with jumps, where the distinction between $H(s-)$ and $H(s)$ matters critica
lly (e.g., in Itô's formula).
