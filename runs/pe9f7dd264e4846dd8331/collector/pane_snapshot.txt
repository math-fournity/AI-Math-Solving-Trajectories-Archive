## Solution

We prove that the supremum of $\frac{\text{area}(M)}{\text{diam}^2(M)}$ over all
 connected (indeed, all bounded measurable) sets $M \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ is $\d
frac{\pi}{4}$, and that this value is attained by a disk.

### The Isodiametric Inequality

**Claim.** For every bounded measurable set $M \subset \mathbb{R}^2$,
$$\operatorname{area}(M) \le \frac{\pi}{4}\,\operatorname{diam}(M)^2.$$

**Proof via Steiner symmetrization.**

**Definition (Steiner symmetrization).** Let $\ell$ be a line in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
 For a bounded measurable set $M$, the Steiner symmetrization $S_\ell(M)$ is obt
ained as follows: for each line $\ell_\perp$ perpendicular to $\ell$, the slice
$M \cap \ell_\perp$ is a (possibly empty) measurable subset of $\ell_\perp$. Rep
lace it by the closed interval centered on $\ell$ having the same 1-dimensional
measure. The union of these intervals is $S_\ell(M)$.

**Key properties of Steiner symmetrization:**

1. **Area is preserved:** $\operatorname{area}(S_\ell(M)) = \operatorname{area}(
M)$. This is immediate from Fubini's theorem: each perpendicular slice has the s
ame length before and after, so the 2-dimensional measure is unchanged.

2. **Diameter does not increase:** $\operatorname{diam}(S_\ell(M)) \le \operator
name{diam}(M)$.

   *Proof of (2):* Let $d = \operatorname{diam}(M)$. Take two points $p, q \in S
_\ell(M)$. They lie on (possibly different) perpendicular slices $\ell_\perp^a$
and $\ell_\perp^b$ at signed distances $a, b$ from $\ell$ along $\ell$'s directi
on. The slice at position $a$ has length $L_a = |M \cap \ell_\perp^a|$ (centered
 on $\ell$), and similarly $L_b$. So $p$ is at distance $\le L_a/2$ from $\ell$
(perpendicular distance), and $q$ is at distance $\le L_b/2$ from $\ell$.

   The horizontal separation is $|a - b|$ and the vertical separation is at most
 $L_a/2 + L_b/2$. Thus
   $$|p - q|^2 \le (a-b)^2 + \left(\tfrac{L_a}{2} + \tfrac{L_b}{2}\right)^2.$$

   Now, in the original set $M$, there exist points at horizontal position $a$ w
ith vertical coordinate up to $L_a/2$ from $\ell$ (by definition of $L_a$), and
similarly for position $b$. The diameter constraint gives: any point at position
 $a$ and any point at position $b$ are within distance $d$. In particular, the "
topmost" point at position $a$ and the "bottommost" point at position $b$ satisf
y:
   $$(a-b)^2 + \left(\tfrac{L_a}{2} + \tfrac{L_b}{2}\right)^2 \le d^2.$$
   (Here we use that the original set, having diameter $d$, cannot have points a
t position $a$ and position $b$ whose vertical coordinates differ by more than $
\sqrt{d^2 - (a-b)^2}$; the maximum possible sum of half-lengths is bounded by th
is.) Therefore $|p-q| \le d$, proving $\operatorname{diam}(S_\ell(M)) \le d$.

3. **Convexity and symmetry:** $S_\ell(M)$ is symmetric about $\ell$.

**Iterated symmetrization converges to a disk.** Let $d = \operatorname{diam}(M)
$. Choose a sequence of directions $\theta_1, \theta_2, \ldots$ dense in $[0, \p
i)$, and set $M_0 = M$, $M_n = S_{\ell_{\theta_n}}(M_{n-1})$. By properties (1)
and (2):
- $\operatorname{area}(M_n) = \operatorname{area}(M)$ for all $n$.
- $\operatorname{diam}(M_n) \le d$ for all $n$.

A classical result (see, e.g., the Blaschke selection theorem applied to the fam
ily $\{M_n\}$, which is uniformly bounded since $\operatorname{diam} \le d$) sho
ws that a subsequence converges in the Hausdorff metric to a compact convex set
$K$ that is symmetric about every direction $\theta$ in the dense set—hence symm
etric about *all* directions. A compact convex set symmetric about all direction
s through its center is a **disk**.

Since area is continuous under Hausdorff convergence of convex bodies,
$$\operatorname{area}(K) = \operatorname{area}(M), \qquad \operatorname{diam}(K)
 \le d.$$

A disk with diameter $\le d$ has area $\le \pi(d/2)^2 = \frac{\pi}{4}d^2$. There
fore:
$$\operatorname{area}(M) = \operatorname{area}(K) \le \frac{\pi}{4}d^2 = \frac{\
pi}{4}\operatorname{diam}(M)^2. \qquad \square$$

### Equality is attained by a disk

Let $M$ be the closed disk of radius $r$. Then:
- $\operatorname{area}(M) = \pi r^2$,
- $\operatorname{diam}(M) = 2r$,
- $\dfrac{\operatorname{area}(M)}{\operatorname{diam}(M)^2} = \dfrac{\pi r^2}{4r
^2} = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$.

A disk is connected (indeed convex), so this value is achieved within the class
of connected sets.

### Conclusion

The isodiametric inequality gives $\frac{\operatorname{area}(M)}{\operatorname{d
iam}(M)^2} \le \frac{\pi}{4}$ for every bounded measurable set, and the disk—a c
onnected set—attains equality. Therefore:

$$\boxed{\sup \frac{\operatorname{area}(M)}{\operatorname{diam}^2(M)} = \frac{\p
i}{4}}.$$
