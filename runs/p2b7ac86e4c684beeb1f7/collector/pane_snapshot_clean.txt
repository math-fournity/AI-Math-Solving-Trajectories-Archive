## Proof

**Claim.** For every $n \geq 1$, there exists a function $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\math
bb{R}$ that is (Fréchet) differentiable at a point $p$, yet is not continuous at
 any other point of any neighborhood of $p$. In particular $f$ is not continuous
 on any neighborhood of $p$.

**Construction.** Let $\mathbb{Q}^n = \{x\in\mathbb{R}^n : \text{every coordinat
e of }x\text{ is rational}\}$, and define
$$f(x) = \|x\|^2\,\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{Q}^n}(x) =
\begin{cases}
\|x\|^2, & x\in\mathbb{Q}^n,\\[4pt]
0, & x\notin\mathbb{Q}^n.
\end{cases}$$
We take the point $p = \mathbf{0}$.

**Differentiability at $\mathbf{0}$.** We have $f(\mathbf{0})=0$. Claim the Fréc
het derivative at $\mathbf{0}$ is the zero linear map $L=0$. Indeed,
$$\frac{|f(h)-f(\mathbf{0})-L(h)|}{\|h\|} = \frac{|f(h)|}{\|h\|} = \frac{\|h\|^2
\,\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{Q}^n}(h)}{\|h\|} \le \frac{\|h\|^2}{\|h\|} = \|h\|\xrighta
rrow[\|h\|\to 0]{}0.$$
The bound holds regardless of whether $h\in\mathbb{Q}^n$ (then the ratio equals
$\|h\|$) or $h\notin\mathbb{Q}^n$ (then the ratio equals $0$). Hence $f$ is Fréc
het differentiable at $\mathbf{0}$ with $Df(\mathbf{0})=0$. (For $n=1$ this is o
rdinary differentiability with $f'(0)=0$.)

**Discontinuity at every $x_0\neq\mathbf{0}$.** Both $\mathbb{Q}^n$ and $\mathbb
{R}^n\setminus\mathbb{Q}^n$ are dense in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

- If $x_0\in\mathbb{Q}^n$, then $f(x_0)=\|x_0\|^2>0$. Choose a sequence $(y_k)\s
ubset\mathbb{R}^n\setminus\mathbb{Q}^n$ with $y_k\to x_0$; then $f(y_k)=0$ for a
ll $k$, so $\lim f(y_k)=0\neq\|x_0\|^2=f(x_0)$. Thus $f$ is discontinuous at $x_
0$.

- If $x_0\notin\mathbb{Q}^n$, then $f(x_0)=0$. Choose a sequence $(z_k)\subset\m
athbb{Q}^n$ with $z_k\to x_0$; then $f(z_k)=\|z_k\|^2\to\|x_0\|^2>0\neq 0=f(x_0)
$. Thus $f$ is discontinuous at $x_0$.

So $f$ is discontinuous at **every** point $x_0\neq\mathbf{0}$.

**No neighborhood of continuity.** Any neighborhood $U$ of $\mathbf{0}$ contains
 some $x_0\neq\mathbf{0}$, and $f$ is discontinuous at $x_0$. Hence $f$ is not c
ontinuous on $U$. Since $U$ was arbitrary, $f$ is not continuous on any neighbor
hood of $\mathbf{0}$.

**Conclusion.** Such functions exist in every dimension $n\ge 1$, including $n>1
$. The function $f(x)=\|x\|^2\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{Q}^n}(x)$ is Fréchet differenti
able at the origin (with derivative $0$) yet is discontinuous at every other poi
nt, so it is not continuous in any neighborhood of the origin. $\blacksquare$
