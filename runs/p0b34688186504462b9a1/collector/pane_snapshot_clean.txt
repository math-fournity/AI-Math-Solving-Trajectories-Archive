## Solution

**Answer: No.** The statement is false. Below is a counterexample.

---

### Counterexample

Let $R = \mathbb{C}[[x,y]]$ (so $n=2$) and set $s = 1$. Define a chain of ideals
 $(I_k)_{k \in \mathbb{N}}$ by

$$
I_1 = (x), \qquad I_k = (x) \cap (y) \cap (x+y) \cap (x+2y) \cap \cdots \cap (x+
(k-2)y) \quad (k \ge 2).
$$

We verify all the hypotheses and then show $\bigcap_k I_k = (0)$, which is prime
 of height $0 \neq 1 = s$.

---

### Verification of the hypotheses

**(1) Each $I_k$ is radical and unmixed of height $1$.**

Each factor $(x + ay)$ ($a \in \mathbb{C}$) is a prime ideal of height $1$ in th
e regular local ring $R$ (it is generated by an irreducible element of the UFD $
\mathbb{C}[[x,y]]$). An intersection of prime ideals is radical. Since $R/I_k$ i
s reduced and $R$ is Noetherian, the associated primes of $I_k$ are exactly its
minimal primes, which are $(x), (y), (x+y), \dots, (x+(k-2)y)$—all of height $1$
. Hence $I_k$ is unmixed (no embedded primes, all minimal primes of the same hei
ght) with $\operatorname{ht}(I_k) = 1 = s$.

**(2) The chain is strictly decreasing: $I_1 \supsetneq I_2 \supsetneq I_3 \sups
etneq \cdots$.**

- $I_1 = (x) \supsetneq I_2 = (x)\cap(y) = (xy)$: clearly $xy \in (x)$, but $x \
notin (xy)$.
- For $k \ge 2$, $I_{k+1} = I_k \cap (x+(k-1)y) \subseteq I_k$. To see the conta
inment is strict, note that $xy \in I_k$ (since $xy \in (x)$ and $xy \in (y)$, h
ence in every factor). But $xy \notin (x+(k-1)y)$: if $xy \in (x+(k-1)y)$, then
evaluating at $x = -(k-1)y$ gives $-(k-1)y^2 = 0$, a contradiction. So $xy \in I
_k \setminus I_{k+1}$, giving $I_k \supsetneq I_{k+1}$.

**(3) $I_1$ is prime.** $I_1 = (x)$ is prime in the domain $\mathbb{C}[[x,y]]$,
with $\operatorname{ht}(I_1) = 1 = s$.

---

### The intersection is $(0)$

We compute

$$
\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty} I_k = (x) \cap (y) \cap (x+y) \cap (x+2y) \cap (x+3y) \ca
p \cdots = (x) \cap \Bigl(\bigcap_{a=0,1,2,\dots} (x+ay)\Bigr).
$$

**Claim.** $\bigcap_{a=0,1,2,\dots} (x+ay) = (0)$.

*Proof of claim.* Suppose $f \in \bigcap_{a \ge 0} (x+ay)$. Write $f = \sum_{i,j
 \ge 0} c_{ij}\, x^i y^j$. The condition $f \in (x+ay)$ means $f(-ay,\, y) = 0$
in $\mathbb{C}[[y]]$, i.e.,

$$
\sum_{m \ge 0} \underbrace{\Bigl(\sum_{\substack{i+j=m}} c_{ij}\,(-a)^{i}\Bigr)}
_{=:\, P_m(a)} \, y^{m} = 0 \quad \text{for all } a = 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots
$$

So for each $m$, the polynomial $P_m(a) = \sum_{i+j=m} c_{ij}(-1)^i a^i$ vanishe
s at infinitely many values of $a$ (all non-negative integers). Hence $P_m \equi
v 0$ as a polynomial, which forces every $c_{ij}$ with $i+j = m$ to be zero. Sin
ce this holds for all $m$, we get $f = 0$. $\square$

Therefore $\bigcap_k I_k = (x) \cap (0) = (0)$.

---

### Conclusion

The ideal $\mathfrak{p} = (0)$ is prime (since $\mathbb{C}[[x,y]]$ is a domain)
and has

$$
\operatorname{ht}(\mathfrak{p}) = \operatorname{ht}(0) = 0 \;\neq\; 1 = s.
$$

All hypotheses are satisfied—strictly decreasing chain of unmixed radical ideals
 of common height $s=1$, with $I_1$ prime and $\bigcap I_k = \mathfrak{p}$ prime
—yet $\operatorname{ht}(\mathfrak{p}) \neq s$.

**The answer is no:** $\operatorname{ht}(\mathfrak{p}) = s$ need not hold. $\bla
cksquare$
