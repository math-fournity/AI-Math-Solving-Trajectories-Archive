## Proof

**Claim:** For every integer $n \geq 1$, there does **not** exist a stochastic m
atrix $P \in [0,1]^{n \times n}$ such that $\ker\big((P-I)^T\big)$ contains only
 nonpositive vectors.

Here "nonpositive vector" means a vector all of whose entries are $\leq 0$.

---

**Step 1: The kernel is always nontrivial.**

Let $P$ be stochastic, so each row sums to $1$, i.e. $P\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}$
where $\mathbf{1} = (1,\dots,1)^T$. Hence $1$ is an eigenvalue of $P$. Since a m
atrix and its transpose share the same eigenvalues, $1$ is also an eigenvalue of
 $P^T = (P-I)^T + I$. Therefore $(P-I)^T$ is singular, and

$$K := \ker\big((P-I)^T\big) \neq \{0\}.$$

Concretely, $K$ is the space of (signed) stationary measures: $v \in K \iff P^T
v = v$.

**Step 2: A nontrivial subspace cannot lie inside the nonpositive orthant.**

Let $\mathcal{C} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : x_i \leq 0 \text{ for all } i\}$ be th
e nonpositive orthant. This is a cone: closed under addition and under multiplic
ation by nonnegative scalars, but **not** closed under negation.

Suppose $L$ is a linear subspace with $L \subseteq \mathcal{C}$. Take any $v \in
 L$. Since $L$ is a subspace, $-v \in L \subseteq \mathcal{C}$. Now $v \in \math
cal{C}$ gives $v_i \leq 0$ for all $i$, and $-v \in \mathcal{C}$ gives $-v_i \le
q 0$, i.e. $v_i \geq 0$ for all $i$. Together, $v_i = 0$ for all $i$, so $v = 0$
. Thus $L = \{0\}$.

**Step 3: Conclusion.**

From Step 1, $K$ is a nontrivial subspace. From Step 2, any subspace contained i
n $\mathcal{C}$ must be trivial. Hence $K \not\subseteq \mathcal{C}$: there exis
ts a vector in $K$ that is **not** nonpositive.

Equivalently, pick any nonzero $v \in K$. Since $v \neq 0$, some entry $v_i \neq
 0$. If $v_i > 0$, then $v$ itself is not nonpositive. If $v_i < 0$, then $-v \i
n K$ (subspace) and $(-v)_i > 0$, so $-v$ is not nonpositive. In either case $K$
 contains a vector failing to be nonpositive.

This argument uses only the fact that $P$ is stochastic (so that $1$ is an eigen
value) and the subspace structure of the kernel. It applies to **every** stochas
tic matrix and **every** $n \geq 1$ (including $n=1$, where $P=[1]$ and $(P-I)^T
 = [0]$, whose kernel is all of $\mathbb{R}$, plainly containing positive vector
s).

Therefore, for no $n \geq 1$ does such a stochastic matrix exist. $\blacksquare$
