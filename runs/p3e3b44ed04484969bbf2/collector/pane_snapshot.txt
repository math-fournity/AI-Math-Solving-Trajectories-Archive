## Proof

**Case 1: $(a,b) = (0,0)$.** The equation reduces to $h(x,y) = 0$ for all $(x,y)
$, so $h \equiv 0$ trivially.

**Case 2: $(a,b) \neq (0,0)$.** Consider the characteristic curves of the PDE
$$a\,\frac{\partial h}{\partial x} + b\,\frac{\partial h}{\partial y} = h.$$

Fix an arbitrary point $(x_0, y_0) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ and define
$$g(t) := h(x_0 + at,\; y_0 + bt), \qquad t \in \mathbb{R}.$$

Since $h$ has continuous partial derivatives, $g$ is differentiable, and by the
chain rule
$$g'(t) = a\,\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}(x_0+at,\,y_0+bt) + b\,\frac{\partial
h}{\partial y}(x_0+at,\,y_0+bt).$$

By the given PDE, the right-hand side equals $h(x_0+at,\,y_0+bt) = g(t)$. Hence
$g$ satisfies the ODE
$$g'(t) = g(t),$$
whose general solution is
$$g(t) = g(0)\,e^{t}.$$

Now we use the boundedness hypothesis. Since $|h(x,y)| \le M$ for all $(x,y) \in
 \mathbb{R}^2$, and since $(x_0+at,\,y_0+bt) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ for every $t \in
\mathbb{R}$, we have
$$|g(t)| = |g(0)|\,e^{t} \le M \qquad \text{for all } t \in \mathbb{R}.$$

In particular, for all $t \ge 0$:
$$|g(0)| \le M\,e^{-t}.$$

Letting $t \to +\infty$, the right-hand side tends to $0$, so
$$|g(0)| = 0, \qquad \text{i.e., } g(0) = 0.$$

Recalling the definition of $g$, this gives
$$h(x_0, y_0) = g(0) = 0.$$

Since $(x_0, y_0)$ was arbitrary, we conclude
$$h(x, y) = 0 \quad \text{for all } (x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2. \qquad \blacksquare$
$
