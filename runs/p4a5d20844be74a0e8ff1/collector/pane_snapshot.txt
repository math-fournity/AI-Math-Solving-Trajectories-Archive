Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether an unbounded gradient neces
sarily implies a non-vanishing Hessian (determinant or eigenvalue) somewhere.

**Answer: Yes.**

## Proof

We prove the contrapositive: if the Hessian determinant and all its eigenvalues
vanish everywhere (i.e., the Hessian matrix is identically zero on all of $\math
bb{R}^{n-1}$), then the gradient $G = \nabla\Phi$ must be bounded on every subse
t of $\mathbb{R}^{n-1}$, contradicting the existence of $S_1$.

**Assumption for contrapositive.** Suppose the Hessian matrix
$$\mathcal{H}(x) = \left(\frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}(x)\ri
ght)$$
vanishes identically on $\mathbb{R}^{n-1}$, meaning every eigenvalue is zero and
 $\det \mathcal{H}(x) = 0$ for all $x$.

Since the Hessian is a real symmetric matrix, it is diagonalizable. All eigenval
ues being zero at every point $x$ is equivalent to $\mathcal{H}(x) = 0$ (the zer
o matrix) for every $x$. This in turn is equivalent to
$$\frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}(x) = 0 \quad \text{for all }
 i, j \text{ and all } x \in \mathbb{R}^{n-1}.$$

**Consequence: each gradient component is constant.** The $i$-th component of th
e gradient is $G_i = \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial x_i}$. The condition above gi
ves
$$\frac{\partial G_i}{\partial x_j} = \frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial x_j \parti
al x_i} = 0 \quad \text{for all } j \text{ and all } x.$$

Since all partial derivatives of $G_i$ vanish everywhere on $\mathbb{R}^{n-1}$ (
which is connected), each $G_i$ is constant on $\mathbb{R}^{n-1}$. Therefore $G
= (G_1, \ldots, G_{n-1})$ is a constant vector-valued function.

**Contradiction.** A constant function is bounded on every subset of $\mathbb{R}
^{n-1}$. In particular, $G$ is bounded on $S_1$, contradicting the hypothesis th
at $G$ is unbounded on $S_1$.

**Conclusion.** The Hessian cannot be identically zero. Therefore there exists a
t least one point $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{n-1}$ at which the Hessian matrix $\mathc
al{H}(x_0) \neq 0$. Since $\mathcal{H}(x_0)$ is a nonzero real symmetric matrix,
 at least one of its eigenvalues $\lambda_k(x_0) \neq 0$. By continuity of the e
igenvalues (as functions of the matrix entries, which are continuous since $\Phi
$ is $C^2$), there is an open neighborhood $U$ of $x_0$ on which $\lambda_k$ rem
ains nonzero. Taking $S_2 = U$ (or even $S_2 = \{x_0\}$), we obtain a set on whi
ch at least one eigenvalue of the Hessian is non-vanishing. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The key structural facts used are: (1) the Hessian is the Jacobian o
f the gradient, so a vanishing Hessian forces the gradient to be constant; (2) $
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}$ is connected, so vanishing partial derivatives imply constancy
; (3) a real symmetric matrix with all zero eigenvalues is the zero matrix. The
argument shows something slightly stronger: unboundedness of the gradient on *an
y* set forces the Hessian to be nonzero at *some* point (not necessarily on $S_1
$ itself).
