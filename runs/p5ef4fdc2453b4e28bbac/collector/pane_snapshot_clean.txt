ld $K$ can be contained in a division ring $D$ with $K \subseteq Z(D)$.

## Proof

**Yes, it is possible.** We provide two examples — one commutative and one non-c
ommutative — and then prove a structural observation that clarifies exactly how
such extensions can occur.

### Example 1 (Commutative division ring)

Let $K$ be any algebraically closed field (e.g., $K = \mathbb{C}$). Set $D = K(t
)$, the field of rational functions in one indeterminate $t$ over $K$. Then:

- $D$ is a field, hence a division ring.
- Since $D$ is commutative, $Z(D) = D = K(t)$.
- $K \subsetneq K(t) = Z(D)$, so $K \subseteq Z(D)$.

Thus $K$ is an algebraically closed field contained in a division ring $D$ with
$K \subseteq Z(D)$, and in fact $K \subsetneq Z(D)$.

### Example 2 (Non-commutative division ring)

Let $K$ be any algebraically closed field. Consider the free algebra $K\langle x
, y\rangle$ on two non-commuting indeterminates. By the construction of the **fr
ee field** (universal field of fractions of $K\langle x, y\rangle$, due to Cohn)
, there exists a division ring $D$ that is the universal field of fractions of $
K\langle x, y\rangle$. This division ring $D$ is non-commutative, since $x$ and
$y$ do not commute in $D$.

A standard result on free fields states that the center of the free field on $\g
eq 2$ generators over $K$ is $K$ itself (see Cohn, *Free Ideal Rings and Localiz
ation in General Rings*). Therefore:

$$Z(D) = K, \quad \text{so } K = Z(D) \subseteq Z(D).$$

Hence $K \subseteq Z(D)$ holds, and $D$ is a non-commutative division ring stric
tly containing $K$.

### Structural observation

We show that the only way $D$ can extend beyond $K$ is by admitting elements tra
nscendental over $K$.

**Claim.** If $K$ is algebraically closed, $K \subseteq Z(D)$, and $d \in D$ is
algebraic over $K$, then $d \in K$.

*Proof of claim.* Since $K \subseteq Z(D)$, the element $d$ commutes with every
element of $K$. Therefore the subring $K[d] \subseteq D$ is commutative. If $d$
is algebraic over $K$, then $K[d]$ is a finite-dimensional commutative $K$-algeb
ra. Being a subring of the division ring $D$, it is an integral domain. A finite
-dimensional integral domain over a field is a field, so $K[d]$ is a field that
is a finite algebraic extension of $K$. Since $K$ is algebraically closed, the o
nly such extension is $K$ itself. Thus $K[d] = K$, giving $d \in K$. $\square$

**Corollary.** Every element of $D \setminus K$ is transcendental over $K$. In p
articular, if every element of $D$ is algebraic over $K$ (e.g., if $D$ is finite
-dimensional over $K$ and $K = Z(D)$, making $D$ a central division algebra over
 $K$), then $D = K$. This recovers the classical fact that the Brauer group of a
n algebraically closed field is trivial: $\operatorname{Br}(K) = 0$, so the only
 finite-dimensional central division algebra over an algebraically closed field
$K$ is $K$ itself.

However, **without** the finite-dimensionality (or algebraicity) assumption, non
-trivial extensions exist — as demonstrated by both examples above, where the el
ements $t$ (Example 1) and $x, y$ (Example 2) are transcendental over $K$.

### Conclusion

It is indeed possible for an algebraically closed field $K$ to be contained in a
 division ring $D$ with $K \subseteq Z(D)$. This holds for **every** algebraical
ly closed field $K$: the rational function field $K(t)$ provides a commutative e
xample with $K \subsetneq Z(D)$, and the free field of $K\langle x, y\rangle$ pr
ovides a non-commutative example with $K = Z(D)$. The algebraic closure of $K$ f
orces every element of $D \setminus K$ to be transcendental over $K$, but places
 no obstruction on the existence of such transcendental elements.
