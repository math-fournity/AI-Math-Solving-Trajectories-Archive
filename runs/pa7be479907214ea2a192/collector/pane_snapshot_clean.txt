**Answer: Yes.** The resolvent set of an operator with compact resolvent on a Hi
lbert space is dense in $\mathbb{C}$.

---

**Proof.**

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space and $A$ a (closed, densely defined) operator on $H$ w
ith compact resolvent. By definition there exists $\lambda_0 \in \rho(A)$ such t
hat $R(\lambda_0) := (A - \lambda_0 I)^{-1}$ is a compact operator. We must show
 $\rho(A)$ is dense in $\mathbb{C}$, i.e. that $\sigma(A) = \mathbb{C} \setminus
 \rho(A)$ has empty interior.

**Step 1. Compactness propagates to the entire resolvent.**

For any $\lambda \in \rho(A)$, the resolvent identity gives
$$
R(\lambda) = R(\lambda_0) + (\lambda - \lambda_0)\, R(\lambda)\, R(\lambda_0).
$$
Since $R(\lambda_0)$ is compact and the compact operators form a two-sided ideal
 in $\mathcal{B}(H)$, the right-hand side is compact. Hence $R(\lambda)$ is comp
act for **every** $\lambda \in \rho(A)$.

**Step 2. Reduction to the spectral theory of a single compact operator.**

Fix any $\lambda \in \rho(A)$ and set $K := R(\lambda) = (A - \lambda I)^{-1}$,
which is compact by Step 1. The resolvent satisfies
$$
(A - \mu I)\,R(\lambda) = I + (\lambda - \mu)\,R(\lambda) \quad\text{for all } \
mu \in \mathbb{C}.
$$
From this one obtains the spectral mapping relation for the resolvent:
$$
\sigma(K) \setminus \{0\} = \left\{ \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda} : \mu \in \sigma(A)
\right\},
$$
with the convention that $\mu \mapsto (\mu - \lambda)^{-1}$ sends $\mu = \infty$
 to $0$. Concretely, $\mu \in \sigma(A)$ iff $(\mu - \lambda)^{-1} \in \sigma(K)
$, and $\mu = \infty$ corresponds to $0 \in \sigma(K)$ (which holds whenever $A$
 is unbounded, in particular when $H$ is infinite-dimensional and $K$ is compact
 and injective with non-closed range).

**Step 3. Apply the Riesz–Schauder theorem.**

Since $K$ is compact, the Riesz–Schauder theorem states:
- $\sigma(K)$ is countable;
- the only possible accumulation point of $\sigma(K)$ is $0$;
- every nonzero $\zeta \in \sigma(K)$ is an isolated eigenvalue of finite (algeb
raic) multiplicity.

Translating back via $\mu = \lambda + 1/\zeta$ (for $\zeta \neq 0$), we conclude
:
- $\sigma(A)$ is countable;
- the only possible accumulation point of $\sigma(A)$ is $\infty$;
- every $\mu \in \sigma(A)$ is an isolated eigenvalue of finite multiplicity.

**Step 4. $\sigma(A)$ has empty interior.**

Since the only accumulation point of $\sigma(A)$ is $\infty$, for every disk $\o
verline{B(0,r)} \subset \mathbb{C}$ the intersection $\sigma(A) \cap \overline{B
(0,r)}$ must be **finite**: otherwise, by the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, there
 would be a finite accumulation point, contradicting Step 3.

Being the spectrum of a closed operator, $\sigma(A)$ is closed in $\mathbb{C}$.
Being discrete (every point isolated) with no finite accumulation, it is a close
d discrete subset of $\mathbb{C}$. Such a set has empty interior: no point of a
discrete set is an interior point, and a closed set whose points are all non-int
erior is nowhere dense.

**Step 5. Conclusion.**

Since $\sigma(A)$ is closed with empty interior, its complement
$$
\rho(A) = \mathbb{C} \setminus \sigma(A)
$$
is open and dense in $\mathbb{C}$.

(The finite-dimensional case is immediate: $A$ is a matrix, $\sigma(A)$ is a fin
ite set, and $\rho(A) = \mathbb{C} \setminus \sigma(A)$ is plainly dense.) $\bla
cksquare$
