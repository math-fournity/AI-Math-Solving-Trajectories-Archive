## Analysis

### Step 1: Identifying a Contradiction in the Premise

The problem states that both distributions have **the same expectation $\mu$** a
nd **the same second moment**, then asks us to assume **the variance of $\Phi_2$
 is higher than that of $\Phi_1$**.

Under the standard definition, the second moment is $E[X^2]$ and:
$$\text{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$$

If both distributions share the same $E[X] = \mu$ and the same $E[X^2]$, then:
$$\text{Var}(\Phi_1) = E[X_1^2] - \mu^2 = E[X_2^2] - \mu^2 = \text{Var}(\Phi_2)$
$

The variances **must be equal**, contradicting the hypothesis that $\text{Var}(\
Phi_2) > \text{Var}(\Phi_1)$. Under this literal reading, the premise is impossi
ble, so the implication "$G' - G \geq 0$" holds **vacuously**.

### Step 2: Investigating Under the Natural (Non-Contradictory) Interpretation

The phrase "the same second moment" is likely a mistranslation (possibly from Fr
ench, where "un moment d'ordre 2" = "a second moment" vs. "le même moment d'ordr
e 2" = "the same second moment"). Under the interpretation that both distributio
ns simply **have** finite second moments, with the same mean $\mu$ and $\text{Va
r}(\Phi_2) > \text{Var}(\Phi_1)$, I will show the inequality **does not hold in
general** by providing an explicit counterexample.

### Step 3: Reformulating $G' - G$

Let $\bar{\Phi}_i(x) = 1 - \Phi_i(x)$ be the survival functions. Using the ident
ity $\min(a,b) = \frac{a+b-|a-b|}{2}$ and the fact that $\int_L^\infty \bar{F}(x
)^2\,dx = E[\min(X,X')]$ for iid $X,X' \sim F$, one derives:

$$G = \frac{E[|X_1 - X_1'|]}{2\mu}, \qquad G' = \frac{E[|X_1-X_1'|] + 2E[|X_1-X_
2|] + E[|X_2-X_2'|]}{8\mu}$$

where $X_1, X_1'$ are iid from $\Phi_1$, $X_2, X_2'$ are iid from $\Phi_2$, and
$X_1, X_2$ are independent. Therefore:

$$\boxed{G' - G = \frac{2\,E[|X_1-X_2|] + E[|X_2-X_2'|] - 3\,E[|X_1-X_1'|]}{8\mu
}}$$

### Step 4: Counterexample

Set $L = 0$, $\mu = 1$.

**Distribution $\Phi_1$:** Two-point distribution.
$$P(X_1 = 0) = 0.95, \quad P(X_1 = 20) = 0.05$$
- Mean: $0.05 \times 20 = 1$ ✓
- $E[X_1^2] = 0.05 \times 400 = 20$, so $\text{Var}(\Phi_1) = 20 - 1 = 19$
- $E[|X_1 - X_1'|] = 2 \times 0.95 \times 0.05 \times 20 = 1.9$

**Distribution $\Phi_2$:** Three-point distribution.
$$P(X_2 = 0) = 0.5, \quad P(X_2 = 1.5) = 0.499, \quad P(X_2 = 251.5) = 0.001$$
- Mean: $0.499 \times 1.5 + 0.001 \times 251.5 = 0.7485 + 0.2515 = 1$ ✓
- $E[X_2^2] = 0.499 \times 2.25 + 0.001 \times 63252.25 = 1.12275 + 63.25225 = 6
4.375$
- $\text{Var}(\Phi_2) = 64.375 - 1 = 63.375 > 19 = \text{Var}(\Phi_1)$ ✓

**Computing $E[|X_2 - X_2'|]$** (summing over unordered distinct pairs):
| Pair | Probability | $|x_i - x_j|$ | Contribution |
|------|------------|---------------|-------------|
| $(0, 1.5)$ | $2(0.5)(0.499) = 0.499$ | $1.5$ | $0.7485$ |
| $(0, 251.5)$ | $2(0.5)(0.001) = 0.001$ | $251.5$ | $0.2515$ |
| $(1.5, 251.5)$ | $2(0.499)(0.001) = 0.000998$ | $250$ | $0.2495$ |

$$E[|X_2 - X_2'|] = 0.7485 + 0.2515 + 0.2495 = 1.2495$$

**Computing $E[|X_1 - X_2|]$** (independent $X_1, X_2$):
| $X_1$ | $X_2$ | Probability | $\|x_1 - x_2\|$ | Contribution |
|-------|-------|------------|-----------------|-------------|
| $0$ | $0$ | $0.95 \times 0.5$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| $0$ | $1.5$ | $0.95 \times 0.499$ | $1.5$ | $0.711075$ |
| $0$ | $251.5$ | $0.95 \times 0.001$ | $251.5$ | $0.238925$ |
| $20$ | $0$ | $0.05 \times 0.5$ | $20$ | $0.5$ |
| $20$ | $1.5$ | $0.05 \times 0.499$ | $18.5$ | $0.461575$ |
| $20$ | $251.5$ | $0.05 \times 0.001$ | $231.5$ | $0.011575$ |

$$E[|X_1 - X_2|] = 0.711075 + 0.238925 + 0.5 + 0.461575 + 0.011575 = 1.92315$$

### Step 5: Evaluating $G' - G$

$$G' - G = \frac{2(1.92315) + 1.2495 - 3(1.9)}{8 \times 1} = \frac{3.8463 + 1.24
95 - 5.7}{8} = \frac{-0.6042}{8} = -0.075525$$

### Step 6: Direct Verification via Integrals

**$G$ (direct):** $\bar{\Phi}_1(x) = 0.05$ for $0 \leq x < 20$, else $0$.
$$\int_0^\infty \bar{\Phi}_1^2\,dx = 20 \times 0.0025 = 0.05 \implies G = 1 - 0.
05 = 0.95$$

**$G'$ (direct):** $\frac{\bar{\Phi}_1 + \bar{\Phi}_2}{2}$ takes values:
- $[0, 1.5)$: $\frac{0.05+0.5}{2} = 0.275$
- $[1.5, 20)$: $\frac{0.05+0.001}{2} = 0.0255$
- $[20, 251.5)$: $\frac{0+0.001}{2} = 0.0005$
- $[251.5, \infty)$: $0$

$$\int_0^\infty \left(\tfrac{\bar{\Phi}_1+\bar{\Phi}_2}{2}\right)^2 dx = 1.5(0.2
75)^2 + 18.5(0.0255)^2 + 231.5(0.0005)^2$$
$$= 0.113438 + 0.012030 + 0.000058 = 0.125525$$
$$G' = 1 - 0.125525 = 0.874475$$

$$G' - G = 0.874475 - 0.95 = -0.075525 < 0 \quad \checkmark$$

Both methods agree.

### Conclusion

**Under the literal reading** of the problem (same expectation **and** same seco
nd moment $E[X^2]$), the variances are necessarily equal, making the hypothesis
"$\text{Var}(\Phi_2) > \text{Var}(\Phi_1)$" impossible. The inequality $G' - G \
geq 0$ therefore holds **vacuously**.

**Under the natural non-contradictory reading** (same mean $\mu$, $\Phi_2$ has h
igher variance, both have finite second moments), the inequality **does not hold
 in general**. The counterexample above—two distributions on $[0,\infty)$ with t
he same mean $\mu=1$, where $\text{Var}(\Phi_2) = 63.375 > 19 = \text{Var}(\Phi_
1)$, yet $G' - G = -0.075525 < 0$—demonstrates this conclusively.

The intuition is that $G$ is a Gini-type index measuring *relative* inequality,
which is not monotonically related to variance. Distribution $\Phi_1$ concentrat
es nearly all its mass at $0$ with a small mass at $20$, yielding high Gini ($0.
95$). Distribution $\Phi_2$ spreads mass more evenly among $\{0, 1.5, 251.5\}$,
yielding lower Gini ($0.625$) despite having much higher variance (driven by the
 rare but extreme value $251.5$). Averaging the two CDFs pulls $G'$ below $G$.
