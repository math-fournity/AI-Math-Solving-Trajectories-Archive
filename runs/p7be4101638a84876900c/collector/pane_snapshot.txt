Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether $x = 0$ is the only bounded
 solution to $\dot{x} = A(t)x$ under the given hypotheses.

**Answer: The statement is TRUE. $x = 0$ is the only bounded solution.**

**Proof.**

Let $x(t)$ be a solution that is bounded on all of $\mathbb{R}$. Define the ener
gy (Lyapunov function):
$$V(t) = \|x(t)\|^2 = x(t)^T x(t).$$

Differentiating:
$$\dot{V}(t) = 2\,x(t)^T \dot{x}(t) = 2\,x(t)^T A(t)\, x(t).$$

**Key observation.** For any (possibly non-symmetric) matrix $A$, the quadratic
form satisfies $x^T A\, x = x^T S\, x$ where $S = \frac{A + A^T}{2}$ is the symm
etric part of $A$. Thus:
$$\dot{V}(t) = 2\,x(t)^T S(t)\, x(t), \qquad S(t) := \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(A(t) + A(
t)^T\bigr).$$

Since $A(t)$ is positive definite for $|t| \geq m$ (meaning $x^T A(t)\, x > 0$ f
or all $x \neq 0$), the symmetric part $S(t)$ is a symmetric positive definite m
atrix for $|t| \geq m$.

**Using the limit as $t \to +\infty$.** Since $A(t) \to A^+$ as $t \to +\infty$
and $A^+$ is positive definite, we have $S(t) \to S^+ := \frac{A^+ + (A^+)^T}{2}
$, which is symmetric positive definite. Let
$$\lambda_+ := \lambda_{\min}(S^+) > 0.$$
By convergence, there exists $T \geq m$ such that for all $t \geq T$:
$$\lambda_{\min}(S(t)) \geq \frac{\lambda_+}{2} > 0.$$

**Exponential growth for $t \geq T$.** For $t \geq T$ and $x(t) \neq 0$:
$$\dot{V}(t) = 2\,x(t)^T S(t)\, x(t) \geq 2 \cdot \frac{\lambda_+}{2} \cdot \|x(
t)\|^2 = \lambda_+\, V(t).$$

By Grönwall's inequality (or direct integration of $\dot{V} \geq \lambda_+ V$):
$$V(t) \geq V(T)\, e^{\lambda_+(t - T)} \qquad \text{for all } t \geq T.$$

**Contradiction with boundedness.** If $V(T) > 0$ (i.e., $x(T) \neq 0$), then $V
(t) \to \infty$ as $t \to +\infty$, so $\|x(t)\| \to \infty$, contradicting the
assumption that $x(t)$ is bounded.

Therefore $V(T) = 0$, i.e., $x(T) = 0$.

**Uniqueness forces $x \equiv 0$.** Since $A(t)$ is continuous in $t$, the ODE $
\dot{x} = A(t)x$ with initial condition $x(T) = 0$ has a unique solution, namely
 $x(t) = 0$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Remark.** The hypothesis that $A(t) \to A^+$ (positive definite) is essential:
 it guarantees that the smallest eigenvalue of $S(t)$ is bounded away from zero
for large $t$, forcing exponential growth of any nonzero solution. Without conve
rgence to a positive definite limit—for instance, if $\lambda_{\min}(S(t))$ deca
yed like $1/t^2$—nonzero bounded solutions could exist. The behavior as $t \to -
\infty$ is not needed for this argument; a symmetric argument using $A^-$ would
show that nonzero solutions also blow up backward in time.

$\blacksquare$
