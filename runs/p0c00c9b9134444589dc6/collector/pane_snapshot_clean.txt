## Solution

We find the residue of $f(z) = \dfrac{1}{e^{e^{1/z}}} = e^{-e^{1/z}}$ at $z=0$,
which is an essential singularity.

**Strategy.** The residue at $z=0$ is the coefficient of $z^{-1}$ in the Laurent
 expansion of $f(z)$ about $z=0$. We expand $f$ as a double series and extract t
his coefficient.

**Expansion.** Using the Taylor series of the exponential,

$$e^{-e^{1/z}} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!}\left(e^{1/z}\right)^k = \
sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!}\, e^{k/z}.$$

For each $k \geq 0$,

$$e^{k/z} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{k^n}{n!}\, z^{-n}.$$

Therefore

$$e^{-e^{1/z}} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac
{k^n}{n!}\, z^{-n}.$$

**Extracting the coefficient of $z^{-1}$.** Setting $n=1$, the coefficient of $z
^{-1}$ is

$$\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!} \cdot \frac{k}{1!} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}
 \frac{(-1)^k \, k}{k!} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{(k-1)!}.$$

Substituting $j = k-1$:

$$\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j!} = -\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{j
}}{j!} = -e^{-1}.$$

**Justification of termwise extraction.** The series $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{
(-1)^k}{k!} e^{k/z}$ converges absolutely and uniformly on compact subsets of $\
mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$ (since $|e^{k/z}|$ is bounded on any compact set avoidi
ng $0$, and $\sum 1/k!$ converges). Hence the Laurent series is obtained by legi
timate term-by-term expansion, and coefficient extraction is valid.

**Conclusion.** The residue of $\dfrac{1}{e^{e^{1/z}}}$ at $z=0$ is

$$\boxed{-\dfrac{1}{e}}.$$
