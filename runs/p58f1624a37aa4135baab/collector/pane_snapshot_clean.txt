## Answer

**No.** From $(1-a)A = A$ we can indeed conclude that there exists $x \in A$ wit
h $(1-a)x = 1$ (since $1 \in A = (1-a)A$). However, this alone does **not** prov
e that $1-a$ is invertible (a unit), because a *right* inverse need not be a *tw
o-sided* inverse in a general (possibly non-commutative) ring.

### Detailed justification

**Step 1 — What $(1-a)A = A$ gives.** Since $1 \in A$ and $(1-a)A = A$, the elem
ent $1$ lies in the right ideal $(1-a)A$, so there exists $x \in A$ with
$$(1-a)x = 1.$$
This is a *right inverse* of $1-a$.

**Step 2 — Why a right inverse is insufficient.** In a unital ring, an element w
ith a right inverse need not be a unit. A unit requires a *two-sided* inverse: a
n element $y$ with both $y(1-a) = 1$ and $(1-a)y = 1$. The existence of a right
inverse $x$ with $(1-a)x=1$ does not, by itself, guarantee $x(1-a) = 1$.

**Step 3 — What the complete proof requires.** To conclude $1-a$ is a unit, one
must *also* produce a left inverse. The standard argument proceeds as follows.

First, one shows $1-a$ has a **left** inverse by the maximal-ideal argument: if
$1-a$ had no left inverse, then $A(1-a) \neq A$, so $A(1-a)$ is contained in a m
aximal left ideal $M$. Since $a \in J(A) \subseteq M$, we would have $1 = (1-a)
+ a \in M$, a contradiction. Hence there exists $y$ with
$$y(1-a) = 1, \quad \text{i.e., } y = 1 + ya.$$

Now we promote this one-sided inverse to a two-sided one. Since $J(A)$ is a **tw
o-sided** ideal and $a \in J(A)$, we have $ya \in J(A)$, so $-ya \in J(A)$. Thus
 $y = 1 - (-ya)$ is of the form $1 - b$ with $b \in J(A)$. Repeating the maximal
-ideal argument, $y$ has a left inverse: there exists $z$ with $zy = 1$. Then
$$z = z \cdot 1 = z \cdot y(1-a) = (zy)(1-a) = 1 \cdot (1-a) = 1 - a.$$
So $z = 1-a$, and $zy = (1-a)y = 1$. Combined with $y(1-a) = 1$, we obtain
$$y(1-a) = (1-a)y = 1,$$
proving $1-a$ is a unit with inverse $y$.

### Conclusion

The equality $(1-a)A = A$ yields only a right inverse $(1-a)x = 1$, which is **n
ot sufficient** to conclude that $1-a$ is invertible. The full proof requires *b
oth* a left and a right inverse (or equivalently, both $(1-a)A = A$ and $A(1-a)
= A$), together with the crucial fact that $J(A)$ is a **two-sided** ideal — so
that $ya \in J(A)$ — to upgrade the one-sided inverse to a genuine two-sided inv
erse. The single step from $(1-a)A = A$ to $(1-a)x = 1$ is valid but incomplete;
 it does not, by itself, prove invertibility.
