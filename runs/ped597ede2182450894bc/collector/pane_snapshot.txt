## Proof

**Interpretation.** The problem describes a triangular support with legs $a, b$
and hypotenuse $h$, then imposes the governing equation (Condition 1):
$$h^2 = \frac{a^2+b^2}{2}, \quad\text{i.e.,}\quad a^2+b^2 = 2h^2.$$
We take Condition 1 as the defining relation (the safety protocol overrides the
standard Pythagorean identity), and seek positive integers $a,b,h$ with $h\le 50
$, $\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,h)=\gcd(h,a)=1$.

---

**Step 1: Reduction to primitive Pythagorean triples.**

From $a^2+b^2=2h^2$, parity analysis: $a,b$ cannot have opposite parity (LHS odd
, RHS even). If both even, $\gcd(a,b)\ge2$, forbidden. Hence **$a,b$ both odd**
and $h$ odd.

Set $m=\frac{a+b}{2}$, $n=\frac{|a-b|}{2}$ (both positive integers since $a,b$ o
dd, $a\ne b$). Then
$$m^2+n^2=\frac{(a+b)^2+(a-b)^2}{4}=\frac{a^2+b^2}{2}=h^2.$$
So $(m,n,h)$ is a Pythagorean triple. Since $\gcd(a,b)=1$ and $a,b$ odd, any com
mon divisor of $m,n,h$ divides $m+n=a$ and $m-n=b$, hence divides $\gcd(a,b)=1$.
 Thus **$(m,n,h)$ is a primitive Pythagorean triple**, and the map $(a,b,h)\left
rightarrow(m,n,h)$ is a bijection between solutions and primitive Pythagorean tr
iples with $m,n>0$.

---

**Step 2: The gcd conditions are automatic.**

For a primitive triple $(m,n,h)$ (so $\gcd(m,n)=1$, $m\not\equiv n\pmod 2$, $h$
odd), let $a=m+n$, $b=|m-n|$.

- **$\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(m+n,m-n)$**: any common divisor divides $2m$ and $2n$, hence
 divides $2\gcd(m,n)=2$; but $m+n,m-n$ are both odd, so $\gcd=1$.
- **$\gcd(a,h)=\gcd(m+n,h)$**: $(m+n)^2=h^2+2mn\equiv 2mn\pmod h$. If prime $p\m
id h$ and $p\mid(m+n)$, then $p\mid 2mn$; but $p\mid h$ with primitive triple gi
ves $p\nmid m,n$, and $p$ odd gives $p\nmid 2$ — contradiction. So $\gcd(m+n,h)=
1$.
- **$\gcd(b,h)=\gcd(m-n,h)$**: identical argument using $(m-n)^2\equiv -2mn\pmod
 h$.

**Every primitive Pythagorean triple with $h\le 50$ yields a valid solution.**

---

**Step 3: Enumerate primitive triples with $h\le 50$.**

Primitive triples have $h=r^2+s^2$ with $\gcd(r,s)=1$, $r>s>0$, $r\not\equiv s\p
mod 2$. Checking all $h\le 50$:

| $h$ | $(r,s)$ | $(m,n)$ | $(a,b)$ |
|-----|---------|----------|---------|
| 5 | (2,1) | (3,4) | (7,1) |
| 13 | (3,2) | (5,12) | (17,7) |
| 17 | (4,1) | (15,8) | (23,7) |
| 25 | (4,3) | (7,24) | (31,17) |
| 29 | (5,2) | (21,20) | (41,1) |
| 37 | (6,1) | (35,12) | (47,23) |
| 41 | (5,4) | (9,40) | (49,31) |

No other $h\le 50$ is a hypotenuse of a primitive triple (the next is $h=53$). E
ach $h\le 50$ has a unique primitive representation (the smallest hypotenuse wit
h two representations is $65=5\cdot13$).

**Verification** of one example, $h=5$: $a=7,b=1$: $7^2+1^2=50=2\cdot25=2h^2$ ✓;
 $\gcd(7,1)=\gcd(1,5)=\gcd(7,5)=1$ ✓. All others verified analogously (and guara
nteed by Step 2).

---

**Step 4: Sum.**

$$5+13+17+25+29+37+41 = 167.$$

The sum of all distinct possible values of $h$ is $\boxed{167}$.
