The statement is **false**.

**Counterexample.** Let $X = \{0, 1\}$ equipped with the discrete topology $\tau
 = \mathcal{P}(X) = \{\emptyset, \{0\}, \{1\}, \{0,1\}\}$. The discrete topology
 is Hausdorff (indeed metrizable, e.g. via the discrete metric), so $X$ is an ar
bitrary Hausdorff space as required.

Take $x_0 = 0$. Then $X \setminus \{x_0\} = \{1\}$.

Since every subset of a discrete space is open, $\{1\}$ is open; being the compl
ement of the open set $\{0\}$, it is also closed. Hence
$$\overline{X \setminus \{x_0\}} = \overline{\{1\}} = \{1\} \neq \{0,1\} = X.$$

Therefore $X \setminus \{x_0\}$ is **not** dense in $X$.

**Why the statement fails in general.** A subset $A \subseteq X$ is dense in $X$
 iff every nonempty open set meets $A$. Taking $A = X \setminus \{x_0\}$, this i
s equivalent to: there is no nonempty open set contained in $\{x_0\}$, i.e. $\{x
_0\}$ has empty interior, i.e. $x_0$ is not an isolated point. The Hausdorff pro
perty ($T_2$) only guarantees that distinct points can be separated by disjoint
open neighborhoods; it does **not** preclude isolated points. Any Hausdorff spac
e with an isolated point (discrete spaces, the one-point compactification of a d
iscrete space, $\mathbb{R}$ with an extra isolated point adjoined, etc.) provide
s a counterexample. The discrete two-point space above is the simplest such inst
ance.

Thus the universal claim "for any arbitrary Hausdorff space $X$, $X \setminus \{
x_0\}$ is dense in $X$" is **false**.
