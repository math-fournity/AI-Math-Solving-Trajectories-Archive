## Proof

Let $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$, so for $(x,y)\neq(0,0)$ we have $f(x,y) = e^{-1/r^2}$, an
d $f(0,0)=0$.

### Continuity at $(0,0)$

As $(x,y)\to(0,0)$, we have $r\to 0^+$, hence $1/r^2 \to +\infty$, and therefore
$$e^{-1/r^2} \to 0 = f(0,0).$$
So $f$ is **continuous** at $(0,0)$.

### Partial derivatives at $(0,0)$

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(0,0) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h} = \l
im_{h\to 0}\frac{e^{-1/h^2}}{h}.$$

Setting $t = 1/h^2 \to +\infty$, the limit becomes $\lim_{t\to\infty} \sqrt{t}\,
e^{-t} = 0$, since the exponential dominates every power. Hence $\partial f/\par
tial x(0,0) = 0$. By symmetry, $\partial f/\partial y(0,0) = 0$.

### Differentiability at $(0,0)$

We must check
$$\lim_{(h,k)\to(0,0)}\frac{f(h,k) - f(0,0) - 0\cdot h - 0\cdot k}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2
}} = \lim_{(h,k)\to(0,0)}\frac{e^{-1/(h^2+k^2)}}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2}}.$$

Writing $r = \sqrt{h^2+k^2}\to 0^+$ and $t = 1/r^2\to\infty$, this equals
$$\lim_{t\to\infty} \sqrt{t}\, e^{-t} = 0.$$
Therefore $f$ is **(Fréchet) differentiable** at $(0,0)$, with total derivative
$df(0,0) = 0$.

### Smoothness (bonus)

In fact $f$ is $C^\infty$ at the origin. Every partial derivative of every order
 $n$ at $(0,0)$ exists and equals $0$, because each such derivative along a ray
produces a term of the form $P(1/r)\,e^{-1/r^2}$ for some polynomial $P$, and $\
lim_{r\to 0^+} P(1/r)\,e^{-1/r^2} = 0$ by exponential dominance. The same expone
ntial-dominance argument shows all partial derivatives are continuous at the ori
gin (they vanish there and tend to $0$ along any approach). Hence $f\in C^\infty
(\mathbb{R}^2)$.

### Conclusion

The function $f$ is **continuous** at $(0,0)$, **differentiable** at $(0,0)$ (wi
th $df(0,0)=0$), and in fact **infinitely differentiable** ($C^\infty$) there.
