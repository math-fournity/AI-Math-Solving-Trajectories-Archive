## Proof

### Part (a): YES — with 3 colors, two same-colored points at distance 1 must ex
ist.

**Assume for contradiction** that the plane is 3-colored so that no two points a
t distance 1 share a color.

**Lemma (Diamond Lemma).** *Consider a rhombus ("diamond") with all four sides e
qual to 1, one diagonal equal to 1, and the other diagonal equal to √3. Then the
 two vertices on the long diagonal (length √3) must receive the same color.*

*Proof of Lemma.* Label the vertices $A, B, C, D$ with $AC = \sqrt{3}$ (long dia
gonal), $BD = 1$ (short diagonal), and $AB = BC = CD = DA = 1$. Since every side
 has length 1, adjacent vertices must differ in color: $A \neq B$, $B \neq C$, $
C \neq D$, $D \neq A$. Since $BD = 1$, also $B \neq D$. Thus $B$ and $D$ use two
 distinct colors. Vertex $A$ is at distance 1 from both $B$ and $D$, so $A$ must
 use the third color. Likewise $C$ is at distance 1 from both $B$ and $D$, so $C
$ must also use the third color. Hence $A$ and $C$ share the same color. $\squar
e$

**Constructing the contradiction.** Place two diamonds sharing one endpoint of t
heir long diagonals, with the other endpoints at distance 1:

- **Diamond 1:** vertices $A, B, C, D$ with long diagonal $AC = \sqrt{3}$.
- **Diamond 2:** vertices $A, B', C', D'$ with long diagonal $AC' = \sqrt{3}$.
- Arrange the angle $\angle CAC' = \arccos(5/6)$ so that $CC' = 1$.

This is geometrically realizable: by the law of cosines, $CC'^2 = 3 + 3 - 2\sqrt
{3}\cdot\sqrt{3}\cdot\frac{5}{6} = 6 - 5 = 1$, so $CC' = 1$. One can verify (e.g
., by explicit coordinates) that all seven points $A, B, C, D, B', C', D'$ are d
istinct.

By the Diamond Lemma, $A$ and $C$ share the same color (from Diamond 1), and $A$
 and $C'$ share the same color (from Diamond 2). Therefore $C$ and $C'$ share th
e same color. But $CC' = 1$, contradicting the assumption that no two points at
distance 1 share a color.

**Conclusion for (a):** Yes, with 3 colors there must exist two same-colored poi
nts exactly one inch apart. $\square$

### Part (b): NO — with 9 colors, such a coloring exists.

**Explicit construction.** Tile the plane with axis-aligned squares of side leng
th $s$, where $\frac{1}{2} < s < \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ (for concreteness, take $s
= 0.6$). Color each square $(i, j)$ — occupying $[is, (i{+}1)s] \times [js, (j{+
}1)s]$ — with the color $(i \bmod 3,\; j \bmod 3)$, yielding 9 colors in a repea
ting $3 \times 3$ pattern.

**Verification that no two same-colored points are at distance 1:**

1. **Within the same square:** The maximum distance between two points in a squa
re of side $s$ is $s\sqrt{2}$. Since $s < \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, we have $s\sqrt{2
} < 1$, so no two points in the same square are at distance 1.

2. **In different same-colored squares:** Same-colored squares differ in their g
rid indices by multiples of 3 in each coordinate. The closest pair of same-color
ed squares (differing by 3 in one direction, 0 in the other) has a gap of $3s -
s = 2s$ between their nearest edges. Since $s > \frac{1}{2}$, we have $2s > 1$,
so every pair of points in different same-colored squares is at distance $> 1$.

With $s = 0.6$: $s\sqrt{2} \approx 0.849 < 1$ ✓ and $2s = 1.2 > 1$ ✓.

Since same-colored points are either at distance $< 1$ (same square) or at dista
nce $> 1$ (different squares), **no two same-colored points are at distance exac
tly 1**.

**Conclusion for (b):** No, with 9 colors it is possible to color the plane so t
hat no two same-colored points are exactly one inch apart. $\square$

---

**Summary:** (a) Yes, three colors force a monochromatic unit-distance pair. (b)
 No, nine colors suffice to avoid one.
