The answer is **true**. Every finite subgroup of odd order in $GL(2,\mathbb{R})$
 is cyclic.

## Proof

**Step 1: Reduction to $O(2)$.**

Let $G \le GL(2,\mathbb{R})$ be a finite subgroup. We claim $G$ is conjugate (in
 $GL(2,\mathbb{R})$) to a subgroup of $O(2)$.

Pick any positive-definite symmetric bilinear form on $\mathbb{R}^2$, e.g. the s
tandard dot product, whose matrix is $B_0 = I$. Define a new form by averaging o
ver $G$:
$$B \;=\; \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G} g^{\mathsf T} B_0\, g.$$
Each $g^{\mathsf T} B_0\, g$ is positive-definite, so $B$ is positive-definite,
hence invertible. By construction $B$ is $G$-invariant:
$$g^{\mathsf T} B\, g = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{h\in G}(gh)^{\mathsf T} B_0\,(gh) = B
.$$
Since $B$ is symmetric positive-definite, there exists $P\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})$ w
ith $P^{\mathsf T}P = B$ (e.g. via Cholesky or the square root). Then for every
$g\in G$:
$$g^{\mathsf T} P^{\mathsf T}P\, g = P^{\mathsf T}P \;\Longrightarrow\; (PgP^{-1
})^{\mathsf T}(PgP^{-1}) = I.$$
Thus $PGP^{-1} \le O(2)$. Conjugation preserves order and cyclicity, so it suffi
ces to classify finite subgroups of $O(2)$ of odd order.

**Step 2: Classification of finite subgroups of $O(2)$.**

Recall $O(2) = SO(2) \rtimes \{I, R\}$ where $SO(2)$ is the group of rotations a
nd $R = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1)$ is a reflection. Every element of $O(2)\setminus SO
(2)$ is a reflection (order $2$).

Let $H \le O(2)$ be finite, and set $N = H\cap SO(2)$. Then $N \trianglelefteq H
$ and $H/N \hookrightarrow O(2)/SO(2) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$. The finite subgroups
of $SO(2)\cong S^1$ are exactly the cyclic rotation groups $C_n = \langle \rho_{
2\pi/n}\rangle$ of order $n$. Hence $N \cong C_n$ for some $n$.

- If $H = N$, then $H \cong C_n$ is cyclic.
- If $[H:N]=2$, then $H$ is generated by $C_n$ and a reflection; this is the dih
edral group $D_n$ of order $2n$.

So every finite subgroup of $O(2)$ is either cyclic $C_n$ or dihedral $D_n$ of o
rder $2n$.

**Step 3: Odd order forces cyclic.**

Suppose $|H|$ is odd. The dihedral option $D_n$ has order $2n$, which is even —
impossible. Therefore $H = N \cong C_n$ is cyclic.

(Concretely: any element outside $SO(2)$ is a reflection of order $2$, so if $|H
|$ is odd, by Cauchy's theorem $H$ contains no element of order $2$, hence $H\ca
p(O(2)\setminus SO(2))=\varnothing$, i.e. $H\le SO(2)$, whose finite subgroups a
re all cyclic.)

**Conclusion.**

Every finite subgroup of $GL(2,\mathbb{R})$ of odd order is conjugate to a cycli
c subgroup of $SO(2)$, hence is itself cyclic. The statement is **true**.
