## Proof

Let $q$ be the Hölder conjugate exponent of $p$, i.e. $\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
$. Since $p\in(1,\infty)$, we have $q\in(1,\infty)$ as well.

**Step 1: Hölder's inequality.** For every $x\in\mathbb{R}$, applying Hölder's i
nequality on the interval $[x,x+1]$ with $f$ and the constant function $\mathbf{
1}$ (which belongs to $L^q([x,x+1])$ since the interval has finite measure):

$$\left|\int_x^{x+1} f(t)\,dt\right| \leq \int_x^{x+1}|f(t)|\cdot 1\,dt \leq \le
ft(\int_x^{x+1}|f(t)|^p\,dt\right)^{1/p}\left(\int_x^{x+1}1^q\,dt\right)^{1/q} =
 \left(\int_x^{x+1}|f(t)|^p\,dt\right)^{1/p}\cdot 1.$$

So it suffices to show $\int_x^{x+1}|f(t)|^p\,dt \to 0$ as $x\to\infty$.

**Step 2: Reduction to an $L^1$ statement.** Set $g = |f|^p$. Since $f\in L^p(\m
athbb{R})$, we have $g\in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, i.e. $\int_{\mathbb{R}} g < \infty$.
 We claim $\int_x^{x+1} g(t)\,dt \to 0$ as $x\to\infty$.

**Step 3: The $L^1$ tail vanishes on unit intervals.** Since $g\geq 0$ and $g\in
 L^1(\mathbb{R})$,

$$\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_n^{n+1} g(t)\,dt = \int_{\mathbb{R}} g(t)\,dt <
\infty.$$

A convergent series of non-negative terms has its summands tending to zero, so

$$\int_n^{n+1} g(t)\,dt \to 0 \quad\text{as } n\to\infty \text{ (}n\in\mathbb{Z}
\text{)}.$$

**Step 4: From integer to real $x$.** For arbitrary $x\to\infty$, write $x = n +
 r$ with $n = \lfloor x\rfloor\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $r\in[0,1)$. Then $[x,x+1]\subs
eteq [n, n+2]$, and by monotonicity of the integral (using $g\geq 0$),

$$0 \leq \int_x^{x+1} g(t)\,dt \leq \int_n^{n+2} g(t)\,dt = \int_n^{n+1} g(t)\,d
t + \int_{n+1}^{n+2} g(t)\,dt.$$

As $x\to\infty$ we have $n\to\infty$, and both terms on the right tend to $0$ by
 Step 3. Hence

$$\int_x^{x+1} g(t)\,dt \to 0 \quad\text{as } x\to\infty.$$

**Step 5: Conclusion.** Combining Steps 1 and 4,

$$\left|\int_x^{x+1} f(t)\,dt\right| \leq \left(\int_x^{x+1}|f(t)|^p\,dt\right)^
{1/p} \xrightarrow{x\to\infty} 0.$$

Therefore $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\int_x^{x+1} f(t)\,dt = 0$. $\blacksqua
