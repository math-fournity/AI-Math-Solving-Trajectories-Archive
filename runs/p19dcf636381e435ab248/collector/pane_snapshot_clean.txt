## Answer

**Yes**, such an $n$ exists. Here is the proof.

---

**Theorem.** Let $R$ be a Noetherian ring and $I$ an ideal of $R$. Then there ex
ists $n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $(\sqrt{I})^n \subseteq I$.

**Proof.**

Since $R$ is Noetherian, every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated. In particular
, the radical $\sqrt{I}$ is finitely generated. Write

$$\sqrt{I} = (a_1, a_2, \dots, a_m).$$

For each generator $a_i \in \sqrt{I}$, by the definition of the radical there ex
ists a positive integer $n_i$ such that

$$a_i^{n_i} \in I, \qquad i = 1, 2, \dots, m.$$

**Claim.** Setting $n = (n_1 + n_2 + \cdots + n_m) - m + 1$, we have $(\sqrt{I})
^n \subseteq I$.

**Proof of Claim.** The ideal $(\sqrt{I})^n$ is generated by all $n$-fold produc
ts of elements from a generating set of $\sqrt{I}$, i.e., by all elements of the
 form

$$a_{j_1}\, a_{j_2}\, \cdots\, a_{j_n}, \qquad \text{where each } j_k \in \{1, 2
, \dots, m\}.$$

Consider any such product. For each $i \in \{1, \dots, m\}$, let $k_i$ denote th
e number of occurrences of $a_i$ among the factors, so that

$$k_1 + k_2 + \cdots + k_m = n.$$

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that $k_i \leq n_i - 1$ for every $i$. T
hen

$$n = \sum_{i=1}^{m} k_i \leq \sum_{i=1}^{m}(n_i - 1) = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{m} n_i
\right) - m = n - 1,$$

which is a contradiction. Therefore there exists some index $i$ with $k_i \geq n
_i$, meaning $a_i^{n_i}$ appears as a factor in the product $a_{j_1} \cdots a_{j
_n}$. Since $a_i^{n_i} \in I$ and $I$ is an ideal, the entire product belongs to
 $I$.

Since every generator of $(\sqrt{I})^n$ lies in $I$, we conclude

$$(\sqrt{I})^n \subseteq I. \qquad \blacksquare$$

---

**Remark.** The Noetherian hypothesis is essential: it guarantees that $\sqrt{I}
$ is finitely generated, which is what makes the finite pigeonhole-type argument
 above work. Without finite generation of $\sqrt{I}$, one cannot bound the expon
ent uniformly.
