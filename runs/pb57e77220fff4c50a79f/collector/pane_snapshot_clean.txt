## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. Every nonconstant polynomial $f(x) \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ can be wri
tten as $f(x) = (x - a)\,g(x, a)$ where $g(x,a) \in \mathbb{Q}[x, a]$, for any r
oot $a$ of $f$. This holds regardless of whether $f$ is solvable by radicals.

**Key identity.** For every $n \geq 1$,
$$x^n - a^n = (x - a)\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} a^{j}\, x^{n-1-j},$$
which is a formal identity in $\mathbb{Q}[x, a]$ (verified by expanding the righ
t-hand side: all intermediate terms telescope).

**Construction of $g$.** Let $f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} c_k x^k$ with $c_k \in \math
bb{Q}$. Rewrite each summand using $x^k = (x^k - a^k) + a^k$:

$$f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} c_k\,(x^k - a^k) \;+\; \sum_{k=0}^{n} c_k\, a^k.$$

The second sum is exactly $f(a)$. Applying the key identity to each term of the
first sum (the $k=0$ term vanishes since $x^0 - a^0 = 0$):

$$f(x) = (x - a)\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} a^{j}\, x^{k-1-j
}}_{g(x,\,a)} \;+\; f(a).$$

Since each $c_k \in \mathbb{Q}$, the expression
$$g(x, a) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} a^{j}\, x^{k-1-j}$$
is a polynomial in the two variables $x$ and $a$ with **rational coefficients**,
 i.e., $g(x, a) \in \mathbb{Q}[x, a]$.

**Specializing to a root.** If $a$ is any root of $f$ (so $f(a) = 0$), the remai
nder term vanishes and we obtain
$$f(x) = (x - a)\, g(x, a), \qquad g(x, a) \in \mathbb{Q}[x, a].$$

**Why solvability by radicals is irrelevant.** The entire argument is a *formal*
 algebraic identity in the polynomial ring $\mathbb{Q}[x, a]$. It never requires
 $a$ to be expressed by radicals, never requires $a$ to lie in any particular ex
tension of $\mathbb{Q}$, and never invokes the Galois group of $f$. The identity
 $f(x) = (x-a)\,g(x,a) + f(a)$ holds as an equality of polynomials in two indepe
ndent indeterminates $x$ and $a$; specializing $a$ to any root of $f$ (in any fi
eld extension whatsoever) simply forces the remainder $f(a)$ to zero.

**Concrete illustration.** Take $f(x) = x^5 - x - 1$, whose Galois group is $S_5
$ and which is therefore **not solvable by radicals**. The construction gives
$$g(x, a) = x^4 + a\,x^3 + a^2 x^2 + a^3 x + (a^4 - 1),$$
so $f(x) = (x - a)\,g(x, a)$ for any root $a$, with $g \in \mathbb{Q}[x, a]$, ev
en though $a$ cannot be written using radicals.

**Conclusion.** The factorization $f(x) = (x - a)\,g(x, a)$ with $g(x,a) \in \ma
thbb{Q}[x, a]$ exists for every nonconstant $f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ and every root
 $a$ of $f$, purely as a consequence of the formal division identity $x^k - a^k
= (x-a)\sum_{j=0}^{k-1} a^j x^{k-1-j}$. Solvability by radicals plays no role.
