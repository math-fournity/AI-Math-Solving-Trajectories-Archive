## Solution

**Definition.** A point $p \in \mathbb{R}$ is a *super limit point* (condensatio
n point) of a set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ if every open neighborhood of $p$ con
tains uncountably many points of $S$.

**Claim.** Yes — every uncountable subset of $\mathbb{R}$ has a super limit poin
t.

---

### Proof

Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be uncountable. Suppose, for contradiction, that $S
$ has **no** super limit point. Then for every $x \in \mathbb{R}$, there exists
an open neighborhood $U_x$ of $x$ such that $U_x \cap S$ is countable.

**Step 1: Reduce to rational-endpoint intervals.** For each $x \in \mathbb{R}$,
since $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$, we can choose an open interval $I_x
 = (a_x, b_x)$ with $a_x, b_x \in \mathbb{Q}$ such that
$$x \in I_x \subseteq U_x.$$
Since $I_x \cap S \subseteq U_x \cap S$ and $U_x \cap S$ is countable, the set $
I_x \cap S$ is also countable.

**Step 2: The collection of such intervals is countable.** The set of all open i
ntervals with rational endpoints is indexed by $\mathbb{Q} \times \mathbb{Q}$, w
hich is countable (since $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable). Therefore the collection
$$\mathcal{I} = \{I_x : x \in \mathbb{R}\}$$
is a subcollection of a countable set, hence is itself countable. Enumumerate it
 as $\mathcal{I} = \{J_1, J_2, J_3, \dots\}$.

**Step 3: Cover $S$ and derive a contradiction.** Since every $x \in \mathbb{R}$
 belongs to its corresponding $I_x$, the collection $\mathcal{I}$ covers $\mathb
b{R}$, and in particular covers $S$. Thus:
$$S = S \cap \mathbb{R} = S \cap \left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} J_n\right) = \bigc
up_{n=1}^{\infty} (S \cap J_n).$$

Each $S \cap J_n$ is countable (by construction), so $S$ is a countable union of
 countable sets. By the fact that a countable union of countable sets is countab
le, $S$ is countable.

This **contradicts** the assumption that $S$ is uncountable.

**Conclusion.** The contradiction shows that our supposition was false. Therefor
e $S$ must have at least one super limit point. $\blacksquare$

---

**Remark.** In fact, a stronger result holds: the set of all super limit points
of an uncountable $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is itself uncountable (and has cardin
ality $\mathfrak{c}$). This follows from the Cantor–Bendixson theorem, which dec
omposes any closed subset of $\mathbb{R}$ into a perfect set (whose points are a
ll condensation points) and a countable set. But the question only asks for exis
tence of at least one super limit point, which the argument above establishes di
rectly.
