## Proof

We need to find a positive integer $n$ such that $n(n+2013)$ is a perfect square
.

**Setup.** Let $d = \gcd(n,\, n+2013) = \gcd(n, 2013)$. Write
$$n = d\,a, \qquad n + 2013 = d\,b,$$
where $\gcd(a,b)=1$ and $b - a = \tfrac{2013}{d}$. Then
$$n(n+2013) = d^2\, a\, b.$$
For this to be a perfect square, $ab$ must be a perfect square. Since $\gcd(a,b)
=1$, **both** $a$ and $b$ must individually be perfect squares:
$$a = s^2,\qquad b = t^2,\qquad \gcd(s,t)=1,\qquad t>s\ge 1.$$
The condition $b - a = \tfrac{2013}{d}$ becomes
$$(t-s)(t+s) = \frac{2013}{d}.$$

**Choosing a divisor.** Since $2013 = 3 \times 11 \times 61$ is odd, every facto
r $\tfrac{2013}{d}$ is odd, so $t-s$ and $t+s$ are both odd (hence $s,t$ have op
posite parity, consistent with $\gcd(s,t)=1$).

Take $d = 671 = 11 \times 61$, giving $\tfrac{2013}{d} = 3$. The factorization
$$3 = 1 \times 3$$
yields $t - s = 1$, $t + s = 3$, so $t = 2$, $s = 1$, with $\gcd(2,1)=1$. ✓

Then
$$n = d\, s^2 = 671 \times 1^2 = 671.$$

**Verification.**
$$n + 2013 = 671 + 2013 = 2684 = 4 \times 671 = 2^2 \times 671.$$
Since $671 = 11 \times 61$,
$$n = 671 = 11 \times 61,\qquad n+2013 = 2^2 \times 11 \times 61.$$
Therefore
$$n(n+2013) = (11 \times 61)(2^2 \times 11 \times 61) = 2^2 \times 11^2 \times 6
1^2 = (2 \times 11 \times 61)^2 = 1342^2.$$
Indeed $1342^2 = 1{,}800{,}964$, and $671 \times 2684 = 1{,}800{,}964$. ✓

**Conclusion.** The value $n = 671$ makes $n(n+2013) = 1342^2$ a perfect square,
 so the containers can be arranged in a $1342 \times 1342$ square formation.
