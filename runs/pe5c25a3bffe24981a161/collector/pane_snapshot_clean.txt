**Claim:** Yes, it is always possible. For every $x \in U$, there exists a compa
ct neighborhood $K$ of $x$ with $K \subseteq U$.

**Proof.**

Recall that a *compact neighborhood* of $x$ is a compact set $K$ such that $x \i
n \operatorname{int}(K)$.

**Setup.** Since $X$ is locally compact, $x$ possesses a compact neighborhood $C
$: a compact set with $x \in \operatorname{int}(C)$. Since $X$ is Hausdorff and
$C$ is compact, $C$ is closed in $X$.

Set $V = U \cap \operatorname{int}(C)$. Then $V$ is open in $X$, $x \in V$, and
$V \subseteq C$.

**Working inside $C$.** Endowed with the subspace topology, $C$ is a compact Hau
sdorff space (Hausdorffness is inherited), hence **regular**. Now $V$ is open in
 $C$ (it is open in $X$ and contained in $C$), and $C \setminus V$ is closed in
$C$ with $x \notin C \setminus V$.

By regularity of $C$, there exist disjoint sets $G, G'$, open in $C$, with $x \i
n G$ and $C \setminus V \subseteq G'$.

Since $G \subseteq C \setminus G'$ and $C \setminus G'$ is closed in $C$, we get
$$\overline{G}^{\,C} \subseteq C \setminus G' \subseteq V \subseteq U.$$

**The candidate.** Let $K = \overline{G}^{\,C}$, the closure of $G$ in $C$.

- **Compactness:** $K$ is closed in $C$, and $C$ is compact, so $K$ is compact (
in $C$ and hence in $X$).
- **Contained in $U$:** $K \subseteq V \subseteq U$, as shown above.
- **Neighborhood of $x$:** Since $G$ is open in $C$, write $G = C \cap H$ for so
me $H$ open in $X$. Because $G \subseteq V \subseteq \operatorname{int}(C)$, the
 point $x$ lies in $H \cap \operatorname{int}(C)$, which is open in $X$. Moreove
r,
$$H \cap \operatorname{int}(C) \subseteq H \cap C = G \subseteq K.$$
Thus $x \in H \cap \operatorname{int}(C) \subseteq \operatorname{int}_X(K)$, so
$x \in \operatorname{int}_X(K)$.

Therefore $K$ is a compact neighborhood of $x$ entirely contained in $U$. $\blac
ksquare$

**Remark on the hypothesis $X \setminus U \neq \emptyset$.** This condition mere
ly excludes the trivial case $U = X$; the argument above works for *any* open $U
$ and any $x \in U$. The essential ingredients are local compactness (to get the
 initial compact neighborhood $C$) and the Hausdorff property (to make $C$ close
d and to obtain regularity of $C$, which yields the separation needed to shrink
the neighborhood inside $U$).
