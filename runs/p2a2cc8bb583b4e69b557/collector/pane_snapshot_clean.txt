# Proof

## Setup

Let $B_t$ be a Brownian motion on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathcal{Q})$, and let
$X_t$ satisfy
$$dX_t = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dB_t, \qquad \mu\in\mathbb{R},\ \sigma>0.$$
Integrating, $X_t = X_0 + \mu t + \sigma B_t$. The quadratic variation $\langle
X,X\rangle_t$ is the unique adapted, continuous, non-decreasing process such tha
t $X_t^2 - \langle X,X\rangle_t$ is a (local) martingale.

## Part 1: $\langle X,X\rangle_t = \sigma^2 t$ under $\mathcal{Q}$

**Step 1 — Quadratic variation is bilinear on continuous semimartingales.** Sinc
e $X_t = X_0 + \mu t + \sigma B_t$ is a continuous semimartingale (a continuous
martingale $\sigma B_t$ plus a finite-variation process $X_0+\mu t$), the quadra
tic variation satisfies
$$\langle X,X\rangle_t = \langle X_0+\mu t+\sigma B_t,\; X_0+\mu t+\sigma B_t\ra
ngle_t.$$

**Step 2 — Finite-variation terms contribute zero.** The process $A_t := X_0 + \
mu t$ is continuous and of finite variation on $[0,t]$ for every $t$. A standard
 property of quadratic covariation is that $\langle A, Y\rangle_t = 0$ whenever
$A$ is continuous of finite variation and $Y$ is a semimartingale. Hence
$$\langle A, A\rangle_t = 0,\qquad \langle A, \sigma B\rangle_t = 0.$$

**Step 3 — The martingale part.** By bilinearity,
$$\langle X,X\rangle_t = \langle A,A\rangle_t + 2\langle A,\sigma B\rangle_t + \
langle \sigma B,\sigma B\rangle_t = \sigma^2\langle B,B\rangle_t.$$

**Step 4 — Brownian quadratic variation.** For Brownian motion under $\mathcal{Q
}$, $\langle B,B\rangle_t = t$. This is the classical Lévy characterization / de
finition: $B_t^2 - t$ is a $\mathcal{Q}$-martingale, so the compensator of $B_t^
2$ is $t$.

Therefore
$$\boxed{\langle X,X\rangle_t = \sigma^2 t.}$$

## Part 2: The result holds under every $\mathcal{P}\ll\mathcal{Q}$

The key observation is that **quadratic variation is a pathwise (measure-indepen
dent) object** for continuous semimartingales, defined via a limit in probabilit
y over refining partitions.

**Step 1 — Definition via partition sums.** For a partition $\pi_n: 0=t_0^n<t_1^
n<\cdots<t_{k_n}^n=t$ with mesh $\|\pi_n\|\to 0$, define
$$V_n(\omega) := \sum_{j}\bigl(X_{t_{j+1}^n}(\omega)-X_{t_j^n}(\omega)\bigr)^2.$
$
The quadratic variation is the limit in probability of $V_n$.

**Step 2 — The limit is pathwise for continuous semimartingales.** For a continu
ous semimartingale, the sequence $V_n$ converges *uniformly on compacts in proba
bility* to $\langle X,X\rangle$, and in fact the convergence is *almost sure* al
ong a suitable deterministic refining sequence of partitions (e.g. dyadic partit
ions). Concretely, for the dyadic partition $\pi_n = \{j t/2^n\}$,
$$V_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \langle X,X\rangle_t \quad\text{as } n\to\infty.
$$
This almost-sure convergence holds $\mathcal{Q}$-a.s., and the limit is a *pathw
ise* functional of the sample path $t\mapsto X_t(\omega)$.

**Step 3 — Absolute continuity transfers null sets.** Since $\mathcal{P}\ll\math
cal{Q}$, every $\mathcal{Q}$-null set is $\mathcal{P}$-null. The set of sample p
aths for which $V_n\to\sigma^2 t$ has $\mathcal{Q}$-probability $1$, hence $\mat
hcal{P}$-probability $1$. Therefore
$$V_n \xrightarrow{\mathcal{P}\text{-a.s.}} \sigma^2 t,$$
which means $\langle X,X\rangle_t = \sigma^2 t$ under $\mathcal{P}$ as well.

**Step 4 — Direct argument via convergence in probability (without invoking a.s.
 convergence).** Even using only convergence in $\mathcal{Q}$-probability: $V_n\
xrightarrow{\mathcal{Q}\text{-prob}}\sigma^2 t$ means for every $\varepsilon>0$,
$$\mathcal{Q}\bigl(|V_n-\sigma^2 t|>\varepsilon\bigr)\to 0.$$
By absolute continuity, for any $\delta>0$ there exists $\eta>0$ with $\mathcal{
Q}(A)<\eta\Rightarrow\mathcal{P}(A)<\delta$. Choosing $n$ large enough that $\ma
thcal{Q}(|V_n-\sigma^2 t|>\varepsilon)<\eta$ gives $\mathcal{P}(|V_n-\sigma^2 t|
>\varepsilon)<\delta$. Thus
$$V_n\xrightarrow{\mathcal{P}\text{-prob}}\sigma^2 t,$$
confirming $\langle X,X\rangle_t=\sigma^2 t$ under $\mathcal{P}$.

## Conclusion

The quadratic variation of $X_t = X_0+\mu t+\sigma B_t$ is
$$\langle X,X\rangle_t = \sigma^2\langle B,B\rangle_t = \sigma^2 t,$$
because the finite-variation drift $\mu t$ contributes nothing to quadratic vari
ation, and $\langle B,B\rangle_t=t$ for Brownian motion. Moreover, since quadrat
ic variation is realized as a pathwise limit (in probability, and a.s. along dya
dic partitions) of sums that depend only on the sample path, and since $\mathcal
{P}\ll\mathcal{Q}$ preserves null sets, the identical limit $\sigma^2 t$ is obta
ined under every $\mathcal{P}\ll\mathcal{Q}$.
