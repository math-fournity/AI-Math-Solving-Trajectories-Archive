## Proof

**Step 1: Integral representation.** For $k \ge 1$ and $n \ge 0$,
$$\frac{1}{k2^n + 1} = \int_0^1 x^{k2^n}\,dx.$$

Define the partial sums
$$S_{K,N} = \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}\sum_{n=0}^N \frac{1}{k2^n+1}.$$

Since all sums are finite, we may freely interchange:
$$S_{K,N} = \int_0^1 \sum_{n=0}^N \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}\, x^{k2^n}\,
dx.$$

**Step 2: Take $K \to \infty$.** For $y \in [0,1]$, the series $\sum_{k=1}^\inft
y \frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k} y^k = \ln(1+y)$ converges uniformly (alternating series t
est with terms $y^k/k \downarrow 0$ uniformly). Setting $y = x^{2^n} \in [0,1]$:
$$\lim_{K\to\infty} S_{K,N} = \int_0^1 \sum_{n=0}^N \ln\!\bigl(1+x^{2^n}\bigr)\,
dx.$$

**Step 3: Telescoping product.** The classical identity
$$(1-x)\prod_{n=0}^{N}(1+x^{2^n}) = 1 - x^{2^{N+1}}$$
gives
$$\sum_{n=0}^N \ln\!\bigl(1+x^{2^n}\bigr) = \ln\!\bigl(1-x^{2^{N+1}}\bigr) - \ln
(1-x).$$

Therefore
$$S_{\infty,N} := \lim_{K\to\infty} S_{K,N} = \int_0^1 \ln\!\bigl(1-x^{2^{N+1}}\
bigr)\,dx - \int_0^1 \ln(1-x)\,dx.$$

**Step 4: Take $N \to \infty$.** For $x \in [0,1)$, $x^{2^{N+1}} \to 0$ so $\ln(
1-x^{2^{N+1}}) \to 0$. Since $x^{2^{N+1}} \le x$, we have $1-x^{2^{N+1}} \ge 1-x
$, hence
$$\bigl|\ln\!\bigl(1-x^{2^{N+1}}\bigr)\bigr| \le \bigl|\ln(1-x)\bigr|,$$
and $\int_0^1 |\ln(1-x)|\,dx = 1 < \infty$. By dominated convergence:
$$\lim_{N\to\infty} \int_0^1 \ln\!\bigl(1-x^{2^{N+1}}\bigr)\,dx = 0.$$

Also, $\int_0^1 \ln(1-x)\,dx = \int_0^1 \ln u\,du = \bigl[u\ln u - u\bigr]_0^1 =
 -1$.

Thus
$$\lim_{N\to\infty} S_{\infty,N} = 0 - (-1) = 1.$$

nfty} S_{K,\infty}$. We show $S = \lim_{N\to\infty} S_{\infty,N} = 1$ by boundin

- **Truncation in $k$:** For $f(k) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k2^n+1} \le \sum

$$|S_{\infty,N} - S_{K,N}| \le \sum_{k=K+1}^\infty \frac{f(k)}{k} \le 2\sum_{k=K

- **Truncation in $n$:**
$$|S_{K,\infty} - S_{K,N}| \le \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{1}{k}\sum_{n=N+1}^\infty \frac

ghtarrow{N\to\infty} 0 \quad \text{uniformly in } K.$$

Given $\epsilon > 0$, choose $K$ so that $|S - S_{K,\infty}| < \epsilon/3$ and $
2\sum_{k>K} k^{-2} < \epsilon/3$; then choose $N$ so that $\frac{\pi^2/6}{2^N} <

## Result

$$\boxed{S = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k2
^n+1} = 1.}$$

The key idea: representing $\frac{1}{k2^n+1}$ as $\int_0^1 x^{k2^n}dx$ converts
the $k$-sum into $\ln(1+x^{2^n})$ via the Mercator series, and the $n$-sum then
telescopes via the identity $\prod_{n=0}^{N}(1+x^{2^n}) = \frac{1-x^{2^{N+1}}}{1
-x}$, reducing everything to $\int_0^1 -\ln(1-x)\,dx = 1$.
