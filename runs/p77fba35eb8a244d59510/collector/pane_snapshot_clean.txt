## Proof

**Claim:** The number of maximal ideals of $R$ is $1$.

**Step 1: $I$ is exactly the set of non-units of $R$.**

- If $x \in R \setminus I$, then by hypothesis $x$ is invertible in $R$, so $x$
is a unit.
- Conversely, suppose $x \in I$ is a unit. Then $1 = x \cdot x^{-1} \in I$ (sinc
e $I$ is an ideal and $x \in I$), which forces $I = R$, contradicting that $I$ i
s proper. Hence no element of $I$ is a unit.

Therefore $I = \{x \in R : x \text{ is not a unit in } R\}$.

**Step 2: $I$ is a maximal ideal.**

Consider the quotient $R/I$. Every nonzero element of $R/I$ is the image of some
 $x \in R \setminus I$, which is a unit in $R$; its image in $R/I$ is therefore
a unit. Hence every nonzero element of $R/I$ is invertible, so $R/I$ is a field.
 Thus $I$ is maximal.

**Step 3: $I$ is the unique maximal ideal.**

Let $\mathfrak{m}$ be any maximal ideal of $R$. Since $\mathfrak{m}$ is a proper
 ideal, it cannot contain any unit (a unit in an ideal would force the ideal to
be all of $R$). Hence every element of $\mathfrak{m}$ is a non-unit, so by Step
1, $\mathfrak{m} \subseteq I$. Since $\mathfrak{m}$ is maximal and $I \neq R$ is
 proper, we must have $\mathfrak{m} = I$.

Thus every maximal ideal of $R$ equals $I$, so $R$ has exactly one maximal ideal
. (Note: $R$ is not a field, so $I \neq \{0\}$, confirming that $I$ is a genuine
 nonzero maximal ideal — consistent with the hypothesis.)

**Conclusion:** The number of maximal ideals of $R$ is $\boxed{1}$.
