# Proof: Unique Identification of $B$ from $C = BAB$

**Claim:** Yes, $B$ is uniquely determined.

## Setup and Key Substitution

Since $A$ is diagonal, positive semi-definite, and full rank, all its diagonal e
ntries are positive: $A = \operatorname{diag}(a_1,\dots,a_n)$ with $a_i > 0$. He
nce $A$ is positive definite, and the diagonal matrices $A^{1/2} = \operatorname
{diag}(\sqrt{a_i})$ and $A^{-1/2} = \operatorname{diag}(1/\sqrt{a_i})$ are well-
defined and invertible.

Both $B$ and $C$ are symmetric, positive semi-definite, and full rank, hence pos
itive definite.

Define the **congruence transform**
$$Y \;=\; A^{1/2}\, B\, A^{1/2}.$$
Since $B$ is positive definite and $A^{1/2}$ is invertible, $Y$ is also symmetri
c positive definite. Conversely, $B = A^{-1/2}\, Y\, A^{-1/2}$.

## Reducing to a Matrix Square Root

Substitute $B = A^{-1/2} Y A^{-1/2}$ into $C = BAB$:

$$C \;=\; \bigl(A^{-1/2} Y A^{-1/2}\bigr)\, A\, \bigl(A^{-1/2} Y A^{-1/2}\bigr).
$$

The middle term simplifies: $A^{-1/2}\, A\, A^{-1/2} = I$. Therefore

$$C \;=\; A^{-1/2}\, Y^2\, A^{-1/2}.$$

Multiplying on both sides by $A^{1/2}$:

$$\widetilde{C} \;:=\; A^{1/2}\, C\, A^{1/2} \;=\; Y^2.$$

Note $\widetilde{C}$ is positive definite (congruence by the invertible $A^{1/2}
$ preserves positive definiteness, since $C$ is positive definite).

## Uniqueness of the Positive Definite Square Root

The equation $Y^2 = \widetilde{C}$ with $Y$ symmetric positive definite has a **
unique** solution. Proof:

By the spectral theorem, $\widetilde{C} = P\,\Lambda\, P^T$ where $P$ is orthogo
nal and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_n)$ with all $\mu_j > 0$.
 The matrix
$$Y_0 \;=\; P\,\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\mu_1},\dots,\sqrt{\mu_n})\, P^T$$
is symmetric positive definite and satisfies $Y_0^2 = \widetilde{C}$, so a solut
ion exists.

For uniqueness: suppose $Y_1, Y_2$ are both symmetric positive definite with $Y_
i^2 = \widetilde{C}$. Then each $Y_i$ commutes with $\widetilde{C}$:
$$Y_i\,\widetilde{C} \;=\; Y_i\, Y_i^2 \;=\; Y_i^3 \;=\; Y_i^2\, Y_i \;=\; \wide
tilde{C}\, Y_i.$$
Since $Y_i$ commutes with $\widetilde{C}$, they are simultaneously diagonalizabl
e; in the eigenbasis of $\widetilde{C}$, $Y_i = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_j^{(
i)})$ with $(\lambda_j^{(i)})^2 = \mu_j$. Positive definiteness forces $\lambda_
j^{(i)} > 0$, so $\lambda_j^{(i)} = \sqrt{\mu_j}$ for every $j$. Hence $Y_1 = Y_
2$.

## Conclusion

Since $Y = \widetilde{C}^{1/2}$ is uniquely determined, so is

$$\boxed{B \;=\; A^{-1/2}\,\widetilde{C}^{\,1/2}\, A^{-1/2} \;=\; A^{-1/2}\,\big
l(A^{1/2}\, C\, A^{1/2}\bigr)^{1/2}\, A^{-1/2}.}$$

This $B$ is symmetric (congruence of a symmetric matrix by the symmetric $A^{-1/
2}$) and positive definite (congruence of a positive definite matrix by an inver
tible matrix), and it satisfies $C = BAB$ by construction. No other symmetric po
sitive definite matrix can satisfy the equation.

**Therefore, $B$ can be uniquely identified.**
