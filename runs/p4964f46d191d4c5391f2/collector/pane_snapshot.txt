## Proof

We prove that if $P = N/D \in \mathbb{C}(x,y)$ (with $N, D \in \mathbb{C}[x,y]$,
 $D \neq 0$) satisfies $P(n,m) \in \mathbb{Z}$ for every $(n,m) \in \mathbb{Z}^2
$ (in particular $D(n,m) \neq 0$ for all $(n,m) \in \mathbb{Z}^2$), then $P \in
\mathbb{Q}[x,y]$.

### Step 1: One-variable lemma

**Lemma.** If $f = g/h \in \mathbb{C}(x)$ with $h(n) \neq 0$ for all $n \in \mat
hbb{Z}$ and $f(n) \in \mathbb{Z}$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, then $f \in \mathb
b{Q}[x]$.

*Proof of Lemma.* Perform polynomial division in $\mathbb{C}[x]$: $g = qh + r$ w
ith $\deg r < \deg h$ (or $r = 0$). Then $f = q + r/h$. Set $\varepsilon_n := r(
n)/h(n)$. Since $\deg r < \deg h$, we have $\varepsilon_n \to 0$ as $n \to +\inf
ty$. Now $q(n) = f(n) - \varepsilon_n$ where $f(n) \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $\varepsi
lon_n \to 0$.

**$q$ has real coefficients:** $\operatorname{Im}(q(n)) = -\operatorname{Im}(\va
repsilon_n) \to 0$. Since $\operatorname{Im}(q(n))$ is a real polynomial in $n$
tending to $0$ along positive integers, it is identically $0$. So $q \in \mathbb
{R}[x]$.

**$q$ takes integer values on $\mathbb{Z}$:** We proceed by induction on $\deg q
$. If $q$ is constant, $q = c = \lim_{n\to\infty} f(n) \in \mathbb{Z}$ (since $f
(n) \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $\varepsilon_n \to 0$). For $\deg q = d \geq 1$, the for
ward difference $\Delta q(n) = q(n+1)-q(n)$ is a polynomial of degree $d-1$ sati
sfying $\Delta q(n) = (f(n+1)-f(n)) - (\varepsilon_{n+1}-\varepsilon_n)$, which
is within $o(1)$ of an integer. By induction, $\Delta q \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$, so $
q(n+1)-q(n) \in \mathbb{Z}$ for all $n$. Then $q(n) - q(0) \in \mathbb{Z}$ for a
ll $n$, and $q(0) = f(0) - \varepsilon_0$ is a limit of integers (using $q(n) =
q(0) + \text{integer}$ and $f(n) \in \mathbb{Z}$, $\varepsilon_n \to 0$), so $q(
0) \in \mathbb{Z}$. Hence $q(n) \in \mathbb{Z}$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

**$r = 0$:** Since $q(n) \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $f(n) \in \mathbb{Z}$, we get $r(n)
/h(n) = f(n) - q(n) \in \mathbb{Z}$ for all $n$. But $r(n)/h(n) \to 0$, so $r(n)
/h(n) = 0$ for all sufficiently large $n$, giving $r(n) = 0$ for infinitely many
 $n$, hence $r = 0$.

Therefore $f = q \in \mathbb{R}[x]$ with $q(n) \in \mathbb{Z}$ for all $n$. By t
he classical binomial-coefficient basis theorem (every integer-valued polynomial

### Step 2: $P$ is a polynomial in $x$ over $\mathbb{C}(y)$

Fix any $m \in \mathbb{Z}$. Since $D(n,m) \neq 0$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ and

of the Lemma. Thus $P(x,m) \in \mathbb{Q}[x] \subset \mathbb{C}[x]$ for every $m

in \mathbb{Z}$.

Now divide $N$ by $D$ in $\mathbb{C}(y)[x]$: write $N = Q \cdot D + R$ with $Q,
R \in \mathbb{C}(y)[x]$ and $\deg_x R < \deg_x D$ (if $\deg_x D = 0$, then $P =
N/D \in \mathbb{C}(y)[x]$ directly). For each $m \in \mathbb{Z}$ avoiding the fi
nitely many poles of the coefficients of $Q$ and $R$, substituting $y = m$ gives

]$ and $\deg_x R(x,m) < \deg_x D(x,m)$, we conclude $R(x,m) = 0$.

Write $R(x,y) = \sum_j r_j(y)\, x^j$ with $r_j \in \mathbb{C}(y)$. Then $r_j(m)
= 0$ for all but finitely many $m \in \mathbb{Z}$. Each $r_j$ is a rational func
tion with infinitely many zeros, so $r_j \equiv 0$. Hence $R = 0$ and $P = Q \in
 \mathbb{C}(y)[x]$.

### Step 3: $P \in \mathbb{C}[x,y]$

By the symmetric argument (fixing $x = n \in \mathbb{Z}$ and applying the Lemma
in the $y$-variable), $P \in \mathbb{C}(x)[y]$.

We now show $\mathbb{C}(y)[x] \cap \mathbb{C}(x)[y] = \mathbb{C}[x,y]$. Since $P
 \in \mathbb{C}(y)[x]$, we can write $P = F(x,y)/q(y)$ with $F \in \mathbb{C}[x,
y]$ and $q \in \mathbb{C}[y]\setminus\{0\}$. Since $P \in \mathbb{C}(x)[y]$, $P$

C}(x)[y]$. As $q(y)$ is a primitive polynomial in $\mathbb{C}[x][y]$ (its coeffi
cients lie in $\mathbb{C}$), Gauss's Lemma gives $q(y) \mid F(x,y)$ in $\mathbb{
C}[x,y]$. Thus $P = F/q \in \mathbb{C}[x,y]$.

### Step 4: $P \in \mathbb{Q}[x,y]$

Since $P \in \mathbb{C}[x,y]$, it admits a unique expansion in the binomial basi
s:
$$P(x,y) = \sum_{i=0}^{\deg_x P} \sum_{j=0}^{\deg_y P} c_{ij}\, \binom{x}{i}\bin
om{y}{j}, \qquad c_{ij} = \Delta_x^i \Delta_y^j P(0,0) \in \mathbb{C},$$
where $\Delta_x P(x,y) = P(x+1,y)-P(x,y)$ and $\Delta_y P(x,y) = P(x,y+1)-P(x,y)

difference $\Delta_x^i \Delta_y^j P(0,0)$ is an integer. Hence $c_{ij} \in \math
bb{Z}$. Since each $\binom{x}{i}\binom{y}{j} \in \mathbb{Q}[x,y]$, we conclude $
P \in \mathbb{Q}[x,y]$.

### Conclusion

The statement is **true**: every $P \in \mathbb{C}(x,y)$ with $P(\mathbb{Z}^2) \
subset \mathbb{Z}$ belongs to $\mathbb{Q}[x,y]$.
