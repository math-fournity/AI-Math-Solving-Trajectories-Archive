Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the integral representation
 $f(x) = \int g(u)e^{2\pi iux}\,du$ a.e. uniquely forces $g = \hat{f}$.

**Answer: No, we cannot conclude that $g$ is the Fourier transform of $f$ in gen
eral.**

## Counterexample

Take $g(u) = e^{2\pi i c u}$ for some fixed constant $c \neq 0$. This function i
s not in $L^1(\mathbb{R})$, but the symmetric improper integral exists for a.e.
$x$:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(u)\,e^{2\pi iux}\,du = \lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{-R}^{R
} e^{2\pi i(c+x)u}\,du.$$

For $x \neq -c$:
$$\int_{-R}^{R} e^{2\pi i(c+x)u}\,du = \frac{\sin\bigl(2\pi(c+x)R\bigr)}{\pi(c+x
)} \xrightarrow{R\to\infty} 0,$$
since the numerator is bounded and the denominator is a fixed nonzero constant.
At $x = -c$ the integral equals $2R \to \infty$.

Therefore $f(x) = 0$ for almost every $x \in \mathbb{R}$, so $\hat{f} = 0$. But
$g(u) = e^{2\pi i c u} \neq 0$. Hence $g \neq \hat{f}$, and the conclusion fails
.

The root cause is that **$g$ is not uniquely determined** by the relation $f(x)
= \int g(u)\,e^{2\pi iux}\,du$ when the integral is not required to be an absolu
tely convergent (Lebesgue) integral of an $L^1$ function. Any function $h$ with
$\operatorname{p.v.}\int h(u)\,e^{2\pi iux}\,du = 0$ a.e. can be added to $g$ wi
thout changing $f$.

## When the conclusion does hold

If we additionally assume $g \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ and $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, t
hen $g = \hat{f}$ a.e. Indeed:

1. Since $g \in L^1$, the integral $f(x) = \int g(u)\,e^{2\pi iux}\,du = \hat{g}
(-x)$ is well-defined (Lebesgue), so $f(x) = \hat{g}(-x)$.

2. Since $f \in L^1$, we have $\hat{g} \in L^1$ (because $\hat{g}(\xi) = f(-\xi)
$ and $f \in L^1$).

3. Computing $\hat{f}$:
$$\hat{f}(\xi) = \int f(x)\,e^{-2\pi ix\xi}\,dx = \int \hat{g}(-x)\,e^{-2\pi ix\
xi}\,dx = \int \hat{g}(y)\,e^{2\pi iy\xi}\,dy = \check{\hat{g}}(\xi).$$

4. Since $g \in L^1$ and $\hat{g} \in L^1$, the **Fourier inversion theorem** gi
ves $\check{\hat{g}}(\xi) = g(\xi)$ for a.e. $\xi$.

5. Therefore $\hat{f}(\xi) = g(\xi)$ a.e. $\quad\checkmark$

## Conclusion

The representation $f(x) = \int g(u)\,e^{2\pi iux}\,du$ a.e. does **not** by its
elf guarantee $g = \hat{f}$. The conclusion requires additional hypotheses — spe
cifically, that $g$ and $f$ both belong to $L^1(\mathbb{R})$ (or more generally
to a function space such as $L^2$ where the Fourier transform and its inverse ar
e well-defined and are genuine inverses). Without such assumptions, $g$ need not
 be unique, and the conclusion can fail as the counterexample above demonstrates
.
