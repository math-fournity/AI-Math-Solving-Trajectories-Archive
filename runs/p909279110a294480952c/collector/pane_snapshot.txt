Looking at this problem, I need to find all positive integer solutions $(a,b,c,d
)$ to $\frac{1}{2} = \frac{a^2+b^3}{c^5+d^7}$, i.e., $c^5+d^7 = 2(a^2+b^3)$, and
 determine the sum of all distinct values of $a+b+c+d$.

## Systematic Search

**Parity constraint:** $c^5+d^7$ must be even, so $c$ and $d$ must have the same
 parity.

**Checking small $(c,d)$ pairs:**

| $(c,d)$ | $c^5+d^7$ | $(c^5+d^7)/2$ | Solutions $(a,b)$? |
|---------|-----------|---------------|---------------------|
| $(1,1)$ | 2 | 1 | $a^2+b^3=1$, impossible ($\min=2$) |
| $(1,2)$ | 129 | odd | — |
| $(2,1)$ | 33 | odd | — |
| $(1,3)$ | 2188 | 1094 | No perfect square $a^2=1094-b^3$ for $b=1..10$ |
| $(3,1)$ | 244 | 122 | $b=1: a^2=121 \Rightarrow a=11$ ✓ → sum $=16$ |
| $(2,2)$ | 160 | 80 | $b=4: a^2=16 \Rightarrow a=4$ ✓ → sum $=12$ |
| $(4,2)$ | 1152 | 576 | $b=8: a^2=64 \Rightarrow a=8$ ✓ → sum $=22$ |

**Exhaustive check for sums $< 12$:** For each $s = 4,5,...,11$, I enumerate all
 $(c,d)$ with $c+d \leq s-2$ (since $a+b \geq 2$), check parity, and verify whet
her $a^2+b^3 = (c^5+d^7)/2$ has a solution with $a+b = s-c-d$.

- **Sum 4–8:** All candidate $(c,d)$ pairs either give odd $c^5+d^7$, or the tar
get $a^2+b^3$ is too large for the available $a+b$ budget. No solutions.
- **Sum 9:** All even-parity $(c,d)$ pairs (e.g., $(2,2)$ with $c+d=4$, $a+b=5$)
 yield $a^2+b^3=80$ but max $a^2+b^3$ with $a+b=5$ is $4^2+1^3=17 < 80$. No solu
tions.
- **Sum 10–11:** Same pattern — either odd $c^5+d^7$, or target vastly exceeds w
hat small $a+b$ can produce. No solutions.

**Verification of sum = 12:**

For $c+d=4$, $(c,d)=(2,2)$: $a^2+b^3=80$, $a+b=8$. Testing $b=1..7$: only $b=4$
gives $a^2=16$, $a=4$, $a+b=8$ ✓.
For $c+d=5$: all pairs $(1,4),(2,3),(3,2),(4,1)$ give odd $c^5+d^7$.
For $c+d \geq 6$: targets ($\geq 576$) far exceed achievable $a^2+b^3$ with $a+b
 \leq 6$.

**The unique minimizer is $(a,b,c,d)=(4,4,2,2)$:**
$$\frac{4^2+4^3}{2^5+2^7} = \frac{16+64}{32+128} = \frac{80}{160} = \frac{1}{2}
\checkmark$$
$$a+b+c+d = 4+4+2+2 = 12$$

## Regarding "all distinct possible values"

A parametric family exists (e.g., setting $c=2r^{42}, d=2r^{30}, a=4r^{105}, b=4
r^{70}$ for any positive integer $r$), producing infinitely many solutions with
unbounded sums. Therefore "the sum of all distinct possible values" over *all* s
olutions diverges. The well-posed interpretation is the **minimum** value of $a+
b+c+d$, which is uniquely achieved.
