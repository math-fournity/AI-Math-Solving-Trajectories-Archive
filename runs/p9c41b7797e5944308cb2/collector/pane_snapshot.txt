## Solution

**Claim.** No. A contractive mapping $T:X\to X$ on a (not necessarily complete)
metric space does **not** guarantee the existence of a fixed point. (Uniqueness,
 whenever a fixed point exists, *is* guaranteed — but existence is not.)

Recall that $T$ is *contractive* if $d(Tx,Ty)<d(x,y)$ for all $x\neq y$ in $X$.

---

### Part 1. Uniqueness always holds (no completeness needed)

Suppose $x^\ast, y^\ast$ are both fixed points of $T$ with $x^\ast\neq y^\ast$.
Then
$$d(x^\ast,y^\ast)=d(Tx^\ast,Ty^\ast)<d(x^\ast,y^\ast),$$
a contradiction. Hence $x^\ast=y^\ast$. So **at most one** fixed point can exist
, regardless of completeness. $\square$

---

### Part 2. Existence can fail when $X$ is incomplete — a counterexample

Let
$$X=(0,1)\subset\mathbb{R},\qquad d(x,y)=|x-y|,\qquad T(x)=\frac{x}{2}.$$

**$X$ is a metric space.** It inherits the usual metric from $\mathbb{R}$.

**$X$ is not complete.** The sequence $x_n=\frac{1}{n+1}\in(0,1)$ is Cauchy (it
converges to $0$ in $\mathbb{R}$), yet its limit $0\notin(0,1)$. So $X$ admits a
 Cauchy sequence with no limit in $X$.

**$T$ maps $X$ into $X$.** For $x\in(0,1)$, $\frac{x}{2}\in(0,\tfrac12)\subset(0
,1)$.

**$T$ is contractive.** For $x\neq y$ in $(0,1)$,
$$d(Tx,Ty)=\left|\frac{x}{2}-\frac{y}{2}\right|=\frac{1}{2}|x-y|<|x-y|=d(x,y).$$
(In fact $T$ is a strict contraction with constant $\tfrac12$, which is stronger
 than merely contractive.)

**$T$ has no fixed point in $X$.** A fixed point would satisfy $x=\frac{x}{2}$,
i.e. $x=0$. But $0\notin(0,1)$. Hence $T$ has **no** fixed point in $X$.

---

### Conclusion

The contractive mapping $T(x)=x/2$ on the incomplete metric space $X=(0,1)$ has
no fixed point at all. Therefore a contractive mapping on a non-complete metric
space does **not** guarantee a (unique) fixed point.

What fails is precisely the *existence* half of the Banach fixed-point theorem:
the Picard iterates $x_n=T^n x_0=x_0/2^n$ form a Cauchy sequence, but its limit
$0$ lies in the completion $\overline{X}=[0,1]$ rather than in $X$ itself. Compl
eteness of $X$ is exactly the hypothesis that forces this limit back into $X$, a
nd dropping it allows the limit to "escape" — as the counterexample shows.
