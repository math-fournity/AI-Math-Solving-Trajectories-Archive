## Part (a): $\sum_{n \geq 2} \frac{(\ln n)^{-2p}}{n^p + p^{-(\ln n)^{1/p}}}$

**Analysis of the denominator term** $p^{-(\ln n)^{1/p}} = e^{-(\ln p)(\ln n)^{1
/p}}$:

**Case $p > 1$:** Here $\ln p > 0$, so $p^{-(\ln n)^{1/p}} = e^{-(\ln p)(\ln n)^
{1/p}} \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$. The denominator satisfies $n^p + p^{-(\ln n)^{1
/p}} > n^p$, so
$$0 < \frac{(\ln n)^{-2p}}{n^p + p^{-(\ln n)^{1/p}}} < \frac{(\ln n)^{-2p}}{n^p}
 \leq \frac{1}{n^p}$$
for $n \geq 3$ (since $(\ln n)^{-2p} \leq 1$). Since $\sum \frac{1}{n^p}$ conver
ges for $p > 1$, the series **converges** by comparison.

**Case $p = 1$:** Here $p^{-(\ln n)^{1/p}} = 1^{-(\ln n)} = 1$, so the denominat
or is $n + 1 > n$. Thus
$$0 < \frac{(\ln n)^{-2}}{n+1} < \frac{1}{n(\ln n)^2}.$$
Since $\int_3^\infty \frac{dx}{x(\ln x)^2} = \frac{1}{\ln 3} < \infty$, the seri
es $\sum \frac{1}{n(\ln n)^2}$ converges by the integral test, so our series **c
onverges**.

**Case $0 < p < 1$:** Here $\ln p < 0$, so $p^{-(\ln n)^{1/p}} = e^{|\ln p|(\ln
n)^{1/p}} \to \infty$. Moreover, since $1/p > 1$, the exponent $|\ln p|(\ln n)^{
1/p}$ grows faster than $p \ln n$ (the exponent of $n^p$), because
$$\frac{|\ln p|(\ln n)^{1/p}}{p \ln n} = \frac{|\ln p|}{p} \cdot (\ln n)^{1/p -
1} \to \infty.$$
So $p^{-(\ln n)^{1/p}} \gg n^p$, and the denominator is at least $p^{-(\ln n)^{1
/p}}$. Thus
$$0 < \frac{(\ln n)^{-2p}}{n^p + p^{-(\ln n)^{1/p}}} < \frac{(\ln n)^{-2p}}{e^{|
\ln p|(\ln n)^{1/p}}}.$$
For any $\varepsilon > 0$, since $(\ln n)^{1/p - 1} \to \infty$, we have $|\ln p
|(\ln n)^{1/p} > \varepsilon \ln n$ for all sufficiently large $n$, so the gener
al term is $o(n^{-\varepsilon})$. Taking $\varepsilon = 2$, the term is eventual
ly less than $1/n^2$, so the series **converges** by comparison with $\sum 1/n^2
$.

**Conclusion for (a):** The series converges for **all $p > 0$**.

## Part (b): $\sum \frac{(-1)^{\lfloor \sqrt{n} \rfloor}}{n^p}$

**Block structure.** The sign $(-1)^{\lfloor \sqrt{n} \rfloor}$ is constant on b
locks where $\lfloor \sqrt{n} \rfloor = k$, i.e., $k^2 \leq n \leq k^2 + 2k = (k
+1)^2 - 1$. Define
$$a_k = \sum_{n=k^2}^{k^2+2k} \frac{1}{n^p}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$$
Each block has $2k+1$ terms, all with sign $(-1)^k$. The series becomes $\sum_{k
=0}^{\infty} (-1)^k a_k$.

**Asymptotics of $a_k$.** Since $k^2 \leq n \leq (k+1)^2 - 1 < (k+1)^2$:
$$\frac{2k+1}{(k+1)^{2p}} \leq a_k \leq \frac{2k+1}{k^{2p}}.$$
Both bounds are $\sim \frac{2k}{k^{2p}} = \frac{2}{k^{2p-1}}$, so by squeezing:
$$a_k \sim \frac{2}{k^{2p-1}} \quad (k \to \infty).$$

**Equivalence of convergence.** The partial sum at the end of block $K$ is $S_{(
K+1)^2-1} = \sum_{k=0}^{K} (-1)^k a_k$. Within block $K$, all terms share the si
gn $(-1)^K$, so partial sums are monotone and stay between $S_{(K+1)^2-1}$ and $
S_{K^2-1}$. Therefore the original series converges if and only if the grouped s
eries $\sum (-1)^k a_k$ converges **and** $a_k \to 0$.

**Case $p > 1/2$:** Here $2p - 1 > 0$, so $a_k \to 0$. To apply the Leibniz test
, we need $a_k$ eventually decreasing. From the Riemann sum approximation $a_k =
 \int_{k^2}^{(k+1)^2} x^{-p}\,dx + O(k^{-2p})$, and the integral $I_k = \int_{k^
2}^{(k+1)^2} x^{-p}\,dx$ is decreasing for $p > 1/2$ (by the substitution $I_k =
 \int_0^{2k+1} (k^2+t)^{-p}\,dt$, where the integrand shrinks as $k$ grows, domi
nating the 2 extra terms). Since $a_k = \frac{2}{k^{2p-1}}(1 + O(1/k))$, we get
$$\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} = \left(\frac{k}{k+1}\right)^{2p-1}\!\bigl(1 + O(1/k^2)\bi
gr) = 1 - \frac{2p-1}{k} + O(1/k^2) < 1$$
for large $k$. So $a_k$ is eventually decreasing, and by the Leibniz test the gr
ouped series converges. Since $a_k \to 0$, the original series **converges**.

For $p > 1$, the series converges absolutely since $\sum 1/n^p < \infty$. For $1
/2 < p \leq 1$, convergence is conditional.

**Case $p = 1/2$:** Here $a_k \sim \frac{2}{k^0} = 2 \not\to 0$. The terms $(-1)
^k a_k$ of the grouped series do not tend to 0, so the grouped series diverges.
Since $a_k \not\to 0$, the original partial sums satisfy $|S_{(k+1)^2-1} - S_{k^
2-1}| = a_k \not\to 0$, so they are not Cauchy. The series **diverges**.

**Case $0 < p < 1/2$:** Here $2p - 1 < 0$, so $a_k \sim \frac{2}{k^{2p-1}} \to \
infty$. The same non-Cauchy argument applies. The series **diverges**.

**Conclusion for (b):** The series converges if and only if **$p > \frac{1}{2}$*
*.

## Part (c): $\sum \arccos^p \frac{\nu(n)}{\nu(n+1)}$

**Reduction to nonzero terms.** We have $\nu(n) = \lfloor \log_{10} n \rfloor +
1$, so $\nu(n) = \nu(n+1)$ unless $n+1$ is a power of $10$, i.e., $n = 10^k - 1$
 for some $k \geq 1$.

- If $n \neq 10^k - 1$: $\nu(n) = \nu(n+1)$, so $\frac{\nu(n)}{\nu(n+1)} = 1$, a
nd $\arccos(1) = 0$. The term vanishes.
- If $n = 10^k - 1$: $\nu(n) = k$ and $\nu(n+1) = \nu(10^k) = k+1$, so $\frac{\n
u(n)}{\nu(n+1)} = \frac{k}{k+1}$.

The series reduces to:
$$\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left(\arccos \frac{k}{k+1}\right)^p.$$

**Asymptotics.** Let $\theta_k = \arccos \frac{k}{k+1}$, so $\cos \theta_k = 1 -
 \frac{1}{k+1}$. For small $\theta$, $\cos \theta = 1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2} + O(\
theta^4)$, giving:
$$\frac{\theta_k^2}{2} = \frac{1}{k+1} + O\!\left(\frac{1}{(k+1)^2}\right), \qqu
ad \theta_k = \sqrt{\frac{2}{k+1}}\left(1 + O\!\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)\right).$
$
Therefore:
$$\theta_k^p = \left(\frac{2}{k+1}\right)^{p/2}\!\left(1 + O\!\left(\frac{1}{k}\
right)\right) \sim \frac{2^{p/2}}{k^{p/2}}.$$

**Convergence.** By the limit comparison test with $\sum \frac{1}{k^{p/2}}$ (usi
ng $\lim_{k\to\infty} \theta_k^p \big/ \frac{2^{p/2}}{k^{p/2}} = 1$), the series
 converges if and only if $\frac{p}{2} > 1$, i.e., $p > 2$.

At $p = 2$: $\theta_k^2 \sim \frac{2}{k}$, and $\sum \frac{2}{k}$ diverges (harm
onic series).

**Conclusion for (c):** The series converges if and only if **$p > 2$**.
