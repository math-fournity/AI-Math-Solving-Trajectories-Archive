## Solution

**Given:** $\lim_{n \to \infty} n^n h(n) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-e^x} g(x)\,dx$.

Denote the constant $C := \int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-e^x} g(x)\,dx$. The hypothesis i
s that this integral converges to a finite value, so $C$ is a well-defined real
constant. Assuming $C \neq 0$ (the generic case), we have:

$$n^n h(n) \to C \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$

**Step 1: Extract the asymptotic form of $h(n)$.**

From $n^n h(n) = C + o(1)$, we solve for $h(n)$:

$$h(n) = \frac{C + o(1)}{n^n}.$$

**Step 2: Take logarithms.**

$$\log h(n) = \log\!\big(C + o(1)\big) - \log(n^n) = \log\!\big(C + o(1)\big) -
n\log n.$$

As $n \to \infty$:
- The term $\log(C + o(1)) \to \log|C|$, a fixed constant (i.e., $O(1)$).
- The term $n \log n \to +\infty$.

Therefore:

$$\log h(n) = -n\log n + O(1).$$

**Step 3: Determine the order of magnitude.**

Since the $O(1)$ term is negligible compared to $n \log n \to \infty$:

$$\log h(n) \sim -n \log n.$$

The dominant term is $n \log n$, so:

$$\boxed{\log h(n) = \Theta(n \log n).}$$

More precisely, $\log h(n) = -n\log n + O(1)$, meaning $|\log h(n)|$ grows on th
e order of $n \log n$, with $\log h(n) \to -\infty$ (since $h(n) \to 0$ super-ex
ponentially fast, decaying like $n^{-n}$).

**Remark on the case $C = 0$:** If the integral vanishes ($C = 0$), then $n^n h(
n) \to 0$, so $h(n) = o(n^{-n})$ and $\log h(n) = -n\log n + \log(n^n h(n))$ whe
re the last term $\to -\infty$. In this degenerate case the order of magnitude c
ould be larger than $n\log n$, and the problem would be underdetermined without
further information about $g$. The intended interpretation is $C \neq 0$, giving
 the clean answer $\log h(n) = \Theta(n\log n)$.
