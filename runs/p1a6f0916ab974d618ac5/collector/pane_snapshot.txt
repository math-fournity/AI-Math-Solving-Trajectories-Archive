## Proof

Suppose for contradiction that there exists an integer $n > 1$ such that $n \mid
 2^n - 1$. Let $p$ be the **smallest prime divisor** of $n$.

**Case 1: $p = 2$.** Then $2 \mid n$, and since $n \mid 2^n - 1$, we would need
$2 \mid 2^n - 1$. But $2^n - 1$ is odd, so $2 \nmid 2^n - 1$ — a contradiction.

**Case 2: $p$ is odd.** Since $p \mid n$ and $n \mid 2^n - 1$, we have $p \mid 2
^n - 1$, i.e.,
$$2^n \equiv 1 \pmod{p}.$$
Because $p$ is odd, $\gcd(2, p) = 1$, so $2$ has a well-defined multiplicative o
rder modulo $p$. Let $d = \operatorname{ord}_p(2)$, the smallest positive intege
r with $2^d \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$.

- Since $2^n \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$, we get $d \mid n$.
- By Fermat's little theorem, $2^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$, so $d \mid (p-1)$.

Therefore $d \mid \gcd(n,\, p-1)$.

Now we determine $\gcd(n, p-1)$:
- Every prime factor of $n$ is $\geq p$, since $p$ is the **smallest** prime fac
tor of $n$.
- Every prime factor of $p - 1$ is $\leq p - 1 < p$.

Thus $n$ and $p - 1$ share no common prime factor, so
$$\gcd(n,\, p-1) = 1.$$

Consequently $d \mid 1$, which forces $d = 1$. This means $2^1 \equiv 1 \pmod{p}
$, i.e., $p \mid 1$ — contradicting the fact that $p$ is prime.

Both cases lead to contradictions, so our assumption was false. Hence for every
integer $n > 1$,
$$n \nmid 2^n - 1. \qquad \blacksquare$$
