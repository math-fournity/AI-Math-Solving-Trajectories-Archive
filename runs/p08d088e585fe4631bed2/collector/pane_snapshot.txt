## Proof

**Claim:** There does **not** exist a metric $d$ on $\mathbb{R}$, topologically
equivalent to the standard Euclidean metric, such that $(\mathbb{Q}, d)$ is a co
mplete metric space.

**Step 1: Reduction to the subspace topology on $\mathbb{Q}$.**

Let $d$ be a metric on $\mathbb{R}$ equivalent (i.e., topologically equivalent)
to the Euclidean metric $|\cdot|$. This means $d$ and $|\cdot|$ induce the same
topology on $\mathbb{R}$. The restriction $d\!\restriction_{\mathbb{Q}}$ induces
 on $\mathbb{Q}$ the subspace topology inherited from $(\mathbb{R}, d)$, which —
 since the two metrics agree topologically on $\mathbb{R}$ — is exactly the same
 as the subspace topology inherited from $(\mathbb{R}, |\cdot|)$, i.e., the usua
l Euclidean topology on $\mathbb{Q}$.

Therefore, the question reduces to:

> Is $(\mathbb{Q}, \tau_{\text{Euc}})$ completely metrizable?

where $\tau_{\text{Euc}}$ is the Euclidean subspace topology on $\mathbb{Q}$.

**Step 2: $\mathbb{Q}$ has no isolated points.**

In the Euclidean topology, every open ball $B(q, \varepsilon) \cap \mathbb{Q}$ c
ontains infinitely many rationals distinct from $q$. Hence no singleton $\{q\}$
is open; equivalently, every singleton $\{q\}$ has empty interior in $\mathbb{Q}
$.

**Step 3: Baire category argument.**

Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists a complete metric $\rho$ on $\math
bb{Q}$ inducing $\tau_{\text{Euc}}$. Enumerate the rationals:
$$\mathbb{Q} = \{q_1, q_2, q_3, \ldots\}.$$

Each singleton $\{q_n\}$ is closed in $(\mathbb{Q}, \tau_{\text{Euc}})$ (singlet
ons are closed in any metric topology, and $\rho$ induces $\tau_{\text{Euc}}$).
By Step 2, each $\{q_n\}$ has empty interior, so each $\{q_n\}$ is **nowhere den
se** in $(\mathbb{Q}, \rho)$.

But then
$$\mathbb{Q} = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} \{q_n\}$$
expresses $\mathbb{Q}$ as a countable union of nowhere dense closed sets.

**Step 4: Contradiction via the Baire Category Theorem.**

The Baire Category Theorem states that in a **complete** metric space, the inter
section of countably many dense open sets is dense; equivalently, a complete met
ric space cannot be written as a countable union of nowhere dense sets.

Since $(\mathbb{Q}, \rho)$ is assumed complete, it cannot be a countable union o
f nowhere dense sets. This contradicts Step 3.

**Conclusion.**

The contradiction shows that no complete metric on $\mathbb{Q}$ can induce the E
uclidean subspace topology. Since any metric $d$ on $\mathbb{R}$ equivalent to t
he Euclidean metric restricts to a metric on $\mathbb{Q}$ inducing exactly that
topology, no such $d$ can make $(\mathbb{Q}, d)$ complete.

$$\boxed{\text{No such metric exists.}}$$
