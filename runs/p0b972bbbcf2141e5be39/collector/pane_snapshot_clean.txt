## Solution

**Answer: Yes, such a polynomial exists.** We claim that $P(x) = x^2 - a$ works
for any $a > 2$.

### Verification

Set $P(x) = x^2 - a$. We prove by induction on $n$ that for every $t \in (-a, a)
$, the equation $P_n(x) = t$ has $2^n$ distinct real roots, all lying in $(-a, a
)$.

**Base case ($n = 1$):** $P(x) = t$ gives $x^2 = t + a$, so $x = \pm\sqrt{t+a}$.

- Since $t > -a$, we have $t + a > 0$, so both roots are real and distinct.
- Since $t < a$, we have $t + a < 2a$, so $\sqrt{t+a} < \sqrt{2a}$. Because $a >
 2$, we have $a^2 > 2a$, hence $\sqrt{2a} < a$. Therefore both roots $\pm\sqrt{t
+a}$ lie in $(-a, a)$.

So $P_1(x) = t$ has $2 = 2^1$ distinct real roots in $(-a, a)$. ✓

**Inductive step:** Assume $P_n(x) = t$ has $2^n$ distinct real roots (all in $(
-a, a)$) for every $t \in (-a, a)$.

Consider $P_{n+1}(x) = t$, i.e., $P(P_n(x)) = t$. Solving $P(y) = t$ yields $y =
 r_1 := \sqrt{t+a}$ and $y = r_2 := -\sqrt{t+a}$. By the base-case analysis, bot
h $r_1, r_2 \in (-a, a)$ and $r_1 \neq r_2$.

Thus $P_{n+1}(x) = t$ iff $P_n(x) = r_1$ or $P_n(x) = r_2$.

- Since $r_1 \in (-a, a)$, by the induction hypothesis, $P_n(x) = r_1$ has $2^n$
 distinct real roots.
- Since $r_2 \in (-a, a)$, by the induction hypothesis, $P_n(x) = r_2$ has $2^n$
 distinct real roots.
- Since $r_1 \neq r_2$, the solution sets of $P_n(x) = r_1$ and $P_n(x) = r_2$ a
re disjoint.

Therefore $P_{n+1}(x) = t$ has $2^n + 2^n = 2^{n+1}$ distinct real roots. ✓

### Conclusion

By induction, for every $n \geq 1$ and every $t \in (-a, a)$, the equation $P_n(
x) = t$ has $2^n$ distinct real roots. In particular, for $n = 2024$, the equati
on $P_{2024}(x) = t$ has $2^{2024}$ distinct real roots for all $t \in (-a, a)$.

The key insight is that $a > 2$ ensures the interval $(-a, a)$ is mapped into it
self by the two preimages of $P(x) = x^2 - a$: the condition $\sqrt{2a} < a$ is
equivalent to $a > 2$, which guarantees that the "doubling" of roots at each ite
ration stays within the interval where the induction hypothesis applies.
