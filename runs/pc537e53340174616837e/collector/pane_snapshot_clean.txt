Yes. In fact, $V(t) = H^1(0,1)$ for every $t \in [0,T]$, so density holds trivia
lly.

**Proof.**

Fix $t \in [0,T]$. Since $\alpha : [0,T]\times[0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuou
s and $[0,T]\times[0,1]$ is compact, $\alpha$ is bounded; in particular $x \maps
to \alpha(t,x)$ is bounded on $[0,1]$. Denote $M_t := \sup_{x\in[0,1]}|\alpha(t,
x)| < \infty$.

**Step 1: $H^1(0,1) \subseteq V(t)$.**

Let $v \in H^1(0,1)$. Then $v \in L^2(0,1)$ and $v' \in L^2(0,1)$ (distributiona
l derivative). Since $|\alpha(t,x)| \le M_t$ and $v \in L^2$, we have $\alpha(t,
\cdot)v \in L^2(0,1)$ with
$$\|\alpha(t,\cdot)v\|_{L^2} \le M_t\|v\|_{L^2}.$$
Hence $v' + \alpha(t,\cdot)v \in L^2(0,1)$, being the sum of two $L^2$ functions
. Therefore $v \in V(t)$.

**Step 2: $V(t) \subseteq H^1(0,1)$.**

Let $v \in V(t)$. By definition $v \in L^2(0,1)$ and $w := v' + \alpha(t,\cdot)v
 \in L^2(0,1)$, where $v'$ denotes the distributional derivative. Since $v \in L
^2$ and $\alpha(t,\cdot)$ is bounded, $\alpha(t,\cdot)v \in L^2(0,1)$. Therefore
 the distributional derivative
$$v' = w - \alpha(t,\cdot)v$$
is the difference of two $L^2(0,1)$ functions, hence $v' \in L^2(0,1)$. Together
 with $v \in L^2(0,1)$, this gives $v \in H^1(0,1)$.

**Conclusion.**

Steps 1 and 2 give $V(t) = H^1(0,1)$ for every $t \in [0,T]$. Consequently $V(t)
$ is dense in $H^1(0,1)$ (with respect to the $H^1$-norm) — in fact it coincides
 with $H^1(0,1)$.

The key point is that the boundedness of $\alpha(t,\cdot)$ (guaranteed by contin
uity on the compact interval $[0,1]$) makes the zeroth-order perturbation $\alph
a v$ an $L^2$-valued multiplier, so the condition $v' + \alpha v \in L^2$ is equ
ivalent to $v' \in L^2$. $\square$
