# Solution

**Answer: Yes.** There exists a Banach space with no nontrivial complemented clo
sed subspaces (i.e., no decomposition $B = U \oplus V$ into two infinite-dimensi
onal closed subspaces). This is the celebrated **Gowers–Maurey space** (1993).

---

## Preliminary observation

First, note that the question must be interpreted as asking about *nontrivial* d
ecompositions where both $U$ and $V$ are infinite-dimensional. Indeed, every Ban
ach space $B$ admits finite-dimensional complemented subspaces: for any $x \neq
0$, the Hahn–Banach theorem yields $f \in B^*$ with $f(x)=1$, and $P(y) = f(y)x$
 is a bounded projection onto $\operatorname{span}(x)$ with complement $\ker f$.
 Likewise $U = \{0\}, V = B$ is a trivial decomposition. So the meaningful quest
ion concerns decompositions into two infinite-dimensional closed subspaces — equ
ivalently, whether an **indecomposable** Banach space exists.

---

## The Gowers–Maurey construction

### Definition of the space

Let $X$ be the completion of $c_{00}$ (the space of finitely supported real sequ
ences) under a norm $\|\cdot\|$ defined as follows. One constructs a countable c
ollection $\mathcal{F}$ of functionals on $c_{00}$ (each given by a finitely sup
ported sequence of reals) via an inductive procedure, and sets:

$$\|x\| = \max\Bigl\{\, |x(n)| : n \in \mathbb{N}\Bigr\} \;\vee\; \sup\bigl\{\,
|f(x)| : f \in \mathcal{F}\bigr\}.$$

The collection $\mathcal{F}$ is built inductively. At each stage, one adds funct
ionals of the form $f = \sum_i \lambda_i x_i^*$ where $(x_i)$ is a **special seq
uence** of normalized block vectors satisfying rapidly decreasing growth conditi
ons, and $(\lambda_i)$ are rapidly decreasing coefficients. The notion of "speci
al" is designed so that:

1. **Rapid growth**: consecutive blocks $x_{i+1}$ are much larger (in support) t
han $x_i$, ensuring approximate orthogonality.
2. **Coding**: the construction encodes enough information so that any attempted
 projection onto a subspace is forced to have large norm — specifically, any bou
nded projection must "see" the special structure and fail.

The precise inductive construction ensures $\mathcal{F}$ is closed under the ope
rations needed to make $\|\cdot\|$ a norm, and the resulting space $X$ is separa
ble and reflexive.

### Key property: rigidity of operators

The central theorem of Gowers–Maurey is:

> **Theorem (Gowers–Maurey, 1993).** *The space $X$ is **hereditarily indecompos
able** (HI): no closed subspace $Y \subseteq X$ can be written as $Y = Y_1 \oplu
s Y_2$ with $Y_1, Y_2$ both infinite-dimensional. Moreover, every bounded operat
or $T: X \to X$ has the form $T = \lambda I + S$ where $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$
and $S$ is strictly singular.*

**Sketch of why HI holds.** The proof proceeds by contradiction. Suppose $Y \sub
seteq X$ and $Y = Y_1 \oplus Y_2$ with $Y_1, Y_2$ infinite-dimensional. Then the
 projection $P: Y \to Y_1$ (with kernel $Y_2$) is a bounded operator. One can fi
nd normalized block sequences $(y_i) \subseteq Y_1$ and $(z_i) \subseteq Y_2$ th
at are "compatible" with the special structure of $\mathcal{F}$. The key estimat
e shows that for any such pair of sequences, there exist indices $i, j$ such tha
t $\|y_i + z_j\|$ is much smaller than $\max(\|y_i\|, \|z_j\|)$ — specifically,
the special functionals in $\mathcal{F}$ can be arranged to "align" with $y_i -
z_j$ (or $y_i + z_j$) and make the norm drop. This contradicts the boundedness o
f $P$, since a bounded projection onto $Y_1$ along $Y_2$ forces $\|y + z\| \geq
c \cdot \max(\|y\|,\|z\|)$ for $y \in Y_1, z \in Y_2$ (a lower $c$-estimate), bu
t the special functionals violate this.

More concretely, the coding in the construction ensures: given any two rapidly g
rowing block sequences $(x_i)$ and $(y_i)$, one can find a functional $f \in \ma
thcal{F}$ that "matches" $x_n - y_n$ for some $n$, giving $f(x_n - y_n) \approx
2$ while $\|x_n - y_n\|$ should be at least $2$ if the decomposition were topolo
gical — but the matching forces $\|x_n + y_n\|$ or $\|x_n - y_n\|$ to be small r
elative to what a bounded projection requires. This is the **obstruction to comp
lementation**.

### HI implies indecomposable (no nontrivial complemented subspaces)

If $X = U \oplus V$ with $U, V$ infinite-dimensional closed subspaces, then $X$
itself is decomposable, directly contradicting HI (since $X$ is a closed subspac
e of itself). Therefore:

> **$X$ has no decomposition $X = U \oplus V$ into two infinite-dimensional clos
ed subspaces.**

Equivalently, $X$ has no nontrivial complemented closed subspaces (where "nontri
vial" means infinite-dimensional and of infinite codimension).

### The operator rigidity connection

The fact that every $T \in \mathcal{L}(X)$ is $\lambda I + S$ (strictly singular
 $S$) also directly implies indecomposability: if $X = U \oplus V$ with projecti
on $P$ onto $U$, then $P = \lambda I + S$. Since $P^2 = P$, we get $(\lambda I +
 S)^2 = \lambda I + S$, which forces $\lambda \in \{0, 1\}$. If $\lambda = 0$, t
hen $P = S$ is strictly singular, but a projection onto an infinite-dimensional
subspace cannot be strictly singular (it acts as the identity on $U$). If $\lamb
da = 1$, then $I - P = -S$ is strictly singular, but $I - P$ acts as the identit
y on $V$, again a contradiction. So no such decomposition exists.

## Conclusion

The Gowers–Maurey space $X_{\mathrm{GM}}$ is a separable reflexive Banach space
that is hereditarily indecomposable. In particular, $X_{\mathrm{GM}}$ admits no
decomposition $X_{\mathrm{GM}} = U \oplus V$ into two infinite-dimensional close
d subspaces. Such a Banach space exists.
