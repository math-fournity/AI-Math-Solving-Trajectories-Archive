## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. For any locally compact abelian (LCA) group $G$, there exists a
$C^*$-algebra containing $L^1(G)$ as a (dense) $*$-subalgebra, even though $L^1(
G)$ is not itself a $C^*$-algebra.

### Part 1: $L^1(G)$ is not a $C^*$-algebra

$L^1(G)$ is a commutative Banach $*$-algebra under convolution, with involution
$f^*(x) = \overline{f(-x)}$ (since $G$ is abelian, the modular function is trivi
al). We show the $C^*$-identity $\|f^* * f\|_1 = \|f\|_1^2$ fails in general.

Take $G = \mathbb{Z}$ (a discrete LCA group), so $L^1(\mathbb{Z}) = \ell^1(\math
bb{Z})$. Let $f = \delta_0 + \delta_1 + i\,\delta_2$, so $\|f\|_1 = 3$ and $\|f\
|_1^2 = 9$.

Compute $f^*(n) = \overline{f(-n)}$: we get $f^*(0) = 1$, $f^*(-1) = 1$, $f^*(-2
) = -i$, and zero elsewhere.

Then $(f^* * f)(n) = \sum_k f^*(k)\,f(n-k)$:

- $(f^* * f)(0) = f^*(0)f(0) + f^*(-1)f(1) + f^*(-2)f(2) = 1 + 1 + (-i)(i) = 3$
- $(f^* * f)(1) = f^*(0)f(1) + f^*(-1)f(2) = i + i = 1 + i$
  (Wait: $f^*(0)f(1) = 1 \cdot i = i$; $f^*(-1)f(2) = 1 \cdot i = i$; so $(f^*{*
}f)(1) = 2i$.)

Let me recompute carefully:

- $(f^* * f)(0) = \underbrace{f^*(0)f(0)}_{1 \cdot 1} + \underbrace{f^*(-1)f(1)}
_{1 \cdot i} + \underbrace{f^*(-2)f(2)}_{(-i)\cdot i} = 1 + i + 1 = 2 + i$
- $(f^* * f)(1) = \underbrace{f^*(0)f(1)}_{1 \cdot i} + \underbrace{f^*(-1)f(2)}
_{1 \cdot i} + \underbrace{f^*(-2)f(3)}_{0} = 2i$
- $(f^* * f)(-1) = \underbrace{f^*(0)f(-1)}_{0} + \underbrace{f^*(-1)f(0)}_{1 \c
dot 1} + \underbrace{f^*(-2)f(1)}_{(-i)\cdot i} = 1 + 1 = 2$
- $(f^* * f)(2) = \underbrace{f^*(0)f(2)}_{1 \cdot i} + \underbrace{f^*(-1)f(3)}
_{0} + \underbrace{f^*(-2)f(4)}_{0} = i$
- $(f^* * f)(-2) = \underbrace{f^*(0)f(-2)}_{0} + \underbrace{f^*(-1)f(-1)}_{0}
+ \underbrace{f^*(-2)f(0)}_{(-i)\cdot 1} = -i$

All other values are zero. Therefore:

$$\|f^* * f\|_1 = |2+i| + |2i| + |2| + |i| + |-i| = \sqrt{5} + 2 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 6
 + \sqrt{5} \approx 8.24$$

Since $6 + \sqrt{5} < 9 = \|f\|_1^2$, the $C^*$-identity **fails**. Hence $L^1(G
)$ is not a $C^*$-algebra. $\square$

### Part 2: $L^1(G)$ embeds into a $C^*$-algebra

Let $\widehat{G}$ denote the Pontryagin dual of $G$ (the group of continuous cha
racters $\chi: G \to \mathbb{T}$), which is itself an LCA group. The space $C_0(
\widehat{G})$ of continuous functions on $\widehat{G}$ vanishing at infinity, eq
uipped with the supremum norm, pointwise multiplication, and complex conjugation
, is a **commutative $C^*$-algebra**.

Define the **Fourier transform** $\mathcal{F}: L^1(G) \to C_0(\widehat{G})$ by:

$$\hat{f}(\chi) = \int_G f(x)\,\chi(x)\,dx$$

where $dx$ is Haar measure on $G$.

**$\mathcal{F}$ is well-defined into $C_0(\widehat{G})$:** This is the Riemann–L
ebesgue lemma — $\hat{f}$ is continuous and vanishes at infinity on $\widehat{G}
$.

**$\mathcal{F}$ is an algebra homomorphism:** For $f, g \in L^1(G)$ and $\chi \i
n \widehat{G}$:

$$\widehat{f * g}(\chi) = \int_G (f * g)(x)\,\chi(x)\,dx = \int_G \int_G f(y)\,g
(x - y)\,dy\;\chi(x)\,dx = \hat{f}(\chi)\,\hat{g}(\chi)$$

by Fubini's theorem and the multiplicativity of characters $\chi(x) = \chi(y)\,\
chi(x-y)$.

**$\mathcal{F}$ preserves the involution:** For $f \in L^1(G)$:

$$\widehat{f^*}(\chi) = \int_G \overline{f(-x)}\,\chi(x)\,dx = \overline{\int_G
f(y)\,\overline{\chi(y)}\,dy} = \overline{\hat{f}(\chi)}$$

where we substituted $y = -x$ and used $\chi(-y) = \overline{\chi(y)}$ (since $\
chi$ is a unitary character).

**$\mathcal{F}$ is injective:** This is the uniqueness theorem for the Fourier t
ransform on LCA groups: if $\hat{f} = 0$, then $f = 0$ a.e. This follows from th
e fact that the characters of $G$ separate points and form a total set, or equiv
alently from the Plancherel/inversion theory: the Fourier transform extends to a
n isometry $L^2(G) \to L^2(\widehat{G})$ (Plancherel theorem), so $\hat{f} = 0$
implies $f = 0$.

**Conclusion:** $\mathcal{F}: L^1(G) \to C_0(\widehat{G})$ is an **injective $*$
-homomorphism** of Banach $*$-algebras. Therefore $\mathcal{F}(L^1(G))$ is a $*$
-subalgebra of the $C^*$-algebra $C_0(\widehat{G})$, isomorphic (as a $*$-algebr
a) to $L^1(G)$.

Moreover, $\mathcal{F}(L^1(G))$ is **dense** in $C_0(\widehat{G})$: it is a self
-adjoint ($*$-closed) subalgebra of $C_0(\widehat{G})$ that separates points of
$\widehat{G}$ and vanishes nowhere (for any $\chi_0 \in \widehat{G}$, one can fi
nd $f \in L^1(G)$ with $\hat{f}(\chi_0) \neq 0$). By the Stone–Weierstrass theor
em for locally compact spaces, $\mathcal{F}(L^1(G))$ is dense in $C_0(\widehat{G
})$. $\square$

### Final Statement

Combining Parts 1 and 2: **Yes**, it is possible to construct a $C^*$-algebra co
ntaining $L^1(G)$ as a subalgebra. Specifically, the commutative $C^*$-algebra $
C_0(\widehat{G})$ contains $\mathcal{F}(L^1(G)) \cong L^1(G)$ as a dense $*$-sub
algebra via the Fourier transform, even though $L^1(G)$ itself fails to be a $C^
*$-algebra because the $C^*$-identity $\|f^* * f\|_1 = \|f\|_1^2$ does not hold
under the $L^1$ norm.
