**Answer: No, the condition does not ensure convergence.**

**Counterexample.** Take $X = \mathbb{R}$ with the usual metric $d(x,y)=|x-y|$,
which is complete. Define
$$a_n = 1 + \frac{1}{n+1}, \qquad n \geq 1,$$
so $(a_n)$ is strictly decreasing with $a_n \to 1 > 0$. Define the sequence $(x_
n)_{n\geq 0}$ by $x_0 = 0$ and
$$x_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k+1} a_k, \qquad n \geq 1.$$

**The condition holds.** For every $n \geq 1$,
$$d(x_{n+1}, x_n) = |x_{n+1}-x_n| = a_{n+1} < a_n = |x_n - x_{n-1}| = d(x_n, x_{
n-1}),$$
since $(a_n)$ is strictly decreasing.

**The sequence does not converge.** A necessary condition for convergence of any
 sequence in a metric space is $d(x_{n+1},x_n)\to 0$. But here
$$d(x_{n+1},x_n) = a_{n+1} \to 1 \neq 0.$$
Hence $(x_n)$ is not Cauchy, and since $\mathbb{R}$ is complete, $(x_n)$ does no
t converge.

**Remark.** The condition $d(x_{n+1},x_n) < d(x_n,x_{n-1})$ only guarantees that
 the consecutive distances form a strictly decreasing sequence of nonnegative re
als. Such a sequence need not tend to $0$ — it may tend to a positive limit (as
above, $a_n\to 1$). Without the additional requirement $d(x_{n+1},x_n)\to 0$, th
e sequence need not be Cauchy, and completeness alone cannot force convergence.
Even adding $d(x_{n+1},x_n)\to 0$ would still be insufficient in general (a comp
lete metric space need not have the property that "contracting steps imply conve
rgence" without a uniform contraction factor); but the present counterexample al
ready refutes the claim as stated.
