**Key observation.** Over the real numbers, $A^T A$ is always positive semidefin
ite (its diagonal entries are sums of squares $\sum_k a_{ki}^2 \ge 0$), so $A^T
A = -I$ is impossible over $\mathbb{R}$. We must work over $\mathbb{C}$, and we
use the ordinary transpose $A^T$ (not the conjugate transpose $A^*$).

**Example.** Take the scalar matrix
$$
A = i\,I_n,
$$
where $i^2 = -1$. This is a well-known type of matrix (a scalar/identity-scaled
matrix).

**Verification.** Since $I_n$ is symmetric, $(iI_n)^T = iI_n = A$. Therefore
$$
A^T A = (iI_n)(iI_n) = i^2 I_n = (-1)I_n = -I_n.
$$

Thus $A = iI_n$ satisfies $A^T A = -I$ for every $n \ge 1$. $\blacksquare$
