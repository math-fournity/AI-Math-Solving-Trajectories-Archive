**Answer: Yes — $X$ is necessarily compact.**

We prove the contrapositive: if a metric space $X$ is **not** compact, then ther
e exists an unbounded continuous real-valued function on $X$.

Recall that for metric spaces, compact $\iff$ totally bounded $+$ complete. So i
f $X$ is not compact, at least one of these fails.

---

**Case A: $X$ is not totally bounded.**

There exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $X$ cannot be covered by finitely many
open balls of radius $\varepsilon$. Build a sequence $(x_n)$ inductively: pick $
x_1 \in X$ arbitrary; having chosen $x_1,\dots,x_k$, choose
$$x_{k+1} \in X \setminus \bigcup_{i=1}^{k} B(x_i,\varepsilon),$$
which is possible since finitely many $\varepsilon$-balls do not cover $X$. Then
 $d(x_i,x_j) \ge \varepsilon$ for all $i \ne j$.

Let $S = \{x_n : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$. Every point of $X$ has a neighborhood (of
radius $\varepsilon/2$) meeting $S$ in at most one point, so $S$ has no accumula
tion point in $X$. Hence $S$ is **closed** in $X$ and **discrete** as a subspace
. Define $g : S \to \mathbb{R}$ by $g(x_n) = n$; this is continuous since $S$ is
 discrete. Since $X$ is metric (hence normal), the **Tietze extension theorem**
extends $g$ to a continuous $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$. But $f(x_n) = n$, so $f$ is
unbounded.

**Case B: $X$ is totally bounded but not complete.**

Let $(x_n)$ be a Cauchy sequence in $X$ with no limit in $X$. The set $S_0 = \{x
_n\}$ is infinite (an eventually-constant sequence would converge). We claim $S_
0$ has no accumulation point in $X$: if $p \in X$ were an accumulation point, a
subsequence $x_{n_k} \to p$; since $(x_n)$ is Cauchy, the entire sequence would
then converge to $p$, contradicting the choice of $(x_n)$.

Thus $S_0$ is closed and discrete in $X$. Enumerate its distinct elements as $\{
y_n\}$, define $g(y_n) = n$ (continuous on the discrete closed set $S_0$), and e
xtend by Tietze to a continuous $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$. Again $f(y_n) = n$ is un
bounded.

---

In both cases, non-compactness of $X$ produces an unbounded continuous real-valu
ed function on $X$. Taking the contrapositive:

> **If every real-valued continuous function on a metric space $X$ is bounded, t
hen $X$ is compact.**
