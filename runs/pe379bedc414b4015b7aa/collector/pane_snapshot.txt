**Key observation:** A prime $p$ divides $\gcd(2^n-1, \ldots, m^n-1)$ only if $p

**Proof of $h(n) = n+1 \iff n+1$ is prime (for $n > 2$):**

**($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $n+1$ is prime.** Let $p = n+1$.

*GCD $> 1$ for all $m \leq n$:* For any $i \in \{2,\ldots,m\}$ with $m \leq n$,
since $i < p$ and $p$ is prime, $\gcd(i,p)=1$. By Fermat's little theorem, $i^{p
-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$, i.e., $i^n \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$. So $p \mid i^n - 1$ for a
ll such $i$, hence $p$ divides the GCD, which is thus $> 1$.

*GCD $= 1$ at $m = n+1$:* Now $i = p = n+1$ is included, and $p^n - 1 \equiv -1
\pmod{p}$, so $p$ no longer divides the GCD. Any other prime $q$ dividing the GC
D must satisfy $q > n+1$ (primes $\leq n$ are in the set; $p = n+1$ was just exc
luded). For such $q$, we need $q \mid i^n - 1$ for all $i \in \{2,\ldots,n+1\}$,
 meaning all these $n$ values are $n$-th roots of unity mod $q$. The $n$-th root
s of unity form a subgroup $H \subseteq (\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^*$ of order $\g
cd(n, q-1) \leq n$. But $H$ contains $1$ (trivially) and $\{2,\ldots,n+1\}$ (all
 distinct mod $q$ since $q > n+1$), giving $|H| \geq n+1 > n \geq \gcd(n,q-1)$,
a contradiction. So no such $q$ exists and the GCD is $1$.

Therefore $h(n) = n+1$.

**($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $h(n) = n+1$.** Then the GCD at $m = n$ is $> 1$ (mini

Since $p \mid p^n - 1$ is impossible ($p^n - 1 \equiv -1 \pmod{p}$), $p \notin \

For each $i \in \{2,\ldots,n\}$: $\gcd(i,p) = 1$ (since $i < p$) and $i^n \equiv
 1 \pmod{p}$, so $i$ is an $n$-th root of unity mod $p$. The $n$-th roots of uni

Now $H$ contains $\{1\} \cup \{2,\ldots,n\} = \{1,2,\ldots,n\}$ — these are $n$

**Sum of roots argument:** Since $n \mid p-1$, the polynomial $x^n - 1$ splits c

$$\sum_{\alpha \in H} \alpha \equiv \sum_{i=1}^{n} i = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} \pmod{p}

Therefore $n+1$ is prime. $\square$
