## Proof

**Claim:** $f$ must be constant (in fact, $f \equiv 0$).

**Step 1: $f(0) = 0$.**

Since $D$ is a region (open, connected) containing $0$, there exists $r > 0$ suc
h that the disk $|z| < r$ lies in $D$. Hence $\frac{1}{n} \in D$ for all suffici
ently large $n$. By hypothesis,
$$\left|f\!\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right)\right| \leq \frac{1}{2^n} \xrightarrow{n\to
\infty} 0.$$
Since $f$ is analytic (hence continuous) and $\frac{1}{n} \to 0$,
$$f(0) = \lim_{n\to\infty} f\!\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right) = 0.$$

**Step 2: All Taylor coefficients of $f$ at $0$ vanish.**

Suppose for contradiction that $f$ is not identically zero. Since $f(0)=0$, the
Taylor expansion of $f$ about $0$ has a first nonzero term:
$$f(z) = a_k z^k + a_{k+1} z^{k+1} + \cdots, \qquad a_k \neq 0, \quad k \geq 1.$
$
Factor out $z^k$:
$$f(z) = z^k\bigl(a_k + a_{k+1}z + a_{k+2}z^2 + \cdots\bigr) = z^k \, h(z),$$
where $h$ is analytic near $0$ and $h(0) = a_k \neq 0$. By continuity of $h$, th
ere exists $N$ such that for all $n \geq N$,
$$|h(\tfrac{1}{n})| \geq \frac{|a_k|}{2} > 0.$$
Therefore, for all $n \geq N$,
$$\left|f\!\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right)\right| = \frac{1}{n^k}\left|h\!\left(\tfrac
{1}{n}\right)\right| \geq \frac{|a_k|}{2\, n^k}.$$

**Step 3: Deriving the contradiction.**

Combining the lower bound with the hypothesis $\left|f\!\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right
)\right| \leq \frac{1}{2^n}$:
$$\frac{|a_k|}{2\, n^k} \leq \frac{1}{2^n} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |a_k| \leq
\frac{2\, n^k}{2^n} \qquad \text{for all } n \geq N.$$

But $\frac{n^k}{2^n} \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$ (exponential decay dominates polyn
omial growth). Taking $n \to \infty$:
$$|a_k| \leq \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{2\, n^k}{2^n} = 0,$$
contradicting $a_k \neq 0$.

**Conclusion.**

The assumption that $f$ is not identically zero leads to a contradiction. Hence
$f \equiv 0$, which is constant. The zero function indeed satisfies all the hypo
theses ($|0| \leq 2^{-n}$ for every $n$), so the conclusion is consistent.

Therefore, **$f$ must be constant.** $\blacksquare$
