## Proof

**Claim:** For any submultiplicative matrix norm $\lVert \cdot \rVert$ on $n \ti
mes n$ matrices ($n \geq 1$), and any matrix $M$ with $\det M = 1$, we have $\lV
ert M \rVert \geq 1$.

The proof rests on two standard facts, which I establish first.

**Fact 1 (Spectral radius bound).** *For any submultiplicative matrix norm $\lVe
rt \cdot \rVert$ and any square matrix $A$, the spectral radius $\rho(A)$ satisf
ies $\rho(A) \leq \lVert A \rVert$.*

*Proof of Fact 1.* Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of $A$ with $|\lambda| = \rho(
A)$, and let $x \neq 0$ be a corresponding eigenvector, so $Ax = \lambda x$. Cho
ose a vector $y$ with $y^{\top} x = 1$ (possible since $x \neq 0$), and set $B :
= x y^{\top} \neq 0$. Then
$$
AB = A(x y^{\top}) = (Ax) y^{\top} = (\lambda x) y^{\top} = \lambda \, x y^{\top
} = \lambda B.
$$
By the absolute homogeneity of any norm, $\lVert AB \rVert = \lVert \lambda B \r
Vert = |\lambda| \, \lVert B \rVert$. By submultiplicativity, $\lVert AB \rVert
\leq \lVert A \rVert \, \lVert B \rVert$. Since $B \neq 0$, we have $\lVert B \r
Vert > 0$, so dividing yields
$$
|\lambda| \leq \lVert A \rVert.
$$
Taking the maximum over all eigenvalues gives $\rho(A) \leq \lVert A \rVert$. $\
square$

**Fact 2 (Determinant bound).** *For any submultiplicative matrix norm on $n \ti
mes n$ matrices, $|\det A| \leq \lVert A \rVert^{n}$.*

*Proof of Fact 2.* The determinant equals the product of eigenvalues (counted wi
th algebraic multiplicity): $\det A = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i$. Hence
$$
|\det A| = \prod_{i=1}^{n} |\lambda_i| \leq \prod_{i=1}^{n} \rho(A) = \rho(A)^{n
} \leq \lVert A \rVert^{n},
$$
where the last step uses Fact 1. $\square$

**Main proof.** Let $M$ be an $n \times n$ matrix with $\det M = 1$. Applying Fa
ct 2 with $A = M$:
$$
1 = |\det M| \leq \lVert M \rVert^{n}.
$$
Since $n \geq 1$ and $\lVert M \rVert \geq 0$, taking $n$-th roots gives
$$
\lVert M \rVert \geq 1.
$$

**Conclusion.** The inequality $\lVert M \rVert \geq 1$ holds for **all** matric
es $M$ with determinant $1$, for every submultiplicative matrix norm. The answer
 is **yes**.
