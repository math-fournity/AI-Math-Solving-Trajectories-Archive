## Proof

**Setup.** We work over the finite field $\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$
. The polynomial $f(x)$ has all coefficients in $\{0, 1, \dots, p-1\}$ (i.e., in
 $\mathbb{F}_p$) and degree at most $p$. The Uniqueness Condition states:

$$\forall\, m, n \in \{0,\dots,p-1\}:\quad p \mid \big(f(m)-f(n)\big) \;\Longrig
htarrow\; p \mid (m-n).$$

Since $m, n \in \{0,\dots,p-1\}$, the condition $p \mid (m-n)$ forces $m = n$. T
hus the condition is equivalent to: **$f$ is injective on $\mathbb{F}_p$**, henc
e (by finiteness) **$f$ is a permutation of $\mathbb{F}_p$**.

---

**Upper bound.** The degree of $f$ is stipulated to not exceed $p$, so $\deg f \
leq p$.

---

**Achievability — a permutation polynomial of degree exactly $p$.**

Consider the polynomial:
$$f(x) = x^p.$$

- **Coefficients:** The leading coefficient is $1 \in \{0,\dots,p-1\}$ (valid si
nce $p \geq 2$); all other coefficients are $0$. ✓
- **Degree:** $\deg f = p \leq p$. ✓
- **Permutation property:** By **Fermat's Little Theorem**, for every $x \in \{0
, 1, \dots, p-1\}$:
$$x^p \equiv x \pmod{p}.$$
Thus $f$ acts as the **identity map** on $\mathbb{F}_p$, which is trivially a pe
rmutation.
- **Uniqueness Condition:** For any $m, n \in \{0,\dots,p-1\}$, if $p \mid (f(m)
-f(n))$, then $p \mid (m - n)$ since $f(m) \equiv m$ and $f(n) \equiv n \pmod{p}
$. ✓

---

**Verification for small primes.**

- $p = 2$: $f(x) = x^2$ gives $f(0)=0,\; f(1)=1$. Permutation, degree $2 = p$. ✓

- $p = 3$: $f(x) = x^3$ gives $f(0)=0,\; f(1)=1,\; f(2)=8\equiv 2$. Permutation,
 degree $3 = p$. ✓
- $p = 5$: $f(x) = x^5$ gives $f(0)=0,\; f(1)=1,\; f(2)=32\equiv 2,\; f(3)=243\e
quiv 3,\; f(4)=1024\equiv 4$. Permutation, degree $5 = p$. ✓

**Why degree $p$ is the answer and not less.** One might wonder whether the answ
er should be smaller (e.g., $p-2$, the classical maximum degree of a permutation
 polynomial in its *reduced* form of degree $\leq p-1$). Indeed, a classical res
ult shows that no permutation polynomial over $\mathbb{F}_p$ has degree exactly
$p-1$ in its reduced form: if $f(x) = a_{p-1}x^{p-1} + \cdots + a_0$ is a permut
ation, then $\sum_{x \in \mathbb{F}_p} f(x) = \sum_{x \in \mathbb{F}_p} x = 0$,
but $\sum_{x \in \mathbb{F}_p} f(x) = -a_{p-1}$ (using $\sum_{x \in \mathbb{F}_p
} x^k = 0$ for $1 \leq k \leq p-2$ and $\sum_{x \in \mathbb{F}_p} x^{p-1} = -1$)
, forcing $a_{p-1} = 0$ — a contradiction. This bounds the *reduced* degree at $
p-2$.

However, the problem permits degree up to $p$ (not $p-1$), and the polynomial $x
^p$ — while acting as the identity on $\mathbb{F}_p$ (equivalent to a degree-1 r
educed polynomial) — has **formal degree $p$**, which satisfies all stated const
raints. The degree bound in the problem is on the formal degree of the polynomia
l, not its reduced degree modulo $x^p - x$.
