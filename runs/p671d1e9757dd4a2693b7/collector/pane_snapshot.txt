Looking at this problem, I need to determine the largest integer $n$ for which $
C^n$ approximate isometric embeddings of $\mathbb{H}^2$ into $\mathbb{R}^3$ exis
t for all $\epsilon > 0$.

**Answer: $n = 1$**

## Proof

### Part 1: $C^1$ approximate embeddings exist ($n \geq 1$ works)

By the **Nash–Kuiper theorem** (1954–55), any $n$-dimensional Riemannian manifol
d admitting a short embedding into $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ admits a $C^1$ isometric e
mbedding into $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$. For surfaces ($n=2$), the target is $\mathbb{R
}^3$.

A short embedding of $\mathbb{H}^2$ into $\mathbb{R}^3$ exists: for instance, on
e may take a smooth embedding and scale it down sufficiently so the induced metr
ic is dominated by the hyperbolic metric. The Nash–Kuiper theorem (extended to c
omplete non-compact manifolds via exhaustion by compact sets, since the corrugat
ion/convex-integration technique is local) then yields a **$C^1$ isometric embed
ding** $f: \mathbb{H}^2 \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^3$, i.e., $f^*g_{\text{euc}}
= g_{\mathbb{H}^2}$ exactly.

An exact isometric embedding is trivially an $\epsilon$-approximate embedding fo
r every $\epsilon > 0$. Hence $n = 1$ is achievable.

### Part 2: $C^2$ approximate embeddings fail ($n \geq 2$ impossible)

We show no $C^2$ approximate isometric embedding exists for sufficiently small $
\epsilon$, using **Efimov's theorem** (1963), which generalizes Hilbert's theore
m (1901):

> **Efimov's Theorem.** There is no complete $C^2$ immersed surface in $\mathbb{
R}^3$ whose Gaussian curvature satisfies $K \leq -c$ for some constant $c > 0$ e
verywhere.

Suppose for contradiction that $C^2$ approximate isometric embeddings exist for
all $\epsilon > 0$. Let $f_\epsilon: \mathbb{H}^2 \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^3$
be $C^2$ with induced metric $g_\epsilon = f_\epsilon^* g_{\text{euc}}$, satisfy
ing
$$|d_{g_\epsilon}(p,q) - d_{\mathbb{H}^2}(p,q)| < \epsilon \quad \text{for all }
 p, q \in \mathbb{H}^2.$$

**Step 2a: The surface is complete.** Since $d_{g_\epsilon}$ is $\epsilon$-close
 to $d_{\mathbb{H}^2}$ and $(\mathbb{H}^2, d_{\mathbb{H}^2})$ is complete, any $
d_{g_\epsilon}$-Cauchy sequence is $d_{\mathbb{H}^2}$-Cauchy (up to $2\epsilon$)
, hence converges in $d_{\mathbb{H}^2}$, and therefore in $d_{g_\epsilon}$. So $
(\mathbb{H}^2, g_\epsilon)$ is complete.

**Step 2b: The metric converges uniformly to $g_{\mathbb{H}^2}$.** For any $p \i
n \mathbb{H}^2$ and any unit vector $v \in T_p\mathbb{H}^2$ (in $g_{\mathbb{H}^2
}$), let $\gamma(t) = \exp_p^{\mathbb{H}^2}(tv)$. For small $t$, $d_{\mathbb{H}^
2}(p, \gamma(t)) = t$ and $d_{g_\epsilon}(p, \gamma(t)) = t\sqrt{g_\epsilon(v,v)
} + O(t^2)$ (the $g_\epsilon$-geodesic from $p$ to $\gamma(t)$ is close to $\gam
ma|_{[0,t]}$ for small $t$). The distance condition gives $|t\sqrt{g_\epsilon(v,
v)} - t| \leq \epsilon + O(t^2)$. Choosing $t = \sqrt{\epsilon}$ yields $|\sqrt{
g_\epsilon(v,v)} - 1| = O(\sqrt{\epsilon})$, uniformly over $p$ and $v$. Hence $
\|g_\epsilon - g_{\mathbb{H}^2}\|_{C^0} \to 0$.

**Step 2c: The Gaussian curvature converges uniformly to $-1$.** Let $K_\epsilon
(p)$ be the Gaussian curvature of $(\mathbb{H}^2, g_\epsilon)$ at $p$. The circu
mference of a $g_\epsilon$-geodesic circle of radius $r$ at $p$ satisfies:
$$C_{g_\epsilon}(p, r) = 2\pi r - \frac{\pi K_\epsilon(p)}{3} r^3 + O(r^5).$$
The hyperbolic geodesic sphere $S_{\mathbb{H}^2}(p, r)$ has $\mathbb{H}^2$-circu
mference $2\pi \sinh r = 2\pi r + \frac{\pi r^3}{3} + O(r^5)$. Since $d_{g_\epsi
lon}$ is $\epsilon$-close to $d_{\mathbb{H}^2}$, the surface $S_{\mathbb{H}^2}(p
,r)$ lies in the $g_\epsilon$-annulus of radii $[r-\epsilon, r+\epsilon]$, so it
s $g_\epsilon$-length equals $C_{g_\epsilon}(p,r)(1 + O(\epsilon/r))$ by the fir
st variation formula. Since $g_\epsilon$ is $O(\delta_\epsilon)$-close to $g_{\m
athbb{H}^2}$ (with $\delta_\epsilon \to 0$), the $g_\epsilon$-length of $S_{\mat
hbb{H}^2}(p,r)$ also equals $(2\pi r + \frac{\pi r^3}{3} + O(r^5))(1 + O(\delta_
\epsilon))$. Equating:
$$2\pi r - \frac{\pi K_\epsilon(p)}{3} r^3 + O(r^5) = \left(2\pi r + \frac{\pi r
^3}{3} + O(r^5)\right)(1 + O(\delta_\epsilon) + O(\epsilon/r)).$$
Solving for $K_\epsilon(p)$:
$$K_\epsilon(p) = -1 + O(r^2) + O(\delta_\epsilon / r^2) + O(\epsilon / r^3).$$
Choosing $r = \epsilon^{1/6}$ (so $r^2 = \epsilon^{1/3}$, $\delta_\epsilon/r^2 =
 O(\epsilon^{1/6})$, $\epsilon/r^3 = \epsilon^{1/2}$), all error terms vanish as
 $\epsilon \to 0$, uniformly in $p$. Thus $K_\epsilon \to -1$ uniformly.

**Step 2d: Contradiction via Efimov's theorem.** For $\epsilon$ sufficiently sma
ll, $K_\epsilon(p) \leq -\frac{1}{2}$ for all $p \in \mathbb{H}^2$. The surface
$f_\epsilon(\mathbb{H}^2)$ is a complete $C^2$ surface in $\mathbb{R}^3$ with Ga
ussian curvature $\leq -1/2 < 0$ everywhere. This contradicts Efimov's theorem.

### Conclusion

- $n = 1$: $C^1$ approximate isometric embeddings exist for all $\epsilon > 0$ (
Nash–Kuiper gives exact isometry).
- $n = 2$: impossible for small $\epsilon$ (Efimov's theorem), hence $n \geq 2$
is also impossible since $C^n \subset C^2$.

The largest integer $n$ is $\boxed{1}$.
