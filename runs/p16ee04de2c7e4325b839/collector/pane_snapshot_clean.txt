## Proof

Assume $C \neq \emptyset$ (the degenerate empty case is excluded by convention).
 We show that $f$ has **at least one** minimizer and **at most one** minimizer;
together these give exactly one.

### Step 1. Uniform continuity implies continuity.

A function that is uniformly continuous on $C$ is, in particular, continuous on
$C$: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ the $\delta$ furnished by uniform continuity wo
rks at every point simultaneously, hence at each point individually. Thus $f : C
 \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous.

### Step 2. $f$ attains its minimum on $C$ (at least one minimizer).

The norm on the ambient finite-dimensional vector space induces a metric, making
 $C$ a compact metric space (compactness is given; finite-dimensionality guarant
ees the norm topology is the usual Euclidean-type topology, but compactness itse
lf is already assumed). A continuous real-valued function on a compact topologic
al space attains its infimum and supremum — this is the extreme value theorem, v
alid for any compact space. Concretely, let
$$m := \inf_{x \in C} f(x) > -\infty$$
(the infimum is finite since $f$ is real-valued and continuous on the compact se
t $C$, hence bounded). Take a minimizing sequence $(x_n) \subset C$ with $f(x_n)
 \to m$. By compactness, a subsequence converges to some $x^* \in C$; by continu
ity, $f(x^*) = m$. Hence $x^*$ is a minimizer.

### Step 3. Strict convexity gives at most one minimizer.

Suppose for contradiction that $x, y \in C$ are two distinct minimizers, $x \neq
 y$, with $f(x) = f(y) = m$. Since $C$ is convex, the midpoint $z := \tfrac{x+y}
{2}$ belongs to $C$. By strict convexity,
$$f(z) = f\!\left(\tfrac{x+y}{2}\right) < \tfrac{f(x) + f(y)}{2} = \tfrac{m + m}
{2} = m.$$
This contradicts the definition of $m$ as the infimum of $f$ on $C$. Therefore t
wo distinct minimizers cannot coexist.

### Step 4. Conclusion.

From Step 2 there exists at least one minimizer, and from Step 3 there is at mos
t one. Hence $f$ has **exactly one** minimizer on $C$.

**Remark.** The uniform-continuity hypothesis is stronger than necessary: ordina
ry continuity on $C$ already suffices (and on a compact domain, continuity even
implies uniform continuity by the Heine–Cantor theorem, so the two notions coinc
ide here). The essential ingredients are compactness of $C$ (for existence) and
strict convexity (for uniqueness).
