## Proof

**Key observation.** Since $\cos x - 1 \leq 0$ for all $x$ and $x^2 \geq 0$, the
 numerator $(\cos x - 1)\,x^2 \leq 0$. The denominator $x^p + (x+1)^6$ is strict
ly positive for all $x > 0$ (both terms are positive for $x > 0$). Therefore the
 integrand $f(x) \leq 0$ on $(0,\infty)$, and the integral converges to a finite
 value if and only if

$$\int_0^{+\infty} \frac{(1-\cos x)\,x^2}{x^p + (x+1)^6}\,dx < +\infty,$$

i.e., if and only if it is absolutely convergent. (A non-positive integrand eith
er integrates to a finite negative number or to $-\infty$; there is no condition
al convergence.)

---

### Step 1: Reduction via periodic averaging

Set $h(x) = 1 - \cos x$ and $g(x) = \frac{x^2}{x^p + (x+1)^6}$. Then $h \geq 0$,
 is periodic with period $T = 2\pi$, and $\int_0^T h(x)\,dx = 2\pi > 0$.

The function $g$ is positive on $(0,\infty)$. For large $x$, $g(x) \leq x^{-4}$
(shown below), so $g(x) \to 0$ and $g$ is eventually monotone decreasing.

**Lemma (periodic comparison).** If $h \geq 0$ is periodic with period $T$ and $
\int_0^T h > 0$, and $g > 0$ is eventually monotone decreasing to $0$, then $\in
t_0^\infty h(x)\,g(x)\,dx$ converges if and only if $\int_0^\infty g(x)\,dx$ con
verges.

*Proof of lemma.* For $k$ large enough that $g$ is decreasing on $[kT, (k+1)T]$:
$$g((k+1)T)\int_{kT}^{(k+1)T} h \;\leq\; \int_{kT}^{(k+1)T} h(x)\,g(x)\,dx \;\le
q\; g(kT)\int_{kT}^{(k+1)T} h.$$
Setting $H = \int_0^T h > 0$ and summing:
$$H\sum_k g((k+1)T) \;\leq\; \int_0^\infty h\,g \;\leq\; H\sum_k g(kT).$$
By the integral test for monotone functions, both sums converge if and only if $
\int_0^\infty g$ converges. $\square$

Therefore, it suffices to determine when $\int_0^{+\infty} g(x)\,dx = \int_0^{+\
infty} \frac{x^2}{x^p + (x+1)^6}\,dx$ converges.

---

### Step 2: Convergence of $\int_0^{+\infty} \frac{x^2}{x^p + (x+1)^6}\,dx$ for
all $p$

**Near $x = 0$:** For $x \in (0, 1]$, since $x^p \geq 0$ (for $x > 0$) and $(x+1
)^6 \geq 1$:
$$0 \leq g(x) = \frac{x^2}{x^p + (x+1)^6} \leq \frac{x^2}{(x+1)^6} \leq x^2.$$
Since $\int_0^1 x^2\,dx = \frac{1}{3} < \infty$, the integral converges near $0$
 for every $p \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Near $x = +\infty$:** For $x \geq 1$, again using $x^p \geq 0$:
$$0 \leq g(x) = \frac{x^2}{x^p + (x+1)^6} \leq \frac{x^2}{(x+1)^6} \leq \frac{x^
2}{x^6} = \frac{1}{x^4}.$$
Since $\int_1^{+\infty} x^{-4}\,dx = \frac{1}{3} < \infty$, the integral converg
es near $+\infty$ for every $p \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Conclusion of Step 2:** $\int_0^{+\infty} g(x)\,dx$ converges for **all** $p \
in \mathbb{R}$, independently of $p$.

---

### Step 3: Combining

By Step 1, the original integral converges if and only if $\int_0^\infty g$ conv
erges. By Step 2, $\int_0^\infty g$ converges for all $p \in \mathbb{R}$. Theref
ore:

$$\boxed{\text{The integral converges for all } p \in \mathbb{R}.}$$

---

**Remark.** The term $(x+1)^6$ in the denominator provides a uniform lower bound
 $(x+1)^6 \geq x^6$ that makes $g(x) \leq x^{-4}$ for large $x$, regardless of $
p$. The contribution of $x^p$ (whether it grows, decays, or oscillates in domina
nce) is absorbed by this bound since $x^p \geq 0$ only adds to the denominator.
The non-positivity of $\cos x - 1$ eliminates any conditional-convergence subtle
ty: the integral either converges absolutely or diverges to $-\infty$, and the b
ound $g(x) \leq x^{-4}$ (for $x \geq 1$) and $g(x) \leq x^2$ (for $x \leq 1$) gu
arantees absolute convergence for every real $p$.
