Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether a subspace $U \subset H$ on
 which every element of an infinite-dimensional unital C*-algebra $A \subset B(H
)$ acts as a scalar must be at most one-dimensional.

**Answer: No.** The conclusion does not hold. Here is a counterexample.

---

**Construction.** Let $X = [0,1]$ and let $A = C([0,1])$, which is an infinite-d
imensional unital commutative C*-algebra. Set
$$H = \mathbb{C}^2 \oplus L^2([0,1], m),$$
where $m$ is Lebesgue measure. Define a representation $\pi: C([0,1]) \to B(H)$
by
$$\pi(f) = f(0)\, I_{\mathbb{C}^2} \;\oplus\; M_f,$$
where $M_f$ denotes the multiplication operator $g \mapsto fg$ on $L^2([0,1],m)$
.

**$\pi$ is faithful (so $A \cong \pi(A) \subset B(H)$).** If $\pi(f) = 0$, then
$f(0)\, I_{\mathbb{C}^2} = 0$ (so $f(0)=0$) and $M_f = 0$ (so $f = 0$ $m$-a.e.).
 Since $f$ is continuous and vanishes a.e. on $[0,1]$, we get $f \equiv 0$. Henc
e $\pi$ is injective, and $\pi(A)$ is a C*-subalgebra of $B(H)$ isomorphic to $C
([0,1])$.

**$\pi(A)$ is unital and infinite-dimensional.** The unit is $\pi(1) = I_{\mathb
b{C}^2} \oplus I_{L^2} = I_H$, and $\dim C([0,1]) = \infty$.

**The subspace $U$.** Let $U = \mathbb{C}^2 \oplus \{0\} \subset H$, which is **
2-dimensional**.

**Every element of $\pi(A)$ acts as a scalar on $U$.** For any $f \in C([0,1])$
and any $u = (v, 0) \in U$ with $v \in \mathbb{C}^2$:
$$\pi(f)\, u = \bigl(f(0)\, I_{\mathbb{C}^2}\, v,\; 0\bigr) = f(0)\, u.$$
So $\pi(f)|_U = f(0) \cdot \mathrm{id}_U$, which is scalar (the scalar $\lambda
= f(0)$ depends on $f$, as required).

**Conclusion.** We have produced an infinite-dimensional unital C*-algebra $A \s
ubset B(H)$ and a **2-dimensional** subspace $U \subset H$ such that every $f \i
n A$ restricts to a scalar on $U$. Therefore, we **cannot** conclude that $U$ is
 either zero or one-dimensional.

**Remark.** The key mechanism is that the character $\mathrm{ev}_0: C([0,1]) \to
 \mathbb{C}$ (evaluation at $0$) appears in the representation with **multiplici
ty** $\dim \mathbb{C}^2 = 2$. The "atomic part" of the spectral measure at the p
oint $0$ has rank $2$, giving a 2-dimensional eigenspace. The faithful (non-dege
nerate) part of the representation is carried by the $L^2$ summand, which ensure
s injectivity without constraining the multiplicity of the atomic part. One can
similarly construct examples where $U$ has any finite or even infinite dimension
 by replacing $\mathbb{C}^2$ with $\mathbb{C}^n$ or an infinite-dimensional Hilb
ert space.
