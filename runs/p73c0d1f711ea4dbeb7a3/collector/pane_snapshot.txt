# The Mandelbrot Set is Uncountably Infinite

## Definitions

The **Mandelbrot set** is
$$M = \{c \in \mathbb{C} : \text{the orbit } 0, c, c^2+c, (c^2+c)^2+c, \ldots \t
ext{ under } f_c(z)=z^2+c \text{ is bounded}\}.$$

We show $M$ is **uncountable** (has the cardinality of the continuum), and likew
ise its fractal boundary $\partial M$ is uncountable.

---

## Step 1 — $M$ contains the *main cardioid*, an open set

Define the **main cardioid** as the set of parameters $c$ for which $f_c$ has an
 attracting fixed point:
$$\mathcal{C} = \{c \in \mathbb{C} : \exists\, z^*\text{ with } f_c(z^*)=z^*,\ |
f_c'(z^*)|<1\}.$$

A fixed point satisfies $z^* = (z^*)^2 + c$, i.e. $c = z^* - (z^*)^2$. It is att
racting iff $|f_c'(z^*)|=|2z^*|<1$, i.e. $|z^*|<\tfrac12$. So
$$\mathcal{C} = \varphi\!\left(\{z^* : |z^*|<\tfrac12\}\right), \qquad \varphi(z
^*) = z^* - (z^*)^2.$$

The map $\varphi$ is holomorphic with $\varphi'(z^*) = 1 - 2z^*$, which is **non
vanishing** on $|z^*|<\tfrac12$ (since $|2z^*|<1$). By the **open mapping theore
m**, $\mathcal{C}$ is an **open** subset of $\mathbb{C}$. It is nonempty ($\varp
hi(0)=0 \in \mathcal{C}$).

---

## Step 2 — Every point of $\mathcal{C}$ lies in $M$

Let $c \in \mathcal{C}$, with attracting fixed point $z^*$ ($|z^*|<\tfrac12$). W
e claim the critical point $0$ of $f_c$ lies in the basin of attraction of $z^*$
, so the orbit of $0$ converges to $z^*$ and is bounded.

*Proof of claim.* The immediate basin $B$ of $z^*$ (the connected component of t
he basin containing $z^*$) is open and simply connected (standard for polynomial
s). If the unique critical point $0$ were **not** in $B$, then $f_c\big|_B : B \
to B$ would be a proper holomorphic self-map with no critical point, hence a con
formal automorphism of the simply connected proper domain $B$ — a Möbius map. Bu
t $f_c$ is a polynomial of degree $2$, and a Möbius map that is a polynomial mus
t be affine (degree $1$), a contradiction. Hence $0 \in B$, and the orbit of $0$
 converges to $z^*$. $\square$

Therefore $\mathcal{C} \subseteq M$.

---

## Step 3 — A nonempty open subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$ is uncountable

$\mathcal{C}$ is a nonempty open subset of $\mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{R}^2$, so i
t contains an open ball $B(w_0, r)$ with $r>0$. This ball contains the line segm
ent
$$\{w_0 + t\,v : t \in (0, r/2)\}$$
for any unit vector $v$, which is in bijection with the real interval $(0, r/2)$
. Since every nondegenerate real interval is **uncountable** (cardinality $2^{\a
leph_0}$), the ball — and hence $\mathcal{C}$ — is uncountable.

---

## Step 4 — Conclusion for $M$

Since $\mathcal{C} \subseteq M$ and $\mathcal{C}$ is uncountable,
$$\boxed{|M| = 2^{\aleph_0}, \quad \text{i.e., } M \text{ is uncountably infinit
e.}}$$

---

## Step 5 — The fractal *boundary* $\partial M$ is also uncountable

The boundary $\partial M$ is closed (boundary of any set is closed). It is a **p
erfect set**: it has no isolated points. Indeed, $M$ is the closure of its inter
ior (the interior includes $\mathcal{C}$ and all hyperbolic components, which ar
e dense in $M$ in the sense that every boundary point is a limit of interior poi
nts), and $\mathbb{C}\setminus M$ is open, so every neighborhood of a boundary p
oint meets both $\operatorname{int}(M)$ and $\mathbb{C}\setminus M$, hence conta
ins other boundary points.

By the **Cantor–Bendixson theorem**, every closed subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ decom
poses as $P \cup C$ where $P$ is perfect and $C$ is countable. A nonempty perfec
t subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is uncountable (one embeds a copy of the Cantor set v
ia a binary tree of nested closed balls). Since $\partial M$ is nonempty and per
fect,
$$|\partial M| = 2^{\aleph_0}.$$

(This is consistent with Shishikura's theorem that $\partial M$ has Hausdorff di
mension $2$, which independently forces uncountability.)

---

## Summary

| Set | Cardinality |
|---|---|
| Mandelbrot set $M$ | $2^{\aleph_0}$ (uncountable) |
| Boundary $\partial M$ (the fractal) | $2^{\aleph_0}$ (uncountable) |

The infinity of the Mandelbrot fractal — whether one refers to the full set $M$
or to its fractal boundary $\partial M$ — is **uncountable**.
