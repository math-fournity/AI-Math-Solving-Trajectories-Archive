## Solution

**The answer is NO.** A commutative ring that is Cohen-Macaulay and satisfies $\
dim A_{\mathfrak m}=\dim A$ for every maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$ need not be re
gular. We provide an explicit counterexample.

### Counterexample

Let $k$ be a field and set
$$A = k[x,y,z]/(xy - z^2).$$

We verify that $A$ satisfies all the hypotheses but is not regular.

---

**Step 1: $A$ is a domain and $\dim A = 2$.**

The polynomial $f = xy - z^2$ is irreducible in the UFD $k[x,y,z]$: it is a non-
degenerate quadratic form of rank $3$, which cannot factor as a product of two l
inear forms (any such factorization would yield a form of rank $\le 2$). Hence $
(f)$ is a prime ideal, so $A$ is an integral domain. Since $k[x,y,z]$ has dimens
ion $3$ and $(f)$ is a prime of height $1$, we have
$$\dim A = 3 - 1 = 2.$$

---

**Step 2: $A$ is Cohen-Macaulay.**

The polynomial ring $k[x,y,z]$ is regular, hence Cohen-Macaulay. The element $f
= xy - z^2$ is a non-zero-divisor (non-zero, non-unit) in the domain $k[x,y,z]$.
 A standard theorem states that the quotient of a Cohen-Macaulay ring by a non-z
ero-divisor is again Cohen-Macaulay. Therefore $A$ is Cohen-Macaulay. (Equivalen
tly, $A$ is a hypersurface, and all hypersurfaces are Cohen-Macaulay.)

---

**Step 3: For every maximal ideal $\mathfrak m \subset A$, $\dim A_{\mathfrak m}
 = \dim A = 2$.**

Every maximal ideal of $A$ corresponds to a maximal ideal $\mathfrak M \subset k
[x,y,z]$ with $f \in \mathfrak M$. Since $k[x,y,z]$ is a finitely generated alge
bra over a field, it is catenary and equidimensional: every maximal ideal has he
ight $3$. The prime $(f)$ has height $1$ and is contained in $\mathfrak M$. By t
he dimension formula in catenary rings,
$$\operatorname{ht}_A(\mathfrak m) = \operatorname{ht}_{k[x,y,z]}(\mathfrak M) -
 \operatorname{ht}_{k[x,y,z]}((f)) = 3 - 1 = 2.$$
Since $\dim A_{\mathfrak m} = \operatorname{ht}_A(\mathfrak m)$, we conclude
$$\dim A_{\mathfrak m} = 2 = \dim A \quad \text{for every maximal ideal } \mathf
rak m.$$

---

**Step 4: $A$ is not regular.**

Consider the maximal ideal $\mathfrak m = (x, y, z) \subset A$, corresponding to
 the origin. The localization $A_{\mathfrak m}$ is a local ring with maximal ide
al $\mathfrak m A_{\mathfrak m}$. Its embedding dimension is
$$\operatorname{edim}(A_{\mathfrak m}) = \dim_k\!\left(\mathfrak m A_{\mathfrak
m} / \mathfrak m^2 A_{\mathfrak m}\right).$$
Lifting to $k[x,y,z]$: the maximal ideal $(x,y,z)$ satisfies $\dim_k (x,y,z)/(x,
y,z)^2 = 3$, and the relation $f = xy - z^2$ lies in $(x,y,z)^2$, so it contribu
tes no linear term and does not reduce the embedding dimension. Hence
$$\operatorname{edim}(A_{\mathfrak m}) = 3.$$
But $\dim A_{\mathfrak m} = 2$. A regular local ring must satisfy $\operatorname
{edim} = \dim$. Since $3 \neq 2$, the local ring $A_{\mathfrak m}$ is **not** re
gular.

Therefore $A$ is not a regular ring.

---

### Conclusion

The ring $A = k[x,y,z]/(xy - z^2)$ is:
- **Cohen-Macaulay** (hypersurface in a regular ring),
- **Equidimensional in the strong sense**: $\dim A_{\mathfrak m} = \dim A = 2$ f
or every maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$,
- **Not regular** (the local ring at the origin has embedding dimension $3 > 2 =
 \dim$).

This provides a counterexample, proving that the two stated conditions do **not*
* imply regularity.
