Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether $n$ injective, linearly ind
ependent linear maps $f_i: V \to W$ over an infinite field always admit some $v
\in V$ making $f_1(v), \ldots, f_n(v)$ linearly independent.

**Answer: Yes, such a vector $v$ necessarily exists.**

Let me present the proof.

---

**Proof.**

Let $m = \dim V$ and $p = \dim W$. Since each $f_i$ is injective, $p \geq m$. Th
e hypothesis $n \leq \min(\dim W, \dim V)$ gives $n \leq m$.

**The bad set.** Define
$$B = \{v \in V : f_1(v), \ldots, f_n(v) \text{ are linearly dependent in } W\}.
$$
We show $B \subsetneq V$, which gives the desired $v$.

**$B$ is Zariski closed.** The vectors $f_1(v), \ldots, f_n(v)$ are linearly dep
endent iff $f_1(v) \wedge \cdots \wedge f_n(v) = 0$ in $\bigwedge^n W$. Choosing
 bases for $V$ and $W$, the $p \times n$ matrix $[f_1(v) \mid \cdots \mid f_n(v)
]$ has entries linear in the coordinates $x_1, \ldots, x_m$ of $v$. The coordina
tes of the wedge product are the $n \times n$ minors of this matrix—homogeneous
degree-$n$ polynomials in $x_1, \ldots, x_m$. Thus $B$ is the common zero locus
of these minors, a Zariski-closed subset of $V \cong \mathbb{A}^m$.

**Incidence variety and dimension bound.** Consider
$$\Sigma = \{(v, [c]) \in V \times \mathbb{P}^{n-1} : (c_1 f_1 + \cdots + c_n f_
n)(v) = 0\},$$
a closed subvariety of $V \times \mathbb{P}^{n-1}$ (cut out by $p$ bihomogeneous
 equations, linear in each factor). Let $\pi_1: \Sigma \to V$ and $\pi_2: \Sigma
 \to \mathbb{P}^{n-1}$ be the projections.

*Claim:* $\pi_1(\Sigma) = B$. Indeed, $v \in \pi_1(\Sigma)$ iff there exists $[c
] \in \mathbb{P}^{n-1}$ with $\sum c_i f_i(v) = 0$, iff $f_1(v), \ldots, f_n(v)$
 are linearly dependent, iff $v \in B$.

*Fiber of $\pi_2$:* The fiber over $[c]$ is $\ker(c_1 f_1 + \cdots + c_n f_n) \t
imes \{[c]\}$. Since $f_1, \ldots, f_n$ are linearly independent, $c_1 f_1 + \cd
ots + c_n f_n \neq 0$ for every $[c] \in \mathbb{P}^{n-1}$, so each fiber is a p
roper subspace of $V$ (containing at least the origin).

*Generic fiber dimension is zero:* At $[c] = [1:0:\cdots:0]$, the fiber is $\ker
(f_1) = \{0\}$ since $f_1$ is injective—this is zero-dimensional. By upper semic
ontinuity of fiber dimension, the generic fiber has dimension $\leq 0$; since ev
ery fiber is nonempty (containing $(0,[c])$), the generic fiber dimension is exa
ctly $0$.

*Dimension of $\Sigma$:* By the dimension formula for projections,
$$\dim \Sigma = \dim \mathbb{P}^{n-1} + \dim(\text{generic fiber of } \pi_2) = (
n-1) + 0 = n-1.$$

**$B$ is proper.** Since $\dim B = \dim \pi_1(\Sigma) \leq \dim \Sigma = n-1$ an
d $n \leq m = \dim V$:
$$\dim B \leq n - 1 < n \leq m = \dim V.$$
Thus $B \subsetneq V$ (as varieties over $\bar{k}$). The key step uses injectivi
ty of $f_1$: without it, the fiber over $[1:0:\cdots:0]$ could be positive-dimen
sional, and the dimension bound would fail.

**Finding a $k$-rational point outside $B$.** Since $B \subsetneq V$, the polyno
mial $P(v) = f_1(v) \wedge \cdots \wedge f_n(v)$ is not identically zero. So at
least one $n \times n$ minor $M(x_1, \ldots, x_m) \in k[x_1, \ldots, x_m]$ is a
nonzero polynomial. Since $k$ is infinite, a nonzero polynomial cannot vanish on
 all of $k^m$. Hence there exists $(a_1, \ldots, a_m) \in k^m$ with $M(a_1, \ldo
ts, a_m) \neq 0$, giving $v = a_1 e_1 + \cdots + a_m e_m \in V$ with
$$f_1(v) \wedge \cdots \wedge f_n(v) \neq 0.$$

Therefore $f_1(v), \ldots, f_n(v)$ are linearly independent in $W$. $\blacksquar
e$

**Remark on the role of injectivity.** The injectivity hypothesis is essential.
For example, with $V = W = k^2$, $n = 2$, $f_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pma
trix}$, $f_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$: these are linearly indepen
dent but not injective, and $f_1(v) = (a,0)$, $f_2(v) = (b,0)$ are always linear
ly dependent (both lie in $\operatorname{span}(e_1)$), so no such $v$ exists. He
re $\ker(f_1) = \operatorname{span}(e_2)$ is 1-dimensional, making $\dim \Sigma
= 2 = \dim V$, and the dimension argument cannot exclude $B = V$.
