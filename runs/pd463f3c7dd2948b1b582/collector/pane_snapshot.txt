Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether $\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1
f_n(x)\sin(nx)\,dx$ must exist given only $f_n\to f$ a.e. with $f\in L^2[0,1]$.

**Answer: The limit does not necessarily exist.**

**Proof by counterexample.**

Let $f(x)=0$ for all $x\in[0,1]$. Define
$$
f_n(x)=\begin{cases} n\sin(nx), & x\in[0,\,1/n]\text{ and }n\text{ is even},\\[4
pt] 0, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
$$

**Verification of hypotheses.**

*Each $f_n\in L^2[0,1]$:* For odd $n$, $f_n\equiv 0$. For even $n$,
$$
\int_0^1 f_n(x)^2\,dx = n^2\int_0^{1/n}\sin^2(nx)\,dx = n^2\cdot\frac{1}{n}\int_
0^1\sin^2(u)\,du = n\cdot c < \infty,
$$
where $c=\int_0^1\sin^2(u)\,du=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\sin 2}{4}<\infty$. So every $f
_n\in L^2$.

*$f_n\to f$ a.e.:* For any $x>0$, once $n>1/x$ we have $1/n<x$, so $f_n(x)=0$ fo
r all sufficiently large $n$. At $x=0$, $f_n(0)=n\sin(0)=0$. Thus $f_n(x)\to 0$
for **every** $x\in[0,1]$, hence a.e.

*$f\in L^2[0,1]$:* Trivially, $f\equiv 0\in L^2$.

**The integral does not converge.**

For **even** $n$: substituting $u=nx$,
$$
\int_0^1 f_n(x)\sin(nx)\,dx = n\int_0^{1/n}\sin^2(nx)\,dx = \int_0^1\sin^2(u)\,d
u = \frac{1}{2}-\frac{\sin 2}{4}.
$$
Since $\sin 2<1<2$, we have $\frac{\sin 2}{4}<\frac{1}{4}<\frac{1}{2}$, so this
is a **positive constant** $c_0:=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\sin 2}{4}>0$.

For **odd** $n$: $f_n\equiv 0$, so
$$
\int_0^1 f_n(x)\sin(nx)\,dx = 0.
$$

Therefore the sequence $\left\{\int_0^1 f_n(x)\sin(nx)\,dx\right\}$ equals
$$
0,\; c_0,\; 0,\; c_0,\; 0,\; c_0,\;\ldots
$$
which oscillates between $0$ and the positive constant $c_0$, and hence **does n
ot converge**.

**Remark.** The failure is due to the lack of $L^2$-norm convergence (or even bo
undedness) of $\{f_n\}$. The a.e. convergence alone is too weak to control the i
ntegral against the highly oscillatory weight $\sin(nx)$. One can even make the
integral diverge to $+\infty$ by taking $f_n(x)=n^{3/2}\sin(nx)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{
[0,1/n]}$ (for all $n$), which still satisfies $f_n\to 0$ a.e. and $f_n\in L^2$,
 but gives $\int_0^1 f_n\sin(nx)\,dx = c_0\sqrt{n}\to\infty$.
