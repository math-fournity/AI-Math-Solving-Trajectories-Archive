## Proof

We use the trigonometric identity

$$\sin^2(nx) = \frac{1 - \cos(2nx)}{2}.$$

Therefore

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}} g(x)\sin^2(nx)\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}} g(x)\,dx -
 \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}} g(x)\cos(2nx)\,dx.$$

The first term equals $\frac{1}{2}\cdot 1 = \frac{1}{2}$ by hypothesis.

For the second term, since $g \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, the **Riemann–Lebesgue lemma
** applies: for any $g \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ and any $\xi \in \mathbb{R}$,

$$\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}} g(x)\cos(\xi x)\,dx\right| \le \left|\widehat{g}(\xi)\
right| \to 0 \quad \text{as } |\xi| \to \infty,$$

where $\widehat{g}(\xi) = \int g(x)e^{-i\xi x}\,dx$ is the Fourier transform of
$g$, which vanishes at infinity by the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma. Taking $\xi = 2n
\to \infty$,

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}} g(x)\cos(2nx)\,dx \to 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$

Hence

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}} g(x)\sin^2(nx)\,dx = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}
{2}\cdot 0 = \frac{1}{2}.$$
