## Solution

We seek an asymptotic equivalent of $f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n^x e^{-nx}$ as
$x \to 0^+$.

### Step 1: Reduce the sum to an integral

Set $h(t) = t^x e^{-tx} = e^{x\ln t - tx}$. Its derivative is
$$h'(t) = h(t)\cdot x\!\left(\tfrac{1}{t}-1\right),$$
so $h$ is **decreasing on $[1,\infty)$** (the maximum over $t\ge 1$ is attained
at $t=1$). For a positive decreasing function, the standard integral comparison
gives
$$\int_1^{\infty} h(t)\,dt \;\le\; \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} h(n) \;\le\; h(1) + \int_
1^{\infty} h(t)\,dt.$$
Since $h(1)=e^{-x}\to 1$, we obtain
$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n^x e^{-nx} = \int_1^{\infty} t^x e^{-tx}\,dt + O(1). \tag
{1}$$

### Step 2: Evaluate the integral asymptotically

Substitute $u = tx$ (so $t = u/x$, $dt = du/x$):
$$\int_1^{\infty} t^x e^{-tx}\,dt = \frac{1}{x^{x+1}}\int_x^{\infty} u^x e^{-u}\
,du. \tag{2}$$

**Factor $x^{x+1}$.** Since $x^{x+1} = x\cdot e^{x\ln x}$ and $x\ln x \to 0$ as
$x\to 0^+$, we have
$$x^{x+1} \sim x. \tag{3}$$

**The incomplete integral $\int_x^{\infty} u^x e^{-u}\,du \to 1$.** Split at $u=
1$:
- On $[1,\infty)$: $u^x \le u$ for $0 < x \le 1$, and $u\,e^{-u}$ is integrable,
 so by dominated convergence $\int_1^{\infty} u^x e^{-u}\,du \to \int_1^{\infty}
 e^{-u}\,du = e^{-1}$.
- On $[x,1]$: for $u\in[x,1]$, $|x\ln u|\le |x\ln x|\to 0$, so $u^x = e^{x\ln u}
\to 1$ **uniformly**, giving $\int_x^1 u^x e^{-u}\,du \to \int_0^1 e^{-u}\,du =
1-e^{-1}$.

Adding both parts:
$$\int_x^{\infty} u^x e^{-u}\,du \;\longrightarrow\; (1-e^{-1})+e^{-1} = 1. \tag
{4}$$

Combining (2), (3), (4):
$$\int_1^{\infty} t^x e^{-tx}\,dt \;\sim\; \frac{1}{x}. \tag{5}$$

### Step 3: Conclude

From (1) and (5):
$$f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n^x e^{-nx} = \frac{1}{x} + O(1).$$
Since $\frac{1}{x}\to +\infty$ while the $O(1)$ remainder stays bounded, the rem
ainder is negligible:

$$\boxed{\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n^x e^{-nx} \;\sim\; \frac{1}{x}} \qq
uad (x\to 0^+).$$

### Remark on the hint

The dominant contribution to the sum comes from indices $n$ of order $1/x$. For
such $n$, $x\ln n \sim x\ln(1/x)\to 0$, hence $n^x = e^{x\ln n}\to 1$, and the s
eries behaves like $\sum e^{-nx}\sim 1/x$. The hint "$n\ln n \sim n$" encodes th
e idea that $n^x \approx e^{xn/n}$ is controlled by a quantity of order $x$, con
sistent with $n^x\to 1$ in the dominant range — so the $n^x$ factor does not cha
nge the leading order $\frac{1}{x}$ obtained from the pure exponential $\sum e^{
-nx}$.
