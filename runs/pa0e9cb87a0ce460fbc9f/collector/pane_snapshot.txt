## Proof

**Answer: No.** The fact that a solution is radial and decreasing in $r$ does **
not**, by itself, imply that $u(0)$ is finite. The finiteness of $u$ at the orig
in in the Gidas–Ni–Nirenberg (GNN) theorem is a consequence of the **regularity
hypothesis** of the theorem, not of the radial-decreasing conclusion.

### Justification

**1. What the GNN Theorem 1 actually assumes.**

Theorem 1 of Gidas–Ni–Nirenberg concerns a **classical** positive solution $u \i
n C^{2}(\Omega) \cap C(\overline{\Omega})$ of
$$-\Delta u = f(u), \quad u > 0 \text{ in } \Omega = B_{R}, \quad u = 0 \text{ o
n } \partial B_{R},$$
with $f$ locally Lipschitz. The hypothesis $u \in C^{2}(B_{R}) \cap C(\overline{
B_{R}})$ **already guarantees** that $u$ is finite and continuous at every point
 of $\overline{B_{R}}$, including the origin $r = 0$. The conclusion of the theo
rem — that $u$ is radial about the origin and $u_{r} < 0$ for $r \in (0, R)$ — i
s **derived from** these hypotheses via the method of moving planes. The radial
symmetry and monotonicity are *consequences*, not *assumptions that enforce regu
larity*.

**2. The radial-decreasing property alone does not force boundedness at the orig
in.**

A function can be radial, strictly decreasing in $r$, positive, and still blow u
p at $r = 0$. The structural properties "radial" and "decreasing" place no upper
 bound on $u(0)$; they only state that $u(r) \leq u(0)$ for $r > 0$, which is pe
rfectly compatible with $u(0) = +\infty$ (the inequality is then trivially satis
fied for every finite $u(r)$).

**3. Explicit counterexamples: singular radial decreasing solutions.**

Consider the Lane–Emden equation
$$-\Delta u = u^{p}, \quad u > 0.$$
In dimension $n \geq 3$, for **supercritical** exponent $p > \frac{n+2}{n-2}$, t
here exist **singular radial solutions** that are positive, radial, strictly dec
reasing, and blow up at the origin. Concretely, the ansatz $u(r) = c\, r^{-\alph
a}$ with $\alpha = \frac{2}{p-1}$ and $c = \bigl[\alpha(n-2-\alpha)\bigr]^{1/(p-
1)}$ (valid when $0 < \alpha < n-2$, i.e., $p > \frac{n}{n-2}$) gives a solution
$$u(r) = c\, r^{-2/(p-1)},$$
which satisfies $-\Delta u = u^{p}$, is radial, strictly decreasing ($u'(r) = -\
frac{2c}{p-1}\, r^{-2/(p-1)-1} < 0$), and
$$\lim_{r \to 0^{+}} u(r) = +\infty.$$
Such singular solutions are well documented (e.g., in the work of Joseph–Lundgre
n and subsequent literature on singular semilinear elliptic equations). They are
 precisely the solutions **excluded** from the GNN theorem — not by the symmetry
 conclusion, but by the regularity hypothesis $u \in C^{2}(B_{R})$.

**4. Conclusion.**

The finiteness of $u(0)$ in the setting of GNN Theorem 1 is guaranteed by the **
assumed regularity** ($u \in C^{2} \cap C$, classical positive solution), which
is an input to the theorem. The radial symmetry and monotonicity $u_{r} < 0$ are
 **outputs** derived under that regularity. The output properties (radial + decr
easing) do **not** logically imply the input property (boundedness at the origin
), and indeed singular radial decreasing solutions of $-\Delta u = f(u)$ provide
 concrete counterexamples showing that "radial and decreasing" is compatible wit
h $u(0) = +\infty$.

Therefore, **the radial-decreasing conclusion of GNN Theorem 1 does not by itsel
f imply that $u$ cannot take an infinite value at $r = 0$**; it is the regularit
y hypothesis of the theorem that rules out such a singularity.
