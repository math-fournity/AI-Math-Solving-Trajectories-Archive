Looking at this problem, I need to find $M(n,S)$, the minimum possible second-la
rgest subset sum, given that the largest is $S$.

## Setup and Key Observations

Let $a_1, \dots, a_n$ be nonzero reals summing to $0$. The $2^n - 1$ nonempty su
bset sums are symmetric about $0$: for any proper nonempty subset $A$, its compl
ement has sum $-s(A)$, and the full set has sum $0$.

**Claim:** The subset $A$ achieving the maximum sum $S$ consists entirely of pos
itive numbers, and $A^c$ consists entirely of negative numbers.

*Proof:* If some $a_j \in A$ were negative, then $A \setminus \{j\}$ would have
sum $S - a_j > S$, contradicting maximality. Similarly, if some $a_j \notin A$ w
ere positive, then $A \cup \{j\}$ would have sum $S + a_j > S$. Since all number
s are nonzero, elements of $A$ are $> 0$ and elements of $A^c$ are $< 0$.

## Lower Bound

Let $k = |A|$, so $|A^c| = n - k$. Two specific subset sums constrain the second
-largest value $s_2$:

1. **Removing the smallest element of $A$:** $s_2 \geq S - \min_{j \in A} a_j$.
Since $\min_{j \in A} a_j \leq S/k$ (average), we get $s_2 \geq S(k-1)/k$.

2. **Adding the largest element of $A^c$:** $s_2 \geq S + \max_{j \notin A} a_j$
. Since $\max_{j \notin A} a_j \geq -S/(n-k)$ (average), we get $s_2 \geq S(n-k-
1)/(n-k)$.

Therefore: $$s_2 \geq \max\!\left(\frac{S(k-1)}{k},\; \frac{S(n-k-1)}{n-k}\right
).$$

## Achievability (Upper Bound)

Take $k$ elements equal to $S/k$ and $n-k$ elements equal to $-S/(n-k)$. Every s
ubset sum equals $\frac{iS}{k} - \frac{jS}{n-k}$ for $0 \leq i \leq k$, $0 \leq
j \leq n-k$, not both zero.

- The maximum is $(i,j) = (k,0)$, giving $S$.
- For $j = 0$, $i \neq k$: the largest is $(k{-}1, 0) = S(k{-}1)/k$.
- For $j \geq 1$: the largest is $(k, 1) = S - S/(n{-}k) = S(n{-}k{-}1)/(n{-}k)$
.

So the second-largest equals exactly $\max\!\left(\frac{S(k-1)}{k}, \frac{S(n-k-
1)}{n-k}\right)$, matching the lower bound.

## Optimizing over $k$

Setting $f(k) = 1 - 1/k$ (increasing) and $g(k) = 1 - 1/(n-k)$ (decreasing), we
minimize $\max(f(k), g(k))$ by balancing:

- **Even $n$:** $k = n/2$ gives $M(n,S) = S \cdot \frac{n-2}{n}$.
- **Odd $n$:** $k = \frac{n \pm 1}{2}$ gives $M(n,S) = S \cdot \frac{n-1}{n+1}$.

## Verification

- $n=4$ (even): $M/S = 2/4 = 1/2$. Construction: $\{S/2, S/2, -S/2, -S/2\}$. ✓
- $n=5$ (odd): $M/S = 4/6 = 2/3$. Construction: $\{S/3, S/3, S/3, -S/2, -S/2\}$.
