## Variance of the Number of Cycles in a Permutation of $\underline{n}$

**Setup.** Let $\sigma$ be a uniformly random permutation of $\underline{n} = \{
1,2,\ldots,n\}$, and let $C_n$ denote the number of cycles of $\sigma$. We compu
te $\operatorname{Var}(C_n)$.

---

### Step 1. A convenient decomposition

Write the permutation in its **canonical cycle notation**: in each cycle, place
the largest element first, and order the cycles by increasing first (i.e., large
st) element. For example, $(3)(2\,1\,4)(5)$ for $n=5$.

Define the indicator random variable
$$
Z_i = \mathbf{1}\{\text{element } i \text{ is the largest in its cycle}\}, \qqua
d 1 \le i \le n.
$$
In the canonical notation, $i$ is the largest in its cycle iff it appears as a c
ycle-leader (first element of a cycle). Every cycle has exactly one leader, so
$$
C_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} Z_i.
$$

---

### Step 2. Distribution and independence of the $Z_i$

We use the **Chinese restaurant process** (Feller's coupling). Build a random pe
rmutation by inserting elements $1, 2, \ldots, n$ sequentially. When element $i$
 is inserted, it either

- starts a new cycle (probability $\tfrac{1}{i}$), or
- is inserted into one of the $i-1$ existing positions among elements $1,\ldots,
i-1$ (each with probability $\tfrac{1}{i}$).

Crucially, the choice of whether $i$ starts a new cycle is **independent** of al
l previous choices (it depends only on a fresh uniform draw). Therefore:

- $Z_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{i}\right)$, i.e. $\Pr(Z_i
= 1) = \tfrac{1}{i}$,
- $Z_1, Z_2, \ldots, Z_n$ are **mutually independent**.

(One way to see the independence concretely: the Chinese restaurant process cons
tructs a uniformly random permutation, and the "new cycle" indicators are indepe
ndent coin flips with success probability $1/i$ at step $i$.)

---

### Step 3. Mean

$$
\mathbb{E}[C_n] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{E}[Z_i] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{i} =
H_n,
$$
the $n$-th harmonic number.

---

### Step 4. Variance

Since the $Z_i$ are independent,
$$
\operatorname{Var}(C_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \operatorname{Var}(Z_i).
$$
For a Bernoulli$(p)$ variable, $\operatorname{Var} = p(1-p)$, so
$$
\operatorname{Var}(Z_i) = \frac{1}{i}\left(1 - \frac{1}{i}\right) = \frac{1}{i}
- \frac{1}{i^2}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\operatorname{Var}(C_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{i} - \frac{1}{i^2}\right)
 = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{i} - \sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{i^2} = H_n - H_n^{(2)},
$$
where $H_n = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{i}$ and $H_n^{(2)} = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{i^
2}$ are the harmonic and second-order harmonic numbers.

---

### Result

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(C_n) \;=\; H_n \;-\; H_n^{(2)} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n}\fr
ac{1}{i} \;-\; \sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{i^2}.}
$$

Asymptotically, since $H_n \sim \ln n + \gamma$ and $H_n^{(2)} \to \frac{\pi^2}{
6}$, we have $\operatorname{Var}(C_n) \sim \ln n + \gamma - \frac{\pi^2}{6}$, co
nfirming that the number of cycles concentrates around its mean $\ln n$.
