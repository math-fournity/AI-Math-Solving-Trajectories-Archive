## Proof

**Setup and parametrization.** Let each line $l_i$ be parametrized as $A_i = B_i
 + t_i v_i$, where $B_i$ is a fixed point on $l_i$ and $v_i$ its direction vecto
r. The condition "no three lines parallel to the same plane" means no three of $
v_1, v_2, v_3, v_4$ lie in a common 2-dimensional subspace, i.e., **any three di
rection vectors are linearly independent**.

**Parallelogram condition.** Four points $A_1, A_2, A_3, A_4$ form a parallelogr
am if and only if some pairing of the four indices into two pairs gives opposite
 vertices whose midpoints coincide. There are exactly three pairings:
$$\Pi_1 = (12)(34),\quad \Pi_2 = (13)(24),\quad \Pi_3 = (14)(23).$$
For pairing $\Pi_1$, the condition is $A_1 + A_2 = A_3 + A_4$ (the center $C = \
frac{A_1+A_2}{2} = \frac{A_3+A_4}{2}$); similarly for the others.

**Each pairing yields a line of centers.** Consider pairing $\Pi_1 = (12)(34)$.
The condition $A_1 + A_2 = A_3 + A_4$ becomes:
$$t_1 v_1 + t_2 v_2 - t_3 v_3 - t_4 v_4 = B_3 + B_4 - B_1 - B_2.$$
This is a linear system $M\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{b}$ where $M$ is the $3\times 4$
matrix with columns $v_1, v_2, -v_3, -v_4$. Since any three of $v_1, v_2, v_3, v
_4$ are linearly independent, $M$ has **rank 3**, so its column space is all of
$\mathbb{R}^3$; the system is always consistent, and the solution set is a **1-d
imensional affine subspace** of $\mathbb{R}^4$ (parametrized by a single paramet
er $s$).

The center is $C(s) = \frac{1}{2}(B_1 + B_2 + t_1(s)\,v_1 + t_2(s)\,v_2)$, an af
fine function of $s$. To confirm $C$ traces a genuine line (not a point), let $(
n_1, n_2, n_3, n_4)$ be a nonzero null vector of $M$: $n_1 v_1 + n_2 v_2 = n_3 v
_3 + n_4 v_4$. The direction of $C$ is $\frac{1}{2}(n_1 v_1 + n_2 v_2)$. If this
 were zero, then $n_1 v_1 + n_2 v_2 = 0$, forcing $n_1 = n_2 = 0$ (since $v_1, v
_2$ are not parallel, being skew). Then $n_3 v_3 + n_4 v_4 = 0$ forces $n_3 = n_
4 = 0$, contradicting $(n_1,\dots,n_4) \neq 0$. So $C$ traces a **bona fide line
** $\mathcal{L}_1$.

By identical arguments, pairings $\Pi_2$ and $\Pi_3$ yield lines $\mathcal{L}_2$
 and $\mathcal{L}_3$ of centers.

**The three lines are distinct.** Suppose $\mathcal{L}_1$ and $\mathcal{L}_2$ sh
are a point. Then there exist parameters giving a center $C$ satisfying **both**
 $A_1 + A_2 = A_3 + A_4$ and $A_1 + A_3 = A_2 + A_4$. Subtracting: $A_2 - A_3 =
A_3 - A_2$, so $A_2 = A_3$. But $A_2 \in l_2$ and $A_3 \in l_3$, and $l_2, l_3$
are skew (hence disjoint), so $A_2 \neq A_3$—contradiction. Thus $\mathcal{L}_1
\cap \mathcal{L}_2 = \emptyset$, so $\mathcal{L}_1 \neq \mathcal{L}_2$ (identica
l lines intersect everywhere).

Similarly:
- $\mathcal{L}_1 \cap \mathcal{L}_3 = \emptyset$: both conditions give $A_2 = A_
3$... wait, let me recheck. $\Pi_1$: $A_1+A_2=A_3+A_4$; $\Pi_3$: $A_1+A_4=A_2+A_
3$. Subtracting: $A_2 - A_4 = A_4 - A_2$, so $A_2 = A_4$, impossible since $l_2,
 l_4$ are skew.
- $\mathcal{L}_2 \cap \mathcal{L}_3 = \emptyset$: $\Pi_2$: $A_1+A_3=A_2+A_4$; $\
Pi_3$: $A_1+A_4=A_2+A_3$. Subtracting: $A_3 - A_4 = A_4 - A_3$, so $A_3 = A_4$,
impossible since $l_3, l_4$ are skew.

All three lines are pairwise disjoint, hence pairwise distinct.

**Conclusion.** The centers of all parallelograms formed by planes cutting the f
our skew lines trace out exactly **3** lines.
