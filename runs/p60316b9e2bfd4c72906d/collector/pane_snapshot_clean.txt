# Proof: The Only Normed Division Algebra over ℂ is ℂ Itself

**Setup.** Let $A$ be a finite-dimensional normed division algebra over $\mathbb
{C}$. This means $A$ is a finite-dimensional $\mathbb{C}$-algebra equipped with
a nondegenerate quadratic form $N: A \to \mathbb{C}$ satisfying the multiplicati
vity condition
$$N(xy) = N(x)\,N(y) \quad \text{for all } x, y \in A,$$
and $N(x) = 0 \iff x = 0$. We prove $A \cong \mathbb{C}$.

---

**Step 1: $A$ has no zero divisors.**

Suppose $xy = 0$ with $x \neq 0$. Then $N(x) \neq 0$ (by nondegeneracy) and
$$0 = N(0) = N(xy) = N(x)\,N(y).$$
Since $N(x) \neq 0$, we get $N(y) = 0$, hence $y = 0$. So $A$ is an integral dom
ain (has no zero divisors).

---

**Step 2: $A$ has a multiplicative identity.**

Pick any nonzero $a \in A$. The left-multiplication map $L_a : A \to A$, $x \map
sto ax$, is injective (if $ax = 0$ then $x = 0$ by Step 1). Since $A$ is finite-
dimensional over $\mathbb{C}$, injectivity implies surjectivity, so there exists
 $e \in A$ with $ae = a$. For any $y \in A$:
$$a(ey - y) = (ae)y - ay = ay - ay = 0.$$
Since $a \neq 0$ and $A$ has no zero divisors, $ey = y$ for all $y$. So $e$ is a
 left identity. A symmetric argument (using right multiplication) gives a right
identity $e'$, and $e = e'e = e'$, so $e$ is a two-sided identity. Denote it $1$
.

**Normalization of the norm.** From $N(1) = N(1 \cdot 1) = N(1)^2$, we get $N(1)
 \in \{0, 1\}$. Since $1 \neq 0$ and $N$ is nondegenerate, $N(1) = 1$.

---

**Step 3: Every nonzero element is invertible.**

For any nonzero $a \in A$, the map $L_a$ is injective (Step 1) hence surjective
(finite-dimensionality), so there exists $b$ with $ab = 1$. Similarly, $R_a$ is
surjective, giving $c$ with $ca = 1$. Then
$$b = (ca)b = c(ab) = c \cdot 1 = c.$$
So $b = c$ is a two-sided inverse of $a$. Thus $A$ is a **division algebra**.

---

**Step 4: For any $a \in A$, the subalgebra $\mathbb{C}[a]$ is a field.**

Define $\mathbb{C}[a] = \{p(a) : p(t) \in \mathbb{C}[t]\}$, the commutative suba
lgebra generated by $a$ over $\mathbb{C}$. This is finite-dimensional (as a subs
pace of the finite-dimensional $A$) and commutative.

Since $A$ has no zero divisors (Step 1), $\mathbb{C}[a]$ is a finite-dimensional
 commutative integral domain over $\mathbb{C}$. A finite-dimensional integral do
main over a field is a field: for any nonzero $x \in \mathbb{C}[a]$, the map $m_
x : \mathbb{C}[a] \to \mathbb{C}[a]$, $y \mapsto xy$, is injective (no zero divi
sors) hence surjective, so $x$ has an inverse in $\mathbb{C}[a]$.

Thus $\mathbb{C}[a]$ is a **finite field extension** of $\mathbb{C}$.

---

**Step 5: Algebraic closure forces $\mathbb{C}[a] = \mathbb{C}$.**

Since $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed, it admits no nontrivial finite field
 extensions. Therefore $\mathbb{C}[a] = \mathbb{C}$, which means $a \in \mathbb{
C}$ (i.e., $a$ is a scalar multiple of the identity).

---

**Step 6: Conclusion.**

Since $a \in A$ was arbitrary and every $a$ lies in $\mathbb{C}$, we have $A = \
mathbb{C} \cdot 1 \cong \mathbb{C}$.

**Therefore, the only normed division algebra over $\mathbb{C}$ (up to isomorphi
sm) is $\mathbb{C}$ itself.** $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** This stands in sharp contrast to the real case, where Hurwitz's theo
rem yields four normed division algebras: $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{H
}$, and $\mathbb{O}$ (of dimensions $1, 2, 4, 8$). The crucial difference is tha
t $\mathbb{R}$ is not algebraically closed, allowing nontrivial finite-dimension
al division algebras (quaternions, octonions), whereas over the algebraically cl
osed field $\mathbb{C}$, every element of a finite-dimensional division algebra
must already be scalar, collapsing the algebra to $\mathbb{C}$ itself.
