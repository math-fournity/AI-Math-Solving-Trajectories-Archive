## Proof

**Answer: Yes.** $f(T)$ is the unique bounded operator on $H$ satisfying $f(T)(b
_k) = f(c_k)\,b_k$ for all $k \in \mathbb{N}_0$.

We prove this in two parts: (1) $f(T)$ satisfies the stated action on each basis
 vector, and (2) any bounded operator satisfying this action must equal $f(T)$.

---

### Part 1: $f(T)(b_k) = f(c_k)\,b_k$

Since $T$ is a densely defined self-adjoint operator, the spectral theorem provi
des a projection-valued measure $E$ on $\operatorname{spec}(T) \subseteq \mathbb
{R}$ such that $T = \int_{\operatorname{spec}(T)} \lambda\, dE(\lambda)$. For an
y bounded Borel (in particular, bounded continuous) function $f:\operatorname{sp
ec}(T)\to\mathbb{C}$, the functional calculus defines

$$f(T) = \int_{\operatorname{spec}(T)} f(\lambda)\, dE(\lambda),$$

a bounded operator with $\|f(T)\| \le \sup_{\lambda\in\operatorname{spec}(T)} |f
(\lambda)|$.

Because $T$ is diagonalizable with $T(b_k)=c_k b_k$, the spectral measure is con
centrated on the eigenvalues $\{c_k\}$, and the spectral projection onto the eig
enspace of $c_k$ contains $b_k$. Concretely, $E(\{c_k\})\, b_k = b_k$ (each $b_k
$ lies in the range of $E(\{c_k\})$). Therefore

$$f(T)\, b_k = \int f(\lambda)\, dE(\lambda)\, b_k = f(c_k)\, E(\{c_k\})\, b_k =
 f(c_k)\, b_k.$$

(If an eigenvalue $c_k$ has multiplicity $>1$, the same reasoning applies to eve
ry eigenvector in that eigenspace; the key point is that $b_k$ belongs to the ra
nge of $E(\{c_k\})$.)

---

### Part 2: Uniqueness

Let $A$ be any bounded operator on $H$ with $A\, b_k = f(c_k)\, b_k$ for every $
k \in \mathbb{N}_0$. We show $A = f(T)$.

Let $D = \operatorname{span}\{b_k : k \in \mathbb{N}_0\}$ be the set of finite l
inear combinations of the basis vectors. Since $\{b_k\}$ is an orthonormal basis
 of $H$, the set $D$ is dense in $H$.

For any $x = \sum_{k \in F} \alpha_k\, b_k \in D$ (finite sum, $F \subseteq \mat
hbb{N}_0$ finite), by linearity of $A$:

$$A\, x = \sum_{k \in F} \alpha_k\, A\, b_k = \sum_{k \in F} \alpha_k\, f(c_k)\,
 b_k.$$

The same holds for $f(T)$ by Part 1 and linearity:

$$f(T)\, x = \sum_{k \in F} \alpha_k\, f(c_k)\, b_k.$$

Thus $A$ and $f(T)$ agree on $D$. Since both $A$ and $f(T)$ are **bounded** (hen
ce continuous) operators on $H$, and $D$ is dense in $H$, they agree on all of $
H$:

$$A = f(T).$$

---

### Conclusion

The operator $f(T)$ (defined via the spectral/functional calculus) satisfies $f(
T)(b_k) = f(c_k)\,b_k$ for all $k$, and any bounded operator satisfying this con
dition is uniquely determined—because a bounded operator is determined by its ac
tion on a dense set, and the finite span of an orthonormal basis is dense. Hence
 $f(T)$ is the **unique** bounded operator with this property.
