## Proof

**Answer: Yes.** Under the hypotheses, the polynomial must be $w^n - z$, so for
each $z$ the roots are precisely the $n$-th roots of $z$.

**Key observation.** For every $w \in \mathbb{D}$, set $z = w^n \in \mathbb{D}$.
 Then $w$ is an $n$-th root of $z$, so by hypothesis $w$ is a root of the polyno
mial $P(z,\cdot)$. Hence

$$
G(w) := P(w^n, w) = w^n + f_1(w^n)w^{n-1} + \cdots + f_n(w^n) \equiv 0 \quad \te
xt{on } \mathbb{D}.
$$

**Power-series analysis.** Each $f_k$ is holomorphic on $\mathbb{D}$, so write $
f_k(z) = \sum_{j\ge 0} a_{k,j}\, z^j$ (with $f_0 \equiv 1$, i.e. $a_{0,0}=1$). T
hen

$$
G(w) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} f_k(w^n)\, w^{n-k} = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{j\ge 0} a_{k,j}\,
 w^{\,nj + n - k}.
$$

The exponent $nj + n - k = n(j+1) - k$ is congruent to $-k \equiv n-k \pmod n$.
Thus the contribution from index $k$ lands in the residue class $n-k \pmod n$:

- $k=0$: residue $0$, only the term $w^n$ (from $j=0$).
- $k=n$: residue $0$, terms $a_{n,j}\,w^{nj}$ for $j\ge 0$ (exponents $0, n, 2n,
 \dots$).
- $k \in \{1,\dots,n-1\}$: residue $n-k \in \{1,\dots,n-1\}$, terms $a_{k,j}\,w^
{n(j+1)-k}$.

Since different $k \in \{1,\dots,n-1\}$ occupy *distinct* nonzero residue classe
s mod $n$, and $G\equiv 0$, each such contribution must vanish independently:

$$
\sum_{j\ge 0} a_{k,j}\, w^{n(j+1)-k} \equiv 0 \implies a_{k,j}=0 \;\forall j \im
plies f_k \equiv 0, \quad k=1,\dots,n-1.
$$

The residue-$0$ class combines $k=0$ and $k=n$:

$$
a_{n,0} + (1 + a_{n,1})\,w^n + a_{n,2}\,w^{2n} + \cdots \equiv 0,
$$

giving $a_{n,0}=0$, $a_{n,1}=-1$, and $a_{n,j}=0$ for $j\ge 2$. Hence $f_n(z) =
-z$.

**Conclusion.** $P(z,w) = w^n - z$, whose roots are exactly the $n$-th roots of
$z$. Therefore, for each $z\in\mathbb{D}$, all roots of the polynomial are preci
sely the $n$-th roots of $z$. $\blacksquare$
