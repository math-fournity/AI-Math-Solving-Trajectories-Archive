## Proof

The answer is **yes**. We construct a subfield of $\mathbb{C}$ that is not even
contained in $\mathbb{R}$, yet is isomorphic to a proper subfield of $\mathbb{R}
$.

**Setup.** Let $t = \pi$, which is transcendental over $\mathbb{Q}$. Define
$$F := \mathbb{Q}(\pi) \subset \mathbb{R}.$$
Since $\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$ is countable (it consists of ratios of polynomials in $\
pi$ with rational coefficients) while $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable, $F$ is a **pr
oper** subfield of $\mathbb{R}$.

**A non-real transcendental.** Set $\alpha := i\pi \in \mathbb{C}$.

- $\alpha \notin \mathbb{R}$, since $i\pi$ is purely imaginary with nonzero imag
inary part.
- $\alpha$ is transcendental over $\mathbb{Q}$: if $\alpha$ were algebraic, then
 $\alpha^{2} = -\pi^{2}$ would be algebraic, hence $\pi^{2}$ algebraic, contradi
cting the transcendence of $\pi$.

**The subfield of $\mathbb{C}$.** Define
$$K := \mathbb{Q}(\alpha) = \mathbb{Q}(i\pi) \subset \mathbb{C}.$$
Then $K \not\subseteq \mathbb{R}$, because $i\pi \in K \setminus \mathbb{R}$.

**The isomorphism.** Both $\pi$ and $i\pi$ are transcendental over $\mathbb{Q}$,
 so both $F = \mathbb{Q}(\pi)$ and $K = \mathbb{Q}(i\pi)$ are simple transcenden
tal extensions of $\mathbb{Q}$. The universal property of the rational function
field gives
$$\mathbb{Q}(\pi) \cong \mathbb{Q}(x) \cong \mathbb{Q}(i\pi),$$
via the isomorphism $\varphi: F \to K$ determined by $\varphi|_{\mathbb{Q}} = \m
athrm{id}_{\mathbb{Q}}$ and $\varphi(\pi) = i\pi$. Concretely,
$$\varphi\!\left(\frac{p(\pi)}{q(\pi)}\right) = \frac{p(i\pi)}{q(i\pi)}$$
for all $p, q \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$, $q \neq 0$. This is well-defined and a field i
somorphism because $\pi$ and $i\pi$ satisfy the same polynomial relations over $
\mathbb{Q}$ (namely, none beyond those in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$), both being transcend
ental.

**Conclusion.** $K = \mathbb{Q}(i\pi)$ is a subfield of $\mathbb{C}$ (not contai
ned in $\mathbb{R}$), and $K \cong F = \mathbb{Q}(\pi)$, where $F$ is a proper s
ubfield of $\mathbb{R}$. Hence such a construction is possible.

**Remark.** Even the trivial example $\mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{C}$, with $\mat
hbb{Q} \cong \mathbb{Q} \subsetneq \mathbb{R}$, already answers the question aff
irmatively. The construction above is stronger: the subfield of $\mathbb{C}$ nee
d not even lie inside $\mathbb{R}$.
