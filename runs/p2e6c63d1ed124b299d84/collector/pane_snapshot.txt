## Proof

Let $X = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} Z_j$ be the candidate limit. We first establish tha
t this infinite sum is well-defined in $L^2$, then verify mean-square convergenc
e directly.

**Step 1: The limit $X$ exists in $L^2$ (and a.s.).**

The $Z_j$ are independent with $\mathbb{E}[Z_j]=0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Z_j)=
\mathbb{E}[Z_j^2]=1/2^j$. Since
$$\sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{E}[Z_j^2] = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^j} = 1
< \infty,$$
the martingale convergence theorem (or the $L^2$-convergence theorem for sums of
 independent zero-mean random variables with summable variances) guarantees that
 $X_n=\sum_{j=1}^n Z_j$ converges in $L^2$ — hence in probability and in distrib
ution — to some random variable $X$ with
$$\mathbb{E}[X]=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]=\sum_{j=1}^{\inft
y}\frac{1}{2^j}=1.$$
Moreover, since the $Z_j$ are jointly Gaussian, $X$ is Gaussian, so $X\sim N(0,1
)$. This is consistent with the given fact that $X_n \xrightarrow{d} N(0,1)$.

**Step 2: Direct verification of mean-square convergence.**

Mean-square convergence $X_n \xrightarrow{L^2} X$ means $\mathbb{E}\big[(X_n-X)^
2\big]\to 0$. Now
$$X - X_n = \sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty} Z_j,$$
and by independence of the $Z_j$,
$$\mathbb{E}\big[(X-X_n)^2\big] = \sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[Z_j^2] = \sum_
{j=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^j} = \frac{1/2^{n+1}}{1-1/2} = \frac{1}{2^n}.$$
Therefore
$$\mathbb{E}\big[(X_n-X)^2\big] = \frac{1}{2^n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 0,$$
which is precisely $X_n \xrightarrow{L^2} X$.

**Step 3: Why distributional convergence alone is not the reason.**

It is worth noting *why* the answer is yes here, since in general convergence in
 distribution does **not** imply convergence in mean square. The decisive ingred
ient is the *direct* computation of the tail variance $\mathbb{E}[(X-X_n)^2]=1/2
^n\to 0$, which uses the independence and the summability of the variances. The
given distributional limit $N(0,1)$ merely identifies the limit $X$; it is the s
ummability $\sum_j \operatorname{Var}(Z_j)<\infty$ that forces $L^2$-convergence
. Had the variances not been summable, $X_n$ could still converge in distributio
n (e.g. to a degenerate law under suitable normalization) without converging in
mean square.

**Conclusion.**

Yes, $X_n$ converges to $X\sim N(0,1)$ in the mean-square sense, with
$$\mathbb{E}\big[(X_n-X)^2\big]=\frac{1}{2^n}\to 0.$$
