## Proof

$$L = \lim_{n \to \infty} \lim_{R \to \infty} \int_0^R \sin\!\left(\frac{x}{n}\r
ight)\sin(e^x)\,dx.$$

Set $u = e^x$, so $x = \ln u$, $dx = du/u$. The limits $x: 0 \to R$ become $u: 1
 \to e^R$. Thus
$$\int_0^R \sin\!\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)\sin(e^x)\,dx = \int_1^{e^R} \frac{\sin
(\ln u\,/\,n)}{u}\,\sin u\,du.$$

### Step 2 — The inner limit exists (Dirichlet test)

Define $f(u) = \dfrac{\sin(\ln u\,/\,n)}{u}$ for $u \ge 1$. We have $f(u) \to 0$
 as $u\to\infty$ (bounded numerator, $1/u \to 0$). Moreover $f$ has **bounded va
riation** on $[1,\infty)$, since
$$f'(u) = \frac{\tfrac{1}{n}\cos(\ln u/n) - \sin(\ln u/n)}{u^2}, \qquad \int_1^\
infty |f'(u)|\,du \le \int_1^\infty \frac{1/n+1}{u^2}\,du = 1+\frac1n < \infty.$
$
A function of bounded variation tending to $0$ decomposes (Jordan) as a differen
ce of two non-negative decreasing functions each tending to $0$. Since $\int_1^A
 \sin u\,du$ is bounded, **Dirichlet's test** gives the convergence of
$$I(n) := \int_1^\infty \frac{\sin(\ln u\,/\,n)}{u}\,\sin u\,du = \lim_{R\to\inf

### Step 3 — Integration by parts to obtain an absolutely convergent integral

Integrate by parts with $\sin u = \frac{d}{du}(-\cos u)$:
$$I(n) = \Bigl[-\frac{\sin(\ln u/n)}{u}\cos u\Bigr]_1^\infty + \int_1^\infty \fr
ac{d}{du}\!\left(\frac{\sin(\ln u/n)}{u}\right)\cos u\,du.$$
The boundary term vanishes: at $u=\infty$ because $|\sin(\ln u/n)|/u \to 0$; at
$u=1$ because $\sin(0)=0$. Hence
$$I(n) = \int_1^\infty \frac{\tfrac{1}{n}\cos(\ln u/n) - \sin(\ln u/n)}{u^2}\,\c

This integral is **absolutely convergent**, since
$$\left|\frac{\tfrac{1}{n}\cos(\ln u/n) - \sin(\ln u/n)}{u^2}\cos u\right| \le \
frac{1/n+1}{u^2} \le \frac{2}{u^2}, \qquad \int_1^\infty \frac{2}{u^2}\,du = 2 <
 \infty.$$

$$\sin(\ln u/n) \to 0, \qquad \tfrac{1}{n}\cos(\ln u/n) \to 0.$$
Thus the integrand converges pointwise to $0$, and it is dominated by the integr

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} I(n) = \int_1^\infty 0 \,du = 0.$$
