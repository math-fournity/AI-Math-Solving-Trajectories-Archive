## Proof

**Claim.** The entire functions $f$ such that $f(z)=a$ has finitely many solutio
ns for every $a\in\mathbb{C}$ are exactly the **non-constant polynomials**.

---

### Step 1. Non-constant polynomials satisfy the condition.

Let $f$ be a polynomial of degree $n\geq 1$. For any $a\in\mathbb{C}$, the equat
ion $f(z)=a$ is equivalent to $f(z)-a=0$, a polynomial equation of degree $n$ (t
he leading term is unaffected by subtracting $a$). By the Fundamental Theorem of
 Algebra, it has at most $n$ roots counting multiplicity, hence finitely many di
stinct solutions. ✓

### Step 2. Constant functions fail the condition.

If $f\equiv c$ is constant, then for $a=c$ the equation $f(z)=a$ is satisfied by
 **every** $z\in\mathbb{C}$, giving infinitely many solutions. Hence constant fu
nctions are excluded. ✗

### Step 3. Transcendental entire functions fail the condition.

Let $f$ be a transcendental entire function (entire but not a polynomial). Then
$f$ has an **essential singularity** at $\infty$.

By the **Great Picard Theorem**: in every neighborhood of an essential singulari
ty, an analytic function assumes every complex value, with at most one exception
, **infinitely often**.

Concretely, for every $R>0$, outside the disk $\{|z|\leq R\}$ the function $f$ t
akes every value $a\in\mathbb{C}$, with at most a single exceptional value, infi
nitely many times. Therefore:

- For all but at most one value $a\in\mathbb{C}$, the equation $f(z)=a$ has **in
finitely many** solutions (indeed, infinitely many in $\{|z|>R\}$ for every $R$)
.

Since $\mathbb{C}$ is uncountable, "all but at most one" still leaves uncountabl
y many values $a$ for which $f(z)=a$ has infinitely many solutions. In particula
r, there **exists** at least one $a\in\mathbb{C}$ (in fact, all but at most one)
 for which the solution set is infinite.

Thus the requirement that $f(z)=a$ have **finitely** many solutions for **every*
* $a\in\mathbb{C}$ fails. ✗

### Step 4. Conclusion.

Every entire function is either:
- a constant (Step 2: fails),
- a non-constant polynomial (Step 1: satisfies), or
- a transcendental entire function (Step 3: fails).

The only entire functions satisfying the condition are therefore the **non-const
ant polynomials**. $\blacksquare$
