**Answer: Yes, such a function exists.**

## Construction

Let $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be a continuous, **nowhere-differentiable** f
unction (e.g. the Weierstrass function $g(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a^n \cos(b^n
\pi x)$ with $0 < a < 1$, $ab$ an odd integer, and $ab > 1 + \tfrac{3\pi}{2}$).
Define

$$f(x) = x^{2}\, g(x).$$

Since $x^{2}$ and $g$ are both continuous, $f$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. We
 show $f$ is differentiable **exactly** at $x = 0$.

## Differentiability at $0$

We have $f(0) = 0$, and for $h \neq 0$,

$$\frac{f(h) - f(0)}{h} = \frac{h^{2}\, g(h)}{h} = h\, g(h).$$

Since $g$ is continuous, $g(h) \to g(0)$ as $h \to 0$, so $h\, g(h) \to 0$. Ther
efore

$$f'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(h)-f(0)}{h} = 0,$$

so $f$ is differentiable at $0$.

## Non-differentiability at every $a \neq 0$

Fix $a \neq 0$ and suppose, for contradiction, that $f$ is differentiable at $a$
. Then the limit

$$L := \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

exists and is finite. Expanding,

$$\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} = \frac{(a+h)^{2} g(a+h) - a^{2} g(a)}{h}.$$

Writing $(a+h)^{2} = a^{2} + 2ah + h^{2}$,

$$= \frac{a^{2}\bigl(g(a+h) - g(a)\bigr) + 2ah\, g(a+h) + h^{2}\, g(a+h)}{h}$$

$$= a^{2}\,\frac{g(a+h) - g(a)}{h} \;+\; 2a\, g(a+h) \;+\; h\, g(a+h).$$

By continuity of $g$, as $h \to 0$:

- $2a\, g(a+h) \to 2a\, g(a)$,
- $h\, g(a+h) \to 0$.

Since the left-hand side has a finite limit $L$, and the last two terms have fin
ite limits, the first term must also have a finite limit:

$$\lim_{h \to 0} a^{2}\,\frac{g(a+h) - g(a)}{h} \;\;\text{exists.}$$

Because $a \neq 0$, we have $a^{2} \neq 0$, so

$$\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{g(a+h) - g(a)}{h}$$

exists — i.e., $g$ is differentiable at $a$. This **contradicts** the fact that
$g$ is nowhere differentiable.

Hence $f$ is **not** differentiable at any $a \neq 0$.

## Conclusion

The function $f(x) = x^{2} g(x)$, where $g$ is any continuous nowhere-differenti
able function on $\mathbb{R}$, is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ and differentiable
at exactly one point (namely $x = 0$, with $f'(0) = 0$). Therefore, **there exis
ts a continuous function $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ that is differentiable at o
nly one point.** $\blacksquare$
