Proof

Claim: No such diffeomorphism exists. For every diffeomorphism $f: \mathbb{B}^n
\to \mathbb{R}^n$, we have $\lim_{|x|\to 1}|f(x)| = \infty$.

Setup. Let $f: \mathbb{B}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ be a diffeomorphism (in fact, a
homeomorphism suffices). Denote its inverse by $g = f^{-1}: \mathbb{R}^n \to
\mathbb{B}^n$, which is continuous.

Arguing by contradiction. Suppose $\lim_{|x|\to 1}|f(x)| \neq \infty$. By
definition of the limit being $+\infty$, this means:

$$\exists, M > 0 ;;\forall,\delta > 0 ;;\exists, x \in \mathbb{B}^n:\quad |x| >
1-\delta \text{ and } |f(x)| \le M.$$

Taking $\delta = 1/k$ for $k = 1, 2, 3, \dots$, we obtain a sequence ${x_k}
\subset \mathbb{B}^n$ with

$$|x_k| > 1 - \tfrac{1}{k} \quad\text{and}\quad |f(x_k)| \le M.$$

In particular $|x_k| \to 1$.

Extracting a convergent subsequence in the image. Set $y_k = f(x_k) \in
\mathbb{R}^n$. Since $|y_k| \le M$ for all $k$, the sequence ${y_k}$ is bounded.
By the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, there exists a convergent subsequence
$y_{k_j} \to y^$ for some $y^ \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with $|y^*| \le M < \infty$.

Using continuity of the inverse. Since $g = f^{-1}$ is continuous,

$$x_{k_j} = g(y_{k_j}) ;\longrightarrow; g(y^*).$$

But $|x_{k_j}| \to 1$ (as $|x_k| \to 1$), so by continuity of the norm,

$$|g(y^*)| = \lim_{j\to\infty}|x_{k_j}| = 1.$$

Contradiction. The map $g$ takes values in $\mathbb{B}^n = {x : |x| < 1}$, so
every point in its image satisfies $|g(y)| < 1$. In particular $|g(y^)| < 1$.
This contradicts $|g(y^)| = 1$.

Conclusion. The contradiction shows that our supposition was false. Therefore

$$\lim_{|x|\to 1}|f(x)| = \infty$$

for every diffeomorphism $f: \mathbb{B}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$. Hence no
diffeomorphism $f: \mathbb{B}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ can satisfy $\lim_{|x|\to 1}|
f(x)| \neq \infty$.

Remark. The argument uses only that $f$ is a homeomorphism (continuous with
continuous inverse); the smoothness hypothesis of a diffeomorphism is not
needed. The essential topological fact is that $\mathbb{B}^n$ is not
homeomorphic to a bounded subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ in a way that "folds the
boundary to finite distance"—the inverse, being continuous, must send bounded
sequences to sequences with bounded image, yet the boundary of the ball is at
distance $1$ and cannot be reached.
