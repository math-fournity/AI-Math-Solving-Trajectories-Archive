## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. The condition $\det(A) = bi$ with $b \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\
}$ implies the $n$ vectors are linearly independent over $\mathbb{R}$ (viewing $
\mathbb{C}^n$ as a real vector space).

**Proof.**

Let $v_1, \dots, v_n \in \mathbb{C}^n$ be the given vectors, and let $A$ be the
$n\times n$ matrix whose columns (or rows) are these vectors.

**Step 1: $\det(A) \neq 0$.**

By hypothesis $\det(A) = bi$ with $b \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$. Since $b \ne
q 0$, we have $bi \neq 0$, so $\det(A) \neq 0$.

**Step 2: The vectors are linearly independent over $\mathbb{C}$.**

A standard theorem of linear algebra states that for an $n\times n$ matrix $A$ o
ver a field, the columns of $A$ are linearly independent over that field if and
only if $\det(A) \neq 0$. Applying this over the field $\mathbb{C}$: since $\det
(A) \neq 0$, the vectors $v_1, \dots, v_n$ are linearly independent over $\mathb
b{C}$. Concretely, if $\sum_{j=1}^n c_j v_j = 0$ with $c_j \in \mathbb{C}$, then
 $A c = 0$ where $c = (c_1,\dots,c_n)^T$; since $A$ is invertible over $\mathbb{
C}$, $c = 0$, i.e., every $c_j = 0$.

**Step 3: Linear independence over $\mathbb{C}$ implies linear independence over
 $\mathbb{R}$.**

The field $\mathbb{R}$ is a subfield of $\mathbb{C}$: $\mathbb{R} \subset \mathb
b{C}$. Suppose there exist real coefficients $r_1, \dots, r_n \in \mathbb{R}$ su
ch that
$$\sum_{j=1}^n r_j v_j = 0.$$
Since each $r_j \in \mathbb{R} \subset \mathbb{C}$, this is in particular a comp
lex linear combination of the $v_j$ equal to zero. By the linear independence of
 $\{v_1,\dots,v_n\}$ over $\mathbb{C}$ (Step 2), every $r_j = 0$.

By definition, this is precisely the statement that $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ is lin
early independent over $\mathbb{R}$.

**Step 4: The specific value $bi$ is a red herring.**

The only property of $\det(A) = bi$ used in the argument is that it is nonzero.
The fact that the determinant is purely imaginary (rather than having a nonzero
real part, or being any other nonzero complex number) plays no role. Any nonzero
 determinant — real, purely imaginary, or fully complex — yields the same conclu
sion by the identical argument.

**Conclusion.**

The hypothesis $\det(A) = bi$ with $b \neq 0$ guarantees $\det(A) \neq 0$, hence
 the $n$ vectors are linearly independent over $\mathbb{C}$, and since $\mathbb{
R}\subseteq\mathbb{C}$, linear independence over $\mathbb{C}$ implies linear ind
ependence over $\mathbb{R}$. Therefore the $n$ vectors are linearly independent
when $\mathbb{C}^n$ is regarded as a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$. $\blacksqua
