## Solution

**Claim:** Yes. For every $m \in [0,1]$, there exists a dense subset $A \subsete
q [0,1]$ with Lebesgue measure $m$.

The proof rests on a lemma constructing closed nowhere-dense sets of prescribed
measure, then taking complements.

---

### Lemma

*For every $r \in [0,1)$, there exists a closed, nowhere-dense set $B \subseteq
[0,1]$ with Lebesgue measure $r$.*

**Proof of Lemma.**

**Case $r = 0$.** The classical middle-thirds Cantor set is closed, nowhere dens
e, and has Lebesgue measure $0$.

**Case $r \in (0,1)$.** We use a generalized Cantor construction. Define the seq
uence
$$a_n := 1 - r^{\,1/2^n} \in (0,1), \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$
(each $a_n \in (0,1)$ because $r \in (0,1)$, so $r^{1/2^n} \in (0,1)$).

Construct closed sets $C_n$ recursively:
- $C_0 = [0,1]$, a single closed interval of length $L_0 = 1$.
- Given $C_{n-1}$ consisting of $2^{n-1}$ disjoint closed intervals each of leng
th $L_{n-1}$, form $C_n$ by deleting from each constituent interval the open mid
dle subinterval of relative length $a_n$ (i.e., we remove an open interval of le
ngth $a_n \cdot L_{n-1}$ from the middle of each).

Then $C_n$ consists of $2^n$ disjoint closed intervals, each of length
$$L_n = \frac{1-a_n}{2}\,L_{n-1}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad L_n = \frac{1}{2^n}\prod
_{k=1}^{n}(1-a_k).$$

The Lebesgue measure of $C_n$ is
$$\lambda(C_n) = 2^n \cdot L_n = \prod_{k=1}^{n}(1-a_k).$$

Set $B := \bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty} C_n$. Since each $C_n$ is closed, $B$ is closed
. The measure of $B$ is
$$\lambda(B) = \lim_{n\to\infty} \lambda(C_n) = \prod_{k=1}^{\infty}(1-a_k) = \p
rod_{k=1}^{\infty} r^{1/2^k} = r^{\,\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} 1/2^k} = r^{1} = r.$$

**$B$ is nowhere dense.** At every stage $n$, each interval composing $C_{n-1}$
has its (relative) middle portion removed, so no interval of positive length is
entirely contained in $C_n$. Since $B \subseteq C_n$ for all $n$, $B$ contains n
o interval of positive length. Being closed and containing no interval, $B$ has
empty interior, hence is nowhere dense. $\blacksquare$

### Main Proof

We handle three cases.

**Case $m = 0$.** Take $A = \mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]$. This is dense in $[0,1]$ (th
e rationals are dense in $\mathbb{R}$) and has Lebesgue measure $0$ (being count
able).

**Case $m = 1$.** Take $A = [0,1] \setminus \mathbb{Q}$, the irrationals in $[0,
1]$. This is dense in $[0,1]$ (the irrationals are dense in $\mathbb{R}$) and ha
s Lebesgue measure $1 - \lambda(\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]) = 1 - 0 = 1$.

**Case $0 < m < 1$.** Set $r = 1 - m \in (0,1)$. By the Lemma, there exists a cl
osed, nowhere-dense set $B \subseteq [0,1]$ with $\lambda(B) = r$. Define
$$A := [0,1] \setminus B.$$

- **Measurability and measure:** $B$ is closed, hence measurable; $A$ is measura
ble as the difference of measurable sets. By additivity,
$$\lambda(A) = \lambda([0,1]) - \lambda(B) = 1 - r = m.$$

- **Density:** Since $B$ is nowhere dense, it has empty interior in $[0,1]$. Thu
s every nonempty open subinterval $I \subseteq [0,1]$ satisfies $I \not\subseteq
 B$, i.e., $I \cap A \neq \varnothing$. Therefore $A$ intersects every nonempty
open subinterval of $[0,1]$, which is precisely the statement that $A$ is dense
in $[0,1]$.

### Conclusion

For every $m \in [0,1]$, we have exhibited a dense subset $A \subseteq [0,1]$ wi
th Lebesgue measure exactly $m$:

| $m$ | Construction | Dense? | Measure |
|-----|-------------|--------|---------|
| $0$ | $\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$ | yes | $0$ |
| $(0,1)$ | $[0,1]\setminus B$, $B$ closed nowhere-dense of measure $1-m$ | yes
(empty interior of $B$) | $m$ |
| $1$ | $[0,1]\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ | yes | $1$ |

The answer is **yes**: such a dense subset exists for every $m \in [0,1]$.
