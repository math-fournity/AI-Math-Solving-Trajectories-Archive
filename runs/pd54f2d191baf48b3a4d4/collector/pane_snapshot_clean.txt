## Proof

Let $f(t) = \begin{cases} 1, & t \in \mathbb{Q} \\ 0, & t \notin \mathbb{Q} \end
{cases}$ on $(0,1)$.

### Part 1: The Riemann integral does NOT exist

Consider any partition $P = \{0 = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n = 1\}$ of $[0,1]$. In
 every subinterval $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$ (which has positive length), both rationals
and irrationals are dense, so:

- $\sup_{t \in [x_{k-1},x_k]} f(t) = 1$ (a rational exists in the subinterval),
- $\inf_{t \in [x_{k-1},x_k]} f(t) = 0$ (an irrational exists in the subinterval
).

Therefore the upper and lower Darboux sums are

$$U(P,f) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} 1 \cdot \Delta x_k = 1, \qquad L(P,f) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}
 0 \cdot \Delta x_k = 0,$$

for **every** partition $P$. Hence

$$\overline{\int_0^1} f = \inf_P U(P,f) = 1, \qquad \underline{\int_0^1} f = \su
p_P L(P,f) = 0.$$

Since the upper and lower integrals differ ($1 \neq 0$), $f$ is **not Riemann in
tegrable** on $[0,1]$. Equivalently, $f$ is discontinuous at every point of $(0,
1)$, and the set of discontinuities has Lebesgue measure $1 \neq 0$, violating L
ebesgue's criterion for Riemann integrability.

### Part 2: The Lebesgue integral DOES exist (and equals $0$)

The set $\mathbb{Q} \cap (0,1)$ is countable (as a subset of the countable set $
\mathbb{Q}$), hence has Lebesgue measure zero:

$$m\bigl(\mathbb{Q} \cap (0,1)\bigr) = 0.$$

The function $f$ is the indicator function $\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{Q} \cap (0,1)}$,
 which is a non-negative measurable function. By the definition of the Lebesgue
integral of an indicator function,

$$\int_{(0,1)} f\,dm = m\bigl(\mathbb{Q} \cap (0,1)\bigr) = 0.$$

Moreover, $f = 0$ almost everywhere on $(0,1)$ (since $f$ differs from the zero
function only on the measure-zero set of rationals), which confirms $\int f\,dm
= 0$.

### Conclusion

- **Riemann integral**: does **not** exist (upper integral $=1$, lower integral
$=0$).
- **Lebesgue integral**: **exists** and equals $\mathbf{0}$ (the rationals form
a null set, so $f = 0$ a.e.).

Thus the answer depends on the notion of integral used: the integral fails to ex
ist in the Riemann sense, but exists (with value $0$) in the Lebesgue sense.
