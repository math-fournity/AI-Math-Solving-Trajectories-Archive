## General Solution to Bessel's Equation

**Equation:** $x^2 y'' + xy' + (\mu^2 x^2 - n^2)y = 0$, with $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

### Step 1. Reduction to Standard Form

Divide through by $x^2$:

$$y'' + \frac{1}{x}y' + \left(\mu^2 - \frac{n^2}{x^2}\right)y = 0.$$

Introduce the change of variable $t = \mu x$ (assuming $\mu > 0$; the case $\mu
= 0$ is a Cauchy–Euler equation treated separately). Then

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \mu\frac{dy}{dt}, \qquad \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \mu^2\frac{d^2y}{
dt^2}, \qquad x = \frac{t}{\mu}.$$

Substituting:

$$\frac{t^2}{\mu^2}\cdot\mu^2\frac{d^2y}{dt^2} + \frac{t}{\mu}\cdot\mu\frac{dy}{
dt} + \left(\mu^2\frac{t^2}{\mu^2} - n^2\right)y = 0,$$

which simplifies to the **standard Bessel equation of order $n$**:

$$t^2\frac{d^2y}{dt^2} + t\frac{dy}{dt} + (t^2 - n^2)y = 0. \tag{$\star$}$$

It suffices to find the general solution of $(\star)$ and substitute back $t = \
mu x$.

### Step 2. First Solution via the Frobenius Method ($r = n$)

Equation $(\star)$ has a regular singular point at $t = 0$. We seek a solution o
f the form

$$y(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a_k\, t^{k+r}, \qquad a_0 \neq 0.$$

Computing $y' = \sum (k+r)a_k t^{k+r-1}$ and $y'' = \sum (k+r)(k+r-1)a_k t^{k+r-
2}$, and substituting into $(\star)$:

$$\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\bigl[(k+r)^2 - n^2\bigr]a_k\, t^{k+r} + \sum_{k=0}^{\infty
} a_k\, t^{k+r+2} = 0.$$

Shifting the index in the second sum ($k \to k-2$) and equating coefficients of
$t^{k+r}$:

- **Indicial equation** ($k=0$): $\;(r^2 - n^2)a_0 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; r = \
pm n$.
- **$k=1$:** $\;[(1+r)^2 - n^2]a_1 = 0$. For $r = n$: $(1+2n)a_1 = 0$, so $a_1 =
 0$ (since $n \geq 0$ integer gives $1+2n \neq 0$).
- **Recurrence** ($k \geq 2$): $\;a_k = -\dfrac{a_{k-2}}{(k+r)^2 - n^2}$.

Taking **$r = n$** (with $n \geq 0$):

$$a_k = -\frac{a_{k-2}}{(k+n)^2 - n^2} = -\frac{a_{k-2}}{k(2n+k)}, \qquad k \geq
 2.$$

Since $a_1 = 0$, all odd coefficients vanish. For even indices $k = 2m$:

$$a_{2m} = -\frac{a_{2m-2}}{2m(2n+2m)} = -\frac{a_{2m-2}}{2^2\, m\,(n+m)}.$$

Iterating from $a_0$:

$$a_{2m} = \frac{(-1)^m\, a_0}{2^{2m}\, m!\, (n+1)(n+2)\cdots(n+m)} = \frac{(-1)
^m\, a_0\, n!}{2^{2m}\, m!\, (n+m)!}.$$

Choosing $a_0 = \dfrac{1}{2^n\, n!}$ (the conventional normalization) gives:

$$a_{2m} = \frac{(-1)^m}{2^{2m+n}\, m!\, (n+m)!}.$$

Hence the first solution is the **Bessel function of the first kind of order $n$
**:

$$\boxed{J_n(t) = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^m}{m!\,(n+m)!}\left(\frac{t}{2}
\right)^{2m+n}.}$$

One verifies by direct substitution (the recurrence was derived from the equatio
n) that $J_n$ satisfies $(\star)$. The series converges for all $t$ (ratio test:
 $|a_{2(m+1)}/a_{2m}| = \frac{t^2/4}{(m+1)(n+m+1)} \to 0$).

### Step 3. Failure of $J_{-n}$ as a Second Solution

For the second root $r = -n$, the recurrence becomes

$$a_k = -\frac{a_{k-2}}{k(k - 2n)}, \qquad k \geq 2.$$

- For $k < 2n$ (even $k$): the coefficients $a_2, a_4, \ldots, a_{2n-2}$ are wel
l-defined and nonzero.
- **At $k = 2n$**: the denominator $k(k-2n) = 2n \cdot 0 = 0$, while the numerat
or $a_{2n-2} \neq 0$. The recurrence is **inconsistent** — no pure Frobenius ser
ies with $r = -n$ exists.

Equivalently, one can show (by comparing the series term-by-term) that for integ
er $n$:

$$J_{-n}(t) = (-1)^n J_n(t),$$

so $J_{-n}$ is **linearly dependent** on $J_n$ and cannot serve as the second so
lution. (When $n$ is not an integer, $J_n$ and $J_{-n}$ are independent and form
 a complete basis; the difficulty is specific to integer order.)

For $n = 0$, the indicial equation $r^2 = 0$ has a **double root** $r = 0$, and
the standard Frobenius theory again guarantees that the second solution must con
tain a logarithmic term.

### Step 4. Second Solution — Bessel Function of the Second Kind

Since the two roots $r = n$ and $r = -n$ differ by an integer (or coincide when
$n=0$), the method of Frobenius dictates that the second linearly independent so
lution takes the form

$$y_2(t) = C\, J_n(t)\ln t + t^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} b_k\, t^k,$$

for suitable constants $C, b_k$. Rather than computing the $b_k$ explicitly, it
is conventional to define the second solution via a limiting process that works
uniformly for all $n$.

For **non-integer** $\nu$, the functions $J_\nu$ and $J_{-\nu}$ are independent,
 and one defines:

$$Y_\nu(t) = \frac{J_\nu(t)\cos(\nu\pi) - J_{-\nu}(t)}{\sin(\nu\pi)}.$$

This is a solution of $(\star)$ (with $n$ replaced by $\nu$) by linearity. As $\
nu \to n$ (integer), both numerator and denominator tend to zero (since $J_{-n}
= (-1)^n J_n$), so $Y_n$ is defined by the **limit**:

$$\boxed{Y_n(t) = \lim_{\nu \to n} \frac{J_\nu(t)\cos(\nu\pi) - J_{-\nu}(t)}{\si
n(\nu\pi)}.}$$

L'Hôpital's rule (differentiating with respect to $\nu$) shows this limit exists
 and is finite, yielding a well-defined function that:
- satisfies Bessel's equation of order $n$ (being a limit of solutions),
- is linearly independent of $J_n$ (it contains a logarithmic singularity $\sim
\frac{2}{\pi}\ln t$ as $t \to 0^+$, whereas $J_n$ is bounded or vanishes),
- reduces to the Frobenius form $C\,J_n\ln t + t^{-n}\sum b_k t^k$ mentioned abo
ve.

This is the **Bessel function of the second kind** (also called the Neumann or W
eber function).

### Step 5. General Solution

Since $J_n(t)$ and $Y_n(t)$ are two linearly independent solutions of the second
-order linear ODE $(\star)$ (verified: they satisfy the equation and are indepen
dent by their distinct behavior near $t=0$), the general solution of $(\star)$ i
s

$$y(t) = c_1\, J_n(t) + c_2\, Y_n(t), \qquad c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R}.$$

Substituting back $t = \mu x$:

$$\boxed{y(x) = c_1\, J_n(\mu x) + c_2\, Y_n(\mu x),}$$

where
$$J_n(\mu x) = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^m}{m!\,(n+m)!}\left(\frac{\mu x}{2}
\right)^{2m+n}, \qquad Y_n(\mu x) = \lim_{\nu\to n}\frac{J_\nu(\mu x)\cos(\nu\pi
) - J_{-\nu}(\mu x)}{\sin(\nu\pi)},$$

and $c_1, c_2$ are arbitrary constants.

**Remark on $\mu = 0$:** When $\mu = 0$, the equation reduces to the Cauchy–Eule
r equation $x^2 y'' + xy' - n^2 y = 0$, whose general solution is $y = c_1 x^n +
 c_2 x^{-n}$ (for $n \neq 0$) or $y = c_1 + c_2\ln x$ (for $n = 0$). This is con
sistent with the limiting behavior of $J_n(\mu x)$ and $Y_n(\mu x)$ as $\mu \to
0$.
