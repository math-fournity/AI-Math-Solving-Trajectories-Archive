## Example: The First Weyl Algebra

Let $k$ be a field of **characteristic $0$** (e.g. $k = \mathbb{Q}$). Define the
 **first Weyl algebra**

$$A = A_1(k) = k\langle x, y \rangle / (xy - yx - 1),$$

i.e. the free $k$-algebra on two generators modulo the relation $xy - yx = 1$.

I will prove that $A$ is (1) non-commutative, (2) infinite-dimensional over $k$,
 and (3) simple (has no non-trivial two-sided ideals).

---

### (1) Non-commutative

In $A$ we have $xy - yx = 1 \neq 0$, so $xy \neq yx$. Hence $A$ is non-commutati
ve.

---

### (2) Infinite dimension over $k$

The defining relation gives the reduction rule $yx = xy - 1$. Applying this rule
 repeatedly, any monomial in $x$ and $y$ can be rewritten as a $k$-linear combin
ation of monomials of the form $x^i y^j$ with $i, j \geq 0$. Thus $\{x^i y^j : i
,j \geq 0\}$ spans $A$.

**Linear independence (Bergman's Diamond Lemma).** The only reduction rule is $y
x \to xy - 1$. The only potential overlap ambiguity is in the word $yxyx$, where
 we may reduce either the first or the second occurrence of $yx$:

- Reducing the first: $(yx)yx \to (xy-1)yx = xy(yx) - yx \to xy(xy-1) - (xy-1) =
 x^2y^2 - 2xy + 1$.
- Reducing the second: $yx(yx) \to yx(xy-1) = (yx)xy - yx \to (xy-1)xy - (xy-1)
= x^2y^2 - 2xy + 1$.

Both reductions agree, so the overlap ambiguity resolves. By Bergman's Diamond L
emma, the irreducible monomials $\{x^i y^j : i, j \geq 0\}$ form a $k$-basis of
$A$.

This basis is infinite (e.g. $\{1, x, x^2, \dots\}$ is an infinite linearly inde
pendent subset), so

$$\dim_k A = \aleph_0 = \infty.$$

---

### (3) Simplicity — no non-trivial two-sided ideals

Let $I \neq \{0\}$ be a two-sided ideal of $A$. I will show $I = A$.

**Key commutation relations.** From $xy - yx = 1$ one proves by induction:

$$[x, x^i y^j] := x \cdot x^i y^j - x^i y^j \cdot x = j\, x^i y^{j-1},$$
$$[y, x^i y^j] := y \cdot x^i y^j - x^i y^j \cdot y = -i\, x^{i-1} y^j.$$

(These follow from $x\, y^j = y^j x + j\, y^{j-1}$ and $y\, x^i = x^i y - i\, x^
{i-1}$, each proved by induction on the exponent.)

**Step 1: Reduce to a polynomial in $x$ alone.** Take any non-zero $f \in I$, wr
itten in the basis as $f = \sum a_{ij}\, x^i y^j$. Let $m = \max\{j : a_{ij} \ne
q 0 \text{ for some } i\}$ be the $y$-degree of $f$.

If $m \geq 1$, consider $g = [x, f] = xf - fx \in I$ (since $I$ is a two-sided i
deal). Then

$$g = \sum a_{ij}\, j\, x^i y^{j-1}.$$

The terms with $j = m$ contribute $\sum_i a_{im}\, m\, x^i y^{m-1}$. Since $\ope
ratorname{char}(k) = 0$, we have $m \neq 0$ in $k$, and at least one $a_{im} \ne
q 0$, so these terms are non-zero. No other terms produce $y^{m-1}$, so $g \neq
0$ and $g$ has $y$-degree $m - 1$.

Repeating this $m$ times, we obtain a non-zero element $h \in I$ with $y$-degree
 $0$, i.e.

$$h = \sum_{i=0}^{n} b_i\, x^i, \qquad b_n \neq 0.$$

**Step 2: Reduce to a non-zero constant.** If $n = 0$, then $h = b_0 \in k \setm
inus \{0\}$, so $1 = b_0^{-1} h \in I$, hence $I = A$.

If $n \geq 1$, consider $[y, h] = yh - hy \in I$:

$$[y, h] = \sum_{i=0}^{n} b_i\, (-i)\, x^{i-1}.$$

The leading term is $-n\, b_n\, x^{n-1}$. Since $\operatorname{char}(k) = 0$, $n
 \neq 0$ in $k$, and $b_n \neq 0$, this is non-zero. So $[y,h] \neq 0$ and has $
x$-degree $n - 1$.

Repeating this $n$ times, we obtain a non-zero constant $c \in I \cap k$. Then $
1 = c^{-1} c \in I$, so $I = A$.

**Conclusion of Step 1 & 2:** Every non-zero two-sided ideal of $A$ contains $1$
, hence equals $A$. Therefore $A$ is **simple** — it has no two-sided ideals oth
er than $\{0\}$ and $A$.

### Summary

The first Weyl algebra $A_1(k) = k\langle x,y\rangle/(xy - yx - 1)$ over a field
 $k$ of characteristic $0$ satisfies all three required properties:

| Property | Verification |
|---|---|
| Non-commutative | $xy - yx = 1 \neq 0$ |
| Infinite $k$-dimension | $k$-basis $\{x^i y^j : i,j \geq 0\}$ is countably inf
inite (Diamond Lemma) |
| No non-trivial two-sided ideals | Commutator argument reduces any non-zero ide
al element to a non-zero constant, forcing $1 \in I$ |

Hence such an example **does exist**, and $A_1(k)$ is one.
