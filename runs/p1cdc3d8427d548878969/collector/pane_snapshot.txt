## Proof

**Answer: No.** The Tietze extension theorem cannot be applied to guarantee exte
nsion of a continuous function on a closed subset of a non-normal space. In fact
, non-normality is *precisely* the obstruction: a space fails to be normal if an
d only if there exists a closed subset and a continuous real-valued function on
it that admits no continuous extension.

---

### The Tietze Extension Theorem (equivalence form)

A topological space $X$ is **normal** if and only if for every closed subset $A
\subseteq X$ and every continuous function $f : A \to \mathbb{R}$, there exists
a continuous extension $F : X \to \mathbb{R}$ with $F|_A = f$.

The forward direction (normality $\Rightarrow$ extendability) is the classical T
ietze theorem. We prove the **converse**, which is what the question requires.

---

### Key implication: failure of extension $\Rightarrow$ non-normality (contrapos
itive)

**Claim.** If $X$ is **not** normal, then there exists a closed subset $C \subse
teq X$ and a continuous function $f : C \to \mathbb{R}$ that has **no** continuo
us extension to $X$.

**Proof of the claim.**

Since $X$ is not normal, there exist two **disjoint closed** sets $A, B \subsete
q X$ that cannot be separated by disjoint open neighborhoods. That is, there are
 no open sets $U \supseteq A$, $V \supseteq B$ with $U \cap V = \varnothing$.

Set $C := A \cup B$. Since $A$ and $B$ are closed in $X$, their finite union $C$
 is closed in $X$.

Define $f : C \to \mathbb{R}$ by

$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } x \in A, \\ 1 & \text{if } x \in B. \end{cas
es}
$$

**$f$ is continuous.** The sets $A$ and $B$ are closed in $X$, hence closed in t
he subspace $C$. They are also open in $C$ because $B = C \setminus A$ is closed
 (so $A$ is open in $C$) and $A = C \setminus B$ is closed (so $B$ is open in $C
$). Thus $\{A, B\}$ is a separation of $C$ into two clopen pieces, and $f$ is co
nstant on each piece. For any open $O \subseteq \mathbb{R}$:

- $0, 1 \notin O \Rightarrow f^{-1}(O) = \varnothing$,
- $0 \in O,\; 1 \notin O \Rightarrow f^{-1}(O) = A$,
- $1 \in O,\; 0 \notin O \Rightarrow f^{-1}(O) = B$,
- $0, 1 \in O \Rightarrow f^{-1}(O) = C$,

all of which are open in $C$. So $f$ is continuous.

**$f$ has no continuous extension.** Suppose, for contradiction, that $F : X \to
 \mathbb{R}$ is continuous with $F|_C = f$. Then $F(A) = \{0\}$ and $F(B) = \{1\
}$. Consider the open sets

$$
U := F^{-1}\!\bigl((-1/2,\, 1/2)\bigr), \qquad V := F^{-1}\!\bigl((1/2,\, 3/2)\b
igr).
$$

By continuity of $F$, both $U$ and $V$ are open in $X$. Since $A \subseteq U$ an
d $B \subseteq V$, and $(-1/2, 1/2) \cap (1/2, 3/2) = \varnothing$, we have $U \
cap V = \varnothing$. This gives disjoint open neighborhoods separating $A$ and
$B$—**contradicting** the assumption that $A$ and $B$ cannot be separated.

Hence no such extension $F$ exists. $\square$

### Conclusion

The Tietze extension theorem is an **equivalence**: the extension property for a
ll continuous real-valued functions on closed subsets holds *exactly* in normal
spaces. Therefore:

- In a **non-normal** space, the theorem's hypothesis (normality) is not satisfi
ed, and the claim above shows the conclusion genuinely **fails**—there exists a
closed subset $C$ and a continuous $f : C \to \mathbb{R}$ with no continuous ext
ension to $X$.
- The theorem thus **cannot be applied** to guarantee extension in a non-normal
space; non-normality is exactly the condition under which some extension must fa
il.

(Note: a *particular* function on a closed subset of a non-normal space may stil
l extend—the theorem does not say every function fails. But the theorem cannot b
e *invoked* to guarantee it, and in every non-normal space at least one such fun
ction provably fails to extend.)
