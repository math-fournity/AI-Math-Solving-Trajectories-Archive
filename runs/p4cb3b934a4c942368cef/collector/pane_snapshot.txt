$$F(s \cdot F(t)) = t \cdot F(s) \quad \text{for all } s, t > 0. \tag{$\star$}$$

Condition (b) becomes: $\dfrac{F(s)-1}{s-1}$ is strictly increasing on $(0,1)$ a
nd on $(1,\infty)$.

### Step 2: Determine $F(1)$

Set $t=1$ in $(\star)$: $F(s \cdot F(1)) = F(s)$ for all $s > 0$. Let $c = F(1)
> 0$.

Set $s=1$ in $(\star)$: $F(F(t)) = ct$ for all $t > 0$.

Apply $F$ to $F(F(t)) = ct$: $F(F(F(t))) = F(ct)$. But $F(F(u)) = cu$ with $u =
F(t)$ gives $F(F(F(t))) = cF(t)$. So $F(ct) = cF(t)$. Combined with $F(ct) = F(c
 \cdot t) = F(t)$ (from $F(sc)=F(s)$ with $s=t$), we get $cF(t) = F(t)$, hence $
c=1$ since $F(t)>0$.

### Step 3: Log-space reduction to additive involutions

$$g(p + g(q)) = q + g(p) \quad \text{for all } p, q \in \mathbb{R}. \tag{$\star\
star$}$$

- Setting $p = 0$: $g(g(q)) = q + g(0)$. Since $g(0) = \ln F(1) = \ln 1 = 0$, we

- Setting $p = g(r)$: $g(g(r) + g(q)) = q + r$. Applying $g$: $g(r) + g(q) = g(q
+r)$.

So **$g$ is additive** and **$g \circ g = \mathrm{id}$**.

### Step 4: Condition (b) forces $g = \pm\mathrm{id}$

Define $h(a) = \dfrac{e^{g(a)}-1}{e^a - 1}$. Condition (b) says $h$ is strictly
increasing on $(-\infty, 0)$ and $(0, \infty)$.

As a $\mathbb{Q}$-linear involution, $g$ has eigenvalues $\pm 1$. Let $V_+ = \{a

If $g \neq \mathrm{id}$ and $g \neq -\mathrm{id}$, then both $V_+$ and $V_-$ are

mathbb{R}$.

- For $a \in V_+$: $h(a) = (e^a-1)/(e^a-1) = 1$.
- For $a \in V_-$: $h(a) = (e^{-a}-1)/(e^a-1) = -e^{-a}$.

Since $V_+$ is dense in $(0,\infty)$, $h$ equals $1$ on a dense subset of $(0,\i
nfty)$. But a strictly increasing function on an interval cannot be constant on
a dense subset (any two points $a_1 < a_2$ in $V_+ \cap (0,\infty)$ would give $
h(a_1) = h(a_2) = 1$, contradicting strict increase). Contradiction.

Therefore $g = \mathrm{id}$ or $g = -\mathrm{id}$.
