# Proof: Non-Uniqueness of Continuous Extension of Factorial

**Question.** Can the factorial function be uniquely extended to non-integer val
ues while maintaining continuity?

**Answer.** No. Continuity alone does not determine a unique extension.

---

## Setup

Let $f:\mathbb{N}_0\to\mathbb{R}$ be the factorial, $f(n)=n!$. An *extension* of
 $f$ to non-integer values is a function $F$ defined on (say) $[0,\infty)$ such
that $F(n)=n!$ for every $n\in\mathbb{N}_0$. We ask whether, among all **continu
ous** such $F$, there is exactly one.

## A canonical continuous extension exists

The Gamma function $\Gamma:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}$ is continuous (in fact real-
analytic) and satisfies
$$\Gamma(n+1)=n!\qquad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}_0.$$
So $F_0(x):=\Gamma(x+1)$ is one continuous extension of factorial to $[0,\infty)
$ (with $F_0(0)=\Gamma(1)=1=0!$).

## The extension is not unique

The key observation: if $h:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}$ is **any** continuous functi
on vanishing at every non-negative integer, then
$$F(x):=\Gamma(x+1)+h(x)$$
is again a continuous extension of factorial, since
$$F(n)=\Gamma(n+1)+h(n)=n!+0=n!.$$

It remains to exhibit a non-zero such $h$. Take
$$h(x)=\sin(\pi x).$$
This is continuous on $[0,\infty)$, and $h(n)=\sin(\pi n)=0$ for every integer $
n\ge 0$, yet $h$ is not identically zero (e.g. $h(1/2)=1$).

Therefore
$$F(x)=\Gamma(x+1)+\sin(\pi x)$$
is a continuous function on $[0,\infty)$ with $F(n)=n!$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}_
0$, and $F\neq\Gamma(x+1)$ (they differ, for instance, at $x=1/2$).

More generally, for **any** continuous $h$ vanishing on $\mathbb{N}_0$ — and the
re are infinitely many linearly independent such functions (e.g. $\sin(\pi x)$,
$\sin(2\pi x)$, $x\sin(\pi x)$, …) — we get a distinct continuous extension. Hen
ce there are **infinitely many** continuous extensions, not one.

## Why uniqueness fails, and what rescues it

The obstruction is structural: the integers form a **discrete** subset of $[0,\i
nfty)$, so specifying values on $\mathbb{N}_0$ leaves the function completely fr
ee on each open interval $(n,n+1)$. Continuity only pins down the values at the
endpoints of each interval; it says nothing about the interior. Any continuous "
wiggle" supported inside $(n,n+1)$ and vanishing at $n$ and $n+1$ can be added w
ithout breaking either continuity or agreement with factorial on $\mathbb{N}_0$.

Uniqueness **is** recovered by imposing a stronger condition. The **Bohr–Molleru
p theorem** states that $\Gamma$ is the *unique* extension $F$ of factorial to $
(0,\infty)$ satisfying:

1. $F(x)>0$ for all $x>0$,
2. $\log F$ is **convex** (i.e. $F$ is log-convex),
3. $F(n+1)=n!$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}_0$.

Log-convexity is a genuine constraint on the interior of each interval $(n,n+1)$
 — it forces the graph of $\log F$ to lie below its chords — and this is exactly
 what rules out the $\sin(\pi x)$-type perturbations. Mere continuity imposes no
 such interior constraint.

## Conclusion

The factorial function **cannot** be uniquely extended to non-integer values und
er the sole requirement of continuity: there are infinitely many continuous exte
nsions, obtained by adding to $\Gamma(x+1)$ any continuous function vanishing on
 the non-negative integers. Uniqueness requires a stronger regularity condition,
 such as log-convexity (Bohr–Mollerup).
