I'll solve this directly. Note: the problem uses the term "$S$-differentiable" w
ithout defining it. The standard meaning (Schwarz / symmetric derivative) is:

$$f^S(x_0) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0-h)}{2h}.$$

A function is **$S$-differentiable** at $x_0$ if this limit exists (as a finite
number). I proceed under this standard definition.

---

## Proof

**Claim.** There exist functions that are $S$-differentiable (at every point, ev
en) but not differentiable in the usual sense.

**Construction.** Define
$$f(x)=|x|.$$

### Step 1. $f$ is $S$-differentiable everywhere.

- **At $x_0=0$:** for every $h\neq 0$,
$$\frac{f(0+h)-f(0-h)}{2h}=\frac{|h|-|{-h}|}{2h}=\frac{|h|-|h|}{2h}=\frac{0}{2h}
=0.$$
Hence $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(h)-f(-h)}{2h}=0$, so $f^S(0)=0$.

- **At $x_0\neq 0$:** $f$ is (ordinarily) differentiable in a neighborhood of $x
_0$, with $f'(x_0)=\operatorname{sgn}(x_0)$. Whenever the ordinary derivative ex
ists, the symmetric derivative exists and equals it:
$$\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0-h)}{2h}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}+\frac{
f(x_0)-f(x_0-h)}{h}\Bigr)\xrightarrow[h\to 0]{}\frac{f'(x_0)+f'(x_0)}{2}=f'(x_0)
.$$
So $f^S(x_0)=\operatorname{sgn}(x_0)$ for $x_0\neq 0$.

Thus $f$ is $S$-differentiable at **every** point of $\mathbb{R}$.

### Step 2. $f$ is not differentiable at $0$ in the usual sense.

The one-sided derivatives at $0$ are
$$\lim_{h\to 0^+}\frac{|h|-|0|}{h}=\lim_{h\to 0^+}\frac{h}{h}=1,\qquad \lim_{h\t
o 0^-}\frac{|h|-|0|}{h}=\lim_{h\to 0^-}\frac{-h}{h}=-1.$$
Since $1\neq -1$, the two-sided limit $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(h)-f(0)
}{h}$ does not exist. Hence $f$ is **not** differentiable at $0$.

### Step 3. Conclusion.

The function $f(x)=|x|$ is $S$-differentiable on all of $\mathbb{R}$ yet fails t
o be differentiable at $0$. Therefore such functions exist. $\blacksquare$

---

**Remark (sharpness).** The gap is dramatic: $S$-differentiability does not even
 imply continuity. The function
$$g(x)=\begin{cases}1,&x\neq 0,\\ 0,&x=0\end{cases}$$
satisfies $\frac{g(h)-g(-h)}{2h}=\frac{1-1}{2h}=0$ at $0$, so $g^S(0)=0$, yet $g
$ is discontinuous (hence non-differentiable) at $0$. This shows the symmetric d
erivative ignores the value $f(x_0)$ entirely — it only "sees" the behavior of $
f$ away from $x_0$ on both sides symmetrically — which is precisely why it can e
xist where the ordinary derivative cannot.
