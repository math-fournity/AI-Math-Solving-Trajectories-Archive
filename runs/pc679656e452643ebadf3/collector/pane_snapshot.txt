## Solution

The given condition is precisely the definition that **$F$ is an indefinite inte
gral of $f$** (in the sense of the Riemann integral, since $\delta$ is a constan
t rather than a gauge). We conclude:

> **$f$ is Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$, and $F(x) = F(a) + \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$
for every $x \in [a,b]$. Equivalently, $F$ is an indefinite integral of $f$, and
 $F' = f$ almost everywhere.**

---

### Proof

**Step 1: $f$ is Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$ with $\int_a^b f = F(b)-F(a)$.**

Fix $\varepsilon > 0$ and let $\delta > 0$ be as given. Let $\pi = \{([u_i,v_i],
 w_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ be any partition of $[a,b]$ (a subpartition that covers all of
$[a,b]$) with mesh $\max_i(v_i - u_i) < \delta$. By the hypothesis,

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n \bigl|F(v_i) - F(u_i) - f(w_i)(v_i - u_i)\bigr| < \varepsilon.
$$

By the triangle inequality,

$$
\left|\sum_{i=1}^n \bigl(F(v_i)-F(u_i)\bigr) - \sum_{i=1}^n f(w_i)(v_i-u_i)\righ
t|
\le \sum_{i=1}^n \bigl|F(v_i)-F(u_i)-f(w_i)(v_i-u_i)\bigr| < \varepsilon.
$$

The first sum telescopes:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n \bigl(F(v_i)-F(u_i)\bigr) = F(b) - F(a).
$$

Hence

$$
\left|F(b) - F(a) - \sum_{i=1}^n f(w_i)(v_i - u_i)\right| < \varepsilon
$$

for **every** tagged partition $\pi$ of $[a,b]$ with mesh $< \delta$, regardless
 of the choice of tags $w_i$. This is exactly the Riemann integrability criterio
n: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such that every Riemann
 sum with mesh $< \delta$ is within $\varepsilon$ of the constant $F(b)-F(a)$. T
herefore $f$ is Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$ and

$$
\int_a^b f(t)\,dt = F(b) - F(a). \tag{1}
$$

**Step 2: $F(x) = F(a) + \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ for every $x \in [a,b]$.**

Fix any $x \in [a,b]$. Any subpartition of $[a,x]$ is also a subpartition of $[a
,b]$, so the hypothesis applies with the same $\delta$. Repeating the argument o
f Step 1 on the interval $[a,x]$ (using partitions of $[a,x]$ with mesh $< \delt
a$) gives

$$
\int_a^x f(t)\,dt = F(x) - F(a), \qquad \forall\, x \in [a,b]. \tag{2}
$$

Thus $F$ is an **indefinite integral** of $f$:

$$
F(x) = F(a) + \int_a^x f(t)\,dt. \tag{3}
$$

**Step 3: $F' = f$ almost everywhere.**

Since $f$ is Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$, it is bounded and, by **Lebesgue's c
riterion**, continuous almost everywhere (its set of discontinuities has measure
 zero). At every point $x_0 \in (a,b)$ where $f$ is continuous, the indefinite i
ntegral is differentiable with derivative equal to $f(x_0)$:

$$
F'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{F(x_0+h)-F(x_0)}{h} = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{1}{h}\i
nt_{x_0}^{x_0+h} f(t)\,dt = f(x_0),
$$

where the last equality uses the continuity of $f$ at $x_0$. Since $f$ is contin
uous a.e., we obtain

$$
F'(x) = f(x) \quad \text{for almost every } x \in [a,b]. \tag{4}
$$

**Step 4: $F$ is absolutely continuous.**

Since $f$ is Riemann integrable it is bounded: $|f| \le M$ for some $M$. From (3
), for any finite collection of disjoint subintervals $[u_j, v_j] \subset [a,b]$
,

$$
\sum_j |F(v_j) - F(u_j)| = \sum_j \left|\int_{u_j}^{v_j} f(t)\,dt\right| \le M \
sum_j (v_j - u_j).
$$

Thus $F$ is Lipschitz (hence absolutely continuous), confirming that $F$ is a ge
nuine indefinite integral of a Lebesgue-integrable (in fact Riemann-integrable)
function.

---

### Conclusion

The hypothesis is the defining property of an **indefinite integral** in the Rie
mann sense. We conclude:

1. **$f$ is Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$.**
2. **$F$ is an indefinite integral of $f$**: $\displaystyle F(x) = F(a) + \int_a
^x f(t)\,dt$ for all $x \in [a,b]$.
3. **$F' = f$ almost everywhere** on $[a,b]$.
4. **$F$ is absolutely continuous** (indeed Lipschitz, since $f$ is bounded).

In short, **$f$ is the derivative of $F$ almost everywhere, and $F$ is the indef
inite integral of $f$**.
