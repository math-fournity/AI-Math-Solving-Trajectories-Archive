## Proof

We construct a Lebesgue measurable set that is not Borel using the Cantor functi
on.

**Step 1: The Cantor function.** Let $C \subset [0,1]$ be the middle-thirds Cant
or set, with Lebesgue measure $|C|=0$. Let $g:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ be the Cantor funct
ion (devil's staircase), extended to all of $\mathbb{R}$ by $g(x)=0$ for $x<0$ a
nd $g(x)=1$ for $x>1$. The function $g$ is continuous, non-decreasing, and const
ant on each component interval of $[0,1]\setminus C$; moreover $g(C)=[0,1]$.

**Step 2: A homeomorphism.** Define
$$f(x)=g(x)+x,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.$$
Since $g$ is non-decreasing and $x\mapsto x$ is strictly increasing, $f$ is stri
ctly increasing; being continuous, $f$ is a homeomorphism of $\mathbb{R}$ onto i
ts image. In fact $f([0,1])=[0,2]$, so $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is a homeomor
phism (one checks $f$ is onto by the extension of $g$).

**Step 3: Measure of $f(C)$.** On each component $(a,b)$ of $[0,1]\setminus C$,
$g$ is constant, so $f$ maps $(a,b)$ to $(g(a)+a,\,g(b)+b)$, an interval of leng
th $b-a$. Hence $f$ preserves the length of each complementary interval, and
$$|f([0,1]\setminus C)|=|[0,1]\setminus C|=1.$$
Since $f([0,1])=[0,2]$ has measure $2$ and $f([0,1])=f(C)\sqcup f([0,1]\setminus
 C)$ (disjoint union),
$$|f(C)|=2-1=1.$$
Also $f(C)$ is compact (continuous image of compact), hence Borel, and $f(C)\sub
set[0,2]$.

**Step 4: A non-measurable subset of $f(C)$.** Since $|f(C)|=1>0$, the set $f(C)
$ contains a subset $V\subset f(C)$ that is **not Lebesgue measurable**. (This i
s the standard fact that every set of positive Lebesgue measure contains a non-m
easurable subset: by Steinhaus' theorem $f(C)-f(C)$ contains an interval around
$0$, and the usual Vitali equivalence-class argument restricted to $f(C)$ produc
es a non-measurable $V\subset f(C)$.)

**Step 5: The desired set.** Define
$$E:=f^{-1}(V)\subset C.$$

- **$E$ is Lebesgue measurable:** Since $E\subset C$ and $|C|=0$, completeness o
f Lebesgue measure gives $|E|=0$; in particular $E$ is Lebesgue measurable.

- **$E$ is not Borel:** Suppose for contradiction that $E$ were Borel. Since $f$
 is a homeomorphism, it maps Borel sets to Borel sets (the preimage of a Borel s
et under the continuous map $f^{-1}$ is Borel, i.e. $f(\text{Borel})=\text{Borel
}$). Thus
$$V=f(E)$$
would be Borel, hence Lebesgue measurable — contradicting the choice of $V$ as n
on-measurable.

Therefore $E=f^{-1}(V)$ is a Lebesgue measurable subset of $\mathbb{R}$ (indeed
of measure $0$) that is **not** Borel measurable. $\blacksquare$
