## Proof

Let $f \in L^2(\mathbb{R})$. We prove that for almost every $b \in \mathbb{R}$,

$$\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{2t}\int_{b-t}^{b+t}|f(x)-f(b)|^2\,dx = 0.$$

**Key identity.** Expanding the square and using linearity of the integral,

$$\frac{1}{2t}\int_{b-t}^{b+t}|f(x)-f(b)|^2\,dx = \frac{1}{2t}\int_{b-t}^{b+t}|f
(x)|^2\,dx \;-\; 2f(b)\cdot\frac{1}{2t}\int_{b-t}^{b+t}f(x)\,dx \;+\; f(b)^2. \t
ag{1}$$

**Two applications of the Lebesgue differentiation theorem.**

*First term.* Since $f\in L^2(\mathbb{R})$, we have $|f|^2\in L^1(\mathbb{R})$.
By the Lebesgue differentiation theorem, for almost every $b\in\mathbb{R}$,

$$\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{2t}\int_{b-t}^{b+t}|f(x)|^2\,dx = |f(b)|^2. \tag{2}$$

*Second term.* Since $f\in L^2(\mathbb{R})$ and every bounded interval has finit
e measure, Cauchy–Schwarz gives $f\in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})$. Applying
the Lebesgue differentiation theorem to $f$ (which is locally integrable), for a
lmost every $b\in\mathbb{R}$,

$$\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{2t}\int_{b-t}^{b+t}f(x)\,dx = f(b). \tag{3}$$

**Conclusion.** Let $E\subset\mathbb{R}$ be the set of points $b$ at which **bot
h** (2) and (3) hold. Each of (2) and (3) holds a.e., so their intersection $E$
has full measure: $\mathbb{R}\setminus E$ is null.

For every $b\in E$, taking $t\to 0$ in the identity (1) and using (2) and (3),

$$\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{2t}\int_{b-t}^{b+t}|f(x)-f(b)|^2\,dx = |f(b)|^2 - 2f(b)\
cdot f(b) + f(b)^2 = |f(b)|^2 - 2|f(b)|^2 + |f(b)|^2 = 0.$$

Hence the desired limit equals $0$ for every $b\in E$, i.e. for almost every $b\
in\mathbb{R}$. $\blacksquare$
