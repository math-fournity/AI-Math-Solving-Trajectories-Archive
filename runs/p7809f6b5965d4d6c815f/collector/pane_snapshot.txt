## Proof

**Answer: Yes, it is always possible.** In fact, *any* choice of $M$ distinct in
dices works.

### Setup

Let $x_j = q_{k_j}$ for $j=1,\dots,M$. Since $\{q_k\}$ is strictly decreasing, a
ll $q_k$ are distinct, so any $M$ distinct indices give $M$ distinct positive va
lues $x_1,\dots,x_M$. The matrix has entries $A_{ij}=x_j^{t_i}$.

### Step 1: Reduction

Factor $x_j^{t_1}$ out of column $j$:

$$\det(A)=\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^M x_j^{t_1}\Bigr)\det(B),$$

where $B_{ij}=x_j^{s_i}$ with $s_i=t_i-t_1$. Since $t_1<t_2<\cdots<t_M$, we have
 $0=s_1<s_2<\cdots<s_M$. As $x_j>0$, the prefactor is positive, so $\det(A)\neq
0\iff\det(B)\neq 0$.

### Step 2: Substitution

Set $x_j=e^{u_j}$ (so $u_j=\ln x_j\in\mathbb{R}$, and the $u_j$ are distinct sin
ce the $x_j$ are). Then $B_{ij}=e^{s_i u_j}$, and we need $\det(e^{s_i u_j})\neq
 0$.

### Step 3: Exponential polynomials have at most $M-1$ zeros

**Claim.** An exponential polynomial $g(u)=\sum_{i=1}^M c_i\,e^{s_i u}$ with dis
tinct real exponents $s_1<\cdots<s_M$ has at most $M-1$ real zeros (counting mul
tiplicity) unless all $c_i=0$.

**Proof by induction on $M$.**

*Base case* ($M=1$): $c_1 e^{s_1 u}=0$ has no zeros unless $c_1=0$. ✓

*Inductive step:* Assume the result for $M-1$. Suppose $g$ has $M$ zeros at $u_1
<\cdots<u_M$. Define

$$h(u)=e^{-s_1 u}\,g(u)=c_1+\sum_{i=2}^M c_i\,e^{(s_i-s_1)u}.$$

Since $e^{-s_1 u}\neq 0$, $h$ has the same $M$ zeros. By Rolle's theorem, $h'(u)
=\sum_{i=2}^M c_i(s_i-s_1)\,e^{(s_i-s_1)u}$ has at least $M-1$ zeros. But $h'$ i
s an exponential polynomial with $M-1$ terms and distinct positive exponents $s_
i-s_1$. By the inductive hypothesis, $h'$ has at most $M-2$ zeros unless all its
 coefficients vanish. Two cases:

- **All $c_i(s_i-s_1)=0$ for $i\ge 2$:** Since $s_i\neq s_1$, this forces $c_i=0
$ for $i\ge 2$. Then $g=c_1 e^{s_1 u}$, which has $M\ge 1$ zeros only if $c_1=0$
.
- **$h'$ has $\ge M-1>M-2$ zeros:** This contradicts the inductive hypothesis.

In both cases all $c_i=0$. ✓

### Step 4: Conclusion

The claim shows that the functions $\{e^{s_i u}\}_{i=1}^M$ are linearly independ
ent when evaluated at any $M$ distinct points $u_1,\dots,u_M$. Equivalently, $\d
et(e^{s_i u_j})\neq 0$ for any distinct $u_j$.

Therefore $\det(B)\neq 0$, and

$$\det(A)=\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^M x_j^{t_1}\Bigr)\cdot\det(B)\neq 0.$$

Since $\{q_k\}$ is strictly decreasing (hence all values distinct) and positive,
 **every** selection of $M$ distinct indices $k_1,\dots,k_M$ yields a full-rank
matrix. $\blacksquare$
