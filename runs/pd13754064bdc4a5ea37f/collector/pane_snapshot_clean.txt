## Proof

**Step 1: Identify the roots and simplify the splitting field.**

The roots of $f(X) = (X^3-3)(X^2+3)(X^2+X+1)$ are:
- From $X^3 - 3$: $\sqrt[3]{3},\; \omega\sqrt[3]{3},\; \omega^2\sqrt[3]{3}$, whe
re $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3} = \frac{-1+i\sqrt{3}}{2}$.
- From $X^2 + 3$: $\pm\, i\sqrt{3}$.
- From $X^2 + X + 1$: $\omega,\; \omega^2$.

Since $\omega = \frac{-1+i\sqrt{3}}{2}$, we have $i\sqrt{3} = 2\omega + 1 \in \m
athbb{Q}(\omega)$. Hence the splitting fields of $X^2+3$ and $X^2+X+1$ coincide
and equal $\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$. Therefore

$$L = \mathbb{Q}\!\left(\sqrt[3]{3},\,\omega\right).$$

**Step 2: Determine $[L:\mathbb{Q}]$.**

The polynomial $X^3 - 3$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ (Eisenstein at $p=3$),
 so $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{3}):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$. The minimal polynomial of $\omeg
a$ is $X^2+X+1$, so $[\mathbb{Q}(\omega):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$. Since $\mathbb{Q}(\sq
rt[3]{3}) \subset \mathbb{R}$ while $\mathbb{Q}(\omega) \not\subset \mathbb{R}$,
 we have $\omega \notin \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{3})$, so $[L:\mathbb{Q}] = 3 \cdot 2
 = 6$.

(Equivalently, the discriminant of $X^3-3$ is $-243$, which is not a square in $
\mathbb{Q}$, so $\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q}) \cong S_3$ and $[L:\mathbb{Q}]
=6$.)

**Step 3: Show $\alpha := \sqrt[3]{3} + \omega$ is primitive.**

Clearly $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) \subseteq L$. It suffices to show $[\mathbb{Q}(\alph
a):\mathbb{Q}] = 6$, i.e., $\alpha$ has 6 distinct conjugates over $\mathbb{Q}$.

The Galois group $S_3$ acts on $\{\sqrt[3]{3}, \omega\sqrt[3]{3}, \omega^2\sqrt[
3]{3}\}$ by permuting the three cube roots and on $\{\omega, \omega^2\}$ by conj
ugation (the sign of the permutation determines whether $\omega$ is fixed or sen
t to $\omega^2$). The six conjugates of $\alpha$ are:

$$\sqrt[3]{3}+\omega,\quad \sqrt[3]{3}+\omega^2,\quad \omega\sqrt[3]{3}+\omega,\
quad \omega\sqrt[3]{3}+\omega^2,\quad \omega^2\sqrt[3]{3}+\omega,\quad \omega^2\
sqrt[3]{3}+\omega^2.$$

We verify these are pairwise distinct. Setting any two equal and simplifying yie
lds an equation of the form $c_1\sqrt[3]{3} = c_2$ with $c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{Q}
(\omega)$. For instance:

- $\sqrt[3]{3}+\omega = \omega\sqrt[3]{3}+\omega^2$ gives $\sqrt[3]{3}(1-\omega)
=\omega^2-\omega=-\omega(1-\omega)$, hence $\sqrt[3]{3}=-\omega$, impossible sin
ce $\sqrt[3]{3}\in\mathbb{R}$ but $-\omega\notin\mathbb{R}$.
- $\sqrt[3]{3}+\omega^2 = \omega\sqrt[3]{3}+\omega$ gives $\sqrt[3]{3}(1-\omega)
=\omega-\omega^2=i\sqrt{3}$, hence $\sqrt[3]{3}=\frac{i\sqrt{3}}{1-\omega}=\frac
{i\sqrt{3}-1}{2}\notin\mathbb{R}$, contradiction.
- $\omega\sqrt[3]{3}+\omega = \omega^2\sqrt[3]{3}+\omega^2$ gives $\sqrt[3]{3}(\
omega-\omega^2)=\omega^2-\omega$, hence $\sqrt[3]{3}=-1$, contradiction.

Each of the remaining cases similarly leads to $\sqrt[3]{3}$ equaling a non-real
 element of $\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$ or a rational $\neq \sqrt[3]{3}$, all contradic
tions. (The key point: $\sqrt[3]{3}$ is real of degree 3, while every element of
 $\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ is non-real, and $\sqrt[3]{3}\notin\mat
hbb{Q}$.)

Therefore all six conjugates are distinct, so the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$
 over $\mathbb{Q}$ has degree 6. Since $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) \subseteq L$ and $[L:
\mathbb{Q}]=6$, we conclude

$$\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) = L.$$

**Conclusion.** A primitive element for $L$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is

$$\boxed{\sqrt[3]{3} + \omega}$$

where $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3} = \frac{-1+i\sqrt{3}}{2}$.
