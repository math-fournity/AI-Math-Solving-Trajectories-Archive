**Answer: Yes.** The simplest example is $A = M_2(\mathbb{C})$ with the normaliz

## Proof

### Setup and definitions

Let $A$ be a C*-algebra and $\varphi$ a **faithful state** on $A$ (a positive li
near functional with $\varphi(1)=1$ and $\varphi(a^*a)=0 \Rightarrow a=0$). The
induced norm is
$$\|a\|_\varphi := \varphi(a^*a)^{1/2}.$$
Faithfulness ensures this is a genuine norm (not merely a seminorm). It arises f
rom the inner product $\langle a, b\rangle_\varphi = \varphi(b^*a)$, which is a
true inner product since $\langle a,a\rangle_\varphi = 0 \iff \varphi(a^*a)=0 \i
ff a=0$.

### Part I: Existence — $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ with the normalized trace

Let $A = M_2(\mathbb{C})$ and $\varphi(a) = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(a)$.

- **Non-commutative**: $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ is non-commutative. ✓
- **Faithful state**: $\varphi$ is positive, normalized ($\varphi(I)=1$), and fa
ithful ($\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(a^*a)=0 \Rightarrow a=0$). ✓
- **Cauchy complete**: $A$ is finite-dimensional (dimension 4 over $\mathbb{C}$)
. Every norm on a finite-dimensional space is equivalent to every other and the
space is complete in every norm. In particular, $(A, \|\cdot\|_\varphi)$ is Cauc
hy complete. ✓

So such an algebra exists. $\square$

### Part II: Necessity — any such algebra must be finite-dimensional

Suppose $A$ is a C*-algebra with a faithful state $\varphi$ such that $(A, \|\cd
ot\|_\varphi)$ is Cauchy complete. We show $A$ is finite-dimensional.

**Step 1 (Norm equivalence via the Open Mapping Theorem).** Since $\varphi$ is a
 state, for every positive $b \in A$ we have $\varphi(b) \leq \|b\|$, hence
$$\|a\|_\varphi = \varphi(a^*a)^{1/2} \leq \|a^*a\|^{1/2} = \|a\| \quad \forall\
, a \in A.$$
So the identity map $\mathrm{id}:(A,\|\cdot\|)\to(A,\|\cdot\|_\varphi)$ is a bou
nded linear bijection between Banach spaces (both are complete: $(A,\|\cdot\|)$
because it is a C*-algebra, $(A,\|\cdot\|_\varphi)$ by assumption). By the **Bou
nded Inverse Theorem**, there exists $c>0$ with
$$\|a\| \leq c\,\|a\|_\varphi \quad \forall\, a\in A.$$
Thus $\|\cdot\|$ and $\|\cdot\|_\varphi$ are **equivalent norms**.

**Step 2 (Reflexivity).** The norm $\|\cdot\|_\varphi$ comes from the inner prod
uct $\langle a,b\rangle_\varphi = \varphi(b^*a)$, so $(A,\|\cdot\|_\varphi)$ is
a **Hilbert space**, hence **reflexive**. Since the norms are equivalent, $(A,\|
\cdot\|)$ is isomorphic to $(A,\|\cdot\|_\varphi)$ as a Banach space, so $(A,\|\
cdot\|)$ is also reflexive.

**Step 3 (Key Lemma: a reflexive C*-algebra is finite-dimensional).** We prove t
he contrapositive: every infinite-dimensional C*-algebra is non-reflexive.

*Proof of Lemma.* WLOG $A$ is unital (if not, unitize: $\widetilde{A}=A\oplus\ma
thbb{C}$ is reflexive iff $A$ is, and finite-dimensional iff $A$ is). Suppose $A
$ is infinite-dimensional.

**Case 1: $A$ has a self-adjoint element $h$ with infinite spectrum.** Then $C^*
(h,1)\cong C(\sigma(h))$ is an abelian C*-subalgebra, where $\sigma(h)\subset\ma
thbb{R}$ is an infinite compact set. Since $\sigma(h)$ is an infinite compact me
tric space, it contains a sequence of distinct points $(t_k)$ converging to some
 $t_0\in\sigma(h)$. By passing to a subsequence, we may assume the points are **
separated**: $\inf_{j\neq k}|t_k - t_j| > 0$ for each $k$ (construct inductively
, choosing $t_{k+1}$ with $|t_{k+1}-t_0| < \frac{1}{2}|t_k - t_0|$). Then there
exist pairwise disjoint open neighborhoods $U_k \ni t_k$, and by Urysohn's lemma
, continuous functions $f_k:\sigma(h)\to[0,1]$ with $f_k(t_k)=1$ and $\operatorn
ame{supp}(f_k)\subset U_k$. The $f_k$ have **pairwise disjoint supports**, so fo
r $(\alpha_k)\in c_0$:
$$\left\|\sum_{k=1}^\infty \alpha_k f_k\right\|_\infty = \sup_k |\alpha_k| = \|(
\alpha_k)\|_{c_0}.$$
This gives an **isometric embedding** $c_0 \hookrightarrow C(\sigma(h)) \hookrig
htarrow A$. Since $c_0$ is non-reflexive and a closed subspace of a reflexive sp
ace is reflexive, $A$ is **not reflexive**.

**Case 2: Every self-adjoint element of $A$ has finite spectrum.** Since $A$ is
infinite-dimensional and unital, $A\neq\mathbb{C}\cdot 1$. Pick a nonzero positi
ve $a_1\in A$ with $\|a_1\|=1$ and $a_1\neq 1$. Since $a_1$ has finite spectrum,
 $a_1 = \sum_j \lambda_j p_j$ where $\lambda_j > 0$ and $p_j$ are nonzero spectr
al projections in $A$ (continuous functional calculus). At least one $p_j$ satis
fies $p_j\neq 0, 1$; call it $p_1$. Then $p_1Ap_1$ and $(1-p_1)A(1-p_1)$ are C*-
subalgebras with $A \supseteq p_1Ap_1 \oplus (1-p_1)A(1-p_1)$, so at least one i
s infinite-dimensional. Repeating inside the infinite-dimensional piece, we obta
in an **infinite sequence of mutually orthogonal nonzero projections** $(p_n)_{n
\geq 1}$. The map
$$(\alpha_n)\in c_0 \;\mapsto\; \sum_{n=1}^\infty \alpha_n p_n \in A$$
is an isometric embedding (orthogonality gives $\|\sum_{n=1}^N \alpha_n p_n\| =
\max_{1\leq n\leq N}|\alpha_n|$, and convergence follows from $\alpha_n\to 0$).
So again $c_0\hookrightarrow A$, and $A$ is **not reflexive**. $\square$

**Step 4 (Conclusion).** By Step 2, $A$ is reflexive. By the Lemma, $A$ is finit
e-dimensional. $\square$

### Final statement

A C*-algebra that is Cauchy complete with respect to a faithful state norm exist
s if and only if it is **finite-dimensional**. The non-commutative examples are
precisely the finite-dimensional non-commutative C*-algebras — direct sums $\big
oplus_i M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})$ with at least one $n_i \geq 2$ — equipped with any
faithful state (e.g., a convex combination of normalized traces with all weights
 positive). The simplest example is $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ with $\varphi = \tfrac{1}{
2}\operatorname{tr}$.
