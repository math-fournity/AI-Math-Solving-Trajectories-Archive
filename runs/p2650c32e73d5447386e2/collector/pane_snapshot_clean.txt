## Solution

We evaluate
$$L = \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_1^{\infty} \frac{\sqrt{n+x}}{n}\,e^{-x}\sin(x)\,dx.
$$

**Step 1: Pointwise limit of the integrand.**

Rewrite the prefactor:
$$\frac{\sqrt{n+x}}{n} = \sqrt{\frac{n+x}{n^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}+\frac{x}{n^2
}}.$$

For every fixed $x\geq 1$,
$$\frac{1}{n}+\frac{x}{n^2}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,$$
so
$$\frac{\sqrt{n+x}}{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.$$

Since $e^{-x}\sin(x)$ is independent of $n$, the integrand converges pointwise t
o $0$ for every $x\geq 1$.

**Step 2: Dominating function.**

Using the subadditivity $\sqrt{a+b}\leq \sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}$ for $a,b\geq 0$,
$$\sqrt{n+x}\leq \sqrt{n}+\sqrt{x},$$
hence, for all $n\geq 1$ and $x\geq 1$,
$$\frac{\sqrt{n+x}}{n}\leq \frac{\sqrt{n}}{n}+\frac{\sqrt{x}}{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{
n}}+\frac{\sqrt{x}}{n}\leq 1+\sqrt{x}.$$

Therefore
$$\left|\frac{\sqrt{n+x}}{n}\,e^{-x}\sin(x)\right|\leq (1+\sqrt{x})\,e^{-x}=:g(x
).$$

The function $g(x)=(1+\sqrt{x})e^{-x}$ is integrable on $[1,\infty)$, since $e^{
-x}$ decays exponentially while $\sqrt{x}$ grows sub-polynomially. Concretely, $
(1+\sqrt{x})e^{-x}\leq C e^{-x/2}$ for some constant $C$ and all $x\geq 1$.

**Step 3: Apply the Dominated Convergence Theorem.**

The sequence of integrands $f_n(x)=\frac{\sqrt{n+x}}{n}e^{-x}\sin(x)$ satisfies:
- $f_n(x)\to 0$ pointwise on $[1,\infty)$,
- $|f_n(x)|\leq g(x)$ with $g\in L^1([1,\infty))$.

By the Dominated Convergence Theorem,
$$L=\int_1^{\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}f_n(x)\,dx=\int_1^{\infty}0\,dx=0.$$
