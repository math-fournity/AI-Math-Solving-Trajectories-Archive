Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the FTC holds for $f(x) = x
^\alpha \sin(1/x^\beta)$ when $0 < \alpha - \beta \leq 1$, given that $f'(0)$ do

**Answer: Yes, it is valid.**

**Proof.**

First, compute $f'$ on $(0,1]$:
$$f'(x) = \alpha x^{\alpha-1}\sin(1/x^\beta) - \beta x^{\alpha-\beta-1}\cos(1/x^
\beta).$$

**Integrability check.** Since $|f'(x)| \leq \alpha x^{\alpha-1} + \beta x^{\alp
ha-\beta-1}$, and
- $\int_0^1 x^{\alpha-1}\,dx < \infty$ iff $\alpha > 0$ ✓,
- $\int_0^1 x^{\alpha-\beta-1}\,dx < \infty$ iff $\alpha - \beta > 0$ ✓,

we confirm $f' \in L^1([0,1])$, consistent with the hypothesis.

**Continuity of $f$ at 0.** Since $\alpha > \beta > 0$ implies $\alpha > 0$, we
have $|f(x)| \leq x^\alpha \to 0 = f(0)$, so $f$ is continuous on $[0,1]$.

**Key argument.** Define
$$F(x) = \int_0^x f'(t)\,dt, \qquad g(x) = f(x) - F(x).$$

Since $f' \in L^1([0,1])$, the function $F$ is absolutely continuous (hence cont
inuous) on $[0,1]$, with $F(0) = 0$. Therefore:

1. **$g$ is continuous on $[0,1]$**: difference of two continuous functions.

2. **$g$ is differentiable on $(0,1)$ with $g' \equiv 0$**: Since $f'$ is contin
uous on $(0,1]$, the Lebesgue integral $F$ satisfies $F'(x) = f'(x)$ for every $
x \in (0,1)$ (a standard property: when the integrand is continuous at a point,
the integral is differentiable there with the integrand as derivative). Thus
$$g'(x) = f'(x) - F'(x) = f'(x) - f'(x) = 0 \quad \text{for all } x \in (0,1).$$

3. **Apply the Mean Value Theorem**: $g$ is continuous on $[0,x]$ and differenti
able on $(0,x)$ with $g' = 0$ everywhere. By the MVT, $g(x) - g(0) = g'(c)\cdot
x = 0$ for some $c \in (0,x)$. Hence $g(x) = g(0)$ for all $x \in [0,1]$.

4. **Evaluate at 0**: $g(0) = f(0) - F(0) = 0 - 0 = 0$.

Therefore $g \equiv 0$, which gives
$$f(x) = \int_0^x f'(t)\,dt \quad \text{for all } x \in [0,1].$$

**Why the non-existence of $f'(0)$ is irrelevant.** The Lebesgue integral $\int_
0^x f'$ is insensitive to the value of $f'$ at the single point $\{0\}$ (measure
 zero), so $f'$ need only be defined a.e. Moreover, the MVT requires differentia
bility only on the *open* interval $(0,x)$ and continuity on $[0,x]$—it does not
 require the derivative to exist at the endpoints. Thus the failure of $f'(0)$ t
o exist presents no obstruction.

**Remark on the distinction from the Cantor function.** One might worry about co
unterexamples like the Cantor function, which is continuous, has $f'=0$ a.e. wit
h $f' \in L^1$, yet is not AC and fails FTC. The crucial difference is that the
Cantor function is *not* differentiable everywhere on $(0,1)$—it fails to be dif
ferentiable on the Cantor set. In our problem, $f'$ exists at *every* point of $
(0,1]$, which is what makes the MVT argument work: $g' = 0$ holds *everywhere* o
n $(0,1)$, not just a.e., forcing $g$ to be constant.
