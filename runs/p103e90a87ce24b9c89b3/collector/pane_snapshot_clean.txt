## Solution

We seek the curve $y(x)$ on $[x_1,x_2]$, with fixed endpoints $y(x_1)=y_1$, $y(x
_2)=y_2$, and fixed total length
$$\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx = L,$$
that maximizes the area
$$A[y]=\int_{x_1}^{x_2}y\,dx.$$

### Step 1. Isoperimetric reduction

This is an **isoperimetric problem**. Introducing a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda
$ for the length constraint, the extremals of $A$ subject to the constraint are
stationary curves of the augmented functional
$$J[y]=\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\!\Bigl(y+\lambda\sqrt{1+y'^2}\Bigr)\,dx.$$

### Step 2. Euler–Lagrange / Beltrami identity

The integrand
$$F(y,y')=y+\lambda\sqrt{1+y'^2}$$
has no explicit dependence on $x$. Hence the Beltrami (first-integral) identity
holds:
$$F - y'\,\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} = C,\qquad C\text{ constant}.$$

Computing
$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}=\lambda\,\frac{y'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}},$$
we get
$$y+\lambda\sqrt{1+y'^2}-\lambda\frac{y'^2}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}=C
\;\Longrightarrow\;
y+\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}=C.$$

### Step 3. Solving the ODE

Set $u=C-y$, so $y'=-u'$. The first integral gives
$$\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{1+u'^2}}=u\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u'^2=\frac{\lambda^{2
}-u^{2}}{u^{2}}.$$

Separating variables:
$$\frac{u\,du}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-u^{2}}}=\pm\,dx.$$

Integrating (with $u=\lambda\cos\phi$),
$$-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-u^{2}}=\pm(x-a),$$
for a constant $a$. Squaring and substituting back $u=C-y$:
$$(x-a)^{2}+(C-y)^{2}=\lambda^{2}.$$

### Step 4. Identifying the curve

This is the equation of a **circle** of radius $R=|\lambda|$ centered at $(a,\,C
)$. Therefore the extremal is an **arc of a circle**:
$$(x-a)^{2}+(y-b)^{2}=R^{2},\qquad b:=C.$$

To *maximize* the area under the curve we select the **upper** arc, which bulges
 upward:
$$\boxed{\,y(x)=b+\sqrt{R^{2}-(x-a)^{2}}\,}.$$

### Step 5. Determining the constants

The three parameters $(a,b,R)$ are fixed by the three given conditions:
1. $y(x_1)=y_1$ : $(x_1-a)^2+(y_1-b)^2=R^2$,
2. $y(x_2)=y_2$ : $(x_2-a)^2+(y_2-b)^2=R^2$,
3. arc length: $\displaystyle\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx = R\bigl[\theta_2
-\theta_1\bigr]=L$, where $\theta_i$ is the angle subtended at the center by the
 endpoint $(x_i,y_i)$.

Equivalently, the chord from $(x_1,y_1)$ to $(x_2,y_2)$ has length $d=\sqrt{(x_2
-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}$, and the arc of length $L$ on a circle of radius $R$ subte
nds central angle $\Delta\theta=L/R$, with the chord–angle relation
$$d=2R\sin\!\Bigl(\frac{\Delta\theta}{2}\Bigr)=2R\sin\!\Bigl(\frac{L}{2R}\Bigr).
$$
This transcendental equation determines $R$; then $a,b$ follow from the two endp
oint equations. A solution exists precisely when $L\ge d$ (the length must at le
ast equal the chord), with the degenerate case $L=d$ giving the straight line se
gment.

### Step 6. Why this is a maximum

The augmented functional is regular, and the isoperimetric inequality (Dido's pr
oblem) guarantees that among all curves of fixed length joining two fixed points
, the one enclosing the greatest area with the chord is the circular arc. The Eu
ler–Lagrange analysis above shows the *only* smooth extremal is the circular arc
; combined with the classical isoperimetric inequality (or the second-variation
/ concavity argument on the augmented integrand), this extremal yields the **glo
bal maximum**.

### Conclusion

The curve of fixed length $L$ and fixed endpoints that maximizes the area under
it is an **arc of a circle**:
$$(x-a)^{2}+(y-b)^{2}=R^{2},\qquad y(x)=b+\sqrt{R^{2}-(x-a)^{2}},$$
with the constants $(a,b,R)$ determined by the two endpoint conditions and the f
ixed arc length $L$.
