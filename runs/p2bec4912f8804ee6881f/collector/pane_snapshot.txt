# Solution

**Claim.** An ideal $I$ of a commutative ring $R$ is a free $R$-module if and on
ly if $I = 0$ or $I$ is a principal ideal generated by a non-zero-divisor.

In particular, **yes**, $I$ *can* be a free $R$-module, but only in these specif
ic situations.

---

## Proof

### (⇐) Sufficiency

- **$I = 0$:** The zero module is free of rank $0$ (the empty set is a basis).

- **$I = (a)$ with $a$ a non-zero-divisor:** Define the $R$-linear map
$$\varphi : R \to I, \qquad \varphi(r) = ra.$$
  - *Surjectivity:* $\varphi$ hits every element of $I = (a)$ by definition.
  - *Injectivity:* If $\varphi(r) = 0$, then $ra = 0$. Since $a$ is a non-zero-d
ivisor, $r = 0$.

  Hence $\varphi$ is an isomorphism, so $I \cong R$ is free of rank $1$.

### (⇒) Necessity

Suppose $I$ is a nonzero free $R$-module, and let $\{x_j\}_{j \in J}$ be an $R$-
basis.

**Step 1: The basis has at most one element ($|J| \leq 1$).**

Assume for contradiction that $|J| \geq 2$, and pick two distinct basis elements
 $x_1, x_2$. Since $R$ is commutative and $I \subseteq R$, we may multiply eleme
nts of $I$ by elements of $R$ — and in particular by elements of $I$ itself. Con
sider the $R$-linear combination
$$x_2 \cdot x_1 \;+\; (-x_1)\cdot x_2 \;=\; x_2 x_1 - x_1 x_2 \;=\; 0,$$
where the last equality uses commutativity of $R$. This is a nontrivial relation
: the coefficient $x_2$ on $x_1$ is nonzero (basis elements are nonzero), and th
e coefficient $-x_1$ on $x_2$ is nonzero. This contradicts the linear independen
ce of the basis $\{x_j\}$.

Therefore $|J| = 1$, and $I$ is principal, say $I = (a)$ with $a \neq 0$.

**Step 2: The generator $a$ is a non-zero-divisor.**

Since $\{a\}$ is a basis, the map $\varphi: R \to I$, $r \mapsto ra$, is an isom
orphism (it is surjective since $I = (a)$, and injective by linear independence
of the singleton basis $\{a\}$: $ra = 0 \Rightarrow r = 0$). The injectivity con
dition is exactly the statement that $a$ is a non-zero-divisor. $\blacksquare$

---

## Remarks and Examples

1. **When $R$ is an integral domain**, every nonzero principal ideal $(a)$ is fr
ee (since every nonzero element is a non-zero-divisor). For instance, in $R = \m
athbb{Z}$, the ideal $(n) = n\mathbb{Z}$ is a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module of rank $
1$ for every $n \neq 0$.

2. **A non-principal ideal is never free** (over a commutative ring). For exampl
e, the ideal $(2, X) \subset \mathbb{Z}[X]$ is not principal, hence not a free $
\mathbb{Z}[X]$-module, even though $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ itself is a domain.

3. **A principal ideal generated by a zero-divisor is not free.** For example, i
n $R = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, the ideal $I = (2) = \{0, 2, 4\}$ is principal b
ut $2$ is a zero-divisor ($3 \cdot 2 = 0$). The relation $3 \cdot 2 = 0$ shows $
\{2\}$ is not linearly independent, and $|I| = 3 \neq 6 = |R|$ rules out $I \con
g R$, so $I$ is not free.

4. **The commutativity hypothesis is essential.** Over a noncommutative ring, th
e relation $x_2 x_1 - x_1 x_2 = 0$ need not hold, and free left ideals of rank $
> 1$ can exist (e.g., in matrix rings).
