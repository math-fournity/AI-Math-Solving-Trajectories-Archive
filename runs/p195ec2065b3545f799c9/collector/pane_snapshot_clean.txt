## Spectrum of the Multiplication Operator $B$

**Problem.** Determine the spectrum of $B: D \subset L^2(\mathbb{R}) \to L^2(\ma
thbb{R})$, where $D = \{g \in L^2(\mathbb{R}) : \int |yg(y)|^2\,dy < \infty\}$ a
nd $(Bg)(y) = yg(y)$.

---

### Proof

The operator $B$ is the **multiplication operator** $M_m$ by the function $m(y)
= y$ on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ (with Lebesgue measure), defined on its maximal domain
$$D = \{g \in L^2(\mathbb{R}) : m \cdot g \in L^2(\mathbb{R})\}.$$

We prove that $\sigma(B) = \mathbb{R}$.

---

**Step 1: $B$ is self-adjoint.**

Since $m(y) = y$ is real-valued and measurable, the multiplication operator $M_m
$ on its maximal domain $D$ is self-adjoint. (Standard fact: a multiplication op
erator by a real-valued function on its maximal domain is self-adjoint.) In part
icular, $B$ is closed and densely defined (e.g., $C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R}) \subset
 D$ is dense in $L^2(\mathbb{R})$).

Since $B$ is self-adjoint, $\sigma(B) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.

---

**Step 2: Every $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ belongs to $\sigma(B)$.**

Fix $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$. We show that $(B - \lambda I)^{-1}$ is not a bound
ed operator on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$, which means $\lambda \in \sigma(B)$.

For $\epsilon > 0$, define
$$g_\epsilon(y) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}} \mathbf{1}_{[\lambda - \epsilon,\,
\lambda + \epsilon]}(y).$$
Then $\|g_\epsilon\|_{L^2}^2 = \frac{1}{2\epsilon} \cdot 2\epsilon = 1$, so $\|g
_\epsilon\| = 1$.

Now $(B - \lambda I)g_\epsilon = (y - \lambda)g_\epsilon(y)$, and
$$\|(B - \lambda I)g_\epsilon\|^2 = \int_{\lambda-\epsilon}^{\lambda+\epsilon} \
frac{(y-\lambda)^2}{2\epsilon}\,dy = \frac{1}{2\epsilon}\cdot \frac{2\epsilon^3}
{3} = \frac{\epsilon^2}{3}.$$
So $\|(B - \lambda I)g_\epsilon\| = \frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{3}} \to 0$ as $\epsilo
n \to 0$.

If $(B - \lambda I)^{-1}$ were a bounded operator with bound $C$, we would have
$$1 = \|g_\epsilon\| = \|(B-\lambda I)^{-1}(B-\lambda I)g_\epsilon\| \le C \cdot
 \frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{3}} \to 0,$$
a contradiction. Therefore $(B - \lambda I)^{-1}$ is not bounded, and $\lambda \
in \sigma(B)$.

Since $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ was arbitrary, $\mathbb{R} \subseteq \sigma(B)$.

---

**Step 3: No $\lambda \in \mathbb{C} \setminus \mathbb{R}$ belongs to $\sigma(B)
$.**

Let $\lambda = a + bi$ with $b \neq 0$. Then $|y - \lambda|^2 = (y-a)^2 + b^2 \g
e b^2 > 0$ for all $y \in \mathbb{R}$, so the function $\frac{1}{y - \lambda}$ i
s bounded: $\left|\frac{1}{y-\lambda}\right| \le \frac{1}{|b|}$.

For $f \in L^2(\mathbb{R})$, define $(R_\lambda f)(y) = \frac{f(y)}{y - \lambda}
$. Then
$$\|R_\lambda f\|^2 = \int \frac{|f(y)|^2}{|y-\lambda|^2}\,dy \le \frac{1}{b^2}\
|f\|^2,$$
so $R_\lambda$ is a bounded operator with $\|R_\lambda\| \le 1/|b|$.

Moreover, $R_\lambda = (B - \lambda I)^{-1}$: for $g \in D$,
$$(B - \lambda I)(R_\lambda g) = (y - \lambda)\cdot \frac{g(y)}{y-\lambda} = g(y
),$$
and for $f \in L^2$, $R_\lambda f \in D$ since
$$\int |y|^2 \frac{|f(y)|^2}{|y-\lambda|^2}\,dy \le \frac{1}{b^2}\int |y|^2|f(y)
|^2\,dy < \infty \quad \text{(when } f \in D\text{)},$$
and one verifies $(B-\lambda I)^{-1} = R_\lambda$ on all of $L^2$ by the bounded
ness argument. Thus $\lambda \in \rho(B)$ (the resolvent set).

---

**Conclusion.**

Combining Steps 1–3:
$$\sigma(B) \subseteq \mathbb{R} \quad \text{(self-adjointness)}, \qquad \mathbb
{R} \subseteq \sigma(B) \quad \text{(Step 2)}, \qquad \mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb
{R} \subseteq \rho(B) \quad \text{(Step 3)}.$$

Therefore:
$$\boxed{\sigma(B) = \mathbb{R}.}$$
