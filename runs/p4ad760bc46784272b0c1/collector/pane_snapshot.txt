## Solution

The statement is **true**. It is a fundamental property of the Lebesgue integral
: the integral depends only on the a.e. equivalence class of the integrand. Belo
w is the proof.

---

**Theorem.** Let $f : (a,b) \to \overline{\mathbb{R}}$ be Lebesgue measurable. I
f $f = 0$ $\lambda$-a.e. on $(a,b)$, then for every $x \in (a,b)$,
$$\int_{(a,x)} f \, d\lambda = 0.$$

**Proof.**

Set $E := \{t \in (a,b) : f(t) \neq 0\}$. By hypothesis $\lambda(E) = 0$.

Decompose $f = f^+ - f^-$ where $f^+ = \max(f,0)$ and $f^- = \max(-f,0)$ are non
negative measurable functions. Since $f = 0$ on $(a,b)\setminus E$, both $f^+ =
0$ and $f^- = 0$ on $(a,b)\setminus E$; hence $f^+ = 0$ a.e. and $f^- = 0$ a.e.

**Claim.** If $g \geq 0$ is measurable and $g = 0$ a.e., then $\int g \, d\lambd
a = 0$.

*Proof of claim.* For each $n \in \mathbb{N}$, the set $A_n := \{g > 1/n\}$ sati
sfies $\lambda(A_n) = 0$ (since $A_n \subseteq \{g \neq 0\}$, a null set). Defin
e the simple functions $s_n := \tfrac{1}{n}\,\mathbf{1}_{A_n}$. Then $0 \leq s_n
 \leq g$ and
$$\int s_n \, d\lambda = \tfrac{1}{n}\,\lambda(A_n) = 0.$$
By monotone convergence, $\int g \, d\lambda = \sup_n \int s_n \, d\lambda = 0$.
 (Equivalently, using the layer-cake formula $\int g\,d\lambda = \int_0^\infty \
lambda(\{g > t\})\,dt = 0$ since $\lambda(\{g>t\})=0$ for every $t>0$.) $\square
$

Applying the claim to $f^+$ and $f^-$ (restricted to $(a,x)$, which preserves th
e a.e.-zero property since $\lambda((a,x)\cap E)=0$):
$$\int_{(a,x)} f^+ \, d\lambda = 0, \qquad \int_{(a,x)} f^- \, d\lambda = 0.$$

Both integrals are finite, so the integral of $f$ is well-defined and
$$\int_{(a,x)} f \, d\lambda = \int_{(a,x)} f^+ \, d\lambda - \int_{(a,x)} f^- \
, d\lambda = 0 - 0 = 0.$$

This holds for every $x \in (a,b)$. $\blacksquare$

---

**Remark.** The statement given in the problem is indeed true and follows direct
ly from the definition of the Lebesgue integral. It should be distinguished from
 the *actual* converse of the Lebesgue differentiation theorem — namely, "if $F'
(x)=0$ a.e., then $F$ is constant" — which is **false** in general (the Cantor f
unction provides a counterexample: it is continuous, non-constant, and has $F'=0
$ a.e.). The statement asked about here concerns the integral of an a.e.-vanishi
ng function, not the derivative of its primitive, and is unconditionally true.
