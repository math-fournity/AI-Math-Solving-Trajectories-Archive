$$Y = \{x \in X : P(\{x\}) > 0\} = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} S_n, \qquad S_n := \{x

The equality holds because $P(\{x\}) > 0$ if and only if there exists a positive

**Step 2: Each $S_n$ is finite.**

Since $P$ is a probability measure, $P(X) = 1$. Every element $x \in S_n$ satisf

, x_m$, then

$$1 = P(X) \geq P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{m} \{x_i\}\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} P(\{x
_i\}) \geq \frac{m}{n},$$

so $m \leq n$. Thus $|S_n| \leq n$; in particular $S_n$ is finite.

**Step 3: Each $S_n$ is measurable.**

Every singleton $\{x\}$ is measurable by hypothesis. A finite set is a finite un
ion of singletons, and $\Sigma$ is closed under finite unions. Hence $S_n \in \S
igma$.

**Step 4: $Y$ is measurable.**

$Y = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} S_n$ is a countable union of measurable sets. Since
$\Sigma$ is a $\sigma$-algebra, it is closed under countable unions. Therefore $
Y \in \Sigma$.

**Conclusion.** Yes, $Y$ is necessarily a measurable subset of $X$. The essentia
l reason is that a probability measure can put positive mass on at most countabl
y many singletons, and—since every singleton is measurable—any countable set of
singletons is measurable.
