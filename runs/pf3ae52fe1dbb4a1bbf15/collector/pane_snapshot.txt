**Answer: The statement is TRUE.**

**Proof.**

We use the standard recurrence for divided differences: for distinct points $x_0
, \dots, x_k$,

$$f[x_0, \dots, x_k] = \frac{f[x_1, \dots, x_k] - f[x_0, \dots, x_{k-1}]}{x_k -
x_0}.$$

**Step 1: The $n$-th divided difference is constant.**

The hypothesis states that $f[x_0, x_1, \dots, x_i] = 0$ for every $i \geq n+1$
and every choice of $i+1$ distinct points in $[a,b]$. In particular, for $i = n+
1$ (i.e., any $n+2$ distinct points), $f[x_0, \dots, x_{n+1}] = 0$.

Fix any $n+2$ distinct points $x_0, \dots, x_{n+1}$. By the recurrence,

$$0 = f[x_0, \dots, x_{n+1}] = \frac{f[x_1, \dots, x_{n+1}] - f[x_0, \dots, x_n]
}{x_{n+1} - x_0},$$

so $f[x_1, \dots, x_{n+1}] = f[x_0, \dots, x_n]$. Since the points were arbitrar
y, the value of the $n$-th divided difference $f[x_0, \dots, x_n]$ is **independ
ent of the choice** of the $n+1$ distinct points. Denote this constant value by
$c$.

**Step 2: Use the smoothness hypothesis to identify $f^{(n)}$.**

Since $f \in C^n([a,b])$, the generalized mean value theorem for divided differe
nces gives: for any $n+1$ distinct points $x_0, \dots, x_n$ in $[a,b]$, there ex
ists $\xi$ in the interval $(\min x_i, \max x_i)$ such that

$$f[x_0, \dots, x_n] = \frac{f^{(n)}(\xi)}{n!}.$$

Now fix any point $x \in (a,b)$. Choose $n+1$ distinct points $x_0^{(\varepsilon
)}, \dots, x_n^{(\varepsilon)}$ all lying in $(x-\varepsilon, x+\varepsilon)$ fo
r $\varepsilon \to 0^+$. The corresponding $\xi_\varepsilon$ lies in $(x-\vareps
ilon, x+\varepsilon)$, so $\xi_\varepsilon \to x$. By continuity of $f^{(n)}$,

$$c = f[x_0^{(\varepsilon)}, \dots, x_n^{(\varepsilon)}] = \frac{f^{(n)}(\xi_\va
repsilon)}{n!} \xrightarrow{\varepsilon \to 0} \frac{f^{(n)}(x)}{n!}.$$

(The first equality uses Step 1: the $n$-th divided difference is the constant $
c$ regardless of point selection.) Hence $f^{(n)}(x) = c \cdot n!$ for every $x
\in (a,b)$, and by continuity also at the endpoints.

**Step 3: Conclude $f$ is a polynomial of degree at most $n$.**

Since $f^{(n)}(x) = c \cdot n!$ is constant on $[a,b]$, integrating $n$ times yi
elds

$$f(x) = \frac{c \cdot n!}{n!} x^n + \text{(lower order terms)} = c\, x^n + a_{n
-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0,$$

a polynomial of degree at most $n$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The $C^n$ hypothesis is used only to pass from the constancy of the
$n$-th divided difference to the constancy of $f^{(n)}$ via the mean value theor
em. In fact, the conclusion holds for *any* function $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ (no
smoothness required): the vanishing of all $(n+1)$-th divided differences alone
forces $f$ to be a polynomial of degree $\leq n$, by a purely algebraic inductio
n on the divided-difference recurrence.
