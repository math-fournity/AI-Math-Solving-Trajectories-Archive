## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. If $f$ and $g$ are lower semi-continuous (l.s.c.) and $f+g$ is c
ontinuous, then both $f$ and $g$ must be continuous.

Let $h := f+g$, which is continuous. Fix an arbitrary point $x$.

**Step 1: A useful identity.** Since $h$ is continuous at $x$, $h(y)\to h(x)$ as
 $y\to x$. For any function $\varphi$, if $a_y\to a$ (finite), then
$$\liminf_{y\to x}\bigl(a_y+\varphi(y)\bigr)=a+\liminf_{y\to x}\varphi(y).$$
(Indeed, for $y$ near $x$, $a_y\in(a-\delta,a+\delta)$, so $\inf(a_y+\varphi)=a+
\inf\varphi+O(\delta)$, and letting $\delta\to 0$ gives the identity.) Applying
this with $a_y=h(y)$, $a=h(x)$, and $\varphi=-f$:
$$\liminf_{y\to x} g(y)=\liminf_{y\to x}\bigl(h(y)-f(y)\bigr)=h(x)-\limsup_{y\to
 x} f(y).$$

**Step 2: Combine the two lower semi-continuity inequalities.**

- $f$ l.s.c. at $x$ gives
$$\liminf_{y\to x} f(y)\ge f(x). \tag{1}$$

- $g$ l.s.c. at $x$ gives $\liminf_{y\to x} g(y)\ge g(x)=h(x)-f(x)$. Using Step
1,
$$h(x)-\limsup_{y\to x} f(y)\ge h(x)-f(x),$$
which simplifies to
$$\limsup_{y\to x} f(y)\le f(x). \tag{2}$$

**Step 3: Conclude.** Always $\liminf f\le\limsup f$. Chaining (1) and (2):
$$\liminf_{y\to x} f(y)\;\ge\; f(x)\;\ge\;\limsup_{y\to x} f(y)\;\ge\;\liminf_{y
\to x} f(y).$$
Hence equality holds throughout:
$$\liminf_{y\to x} f(y)=\limsup_{y\to x} f(y)=f(x).$$
So $f$ is continuous at $x$. Since $x$ was arbitrary, $f$ is continuous everywhe
re.

By symmetry (or by writing $f=h-g$ and repeating the argument with the roles of
$f$ and $g$ exchanged), $g$ is also continuous everywhere.

Therefore both $f$ and $g$ must be continuous. $\blacksquare$

### PROOF COMPLETE
