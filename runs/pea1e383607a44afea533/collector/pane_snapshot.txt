## Proof

Let $g: [0,1) \to \mathbb{R}$ denote the radial profile of $f$, defined by $g(r)
 = f(x)$ for any $x \in B(0,1)$ with $|x| = r$. This is well-defined by radial s
ymmetry. Equivalently, $g(r) = f(r, 0, \ldots, 0)$ for $r \in [0,1)$.

**Step 1: $g^{(i)}$ exists on $(0,1)$ for each $1 \leq i \leq k$.**

For $r \in (0,1)$, the point $(r, 0, \ldots, 0)$ lies in the interior of $B(0,1)
$. Since $f$ is $C^k$ on $B(0,1)$, the restriction $r \mapsto f(r, 0, \ldots, 0)
$ is $C^k$ on $(0,1)$ by the chain rule (the map $r \mapsto (r, 0, \ldots, 0)$ i
s smooth). Hence $g$ is $C^k$ on $(0,1)$, so $g^{(i)}$ exists on $(0,1)$ for all
 $1 \leq i \leq k$.

**Step 2: Identification of $g^{(i)}$ with partial derivatives of $f$.**

By the chain rule, for $r \in (0,1)$:
$$g'(r) = \frac{d}{dr} f(r, 0, \ldots, 0) = \partial_1 f(r, 0, \ldots, 0).$$
Applying induction, since the coordinates $x_2, \ldots, x_n$ are identically zer
o along this path:
$$g^{(i)}(r) = \partial_1^i f(r, 0, \ldots, 0), \quad \text{for all } 1 \leq i \
leq k,\ r \in (0,1).$$

**Step 3: The limits $\lim_{r \searrow 0} g^{(i)}(r)$ exist.**

Since $f$ is of class $C^k$ on $B(0,1)$, every partial derivative $\partial_1^i
f$ (for $0 \leq i \leq k$) is continuous on $B(0,1)$. In particular, $\partial_1
^i f$ is continuous at the origin.

By the identification in Step 2:
$$\lim_{r \searrow 0} g^{(i)}(r) = \lim_{r \searrow 0} \partial_1^i f(r, 0, \ldo
ts, 0) = \partial_1^i f(0, 0, \ldots, 0),$$
where the last equality uses the continuity of $\partial_1^i f$ at the origin.

Therefore, for each $1 \leq i \leq k$, the limit $\lim_{r \searrow 0} g^{(i)}(r)
$ exists and equals $\partial_1^i f(0)$.

**Conclusion.** The $C^k$-differentiability of the radially symmetric function $
f$ on $B(0,1) \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ can indeed be read off from the one-dimensio
nal radial profile $g$ on $[0,1)$: for each $1 \leq i \leq k$, the derivative $g
^{(i)}$ exists on $(0,1)$ and the limit $\lim_{r \searrow 0} g^{(i)}(r)$ exists
(equaling $\partial_1^i f(0)$). This holds for all $n \geq 1$.

The key observation is that the radial profile $g$ is simply the restriction of
$f$ to the positive $x_1$-axis, so its derivatives are the corresponding iterate
d $\partial_1$-partial derivatives of $f$ evaluated along that axis. Continuity
of these partial derivatives at the origin—guaranteed by $f \in C^k$—immediately
 yields the existence of the one-sided limits.
