## Proof

Let $X(t)$, $t \ge 0$, be a standard Brownian motion. We wish to compute

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{X(n)}{n}, \qquad n \in \mathbb{N}.$$

**Step 1: Represent $X(n)$ as a sum of i.i.d. increments.**

For each integer $n \ge 1$, write

$$X(n) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \bigl(X(k) - X(k-1)\bigr).$$

Define $Y_k := X(k) - X(k-1)$. By the definition of standard Brownian motion, th
e increments are independent and $Y_k \sim N(0,1)$ for every $k$. Hence $\{Y_k\}
_{k \ge 1}$ is an i.i.d. sequence with $\mathbb{E}[Y_1] = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[|Y_
1|] < \infty$.

**Step 2: Apply the Strong Law of Large Numbers.**

Since $\{Y_k\}$ is i.i.d. with finite first moment and mean $0$, the Strong Law
of Large Numbers gives

$$\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n} Y_k \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \mathbb{E}[Y_1] = 0 \q
quad (n \to \infty).$$

But $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n} Y_k = \frac{X(n)}{n}$, so

$$\frac{X(n)}{n} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} 0.$$

**Step 3: Conclude.**

Almost-sure convergence implies convergence in probability and in distribution,
so the limit is $0$ in every standard sense. Therefore

$$\boxed{\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{X(n)}{n} = 0 \quad \text{almost surely.}}$$
