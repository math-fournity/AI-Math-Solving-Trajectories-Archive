## Answer

**Yes** — under the standard meaning of "equivalent" (topologically equivalent,
i.e. inducing the same topology), the two metrics are equivalent. The hypothesis
 that they share the same Cauchy sequences forces them to share the same converg
ent sequences (with the same limits), and in metric spaces that forces the same
topology.

### Proof

**Step 1 — Same Cauchy sequences ⟹ same convergent sequences (with the same limi
ts).**

Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence in $X$ and $x \in X$. Construct the *interleaved* sequ
ence $(y_n)$ by
$$y_{2n}=x_n,\qquad y_{2n+1}=x\qquad(n\ge 0).$$

*Claim.* $(y_n)$ is Cauchy in $(X,d)$ if and only if $x_n\to x$ in $(X,d)$.

*Proof of claim.*
$(\Leftarrow)$ If $x_n\to x$, then for $\varepsilon>0$ pick $N$ with $d(x_n,x)<\
varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$. For $m,n\ge 2N$, each of $y_m,y_n$ is either $x$ itsel
f or some $x_k$ with $k\ge N$; in either case $d(y_m,y_n)\le d(y_m,x)+d(x,y_n)<2
\varepsilon$. Hence $(y_n)$ is Cauchy.
$(\Rightarrow)$ If $(y_n)$ is Cauchy, then consecutive terms tend to $0$: $d(y_{
2n},y_{2n+1})=d(x_n,x)\to 0$, so $x_n\to x$. $\square$

Now apply the hypothesis. Suppose $x_n\to x$ in $(X,d_1)$. Then $(y_n)$ is $d_1$
-Cauchy, hence (by hypothesis) $d_2$-Cauchy, hence (by the claim) $x_n\to x$ in
$(X,d_2)$. The reverse implication follows by symmetry. Therefore
$$x_n\to x \text{ in } (X,d_1)\iff x_n\to x \text{ in } (X,d_2),\qquad\text{with
 the same limit }x.$$

**Step 2 — Same convergent sequences (same limits) ⟹ same topology.**

Every metric space is first-countable, and in a first-countable space a set is c
losed iff it is sequentially closed: $F$ is closed iff whenever $x_n\in F$ and $
x_n\to x$, one has $x\in F$. Since the convergent sequences and their limits are
 identical for $d_1$ and $d_2$, a subset $F\subseteq X$ is $d_1$-closed iff it i
s $d_2$-closed. Hence the two metrics generate exactly the same family of open s
ets, i.e. the same topology.

**Conclusion.** $d_1$ and $d_2$ are topologically equivalent. $\blacksquare$

*Remark.* If "equivalent" were intended in the stronger senses of *uniform equiv
alence* (identity and its inverse uniformly continuous) or *bi-Lipschitz equival
ence* ($c\,d_1\le d_2\le C\,d_1$), the conclusion would fail — sharing Cauchy se
quences is strictly weaker than those notions. But for the standard topological
notion of equivalence, the answer is **yes**.
