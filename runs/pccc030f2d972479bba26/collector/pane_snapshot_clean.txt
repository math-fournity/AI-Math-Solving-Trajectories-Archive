## Proof

**Yes**, it can be deduced that $F \circ \mathbf{x}: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}
^3$ is Euclidean differentiable.

**Key identity.** Let $\mathbf{x}$ be a coordinate patch of $M$ and let $\mathbf
{y}$ be a coordinate patch of $N$. On the open set
$$U_{\mathbf{y}} = \{u \in \operatorname{dom}(\mathbf{x}) : F(\mathbf{x}(u)) \in
 \operatorname{im}(\mathbf{y})\},$$
we have the factorization
$$F \circ \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y} \circ \bigl(\mathbf{y}^{-1} \circ F \circ \mat
hbf{x}\bigr). \tag{$\star$}$$

**Both factors on the right are differentiable:**

1. $\mathbf{y}^{-1} \circ F \circ \mathbf{x} : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ is
 Euclidean differentiable by hypothesis (it is differentiable for *every* pair o
f patches, hence in particular for this $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$).

2. $\mathbf{y} : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ is Euclidean differentiable beca
use $\mathbf{y}$ is a coordinate patch of a (regular, differentiable) surface $N
 \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ — by definition each component function of $\mathbf{y}$ i
s a differentiable function of $(u_1, u_2)$.

By the **chain rule** for maps between Euclidean spaces, the composition $(\star
)$ is Euclidean differentiable on $U_{\mathbf{y}}$.

**Covering argument.** The coordinate patches of $N$ cover $N$: for every $p \in
 N$ there exists a patch $\mathbf{y}$ with $p \in \operatorname{im}(\mathbf{y})$
. Since $F$ maps $M$ into $N$, for every $u \in \operatorname{dom}(\mathbf{x})$
the point $F(\mathbf{x}(u))$ lies in $N$, hence in the image of some patch $\mat
hbf{y}$. Therefore
$$\operatorname{dom}(\mathbf{x}) = \bigcup_{\mathbf{y}} U_{\mathbf{y}},$$
and on each $U_{\mathbf{y}}$ the map $F \circ \mathbf{x}$ is Euclidean different
iable. Differentiability is a local property, so $F \circ \mathbf{x}$ is Euclide
an differentiable on all of $\operatorname{dom}(\mathbf{x})$.

**Remark on the hypothesis.** The hypothesis that $\mathbf{y}^{-1} \circ F \circ
 \mathbf{x}$ is differentiable for *every* pair of patches is stronger than the
usual definition (which requires only *some* patch near each point). The argumen
t above uses only that it holds for the particular patch $\mathbf{y}$ whose imag
e contains $F(\mathbf{x}(u))$, so even the standard (weaker) definition of a dif
ferentiable map between surfaces would suffice. The crucial point is that $\math
bf{y}$ itself is differentiable as a map $\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$, which
is part of what it means to be a coordinate patch of a regular surface. $\blacks
quare$
