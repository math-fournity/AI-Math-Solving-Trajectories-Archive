## Evaluation of $\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t} - e^{-4t}}{t}\,dt$

### Convergence

- **Near $t = 0$:** Using Taylor expansions, $e^{-t} \approx 1 - t$ and $e^{-4t}
 \approx 1 - 4t$, so $e^{-t} - e^{-4t} \approx 3t$, and the integrand $\approx 3
$. The singularity is removable; the integral is finite near $0$.
- **Near $t = \infty$:** $\left|\frac{e^{-t} - e^{-4t}}{t}\right| \leq \frac{e^{
-t}}{t} + \frac{e^{-4t}}{t}$, both of which are integrable on $[1, \infty)$.

Hence the improper integral converges.

### Proof via differentiation under the integral sign

Define, for $a, b > 0$,
$$F(a) := \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-at} - e^{-bt}}{t}\,dt.$$

**Differentiating with respect to $a$.** For $a \geq a_0 > 0$ (any fixed $a_0$),
 the differentiated integrand $-e^{-at}$ is dominated by $e^{-a_0 t}$, which is
integrable on $[0,\infty)$. By the dominated convergence theorem, differentiatio
n under the integral sign is valid:

$$F'(a) = \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\left(\frac{e^{-at} - e^{-
bt}}{t}\right)dt = \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{-t\,e^{-at}}{t}\,dt = -\int_{0}^{\inft
y}e^{-at}\,dt = -\frac{1}{a}.$$

**Integrating back.** Since $F'(a) = -1/a$,

$$F(a) = -\ln a + C(b),$$

where $C(b)$ is a constant depending on $b$.

**Determining $C(b)$.** Set $a = b$: then the numerator $e^{-bt} - e^{-bt} = 0$,
 so $F(b) = 0$. Therefore

$$0 = -\ln b + C(b) \implies C(b) = \ln b.$$

Thus

$$F(a) = \ln b - \ln a = \ln\frac{b}{a}.$$

### Applying to the given integral

Here $a = 1$ and $b = 4$, so

$$\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t} - e^{-4t}}{t}\,dt = F(1) = \ln\frac{4}{1} = \ln
4 = 2\ln 2.$$

### Result

$$\boxed{\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t} - e^{-4t}}{t}\,dt = \ln 4 = 2\ln 2}$$
