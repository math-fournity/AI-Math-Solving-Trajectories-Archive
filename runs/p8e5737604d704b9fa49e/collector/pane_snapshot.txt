**Answer: Yes.** There always exists a sequence $(x_n)$ with $\|x_n\|=1$ such th

## Proof

Assume, for contradiction, that no such sequence exists. Then the quantity
$$\inf_{\|x\|=1} \|Tx\|$$
must be strictly positive. Indeed, if $\inf_{\|x\|=1}\|Tx\| = 0$, we could pick
$x_n$ with $\|x_n\|=1$ and $\|Tx_n\|\to 0$, contradicting our assumption. Hence
there is $c>0$ with
$$\|Tx\| \ge c\|x\| \quad \text{for all } x\in X. \tag{$\ast$}$$

**Consequence 1: $T$ is injective and has closed range.** Injectivity is immedia
te from $(\ast)$. To see the range is closed, let $Tx_n \to y$. Then $(\ast)$ gi
ves $\|x_n - x_m\| \le c^{-1}\|Tx_n - Tx_m\| \to 0$, so $(x_n)$ is Cauchy, hence
 $x_n \to x$ for some $x\in X$, and by continuity $Tx = y$, so $y \in T(X)$.

**Consequence 2: $T:X \to T(X)$ is an isomorphism of Banach spaces.** Since $T(X
)$ is a closed subspace of the Banach space $Y$, it is itself a Banach space. Th
e map $T:X\to T(X)$ is a continuous bijection, and $(\ast)$ says its inverse $T^
{-1}:T(X)\to X$ is continuous with $\|T^{-1}\|\le 1/c$.

**Consequence 3: $T:X\to T(X)$ is compact.** Compactness of $T:X\to Y$ means $T(
B_X)$ is relatively compact in $Y$. Since $T(X)$ is closed in $Y$, a subset of $
T(X)$ is relatively compact in $T(X)$ iff it is relatively compact in $Y$. Thus
$T(B_X)$ is relatively compact in $T(X)$, i.e. $T:X\to T(X)$ is compact.

**The contradiction.** Let $B_X = \{x : \|x\|\le 1\}$ be the closed unit ball. S
ince $T:X\to T(X)$ is compact, $\overline{T(B_X)}$ is compact in $T(X)$. The inv
erse $T^{-1}:T(X)\to X$ is continuous, and continuous maps send compact sets to
compact sets, so
$$T^{-1}\big(\overline{T(B_X)}\big)$$
is compact in $X$. But $B_X \subseteq T^{-1}\big(\overline{T(B_X)}\big)$ (since
$B_X \subseteq T^{-1}(T(B_X))$), so $B_X$ is a closed subset of a compact set, h
ence compact.

By **Riesz's lemma**, the closed unit ball of a Banach space is compact if and o
nly if the space is finite dimensional. This contradicts the hypothesis that $X$
 is infinite dimensional.

Therefore our assumption was false, and $\inf_{\|x\|=1}\|Tx\| = 0$. Consequently
 there exists a sequence $(x_n)$ with $\|x_n\|=1$ and $\|Tx_n\|\to 0$, i.e. $Tx_
n \to 0$ in $Y$. $\blacksquare$
