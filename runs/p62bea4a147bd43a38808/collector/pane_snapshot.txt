## Solution

The limit **does not exist in general** for $g \in L^1[a,b]$. Below is a proof b
y counterexample, together with an explanation of why the tempting "answer 0" (v
ia Riemann–Lebesgue) is incorrect.

---

### Counterexample

Take $[a,b]=[0,1]$ and $g(x)\equiv 1$, which certainly belongs to $L^1[0,1]$. Th
en

$$
\int_0^1 n\cos(nx)\,dx \;=\; n\cdot\frac{\sin(nx)}{n}\Big|_{0}^{1} \;=\; \sin(n)
.
$$

Hence the expression whose limit is to be evaluated reduces to

$$
\sin(n).
$$

As $n\to\infty$ (whether $n$ ranges over the reals or over the positive integers
), $\sin(n)$ **does not converge**: it is dense in $[-1,1]$ and in particular os
cillates. For instance, along the subsequence $n_k = 2\pi k + \tfrac{\pi}{2}$ we
 have $\sin(n_k)\to 1$, while along $n_k = 2\pi k + \tfrac{3\pi}{2}$ we have $\s
in(n_k)\to -1$.

Therefore

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1 n\cos(nx)\cdot 1\,dx \;=\; \lim_{n\to\infty}\sin(n)
$$

**does not exist**.

---

### Why "0" is not the answer

One might be tempted to invoke the **Riemann–Lebesgue lemma**, which states that
 for $g\in L^1[a,b]$,

$$
\int_a^b \cos(nx)\,g(x)\,dx \;\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\; 0.
$$

However, the problem asks for the limit of $n$ times this integral. The Riemann–
Lebesgue lemma gives no rate of decay, and in general the decay of $\int_a^b \co
s(nx)g(x)\,dx$ is only $o(1)$, **not** $o(1/n)$. Multiplying by $n$ therefore de
stroys the convergence to $0$.

A formal integration by parts (valid when $g$ is absolutely continuous with $g'\
in L^1$) makes the obstruction explicit:

$$
n\int_a^b \cos(nx)\,g(x)\,dx \;=\; \bigl[g(x)\sin(nx)\bigr]_{a}^{b} \;-\; \int_a
^b \sin(nx)\,g'(x)\,dx.
$$

The second term tends to $0$ by Riemann–Lebesgue, but the boundary term

$$
g(b)\sin(nb)-g(a)\sin(na)
$$

generally **fails to converge** as $n\to\infty$ (unless $g(a)=g(b)=0$, in which
case the limit is $0$). For the constant function $g\equiv 1$ this boundary term
 is exactly $\sin(n)-\sin(0)=\sin(n)$, recovering the counterexample above.

---

### Conclusion

Since for the $L^1$ function $g\equiv 1$ on $[0,1]$ the quantity $\int_0^1 n\cos
(nx)\,g(x)\,dx = \sin(n)$ has no limit as $n\to\infty$, the requested limit

$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_a^b n\cos(nx)\,g(x)\,dx}
$$

**does not exist in general** for $g\in L^1[a,b]$.
