## Proof

**Claim.** It is possible to construct a cube root using three-dimensional (soli
d) constructions. Specifically, for any constructible length $a > 0$, the length
 $\sqrt[3]{a}$ is constructible.

### 1. What 3D (solid) constructions provide

In the classical hierarchy of constructions:
- **Plane constructions** (straightedge + compass) produce only quadratic field
extensions, hence only lengths whose degree over $\mathbb{Q}$ is a power of $2$.
- **Solid constructions** add the use of **conic sections** (parabolas, ellipses
, hyperbolas), which arise naturally in three dimensions as the intersection of
a plane with a (double) cone. A right circular cone is obtained by rotating a li
ne about an axis, and a plane is determined by three constructible points; their
 intersection curve is a conic, which is therefore a legitimate object of 3D con
struction.

The decisive extra power: the intersection of two conics requires solving a poly
nomial equation of degree up to $4$, in particular **cubics**, which plane const
ructions cannot handle.

### 2. Menaechmus' construction of $\sqrt[3]{a}$

Given a constructible segment of length $a$, construct two parabolas in a common
 plane:

$$\mathcal{P}_1:\ y = x^{2}, \qquad \mathcal{P}_2:\ y^{2} = ax.$$

- $\mathcal{P}_1$ is the standard parabola (vertex at the origin, axis along the
 $y$-axis, latus rectum $1$).
- $\mathcal{P}_2$ is a parabola with vertex at the origin, axis along the $x$-ax
is, and latus rectum $a$.

Both are constructible conics: $\mathcal{P}_1$ uses only the unit, and $\mathcal
{P}_2$ uses the given length $a$ as its parameter, which is constructible by hyp
othesis.

### 3. Solving at the intersection

At a nonzero intersection point $(x,y)$:

$$y = x^{2} \quad\text{and}\quad y^{2} = ax.$$

Substituting the first equation into the second:

$$x^{4} = ax \;\Longrightarrow\; x^{3} = a \quad (\text{for } x \neq 0) \;\Longr
ightarrow\; x = \sqrt[3]{a}.$$

The origin $(0,0)$ is the trivial intersection (both parabolas share the vertex)
. The **other** intersection point has $x = \sqrt[3]{a}$ and $y = a^{2/3}$, both
 real and positive. This point is obtained as the intersection of two constructi
ble conics — a valid solid construction — and its $x$-coordinate is the desired
cube root.

### 4. This is genuinely beyond plane constructions

For $a = 2$, the polynomial $x^{3} - 2$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ (Eisens
tein, $p = 2$), so

$$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) : \mathbb{Q}] = 3,$$

which is **not** a power of $2$. Since every plane-constructible number has degr
ee a power of $2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, $\sqrt[3]{2}$ is not plane-constructible. T
hus the 3D construction is strictly necessary, confirming that solid constructio
ns are strictly more powerful than plane constructions and that cube roots lie w
ithin their reach.

### 5. Conclusion

The intersection of two parabolas (conic sections obtainable from 3D constructio
ns) reduces to the cubic equation $x^{3} = a$, whose real positive root is $\sqr
t[3]{a}$. Therefore **a cube root is constructible by three-dimensional (solid)
constructions**. $\blacksquare$
