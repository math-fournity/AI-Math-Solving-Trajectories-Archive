The answer is **yes**. In fact, the continuity hypothesis is unnecessary — the l
imit condition alone is equivalent to the $C_0$ property. Here is the proof.

---

**Claim.** For $f:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}$, the following are equivalent:

1. $\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to\infty} f(x)=0$.
2. For every $\epsilon>0$ there exists a compact set $K_\epsilon\subset\mathbb{R
}^2$ such that $|f(x)|<\epsilon$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^2\setminus K_\epsilon$.

In particular, (1) (together with continuity, which is not even needed) implies
that $f$ belongs to the $C_0$ class.

---

**Proof of $(1)\Rightarrow(2)$.**

Assume $\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to\infty}f(x)=0$. By the definition of this limit
, for every $\epsilon>0$ there exists $R>0$ such that

$$|x|>R \;\Longrightarrow\; |f(x)|<\epsilon.$$

Set

$$K_\epsilon \;=\; \overline{B(0,R)} \;=\; \{x\in\mathbb{R}^2 : |x|\le R\}.$$

This is a closed and bounded subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$, hence compact by the Hein
e–Borel theorem. If $x\in\mathbb{R}^2\setminus K_\epsilon$, then $|x|>R$, and so
 by the implication above, $|f(x)|<\epsilon$. Thus (2) holds. $\checkmark$

---

**Proof of $(2)\Rightarrow(1)$ (for completeness, showing equivalence).**

Assume (2). Given $\epsilon>0$, let $K_\epsilon$ be the compact set supplied by
(2). Since $K_\epsilon$ is compact in $\mathbb{R}^2$, it is bounded, so there ex
ists $R>0$ with $K_\epsilon\subseteq \overline{B(0,R)}$. Consequently, whenever
$|x|>R$, we have $x\notin K_\epsilon$, and hence $|f(x)|<\epsilon$. This is exac
tly the definition of $\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to\infty}f(x)=0$. $\checkmark$

---

**Role of continuity.** The argument above uses only the limit condition $\lim_{
|x|\to\infty}f(x)=0$; continuity of $f$ is never invoked. Hence the implication
asked about in the problem holds *a fortiori*: a continuous $f:\mathbb{R}^2\to\m
athbb{R}$ satisfying $\lim_{|x|\to\infty}f(x)=0$ automatically satisfies the com
pact-set formulation, i.e. $f\in C_0(\mathbb{R}^2)$.

The key topological fact making this work is that $\mathbb{R}^2$ is **locally co
mpact and $\sigma$-compact** with a cofinal family of compact sets given by clos
ed balls: every compact set is contained in some closed ball, and every closed b
all is compact. This is exactly what lets one translate freely between the "$|x|
>R$" formulation of "vanishing at infinity" and the "outside a compact set" form
ulation.
