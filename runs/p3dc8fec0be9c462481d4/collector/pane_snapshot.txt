## Proof

**Key idea.** We use the Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, where $\arg(1+ki) =
\arctan(k)$. The question reduces to a unique-factorization argument about which
 Gaussian primes appear.

### Step 1. Reduction modulo $\pi$

Since $\arctan(1) = \pi/4$, we have $\pi = 4\arctan(1)$. Thus $\sum_{k=1}^{15} m
_k \arctan(k) = \arctan(16)$ is equivalent to $\sum_{k=1}^{15} m_k \arctan(k) \e
quiv \arctan(16) \pmod{\pi}$: if the congruence holds with $\sum = \arctan(16) +
 n\pi$, we replace $m_1$ by $m_1 - 4n$ to get exact equality.

### Step 2. The argument group via Gaussian integer factorization

For each $k$, write $1 + ki = u_k \prod_p \pi_p^{a_p^{(k)}} \bar{\pi}_p^{b_p^{(k
)}}$ where $u_k \in \{\pm1, \pm i\}$ is a unit, and $\pi_p, \bar{\pi}_p$ are the
 conjugate Gaussian primes above each rational prime $p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ (with
 $\pi_2 = 1+i$ for $p=2$). Then

$$\arctan(k) = \arg(1+ki) \equiv \arg(u_k) + \sum_p e_p^{(k)} \arg(\pi_p) \pmod{
\pi},$$

where $e_p^{(k)} = a_p^{(k)} - b_p^{(k)}$. The unit contribution $\arg(u_k) \in
\{0, \pi/2\}$ is a multiple of $\arg(\pi_2) = \pi/4$, so it is absorbed into the
 $p=2$ component.

**Linear independence.** We claim the generators $\{\arg(\pi_p) : p = 2 \text{ o
r } p \equiv 1\pmod{4}\}$ satisfy: if $\sum_p e_p \arg(\pi_p) \equiv 0 \pmod{\pi
}$, then $e_p = 0$ for all $p > 2$ with $p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, and $e_2 \equiv 0
 \pmod{4}$.

*Proof of claim.* The congruence means $z = \prod_{e_p > 0} \pi_p^{e_p} \prod_{e
_p < 0} \bar{\pi}_p^{-e_p}$ is real, i.e., $z = \bar{z}$, i.e., $z/\bar{z} = 1$.
 Now $z/\bar{z} = \prod_p (\pi_p/\bar{\pi}_p)^{e_p}$. For $p = 2$: $\pi_2/\bar{\
pi}_2 = (1+i)/(1-i) = i$, contributing $i^{e_2}$. For $p > 2$, $p \equiv 1 \pmod
{4}$: $\pi_p$ and $\bar{\pi}_p$ are **non-associate** Gaussian primes, so in the
 unique factorization of $z/\bar{z} = 1$ in $\mathbb{Q}(i)$, the exponent of $\p
i_p$ is $e_p$ and must equal $0$. Thus $e_p = 0$ for all $p > 2$, $p \equiv 1\pm
od{4}$, and $i^{e_2} = 1$ gives $e_2 \equiv 0 \pmod{4}$. $\square$

So the argument group modulo $\pi$ is $(\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}) \oplus \bigoplus
_{p \equiv 1(4),\, p>2} \mathbb{Z}$, freely generated (over $\mathbb{Z}$) by $\{
\arg(\pi_p) : p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}, p > 2\}$ plus the $\pi/4$ from $p=2$.

### Step 3. The prime $257$ does not appear for $k \leq 15$

The Gaussian integer $1 + 16i$ has norm $1 + 16^2 = 257$. Since $257$ is a ratio
nal prime (it is the Fermat prime $F_3 = 2^8 + 1$; checking divisibility by $2,
3, 5, 7, 11, 13$ confirms primality), $1 + 16i$ is itself a **Gaussian prime**.
Thus

$$\arctan(16) = \arg(1 + 16i) = \arg(\pi_{257}),$$

with coefficient $e_{257} = 1$ and $e_p = 0$ for all other $p$.

For any $k \in \{1, \ldots, 15\}$, the Gaussian prime $\pi_{257}$ divides $1 + k
i$ if and only if $257 \mid (1 + k^2)$, i.e., $k^2 \equiv -1 \pmod{257}$.

Since $16^2 = 256 \equiv -1 \pmod{257}$, the solutions to $x^2 \equiv -1 \pmod{2
57}$ are $x \equiv \pm 16 \pmod{257}$, i.e., $x \equiv 16$ or $x \equiv 241 \pmo
d{257}$.

For $k \in \{1, 2, \ldots, 15\}$, we have $k < 16$, so $k \not\equiv 16 \pmod{25
7}$ and $k \not\equiv 241 \pmod{257}$. Therefore:

$$257 \nmid (1 + k^2) \quad \text{for all } k \in \{1, \ldots, 15\}.$$

### Step 4. Conclusion

Every $\arctan(k)$ for $k \in \{1, \ldots, 15\}$ has $e_{257}^{(k)} = 0$ in its
argument decomposition, since $257$ does not divide $1 + k^2$. Therefore any int
eger linear combination $\sum_{k=1}^{15} m_k \arctan(k)$ also has $e_{257} = 0$.

But $\arctan(16) = \arg(\pi_{257})$ has $e_{257} = 1 \neq 0$.

By the linear independence established in Step 2, $\arctan(16)$ is **not** in th
e subgroup of $\mathbb{R}/\pi\mathbb{Z}$ generated by $\arctan(1), \ldots, \arct
an(15)$.

By the reduction in Step 1, there do **not** exist integers $m_1, \ldots, m_{15}
$ such that $\sum_{k=1}^{15} m_k \arctan(k) = \arctan(16)$.

$\blacksquare$
