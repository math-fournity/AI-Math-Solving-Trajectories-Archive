## Solution

We **disprove** the statement: no real $2\times 2$ matrix $A$ satisfies $\sin A
= \begin{pmatrix}1&1996\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$.

Let $B = \begin{pmatrix}1&1996\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = I + 1996N$, where $N=\begin{p
matrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ is nilpotent with $N^2=0$. Note $B$ has a **repeat
ed eigenvalue $1$** and is **not diagonalizable** (it is a nontrivial Jordan blo
ck).

We use the fact that for any analytic function $f$ and any $2\times2$ real matri
x $A$, $f(A)$ is determined by the Jordan form of $A$. We split into cases based
 on the eigenvalue structure of $A$.

---

**Case 1: $A$ has two distinct eigenvalues (real or complex).**

Then $A$ is diagonalizable (over $\mathbb{C}$), so $\sin A = P\,\mathrm{diag}(\s
in\lambda_1,\sin\lambda_2)\,P^{-1}$ is diagonalizable over $\mathbb{C}$. But $B$
 is *not* diagonalizable over $\mathbb{C}$. Contradiction.

(If the two eigenvalues are complex conjugates $a\pm bi$ with $b\neq 0$, they ar
e distinct, so $A$ is diagonalizable over $\mathbb{C}$, and the same argument ap
plies. One may also verify directly: the eigenvalues of $\sin A$ would be $\sin
a\cosh b \pm i\cos a\sinh b$; for these to both equal $1$ requires $\cos a\,\sin
h b=0$, forcing $b=0$ or $\cosh b=\pm1$ with the wrong sign—impossible for $b\ne
q0$.)

---

**Case 2: $A$ has a repeated real eigenvalue $\lambda$ and is diagonalizable, i.
e. $A=\lambda I$.**

Then $\sin A = \sin\lambda\, I$, a scalar matrix. This cannot equal $B$, which i
s not scalar. Contradiction.

---

**Case 3: $A$ has a repeated real eigenvalue $\lambda$ and is not diagonalizable
.**

Then $A$ is similar over $\mathbb{R}$ to a Jordan block: $A = P(\lambda I + E)P^
{-1}$ with $E=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, $E^2=0$. Using the power se
ries and $(\lambda I+E)^k = \lambda^k I + k\lambda^{k-1}E$,

$$\sin(\lambda I+E) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{(2n+1)!}\bigl(\lambda^{2n
+1}I+(2n+1)\lambda^{2n}E\bigr) = \sin\lambda\, I + \cos\lambda\, E.$$

Hence
$$\sin A = \sin\lambda\, I + \cos\lambda\,(PEP^{-1}),$$
where $M:=PEP^{-1}$ is nilpotent of rank $1$ (a conjugate of $E$).

Setting this equal to $B = I + 1996N$ and taking the trace gives
$$2\sin\lambda = \mathrm{tr}(B)=2 \implies \sin\lambda = 1.$$
But $\sin\lambda=1$ implies $\lambda = \frac{\pi}{2}+2k\pi$, at which $\cos\lamb
da = 0$. Then the remaining equation becomes
$$\cos\lambda\cdot M = 0 = 1996\,N,$$
which is impossible since $N\neq 0$. Contradiction.

---

All possible Jordan structures for a real $2\times2$ matrix lead to contradictio
ns. Therefore **no real $2\times2$ matrix $A$ satisfies $\sin A = \begin{pmatrix
}1&1996\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$**.

The obstruction is fundamental: the only way $\sin A$ can be non-diagonalizable
is for $A$ itself to be a non-diagonalizable Jordan block (Case 3), but then $\s
in A = \sin\lambda\,I+\cos\lambda\,M$ requires $\sin\lambda=1$ (to match the eig
envalue $1$ of $B$), which forces $\cos\lambda=0$, killing the off-diagonal part
 entirely—so the nilpotent component of $\sin A$ vanishes exactly when its scala
r part is $1$.
