# Convergence of $\sum a(n)$ where $a(n)=\prod_{k=0}^{n} f(k)$

## Setup and reduction to the tail

Since $f$ is defined on $\mathbb{R}^+$ and the asymptotic governs only $t\to+\in
fty$, the finitely many factors $f(0),f(1),\dots,f(N)$ (for any fixed $N$) contr
ibute a fixed nonzero multiplicative constant to $a(n)$ (provided none of them i
s $0$; if some $f(k_0)=0$ then $a(n)\equiv 0$ for $n\ge k_0$ and the series triv
ially converges). Hence convergence is determined entirely by the tail, and we m
ay study

$$P_n \;=\; \prod_{k=K}^{n} f(k), \qquad K \text{ large enough that } k\ln k>0.$
$

The ratio $a(n)/a(n-1)=f(n)$, so the **ratio test** settles every case with $|a|
\neq 1$ immediately.

---

## Case 1: $|a|>1$ — divergence

$|f(n)|\to|a|>1$, so $|a(n)/a(n-1)|\to|a|>1$. The ratio test gives divergence (i
n fact $|a(n)|\to\infty$).

## Case 2: $0<|a|<1$ — absolute convergence

$|a(n)/a(n-1)|\to|a|<1$; the ratio test gives absolute convergence. (More precis
ely $\log|a(n)|=n\log|a|+O(\ln\ln n)$, so $|a(n)|=|a|^n(\ln n)^{O(1)}\to 0$ expo
nentially.)

## Case 3: $a=0$ — absolute convergence

Then $f(t)=\dfrac{b}{t\ln t}\bigl(1+O((t\ln t)^{-1})\bigr)$, so $|f(k)|\sim\dfra
c{|b|}{k\ln k}\to 0$. Consequently

$$|a(n)|\;\sim\;C\prod_{k=K}^{n}\frac{|b|}{k\ln k}\;=\;\frac{C\,|b|^{\,n-K+1}}{\
prod_{k=K}^{n}(k\ln k)},$$

which decays super-exponentially (the denominator grows faster than $n!$). Hence
 $\sum|a(n)|$ converges. (If $b=0$ as well, $f(t)=O((t\ln t)^{-2})$ and the deca
y is even faster.)

---

## Case 4: $a=1$ — divergence for every $b$

Here $f(t)=1+\dfrac{b}{t\ln t}+O\!\bigl((t\ln t)^{-2}\bigr)$. For large $k$,

$$\log f(k)=\frac{b}{k\ln k}+O\!\bigl((k\ln k)^{-2}\bigr).$$

Summing,

$$\log a(n)=\sum_{k=K}^{n}\log f(k)+\mathrm{const}
= b\!\!\sum_{k=K}^{n}\frac{1}{k\ln k}+O(1).$$

By comparison with the integral $\displaystyle\int_K^n\frac{dx}{x\ln x}=\ln\ln n
+O(1)$, and since $\sum_{k}(k\ln k)^{-2}<\infty$ (integral test),

$$\boxed{\;\log a(n)=b\,\ln\ln n+O(1),\qquad a(n)=\Theta\!\bigl((\ln n)^{b}\bigr
).\;}$$

- If $b>0$: $a(n)\to+\infty$ — terms do not tend to $0$.
- If $b=0$: $a(n)\to C\neq 0$ — terms do not tend to $0$.
- If $b<0$: $a(n)\sim C\,(\ln n)^{b}\to 0$, but $(\ln n)^{b}\gg n^{-1}$, so $\su
m a(n)$ dominates a constant multiple of $\sum n^{-1}$ and **diverges**.

**In every sub-case the series diverges.**

---

## Case 5: $a=-1$ — conditional convergence iff $b>0$

Now $f(t)=-1+\dfrac{b}{t\ln t}+O\!\bigl((t\ln t)^{-2}\bigr)$. For large $k$, $f(
k)<0$ and

$$|f(k)|=\Bigl|-1+\tfrac{b}{k\ln k}+O\!\bigl((k\ln k)^{-2}\bigr)\Bigr|=1-\frac{b
}{k\ln k}+O\!\bigl((k\ln k)^{-2}\bigr),$$

$$\log|f(k)|=-\frac{b}{k\ln k}+O\!\bigl((k\ln k)^{-2}\bigr).$$

Repeating the computation of Case 4,

$$\boxed{\;\log|a(n)|=-b\,\ln\ln n+O(1),\qquad |a(n)|=\Theta\!\bigl((\ln n)^{-b}
\bigr).\;}$$

**Sign pattern.** Since $f(k)<0$ for all large $k$, each new factor flips the si
gn, so for $n$ large

$$a(n)=C_s\,(-1)^{n}\,|a(n)|$$

with $C_s\in\{+1,-1\}$ fixed. Thus $\sum a(n)$ is eventually an **alternating se
ries** $\sum (-1)^n c_n$ with $c_n=|a(n)|$.

- **$b>0$:** $c_n=\Theta((\ln n)^{-b})\to 0$, and $c_n$ is eventually decreasing
 (because $\log c_{n}-\log c_{n-1}=\log|f(n)|=-\frac{b}{n\ln n}+O((n\ln n)^{-2})
<0$ for large $n$). By the **Leibniz alternating-series test**, $\sum a(n)$ **co
nverges**. The convergence is *conditional*: $\sum|a(n)|=\Theta\!\bigl(\sum(\ln
n)^{-b}\bigr)=+\infty$ (since $(\ln n)^{-b}\gg n^{-1}$).

- **$b=0$:** $|a(n)|\to C'\neq 0$, so $a(n)\not\to 0$ — **diverges**.

- **$b<0$:** $|a(n)|=\Theta((\ln n)^{|b|})\to\infty$, so $a(n)\not\to 0$ — **div
erges**.

---

## Complete classification

| Value of $a$ | Condition on $b$ | Behavior of $\displaystyle\sum a(n)$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---|
| $|a|>1$ | any $b$ | **diverges** |
| $0<|a|<1$ | any $b$ | **converges absolutely** |
| $a=0$ | any $b$ | **converges absolutely** |
| $a=1$ | any $b$ | **diverges** |
| $a=-1$ | $b>0$ | **converges conditionally** |
| $a=-1$ | $b\le 0$ | **diverges** |

(Throughout, the degenerate possibility that some $f(k_0)=0$ forces $a(n)\equiv0
$ for $n\ge k_0$ and the series converges trivially; this is not determined by t
he asymptotic data and is excluded from the table.)
