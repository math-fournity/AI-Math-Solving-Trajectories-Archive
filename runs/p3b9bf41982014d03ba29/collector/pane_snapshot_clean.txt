## Proof

**Claim.** The topology on $X$ is the discrete topology: every subset of $X$ is
open (and closed).

**Definitions.** Recall that a point $x \in X$ is an *accumulation point* (limit
 point) of $X$ if every open neighborhood $U$ of $x$ satisfies $U \cap (X \setmi
nus \{x\}) \neq \varnothing$. The hypothesis "$X$ has no accumulation points" me
ans that **no** point of $X$ is an accumulation point of $X$.

**Step 1: Every singleton is open.**

Let $x \in X$. Since $x$ is not an accumulation point of $X$, by negating the de
finition there exists an open neighborhood $U_x$ of $x$ such that
$$U_x \cap (X \setminus \{x\}) = \varnothing.$$
Because $U_x \subseteq X$ and $x \in U_x$, this forces $U_x = \{x\}$. Hence $\{x
\}$ is open.

**Step 2: Every subset is open.**

Let $A \subseteq X$ be arbitrary. Then
$$A = \bigcup_{x \in A} \{x\},$$
a union of open singletons, hence open. Since every subset is open, every subset
 is also closed (its complement is open). Thus the topology is exactly $\mathcal
{P}(X)$, the discrete topology.

**Step 3: The uncountability hypothesis is consistent but does not alter the con
clusion.**

The argument in Steps 1–2 used only the absence of accumulation points; it never
 used countability or uncountability. Therefore the conclusion holds for *any* s
et (finite, countable, or uncountable) without accumulation points. The hypothes
is that $X$ is uncountable simply tells us the resulting discrete space is an *u
ncountable* discrete space — a perfectly valid topological space, though one tha
t cannot be realized as a subspace of $\mathbb{R}$ (or any second‑countable $T_1
$ space), since every discrete subspace of a second‑countable space is countable
. This is why such an $X$ must be considered as an abstract topological space ra
ther than as a subset of a familiar Euclidean space.

**Conclusion.** An uncountable set without accumulation points carries exactly o
ne possible topology consistent with that hypothesis: the discrete topology, in
which every subset is both open and closed.
