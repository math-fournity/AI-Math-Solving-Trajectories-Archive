## Solution

**Answer: False.**

The statement claims that for *any* $A$-module $V$ and ideal $I$ of $A$, the quo
tient $V/I$ is Jacobson semisimple (i.e., has zero Jacobson radical). I exhibit
a counterexample.

### Counterexample

Take $A = \mathbb{Z}$, $V = \mathbb{Z}$ (viewed as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module over it
self), and $I = 4\mathbb{Z}$ (an ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$, hence a submodule of $V$
).

The quotient module is
$$V/I = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}.$$

### Computing the Jacobson radical

A module $M$ is **Jacobson semisimple** if $J(M) = 0$, where $J(M)$ is the Jacob
son radical (the intersection of all maximal submodules of $M$).

For the ring $R = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, the Jacobson radical $J(R)$ equals th
e intersection of all maximal ideals of $R$. By the lattice isomorphism theorem,
 the maximal ideals of $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ correspond bijectively to the ma
ximal ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$ that contain $4\mathbb{Z}$.

The maximal ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$ are $p\mathbb{Z}$ for primes $p$. The conditi
on $4\mathbb{Z} \subseteq p\mathbb{Z}$ requires $p \mid 4$, so $p = 2$. Thus the
 **unique** maximal ideal of $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ is $2\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z
}$.

Therefore:
$$J(\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}) = 2\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{
Z} \neq 0.$$

### Conclusion

Since $J(V/I) = J(\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}) = 2\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \neq 0$, the
 quotient module $V/I$ is **not** Jacobson semisimple.

This provides an explicit $A$-module $V$ and ideal $I$ of $A$ for which $V/I$ fa
ils to be Jacobson semisimple, refuting the universal claim.

**The statement is False.**
