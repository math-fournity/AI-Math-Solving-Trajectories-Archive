## Proof

We prove the answer is **yes**: the infimum of upper growth exponents equals the
 supremum of lower growth exponents.

### Step 1: Analytic setup and reduction

Since $\{a_n\}$ is increasing and $a_n < Cn^d$, we have $|a_n| \leq C' n^d$ for
some $C' > 0$, giving $\limsup |a_n|^{1/n} \leq 1$. So $a(z)$ has radius of conv
ergence $\rho \geq 1$.

**If $\rho > 1$:** Then $a_n = O(\rho^{-n})$ decays exponentially. Since $\{a_n\
}$ is increasing, this forces $a_n \leq 0$ for all $n$ with $a_n \to 0$. An alge
braic function with exponentially decaying coefficients extends beyond $|z|=1$;
being algebraic and entire, it must be a polynomial. With $a_n$ increasing and $
a_n \to 0$, we get $a_n = 0$ for all sufficiently large $n$ — a trivial case whe
re both exponents coincide (both $-\infty$).

**So assume $\rho = 1$.** Since $\{a_n\}$ is increasing and bounded above by $Cn
^d$, either $a_n \to L < \infty$ or $a_n \to +\infty$.

**Case $a_n \to L < \infty$:** Then $a_n$ is bounded. If $L > 0$, then $a_n > L/
2 > 0$ for large $n$, so $a_n = \Theta(1) = \Theta(n^0)$: both exponents equal $
0$. (If $L \leq 0$, the sequence doesn't "grow" and the statement is vacuous.)

**Case $a_n \to +\infty$ (main case):** $a_n$ is eventually positive. This is th
e case we analyze below.

### Step 2: Asymptotic expansion for algebraic functions

Since $a(z)$ is algebraic with radius of convergence $1$, it has finitely many s
ingularities on $|z| = 1$. By the theory of Puiseux expansions and singularity a
nalysis for algebraic functions (Flajolet–Sedgewick, *Analytic Combinatorics*, C
h. VII), each singularity $\omega$ on $|z|=1$ contributes to the coefficient asy
mptotics via its local Puiseux expansion. The coefficients admit:

$$a_n = n^s \sum_{j \in J} C_j\, \omega_j^{-n} + O(n^{s - \varepsilon}), \qquad
n \to \infty,$$

where:
- $s \in \mathbb{Q}$ is the **leading Puiseux exponent** (the maximum over all d
ominant singularities of the exponent contributed by the most singular term in t
he Puiseux expansion),
- $J$ is the finite set of **dominant singularities** $\omega_j \in \{|z|=1\}$ a
chieving this leading exponent $s$,
- $C_j \neq 0$ are constants,
- $\varepsilon > 0$ is a positive rational (the gap to the next exponent, guaran
teed because Puiseux exponents are rational with bounded denominators from the N
ewton polygon of $F$).

Set $Q(n) := \sum_{j \in J} C_j\, \omega_j^{-n}$, a bounded sequence.

### Step 3: Monotonicity forces $Q$ to be constant

Suppose for contradiction that $Q$ is non-constant. Then some $\omega_j \in J$ w
ith $\omega_j \neq 1$ has $C_j \neq 0$.

Compute the difference:
$$a_{n+1} - a_n = \bigl[(n+1)^s Q(n+1) - n^s Q(n)\bigr] + O(n^{s-\varepsilon}).$
$

Expanding:
$$(n+1)^s Q(n+1) - n^s Q(n) = n^s\underbrace{\bigl[Q(n+1) - Q(n)\bigr]}_{=:\, D(
n)} + \bigl[(n+1)^s - n^s\bigr] Q(n+1).$$

Since $(n+1)^s - n^s = O(n^{s-1})$ and $Q$ is bounded, the second term is $O(n^{
s-1})$. Thus:

$$a_{n+1} - a_n = n^s\, D(n) + O(n^{s-\delta}), \qquad \delta := \min(1, \vareps
ilon) > 0.$$

Now analyze $D(n) = Q(n+1) - Q(n) = \sum_{j \in J} C_j(\omega_j^{-1} - 1)\,\omeg
a_j^{-n}$.

- For $\omega_j = 1$: the coefficient $C_j(\omega_j^{-1}-1) = 0$, so this term v
anishes.
- For $\omega_j \neq 1$: the coefficient $C_j(\omega_j^{-1}-1) \neq 0$.

Since $Q$ is non-constant, at least one $\omega_j \neq 1$ contributes, so $D(n)$
 is a **non-trivial** almost periodic sequence.

**$D(n)$ has Cesàro mean $0$:** For each $\omega_j \neq 1$, $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=
1}^N \omega_j^{-n} \to 0$.

**$D(n)$ takes both signs bounded away from $0$:** By Kronecker's theorem (for n
on-root-of-unity $\omega_j$) or direct periodicity (for root-of-unity $\omega_j$
), the sequence $(\omega_j^{-n})_{j \in J}$ is dense in a subtorus of $(S^1)^{|J
|}$. The function $D$ corresponds to a continuous function on this torus with in
tegral $0$ that is not identically $0$. Hence it attains both positive and negat
ive values, and by density, $D(n)$ exceeds some $\eta > 0$ for infinitely many $
n$ and drops below $-\eta$ for infinitely many $n$.

**Contradiction with monotonicity:** For $n$ with $|D(n)| > \eta$, the leading t
erm $n^s D(n)$ has magnitude $\geq \eta\, n^s$, while the remainder is $O(n^{s-\
delta})$. For sufficiently large $n$, the leading term dominates, so:
- When $D(n) > \eta$: $a_{n+1} - a_n > 0$.
- When $D(n) < -\eta$: $a_{n+1} - a_n < 0$.

This contradicts $\{a_n\}$ being increasing. Therefore **$Q$ must be constant**.

### Step 4: Conclusion

Since $Q$ is constant, all oscillating terms ($\omega_j \neq 1$) have zero net c
ontribution, leaving only the singularity at $\omega = 1$:

$$a_n \sim C_0 \cdot n^s, \qquad C_0 > 0.$$

(The singularity at $z=1$ must be present: if the only dominant singularity were
 at $\omega \neq 1$, then $a_n \sim C\,\omega^{-n} n^s$ would oscillate in sign,
 contradicting $a_n$ being eventually positive. And $C_0 > 0$ because $a_n \to +
\infty$.)

Since $a_n \to +\infty$ and $a_n \sim C_0 n^s$ with $C_0 > 0$, we have $s > 0$.
The asymptotic gives $a_n = \Theta(n^s)$: there exist $c_1, c_2 > 0$ and $N$ wit
h $c_1 n^s \leq a_n \leq c_2 n^s$ for all $n \geq N$.

Therefore:
- **Infimum of upper exponents** $d_1$ (such that $a_n < C_1 n^{d_1}$ eventually
) $= s$.
- **Supremum of lower exponents** $d_2$ (such that $a_n > C_2 n^{d_2}$ eventuall
y) $= s$.

These coincide. $\blacksquare$
