## Proof

**Answer: Yes.** For any positive constant $K$, there exist infinitely many $n$
with $f_q(n) \le Kn$.

**Setup.** A $q$-th residue modulo $n$ is an element $a \in \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{
Z}$ such that $x^q \equiv a \pmod{n}$ is solvable. We use two standard facts:

1. **CRT decomposition:** If $n = p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_k^{a_k}$ with distinct prime
s $p_i$, then $f_q(n) = \prod_{i=1}^k f_q(p_i^{a_i})$.

2. **Dirichlet's theorem:** There are infinitely many primes $p \equiv 1 \pmod{q
}$ (since $\gcd(1,q)=1$).

**Counting $f_q(p)$ when $q \mid p-1$.** Let $p$ be a prime with $p \equiv 1 \pm
od{q}$ (so $p \neq q$). The multiplicative group $\mathbb{F}_p^*$ is cyclic of o
rder $p-1$. The map $x \mapsto x^q$ on $\mathbb{F}_p^*$ has image of size $(p-1)
/\gcd(q, p-1) = (p-1)/q$. Including $a=0$ (which is $0^q$), we get:
$$f_q(p) = \frac{p-1}{q} + 1 = \frac{p+q-1}{q}.$$

**Constructing the sequence.** Let $p_1 < p_2 < p_3 < \cdots$ be the infinitely
many primes with $p_i \equiv 1 \pmod{q}$ (guaranteed by Dirichlet). For each $k
\ge 1$, set $n_k = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k$. By CRT:
$$\frac{f_q(n_k)}{n_k} = \prod_{i=1}^k \frac{f_q(p_i)}{p_i} = \prod_{i=1}^k \fra
c{p_i + q - 1}{q\, p_i} = \prod_{i=1}^k \frac{1}{q}\!\left(1 + \frac{q-1}{p_i}\r
ight).$$

**Bounding the ratio.** Since $p_i \equiv 1 \pmod{q}$ and $p_i$ is prime, we hav
e $p_i \ge q+1$. Therefore:
$$\frac{1}{q}\!\left(1 + \frac{q-1}{p_i}\right) \le \frac{1}{q}\!\left(1 + \frac
{q-1}{q+1}\right) = \frac{1}{q}\cdot\frac{2q}{q+1} = \frac{2}{q+1}.$$

Since $q \ge 2$, we have $\frac{2}{q+1} \le \frac{2}{3} < 1$, so:
$$\frac{f_q(n_k)}{n_k} \le \left(\frac{2}{q+1}\right)^k \xrightarrow{k\to\infty}
 0.$$

**Conclusion.** Given any $K > 0$, choose $k_0$ such that $\left(\frac{2}{q+1}\r
ight)^k \le K$ for all $k \ge k_0$. Then for every $k \ge k_0$:
$$f_q(n_k) \le K\, n_k.$$
The integers $n_k$ are distinct (they are products of distinct sets of primes),
so there are **infinitely many** such $n$.
