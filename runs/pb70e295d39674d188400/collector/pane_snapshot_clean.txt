**Answer: Yes, such a $C^\infty$ function exists.**

## Proof

We use **Borel's Theorem** (Borel's Lemma):

> **Borel's Theorem.** For any sequence $\{c_n\}_{n=0}^\infty$ of real numbers,
there exists a function $f \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ such that $f^{(n)}(0) = c_n
$ for every $n \geq 0$.

### Proof of Borel's Theorem

Let $\phi \in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ be a bump function with $\phi \equiv 1$ on
 $[-1,1]$ and $\operatorname{supp} \phi \subset [-2,2]$. Define

$$f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{c_n}{n!}\, x^n\, \phi(\lambda_n x),$$

where the positive numbers $\lambda_n$ are chosen inductively to grow sufficient
ly fast.

Set $g_n(x) = \frac{c_n}{n!} x^n \phi(\lambda_n x)$, so $\operatorname{supp} g_n
 \subset [-2/\lambda_n, 2/\lambda_n]$. By the Leibniz rule, for each $k \geq 0$:

$$g_n^{(k)}(x) = \frac{c_n}{n!} \sum_{j=0}^{\min(k,n)} \binom{k}{j} \frac{n!}{(n
-j)!}\, x^{n-j}\, \lambda_n^{k-j}\, \phi^{(k-j)}(\lambda_n x).$$

On the support, $|x| \leq 2/\lambda_n$, so $|x|^{n-j} \leq (2/\lambda_n)^{n-j}$.
 Bounding each term:

$$\|g_n^{(k)}\|_\infty \leq |c_n| \sum_{j=0}^{\min(k,n)} \binom{k}{j} \frac{2^{n
-j}\,\lambda_n^{k-n}}{(n-j)!}\, \|\phi^{(k-j)}\|_\infty.$$

The factor $\lambda_n^{k-n} = \lambda_n^k / \lambda_n^n$. Since $\lambda_n \to \
infty$, the term $\lambda_n^n$ dominates. Concretely, at step $n$ we choose $\la
mbda_n$ large enough (depending on $c_0, \dots, c_n$ and $n$) so that

$$\|g_n^{(k)}\|_\infty \leq 2^{-n} \quad \text{for all } k = 0, 1, \dots, n.$$

This is possible because every term in the bound above carries a factor $\lambda
_n^{-n}$ (up to a fixed power $\lambda_n^k$ with $k \leq n$) and $2^{n-j}/(n-j)!
 \to 0$. Then for each fixed $k$, the tail $\sum_{n \geq k} \|g_n^{(k)}\|_\infty
 \leq \sum_{n \geq k} 2^{-n} < \infty$, while the finite head $\sum_{n < k}$ is
trivially finite. Hence $\sum_n g_n^{(k)}$ converges uniformly for every $k$, wh
ich gives $f \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ with $f^{(k)} = \sum_n g_n^{(k)}$.

**Evaluation at the origin.** Since $\phi(0) = 1$ and $\phi^{(m)}(0) = 0$ for $m
 \geq 1$ (as $\phi \equiv 1$ near $0$), we get $g_n^{(k)}(0) = \frac{c_n}{n!}\cd
ot \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}\cdot 0^{n-k}\cdot \phi(0) = c_k$ when $n = k$, and $0$ othe
rwise (the factor $x^{n-k}$ vanishes at $0$ when $n > k$, and $x^n$ differentiat
ed more than $n$ times vanishes when $n < k$). Therefore

$$f^{(k)}(0) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty g_n^{(k)}(0) = c_k. \qquad \blacksquare$$

### Applying Borel's Theorem

Choose the sequence $c_n = (n!)^2$. By Borel's Theorem, there exists $f \in C^\i
nfty(\mathbb{R})$ with

$$f^{(n)}(0) = (n!)^2, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$

The Taylor series of $f$ at $x_0 = 0$ is

$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}\, x^n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} n!\, x^n
.$$

Its radius of convergence $R$ is determined by the Cauchy–Hadamard formula:

$$\frac{1}{R} = \limsup_{n \to \infty} |a_n|^{1/n}, \qquad a_n = n!.$$

By **Stirling's approximation**, $n! \sim \sqrt{2\pi n}\,(n/e)^n$, so

$$(n!)^{1/n} \sim \frac{n}{e}\,(2\pi n)^{1/(2n)} \;\longrightarrow\; \infty \qua
d \text{as } n \to \infty.$$

Therefore $\frac{1}{R} = \limsup_{n\to\infty} (n!)^{1/n} = \infty$, which gives

$$R = 0.$$

### Conclusion

The $C^\infty$ function $f$ furnished by Borel's Theorem with $f^{(n)}(0) = (n!)
^2$ has a Taylor series at $x_0 = 0$ with radius of convergence exactly $0$. Hen
ce such a function exists. $\blacksquare$
