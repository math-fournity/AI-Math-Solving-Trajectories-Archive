## Proof

We evaluate
$$I(k) \;=\; \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{x\sin(kx)}{x^2+a^2}\,dx, \qquad k>0,\
; a>0.$$

### Step 1. Reduction to a half-line integral

The integrand is even ($x$ and $\sin(kx)$ are both odd, their product is even; t
he denominator is even), so
$$I(k) = 2\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{x\sin(kx)}{x^2+a^2}\,dx.$$
This integral converges conditionally by **Dirichlet's test**: $\frac{x}{x^2+a^2
}$ is decreasing for $x>a$ and tends to $0$, while $\int_0^R \sin(kx)\,dx$ is bo
unded.

### Step 2. A Laplace-transform representation of the kernel

For $a>0$ and any real $x$,
$$\int_0^\infty e^{-at}\sin(xt)\,dt = \frac{x}{a^2+x^2},$$
which is the standard Laplace transform $\int_0^\infty e^{-st}\sin(\omega t)\,dt
 = \frac{\omega}{s^2+\omega^2}$ with $s=a$, $\omega=x$. Thus
$$\frac{x}{x^2+a^2} = \int_0^\infty e^{-at}\sin(xt)\,dt. \tag{1}$$

### Step 3. Regularization and Fubini

Since the integral in Step 1 is only conditionally convergent, we introduce an A
bel regularizer $e^{-\epsilon x}$ ($\epsilon>0$) and define
$$I_\epsilon(k) := 2\int_0^\infty e^{-\epsilon x}\,\frac{x\sin(kx)}{x^2+a^2}\,dx
.$$
By **Abel's theorem** for improper integrals (applicable because the original in
tegral converges by Dirichlet's test),
$$I(k) = \lim_{\epsilon\to 0^+} I_\epsilon(k). \tag{2}$$

Substituting (1) into $I_\epsilon$:
$$I_\epsilon(k) = 2\int_0^\infty e^{-\epsilon x}\sin(kx)\left(\int_0^\infty e^{-
at}\sin(xt)\,dt\right)dx.$$
For $\epsilon>0$ the double integral is absolutely convergent:
$$\int_0^\infty\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-\epsilon x}e^{-at}|\sin(kx)\sin(xt)|\,dt\,d
x \le \int_0^\infty e^{-\epsilon x}dx\cdot\int_0^\infty e^{-at}dt = \frac{1}{\ep
silon}\cdot\frac{1}{a}<\infty.$$
Hence **Fubini's theorem** justifies swapping the order:
$$I_\epsilon(k) = 2\int_0^\infty e^{-at}\left(\int_0^\infty e^{-\epsilon x}\sin(
kx)\sin(xt)\,dx\right)dt. \tag{3}$$

### Step 4. Evaluating the inner integral

Using the product-to-sum identity $\sin(kx)\sin(tx)=\tfrac12[\cos((k{-}t)x)-\cos
((k{+}t)x)]$ and the standard Laplace transform $\int_0^\infty e^{-\epsilon x}\c
os(\alpha x)\,dx = \frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^2+\alpha^2}$:
$$\int_0^\infty e^{-\epsilon x}\sin(kx)\sin(tx)\,dx = \frac12\left[\frac{\epsilo
n}{\epsilon^2+(k-t)^2}-\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^2+(k+t)^2}\right].$$

Substituting into (3):
$$I_\epsilon(k) = \int_0^\infty e^{-at}\,\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^2+(k-t)^2}\,dt
 \;-\; \int_0^\infty e^{-at}\,\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^2+(k+t)^2}\,dt. \tag{4}$$

### Step 5. The limit $\epsilon\to 0^+$ via the Poisson kernel

The family $P_\epsilon(u)=\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^2+u^2}$ is (up to a factor of
 $\pi$) the **Poisson kernel**: it satisfies $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_\epsilon(
u)\,du=\pi$ for all $\epsilon>0$, and as $\epsilon\to0^+$ it concentrates all it
s mass at $u=0$. Consequently, for any continuous function $\phi$,
$$\lim_{\epsilon\to0^+}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(u)\,P_\epsilon(u)\,du = \pi\,
\phi(0).$$

**First term of (4).** With the substitution $u=t-k$ (so $t=u+k$, and $t\ge0\iff
 u\ge -k$):
$$\int_0^\infty e^{-at}\,\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^2+(k-t)^2}\,dt = \int_{-k}^{\i
nfty} e^{-a(u+k)}\,\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^2+u^2}\,du = e^{-ak}\int_{-k}^{\inft
y} e^{-au}\,\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^2+u^2}\,du.$$
Since $k>0$, the point $u=0$ lies in the interior of $[-k,\infty)$. The function
 $\phi(u)=e^{-au}$ (extended continuously to all of $\mathbb{R}$) is continuous
at $0$ with $\phi(0)=1$, and the Poisson kernel's mass outside any neighborhood
of $0$ vanishes as $\epsilon\to0^+$. Therefore
$$\lim_{\epsilon\to0^+} e^{-ak}\int_{-k}^{\infty} e^{-au}\,\frac{\epsilon}{\epsi
lon^2+u^2}\,du = e^{-ak}\cdot\pi\cdot\phi(0) = \pi\,e^{-ak}. \tag{5}$$

**Second term of (4).** Here $\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^2+(k+t)^2}$ is centered a
t $t=-k<0$, which lies **outside** the integration domain $[0,\infty)$. For $t\g
e0$ we have $|k+t|\ge k>0$, so
$$0\le \int_0^\infty e^{-at}\,\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^2+(k+t)^2}\,dt \le \frac{
\epsilon}{k^2}\int_0^\infty e^{-at}\,dt = \frac{\epsilon}{ak^2}\;\xrightarrow{\e
psilon\to0^+}\;0. \tag{6}$$

### Step 6. Conclusion

Combining (2), (4), (5), and (6):
$$I(k) = \lim_{\epsilon\to0^+} I_\epsilon(k) = \pi\,e^{-ak} - 0 = \pi\,e^{-ak}.$
$

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{x\sin(kx)}{x^2+a^2}\,dx \;=\
; \pi\,e^{-ak}, \qquad k>0,\; a>0.}$$
