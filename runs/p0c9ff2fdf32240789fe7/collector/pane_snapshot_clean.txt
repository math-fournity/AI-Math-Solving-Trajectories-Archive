## Proof

**Yes**, such a sequence exists. The construction is the classical **typewriter
sequence** (enumeration of dyadic interval indicators).

### Construction

For each level $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ and index $j = 0, 1, \dots, 2^k - 1$, define
 the indicator
$$g_{k,j}(x) = \mathbf{1}_{[j/2^k,\,(j+1)/2^k]}(x).$$
Enumerate these functions in order of increasing $k$ (and within each level, inc
reasing $j$) to obtain the sequence $\{f_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$:
$$f_1 = g_{0,0},\quad f_2 = g_{1,0},\; f_3 = g_{1,1},\quad f_4 = g_{2,0},\; f_5
= g_{2,1},\; f_6 = g_{2,2},\; f_7 = g_{2,3},\quad \dots$$

### The $L^1$ norm tends to $0$

At level $k$, every function $g_{k,j}$ is the indicator of an interval of length
 $1/2^k$, so
$$\int_0^1 |g_{k,j}(x)|\,dx = \frac{1}{2^k}.$$
As $n \to \infty$, the corresponding level $k(n) \to \infty$, hence
$$\int_0^1 |f_n(x)|\,dx = \frac{1}{2^{k(n)}} \longrightarrow 0.$$

### Pointwise divergence at every $x \in [0,1]$

Fix any $x \in [0,1]$.

**$f_n(x) = 1$ infinitely often.** At each level $k$, the $2^k$ intervals $\bigl
[j/2^k, (j+1)/2^k\bigr]$, $j = 0,\dots,2^k-1$, cover $[0,1]$. Therefore $x$ belo
ngs to at least one of them, so there exists $j$ with $g_{k,j}(x) = 1$. Since th
is holds for every $k$, the value $1$ is attained at infinitely many indices $n$
.

**$f_n(x) = 0$ infinitely often.** At level $k$, the point $x$ belongs to **at m
ost two** of the $2^k$ dyadic intervals (two only if $x$ is a dyadic rational th
at is a shared endpoint). Hence at least $2^k - 2$ of the functions at level $k$
 satisfy $g_{k,j}(x) = 0$. For all $k \geq 2$, we have $2^k - 2 \geq 2 > 0$, so
at least one function at each such level gives value $0$ at $x$. Thus the value
$0$ is also attained at infinitely many indices $n$.

Since the sequence $\{f_n(x)\}$ takes the value $1$ infinitely often and the val
ue $0$ infinitely often, it **does not converge** for any $x \in [0,1]$.

### Conclusion

The typewriter sequence $\{f_n\}$ satisfies both:
- $\displaystyle\int_0^1 |f_n(x)|\,dx \to 0$, and
- $\{f_n(x)\}$ diverges for every $x \in [0,1]$.

Therefore such a sequence exists. $\blacksquare$
