**Key identity (Rodrigues' formula).** The Legendre polynomial of degree $n$ is

$$P_n(x) = \frac{1}{2^n\, n!}\,\frac{d^n}{dx^n}(x^2-1)^n.$$

Setting $n=50$,

$$g(x) = \frac{d^{50}}{dx^{50}}(x^2-1)^{50} = 2^{50}\cdot 50!\cdot P_{50}(x).$$

Since $2^{50}\cdot 50! \neq 0$, the roots of $g$ are exactly the roots of $P_{50
}$.

**Degree of $g$.** $(x^2-1)^{50}$ has degree $100$; differentiating $50$ times y
ields a polynomial of degree $50$. Hence $g$ has at most $50$ roots (counting mu
ltiplicity), consistent with $P_{50}$ being degree $50$.

**Roots of $P_{50}$.** A classical theorem on Legendre polynomials states that $
P_n$ has exactly $n$ distinct real roots, all lying in the open interval $(-1,1)
$. (Proof sketch: $P_n$ is orthogonal to all polynomials of degree $<n$ on $[-1,
1]$ with weight $1$. If $P_n$ changed sign at only $k<n$ points $r_1,\dots,r_k$
in $(-1,1)$, the polynomial $q(x)=\prod(x-r_i)$ would satisfy $\int_{-1}^{1} P_n
(x)\,q(x)\,dx \neq 0$ since $P_n q$ keeps constant sign — contradicting orthogon
ality, as $\deg(P_n q)=n+k<2n$... more directly, $q$ has degree $k<n$, so $\int_
{-1}^1 P_n q = 0$, yet $P_n q$ has constant sign, forcing the integral to be non
zero, a contradiction. Thus $P_n$ has at least $n$ sign changes in $(-1,1)$, hen
ce at least $n$ distinct roots there; being degree $n$, it has exactly $n$.)

For $n=50$: $P_{50}$ has exactly $50$ distinct roots, all in $(-1,1)$.

**Containment in $[-10,10]$.** Since $(-1,1)\subset[-10,10]$, all $50$ roots of
$P_{50}$ — and hence of $g$ — lie in $[-10,10]$.

**Conclusion.** The equation $g(x)=0$ has exactly $\boxed{50}$ roots in $[-10,10
]$.
