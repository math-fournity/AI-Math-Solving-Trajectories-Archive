## Solution

**Answer: No, we cannot conclude that $Q = P$.** The equality $h^0(C, \mathcal{O
}(P+Q)) = h^0(C, \mathcal{O}(2P))$ does not force $Q = P$. We provide an explici
t counterexample.

### Counterexample: Non-hyperelliptic genus 3 curve (plane quartic)

Let $C \subset \mathbb{P}^2$ be a smooth plane quartic (genus $g = 3$). Such a c
urve is non-hyperelliptic, since a non-hyperelliptic genus 3 curve is canonicall
y embedded as a smooth plane quartic.

Let $P, Q \in C$ be any two distinct points. We compute $h^0$ for both divisors
of degree 2 using Riemann–Roch.

**Riemann–Roch setup.** For any divisor $D$ of degree 2 on $C$:
$$h^0(D) = \deg D + 1 - g + h^0(K_C - D) = 2 + 1 - 3 + h^0(K_C - D) = h^0(K_C -
D).$$

The canonical divisor $K_C$ has degree $2g - 2 = 4$ and is cut out by lines: a l
ine $L \subset \mathbb{P}^2$ meets $C$ in a canonical divisor $L \cdot C$ (degre
e 4). Thus $h^0(K_C - D)$ counts the dimension of the family of lines $L$ such t
hat $L \cdot C \geq D$ (as divisors on $C$), i.e., lines whose intersection with
 $C$ contains $D$.

**Computing $h^0(2P)$.** We need lines $L$ with $\operatorname{mult}_P(L \cdot C
) \geq 2$, i.e., lines tangent to $C$ at $P$. There is a **unique** tangent line
 to $C$ at $P$. Hence
$$h^0(K_C - 2P) = 1 \implies h^0(2P) = 1.$$

**Computing $h^0(P+Q)$ for $P \neq Q$.** We need lines $L$ passing through both
$P$ and $Q$. There is a **unique** line through two distinct points in $\mathbb{
P}^2$. Hence
$$h^0(K_C - P - Q) = 1 \implies h^0(P+Q) = 1.$$

**Conclusion.** For every pair of distinct points $P \neq Q$ on a smooth plane q
uartic $C$:
$$h^0(C, \mathcal{O}(P+Q)) = 1 = h^0(C, \mathcal{O}(2P)),$$
yet $Q \neq P$. The equality holds trivially (both sides equal 1) and carries no
 information about whether $Q$ coincides with $P$.

### Why the equality is uninformative here

On a non-hyperelliptic genus 3 curve, every degree-2 divisor $D$ satisfies $h^0(
D) = 1$ (since the unique line through the support of $D$ is the only witness in
 $|K_C - D|$, and no degree-2 divisor moves in a pencil — a $g^1_2$ would make t
he curve hyperelliptic). Thus the two quantities are equal for **all** choices o
f $P, Q$, distinct or not, and the hypothesis cannot distinguish $Q = P$ from $Q
 \neq P$.

Therefore, **we cannot conclude that $Q = P$.**
