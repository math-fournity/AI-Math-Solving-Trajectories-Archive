## Solution

**Yes.** In fact $\mathbb{Z}^2 = \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$ is a quotient of
$\mathbb{Z}[x_1]$ (so $n = 1$ suffices).

**Construction.** Define the evaluation homomorphism
$$\varphi : \mathbb{Z}[x] \longrightarrow \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}, \qquad \
varphi(f) = \bigl(f(0),\, f(1)\bigr).$$
This is a ring homomorphism (it is the product of the two evaluation maps $\math
rm{ev}_0$ and $\mathrm{ev}_1$).

**Surjectivity.** Given any $(a, b) \in \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$, the polyn
omial $f(x) = a + (b - a)x$ satisfies $f(0) = a$ and $f(1) = b$. Hence $\varphi$
 is surjective.

**The kernel.** We have
$$\ker \varphi = \{f \in \mathbb{Z}[x] : f(0) = 0 \text{ and } f(1) = 0\} = \{f
: x \mid f \text{ and } (x-1) \mid f\}.$$
The ideals $(x)$ and $(x-1)$ are comaximal in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, since
$$x - (x - 1) = 1 \in (x) + (x-1),$$
so $(x) + (x-1) = \mathbb{Z}[x]$. Therefore, by the Chinese Remainder Theorem,
$$(x) \cap (x-1) = (x)\cdot(x-1) = \bigl(x(x-1)\bigr) = (x^2 - x).$$

**Conclusion.** By the First Isomorphism Theorem (combined with CRT),
$$\mathbb{Z}[x]\,\big/\,(x^2 - x) \;\cong\; \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x) \times \mathbb{Z}[
x]/(x-1) \;\cong\; \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}.$$

Thus $\mathbb{Z}^2$ is a quotient of $\mathbb{Z}[x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n]$ with $n
= 1$ (and hence for any $n \geq 1$, by ignoring the remaining variables). $\blac
ksquare$
