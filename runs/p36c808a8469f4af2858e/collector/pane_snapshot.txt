# Proof

**Claim:** Let $f$ be a continuous function (on, say, $\mathbb{R}$ or an interva
l $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$). If $f = 0$ almost everywhere (with respect to Lebes
gue measure), then $f = 0$ everywhere. In particular, the additional hypothesis
that $f$ has zero Lebesgue integral is consistent but redundant.

**Proof.**

We prove the contrapositive-free direct argument. Suppose $f$ is continuous and
$f = 0$ a.e. We show $f(x) = 0$ for every $x$.

Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that there exists a point $x_0$ with $f(x
_0) \neq 0$. Without loss of generality, $f(x_0) > 0$ (the case $f(x_0) < 0$ is
handled symmetrically by considering $-f$).

Set $\varepsilon = \frac{f(x_0)}{2} > 0$. By continuity of $f$ at $x_0$, there e
xists $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x$ with $|x - x_0| < \delta$,

$$|f(x) - f(x_0)| < \varepsilon,$$

which implies

$$f(x) > f(x_0) - \varepsilon = \frac{f(x_0)}{2} > 0.$$

Let $U = (x_0 - \delta, x_0 + \delta)$. This is a nonempty open interval, so it
has positive Lebesgue measure:

$$m(U) = 2\delta > 0.$$

For every $x \in U$, we have $f(x) > 0$, hence $f(x) \neq 0$. Therefore

$$U \subseteq \{x : f(x) \neq 0\}.$$

Taking Lebesgue measures,

$$m(\{x : f(x) \neq 0\}) \geq m(U) = 2\delta > 0.$$

But this contradicts the hypothesis that $f = 0$ almost everywhere, which means
precisely that $m(\{x : f(x) \neq 0\}) = 0$.

The contradiction shows that no such $x_0$ exists. Hence $f(x) = 0$ for every $x
$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark on the hypotheses.** The condition "$f$ has zero Lebesgue integral" is
automatically satisfied whenever $f = 0$ a.e. (since the Lebesgue integral of an
y a.e.-zero function is zero, regardless of continuity). Thus the integral condi
tion adds no additional power beyond the hypothesis $f = 0$ a.e. The essential i
ngredient is **continuity**, which promotes the measure-theoretic conclusion "$f
 = 0$ a.e." to the pointwise conclusion "$f = 0$ everywhere." Without continuity
 the conclusion fails: for instance, the indicator function $\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}$
 is zero a.e. (and has zero integral) but is not zero at the origin.
