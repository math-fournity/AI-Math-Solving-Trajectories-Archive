## Solution

**Yes**, the restriction of a non-compact operator to an infinite-dimensional su
bspace can be compact.

### Proof

We construct an explicit example.

**Setup.** Let $X = \ell^2 \oplus \ell^2$ and $Y = \ell^2$. Define $T: X \to Y$
by
$$T(x_1,\, x_2) = x_1, \qquad x_1, x_2 \in \ell^2.$$
This is the projection onto the first summand, a bounded linear operator with $\
|T\| = 1$.

**$T$ is not compact.** Let $\{e_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ be the standard orthonormal b
asis of $\ell^2$. The sequence $\{(e_n, 0)\}_{n=1}^\infty$ is bounded in $X$ (ea
ch element has norm $1$). Its image under $T$ is $T(e_n, 0) = e_n$, which has no
 convergent subsequence in $\ell^2$ since $\|e_n - e_m\| = \sqrt{2}$ for $n \neq
 m$. Hence $T$ is not compact.

**The restriction to an infinite-dimensional subspace is compact.** Set
$$M = \{0\} \oplus \ell^2 \subseteq X.$$
This is a closed infinite-dimensional subspace of $X$. For every $(0, x_2) \in M
$,
$$T(0, x_2) = 0.$$
Therefore $T|_M = 0$, the zero operator, which is compact (the image of the unit
 ball of $M$ is $\{0\}$, a compact set).

### Remark

The key mechanism is that $\ker(T) = M$ is infinite-dimensional. Whenever a non-
compact operator has an infinite-dimensional kernel, its restriction to that ker
nel is the zero operator, hence compact. More generally, the restriction of $T$
to any infinite-dimensional subspace on which $T$ has finite rank (or is a limit
 of finite-rank operators) will be compact, regardless of $T$'s behavior on the
rest of $X$.

Conversely, this is not always possible: the identity $I: \ell^2 \to \ell^2$ is
non-compact, and its restriction to *any* infinite-dimensional subspace remains
the identity on that subspace, hence is never compact. So the phenomenon depends
 on the operator.
