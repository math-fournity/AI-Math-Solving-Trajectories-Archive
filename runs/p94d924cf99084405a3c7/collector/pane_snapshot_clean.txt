## Stone's Theorem

**Statement.** Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a complex Hilbert space and let $\{U(t)\}_{t
 \in \mathbb{R}}$ be a strongly continuous one-parameter unitary group on $\math
cal{H}$; that is,

- each $U(t) : \mathcal{H} \to \mathcal{H}$ is unitary,
- $U(0) = I$ and $U(s+t) = U(s)U(t)$ for all $s, t \in \mathbb{R}$,
- for every $\psi \in \mathcal{H}$, the map $t \mapsto U(t)\psi$ is continuous i
n the norm topology.

Then there exists a **unique** self-adjoint operator $A$ on $\mathcal{H}$ such t
hat

$$U(t) = e^{itA}, \qquad t \in \mathbb{R}.$$

Conversely, every self-adjoint operator $A$ on $\mathcal{H}$ generates, via $U(t
) := e^{itA}$, a strongly continuous one-parameter unitary group.

The operator $A$ is called the **infinitesimal generator** (or simply the genera
tor) of the group $\{U(t)\}$, and the relationship between $A$ and the group is

$$A\psi = \frac{1}{i}\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0} U(t)\psi = -i\left.\frac{d}
{dt}\right|_{t=0} U(t)\psi,$$

defined on the dense domain

$$D(A) = \left\{\psi \in \mathcal{H} : \lim_{t \to 0}\frac{U(t)\psi - \psi}{t} \
text{ exists in } \mathcal{H}\right\}.$$

---

### Explanation

**1. The generator and its domain.** Define the derivative at $t = 0$:

$$B\psi := \left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0} U(t)\psi = \lim_{t \to 0}\frac{U(t)\p
si - \psi}{t},$$

on the domain $D(B)$ consisting of those $\psi$ for which the limit exists. The
operator $B$ is the **generator** in the "group-theoretic" sense (the derivative
 of $U(t)$ at $t = 0$). Because $U(t)$ is unitary, $B$ is **skew-adjoint**: $B^*
 = -B$. Setting $A := -iB$ (equivalently $B = iA$) converts skew-adjointness int
o self-adjointness:

$$A^* = (-iB)^* = iB^* = i(-B) = -iB = A.$$

So $A$ is self-adjoint, and the generator of the unitary group is $B = iA$, i.e.
 **$i$ times a self-adjoint operator**. This is the precise relationship the pro
blem asks about: *the generator of a strongly continuous unitary group is of the
 form $iA$ where $A$ is self-adjoint, and conversely every $iA$ with $A$ self-ad
joint generates such a group.*

**2. Why self-adjointness (not merely symmetric).** The requirement that $A$ be
**self-adjoint** ($A = A^*$, including equality of domains) is essential. It is
what guarantees that $A$ admits a spectral resolution

$$A = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \lambda \, dE(\lambda)$$

via the Spectral Theorem, and hence that the exponential $e^{itA}$ is well-defin
ed as a bounded operator:

$$e^{itA} = \int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{it\lambda}\, dE(\lambda).$$

Since $|e^{it\lambda}| = 1$ for real $t, \lambda$, this operator is unitary. Str
ong continuity follows from the dominated convergence theorem applied to the spe
ctral measure. A merely symmetric (but not self-adjoint) operator does **not** i
n general have a spectral resolution, so $e^{itA}$ need not be definable as a un
itary operator on all of $\mathcal{H}$.

**3. The converse direction.** Given a self-adjoint $A$ with spectral measure $E
$, define

$$U(t) := e^{itA} = \int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{it\lambda}\, dE(\lambda).$$

Then:
- **Unitarity:** $U(t)^* = e^{-itA} = U(t)^{-1}$, so $U(t)$ is unitary.
- **Group property:** $U(s)U(t) = e^{isA}e^{itA} = e^{i(s+t)A} = U(s+t)$ by the
functional calculus.
- **Strong continuity:** For any $\psi \in \mathcal{H}$,

$$\|U(t)\psi - \psi\|^2 = \int_{\mathbb{R}} |e^{it\lambda} - 1|^2\, d\langle E(\
lambda)\psi, \psi\rangle \to 0 \quad (t \to 0)$$

by dominated convergence, since $|e^{it\lambda}-1|^2 \to 0$ pointwise and is bou
nded by $4$.

**4. Uniqueness.** If two self-adjoint operators $A_1, A_2$ generate the same gr
oup, then $e^{itA_1} = e^{itA_2}$ for all $t$. Differentiating at $t = 0$ on the
 common core shows $A_1 = A_2$ on a core, hence everywhere by self-adjointness.
Equivalently, the spectral measures coincide because the Fourier transforms of t
he spectral measures agree: $\int e^{it\lambda}\,dE_1 = \int e^{it\lambda}\,dE_2
$ for all $t$ implies $E_1 = E_2$ by uniqueness of the Fourier transform / Stone
–Weierstrass.

**5. Physical significance.** In quantum mechanics, observables are represented
by self-adjoint operators $A$, and the time-evolution (or the one-parameter grou
p generated by the Hamiltonian $H$) is $U(t) = e^{-itH/\hbar}$. Stone's theorem
is precisely the mathematical fact that makes this correspondence rigorous: *eve
ry continuous symmetry group of unitary transformations has a self-adjoint infin
itesimal generator, and vice versa.* The factor of $i$ bridges the real-valued s
pectrum of the observable (self-adjoint $A$) with the unitary (norm-preserving,
"rotational" in $\mathcal{H}$) character of the group $e^{itA}$.

---

### Summary of the key relationship

| Object | Property | Role |
|---|---|---|
| $A$ | self-adjoint ($A = A^*$) | observable / generator data |
| $iA$ | skew-adjoint ($(iA)^* = -iA$) | Lie-algebra generator of the unitary gr
oup |
| $U(t) = e^{itA}$ | strongly continuous unitary group | the one-parameter group
 |

The theorem establishes a **bijective correspondence**

$$\{\text{strongly continuous unitary groups } U(t)\} \;\longleftrightarrow\; \{
\text{self-adjoint operators } A\}, \qquad U(t) = e^{itA},\quad A = -i\left.\fra
c{d}{dt}\right|_{0} U(t).$$

The generator of the unitary group is $iA$ (a skew-adjoint operator), and $A$ is
 the unique self-adjoint operator obtained by dividing the derivative by $i$. Th
is is the content of Stone's theorem.
