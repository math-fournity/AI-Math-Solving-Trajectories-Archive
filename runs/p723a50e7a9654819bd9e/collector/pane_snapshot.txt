**Answer: No.**

**Counterexample.** Let $U = \mathbb{R}^2$ (open and convex) and define $f:\math
bb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}$ by
$$f(x,y)=x^2-y^2.$$
Then $f$ has continuous first and second partial derivatives on all of $\mathbb{
R}^2$. Its Hessian is constant:
$$H_{f(x,y)}=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&-2\end{pmatrix},$$
so
$$\det(H_{f(x,y)})=(2)(-2)-0=-4\neq 0$$
for every $(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2$.

Now take the nonzero vector $v=(1,1)^T$. At any point (say the origin),
$$v^T H_{f(0,0)}\,v=(1,1)\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&-2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix}=(1,1)\begin{pmatrix}2\\-2\end{pmatrix}=2-2=0.$$

Thus $\det(H_{f(X)})\neq 0$ for all $X\in U$, yet there exists a nonzero $v$ wit
h $v^T H_{f(X)}v=0$.

**Justification of why this is the right obstruction.** The condition $\det(H)\n
eq 0$ only guarantees that $H$ has no zero eigenvalue; it says nothing about the
 *signs* of the eigenvalues. The quadratic form $v\mapsto v^T H v$ vanishes on s
ome nonzero $v$ precisely when $H$ is **indefinite** (has both positive and nega
tive eigenvalues), and an indefinite symmetric matrix can perfectly well be nons
ingular. The determinant condition rules out singularity, not indefiniteness. In
 the example above the eigenvalues are $+2$ and $-2$, so $H$ is nonsingular but
indefinite, producing the isotropic direction $v=(1,1)$.

Therefore the implication does **not** hold in general.
