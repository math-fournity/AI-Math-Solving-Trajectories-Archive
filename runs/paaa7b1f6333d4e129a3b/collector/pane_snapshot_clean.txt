## Interpretation

The statement "extended by zero to the whole of $\Omega$, such that the extensio
n lies in $H^1(\Omega)$" cannot be read literally, since $u\in H^1_0(\Omega)$ is
 already defined on all of $\Omega$ and already belongs to $H^1(\Omega)$. The me
aningful, standard question — and the one the problem intends — is:

> **Can $u\in H^1_0(\Omega)$ be extended by zero to the whole of $\mathbb R^n$ s
o that the extension lies in $H^1(\mathbb R^n)$?**

The answer is **yes**, and in fact this property characterizes $H^1_0(\Omega)$.

## Proof

Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^n$ be open (Lipschitz regularity is used only for th
e converse, see Remark 2). Define the **zero-extension operator**
$$
(Eu)(x)=\begin{cases}u(x),&x\in\Omega,\\0,&x\in\mathbb R^n\setminus\Omega.\end{c
ases}
$$

**Claim.** For every $u\in H^1_0(\Omega)$, we have $Eu\in H^1(\mathbb R^n)$, wit
h
$$
\nabla(Eu)=E(\nabla u)\quad\text{a.e. in }\mathbb R^n,
\qquad
\|Eu\|_{H^1(\mathbb R^n)}=\|u\|_{H^1(\Omega)}.
$$

### Step 1. Isometry on the core $C_c^\infty(\Omega)$

Recall that $H^1_0(\Omega)$ is *defined* as the closure of $C_c^\infty(\Omega)$
in the $H^1(\Omega)$-norm. For $\varphi\in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$, its zero extensi
on $\tilde\varphi:=E\varphi$ belongs to $C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^n)$, hence to $H^1
(\mathbb R^n)$. Since $\varphi$ and all its derivatives vanish on a neighbourhoo
d of $\partial\Omega$,
$$
\partial_i\tilde\varphi = \widetilde{\partial_i\varphi}\quad\text{on }\mathbb R^
n,
$$
and therefore
$$
\|\tilde\varphi\|_{H^1(\mathbb R^n)}^2
=\int_{\mathbb R^n}|\tilde\varphi|^2+\sum_i|\widetilde{\partial_i\varphi}|^2
=\int_\Omega|\varphi|^2+\sum_i|\partial_i\varphi|^2
=\|\varphi\|_{H^1(\Omega)}^2.
$$
Thus $E:C_c^\infty(\Omega)\to H^1(\mathbb R^n)$ is a **linear isometry** (in the
 $H^1$ norm).

### Step 2. Extension by density

Since $C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ is dense in $H^1_0(\Omega)$ and $E$ is an isometry in
to the Banach space $H^1(\mathbb R^n)$, the BLT (bounded linear transformation)
theorem extends $E$ uniquely to a linear isometry
$$
E:H^1_0(\Omega)\hookrightarrow H^1(\mathbb R^n).
$$

### Step 3. The isometric extension is the pointwise zero extension

Let $u\in H^1_0(\Omega)$ and pick $\varphi_k\in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ with $\varph
i_k\to u$ in $H^1_0(\Omega)$. Then $E\varphi_k=\tilde\varphi_k\to Eu$ in $H^1(\m
athbb R^n)$, hence (up to a subsequence) a.e. in $\mathbb R^n$. On $\Omega$, $\t
ilde\varphi_k=\varphi_k\to u$ a.e.; on $\mathbb R^n\setminus\Omega$, $\tilde\var
phi_k\equiv 0$. Consequently
$$
(Eu)(x)=u(x)\cdot\mathbf 1_\Omega(x)\quad\text{a.e.},
$$
i.e. $Eu$ is exactly the pointwise zero extension of $u$.

### Step 4. The gradient formula

The same density argument gives $\partial_i(Eu)=E(\partial_i u)$: indeed $\parti
al_i\tilde\varphi_k=\widetilde{\partial_i\varphi_k}\to E(\partial_i u)$ in $L^2(
\mathbb R^n)$, while $\partial_i\tilde\varphi_k\to\partial_i(Eu)$ in $L^2(\mathb
b R^n)$ by definition of the weak derivative of the $H^1$-limit. Uniqueness of t
he limit yields
$$
\nabla(Eu)=E(\nabla u)\quad\text{a.e. in }\mathbb R^n.
$$
In particular $\nabla(Eu)\equiv 0$ on $\mathbb R^n\setminus\Omega$.

### Step 5. Conclusion

We have shown $Eu\in H^1(\mathbb R^n)$ with $\|Eu\|_{H^1(\mathbb R^n)}=\|u\|_{H^
1(\Omega)}$. Hence **every** $u\in H^1_0(\Omega)$ admits a zero extension in $H^
1(\mathbb R^n)$. $\quad\blacksquare$

### Remark 1 (Converse — characterization)

The converse also holds: if $u\in H^1(\Omega)$ and $Eu\in H^1(\mathbb R^n)$, the
n $u\in H^1_0(\Omega)$. Indeed, for Lipschitz $\Omega$ the trace operator $\gamm
a_0:H^1(\Omega)\to H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)$ is bounded, and $H^1_0(\Omega)=\ker\
gamma_0$. Since $Eu\in H^1(\mathbb R^n)$ has $\nabla(Eu)=0$ on $\mathbb R^n\setm
inus\overline\Omega$ (a set of positive measure adjacent to $\partial\Omega$), t
he trace of $u$ on $\partial\Omega$ from the inside equals the trace of $Eu$ fro
m the outside, which is $0$. Hence $\gamma_0 u=0$, so $u\in H^1_0(\Omega)$.

Combining both directions:
$$
u\in H^1_0(\Omega)\iff Eu\in H^1(\mathbb R^n).
$$
This is the precise sense in which "$H^1_0(\Omega)$ is exactly the subspace of $
H^1(\Omega)$ whose elements extend by zero to $H^1(\mathbb R^n)$."

### Remark 2 (Role of regularity)

The forward direction (the question asked) holds for **any** open set $\Omega$ —
 no Lipschitz assumption is needed, because it relies only on the definition of
$H^1_0$ as the closure of $C_c^\infty(\Omega)$. The Lipschitz hypothesis is used
 solely in the converse, where the trace characterization of $H^1_0(\Omega)$ req
uires enough boundary regularity to define the trace.

### Remark 3 (Contrast with $H^1(\Omega)\setminus H^1_0(\Omega)$)

For a general $u\in H^1(\Omega)$ with nonzero trace, the zero extension is **not
** in $H^1(\mathbb R^n)$: a distributional derivative picks up a boundary measur
e term $-u|_{\partial\Omega}\,\nu\,\mathcal H^{n-1}|_{\partial\Omega}$, which is
 not $L^2$. This shows the result is sharp — vanishing trace (equivalently, memb
ership in $H^1_0$) is exactly the obstruction.
