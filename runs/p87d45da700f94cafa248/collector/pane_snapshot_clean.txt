Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the condition on isotropic
vectors forces a symmetric matrix to be zero.

**Answer:** $A = 0$ if and only if $n \geq 3$. For $n = 1, 2$, $A$ need not be z

## Proof

Let $Q(x) = \sum_{j=1}^n (x^j)^2$ and $B(x,y) = \sum_j x^j y^j$ be the associate
d symmetric bilinear form. Isotropic vectors satisfy $Q(x) = B(x,x) = 0$.

### Case $n = 1$: No (vacuous)

The only isotropic vector is $0$, and no two linearly independent vectors exist
in $\mathbb{C}^1$. The condition is vacuous; $A = (a_{11})$ is arbitrary.

### Case $n = 2$: No (counterexample)

Isotropic vectors satisfy $(x^1)^2 + (x^2)^2 = 0$, so $x^1 = \pm i x^2$. They li
e on two lines: $\ell_+ = \operatorname{span}\{(1,i)\}$ and $\ell_- = \operatorn
ame{span}\{(1,-i)\}$. Any two linearly independent isotropic vectors must be $\a
lpha = a(1,i)$, $\beta = b(1,-i)$ with $a,b \neq 0$.

Computing: $A(\alpha,\beta) = ab\bigl(a_{11} + a_{22}\bigr)$ (the $a_{12}$ terms
 cancel: $-ia_{12}ab + ia_{21}ab = 0$). The condition forces only $a_{11} + a_{2
2} = 0$, leaving $a_{12}$ free. For example, $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0
 \end{pmatrix} \neq 0$ satisfies the condition.

### Case $n \geq 4$: Yes

**Step 1: $A(\alpha,\alpha) = 0$ for every isotropic $\alpha$.**

Given isotropic $\alpha \neq 0$, consider $\alpha^\perp = \{x : B(\alpha, x) = 0
\}$, a hyperplane of dimension $n-1$. Since $\alpha$ is isotropic, $\alpha \in \
alpha^\perp$. The radical of $B|_{\alpha^\perp}$ is $\alpha^\perp \cap (\alpha^\
perp)^\perp = \alpha^\perp \cap \langle \alpha \rangle = \langle \alpha \rangle$
, so $Q$ descends to a **nondegenerate** quadratic form $\bar{Q}$ on $\alpha^\pe
rp / \langle \alpha \rangle$, which has dimension $n - 2 \geq 2$.

Over $\mathbb{C}$, every nondegenerate quadratic form on a space of dimension $\
geq 2$ has a nonzero isotropic vector. So there exists $\gamma \in \alpha^\perp$
 with $Q(\gamma) = 0$ and $\gamma \notin \langle \alpha \rangle$.

For any $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$, the vector $\lambda\alpha + \gamma$ is isotrop
ic (since $Q(\lambda\alpha + \gamma) = 2\lambda B(\alpha,\gamma) + Q(\gamma) = 0
$) and linearly independent from $\alpha$. By hypothesis, $A(\alpha, \lambda\alp
ha + \gamma) = 0$ for all $\lambda$, i.e., $\lambda\, A(\alpha,\alpha) + A(\alph
a,\gamma) = 0$ for all $\lambda$. This forces $A(\alpha,\alpha) = 0$ (and $A(\al
pha,\gamma) = 0$).

**Step 2: $A = 0$.**

We now have $A(\alpha,\beta) = 0$ for **all** pairs of isotropic vectors (linear
ly independent pairs by hypothesis; $\alpha = \beta$ by Step 1). The isotropic v
ectors span $V$: for instance, $e_j = \frac{1}{2}\bigl[(e_j + ie_1) + (e_j - ie_
1)\bigr]$ for $j \geq 2$, and $e_1 = \frac{1}{2}\bigl[(e_1+ie_2)+(e_1-ie_2)\bigr
]$, where each summand is isotropic. Since $A$ vanishes on all pairs from a span
ning set, $A \equiv 0$.

### Case $n = 3$: Yes (coordinate argument)

The general argument above fails because $\alpha^\perp/\langle\alpha\rangle$ has
 dimension $1$, where a nondegenerate form has no nonzero isotropic vectors. We
proceed by direct computation.

**Diagonal entries.** The pairs $(1,i,0),(1,-i,0)$ and $(1,0,i),(1,0,-i)$ and $(
0,1,i),(0,1,-i)$ are each pairs of linearly independent isotropic vectors. Compu
ting $A(\alpha,\beta)$ for each gives:
$$a_{11}+a_{22}=0, \quad a_{11}+a_{33}=0, \quad a_{22}+a_{33}=0.$$
Adding the first two and subtracting the third yields $a_{11}=0$, hence $a_{22}=
a_{33}=0$.

**Off-diagonal entries.** The pair $(1,i,0),(0,1,i)$ — both isotropic, linearly
independent — gives:
$$a_{12} + ia_{13} - a_{23} = 0. \quad (\star)$$
The pair $(1,i,0),(0,1,-i)$ gives:
$$a_{12} - ia_{13} + a_{23} = 0. \quad (\star\star)$$
Adding $(\star)$ and $(\star\star)$: $a_{12} = 0$. Subtracting: $ia_{13} = a_{23
}$.

The pair $(1,0,i),(0,1,i)$ — both isotropic, linearly independent — gives (using
 $a_{12}=a_{33}=0$):
$$ia_{13} + a_{23} = 0.$$
Combined with $ia_{13} = a_{23}$, this yields $2a_{23} = 0$, so $a_{23} = 0$ and
 $a_{13} = 0$.

All entries are zero, so $A = 0$.

---

**Conclusion.** The implication $A = 0$ holds **if and only if $n \geq 3$**. For
 $n = 1$ the condition is vacuous; for $n = 2$ the matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0
 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is a counterexample.
