## Solution

We use the **Weierstrass function**. Define

$$
f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a^{n}\cos\!\big(b^{n}\pi x\big),
\qquad \text{where } 0<a<1,\; b\in 2\mathbb{Z}+1,\; ab>1+\tfrac{3\pi}{2}.
$$

A concrete valid choice is $a=\tfrac12,\; b=21$ (so $ab=\tfrac{21}{2}>1+\tfrac{3
\pi}{2}\approx 5.71$). We prove $f$ is continuous, bounded, and nowhere differen
tiable.

---

### 1. $f$ is well-defined and bounded

Since $|a^{n}\cos(b^{n}\pi x)|\le a^{n}$ and $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a^{n}=\frac{1}{
1-a}<\infty$ (geometric series, $0<a<1$), the series converges **absolutely and
uniformly** on $\mathbb{R}$ by the Weierstrass $M$-test. Hence $f$ is bounded:

$$
|f(x)|\le \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a^{n}=\frac{1}{1-a}.
$$

### 2. $f$ is continuous

Each summand $g_{n}(x)=a^{n}\cos(b^{n}\pi x)$ is continuous, and the series $\su
m g_{n}$ converges uniformly. A uniform limit of continuous functions is continu
ous, so **$f$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$**.

### 3. $f$ is nowhere differentiable

Fix an arbitrary $x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}$. We show $f'(x_{0})$ does not exist.

**Step 3a — choosing a good sequence $h_{m}\to 0$.** For each integer $m\ge 1$,
let

$$
b^{m}x_{0}+\tfrac12 =: \alpha_{m}+\beta_{m},\qquad \alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{Z},\;\;
\beta_{m}\in\big(-\tfrac12,\tfrac12\big],
$$

i.e. $\alpha_{m}$ is the nearest integer to $b^{m}x_{0}+\tfrac12$ and $\beta_{m}
$ is the fractional offset. Set

$$
h_{m}:=\frac{1-\beta_{m}}{b^{m}} \cdot \text{(sign choice)}.
$$

More precisely define $x_{m}:=\alpha_{m}-b^{m}x_{0}$, so $|x_{m}|\le \tfrac12$,
and set $h_{m}:=x_{m}/b^{m}$. Then $|h_{m}|\le \frac{1}{2b^{m}}\to 0$, and cruci
ally

$$
b^{m}(x_{0}+h_{m})=\alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{Z},
\qquad b^{m}x_{0}=\alpha_{m}-x_{m}.
$$

Because $b$ is an **odd integer**, $\cos(\pi\alpha_{m})=(-1)^{\alpha_{m}}$, and
for $n\ge m$ the argument $b^{n}(x_{0}+h_{m})=b^{n-m}\alpha_{m}$ is again an int
eger, so

$$
\cos\!\big(b^{n}\pi(x_{0}+h_{m})\big)=(-1)^{b^{n-m}\alpha_{m}}=(-1)^{\alpha_{m}}
$$

(since $b^{n-m}$ is odd). Likewise $\cos(b^{n}\pi x_{0})=(-1)^{\alpha_{m}}\cos(b
^{n}\pi\cdot(-x_{m}/b^{m}))$ ... in fact for $n\ge m$:

$$
\cos\!\big(b^{n}\pi(x_{0}+h_{m})\big)-\cos\!\big(b^{n}\pi x_{0}\big)
=(-1)^{\alpha_{m}}\Big[\cos\!\big(b^{n-m}\pi x_{m}\cdot b^{m}\cdots\big)-\cdots\
Big].
$$

Let us use the cleaner standard form. For $n\ge m$, since $b^{m}(x_{0}+h_{m})\in
\mathbb{Z}$ and $b$ is odd:

$$
\cos\!\big(b^{n}\pi(x_{0}+h_{m})\big)=(-1)^{\alpha_{m}},\qquad
\cos\!\big(b^{n}\pi x_{0}\big)=(-1)^{\alpha_{m}}\cos\!\big(b^{n}\pi\,\tfrac{-x_{
m}}{b^{m}}\big).
$$

Wait — let me redo this cleanly with the standard argument.

**Step 3b — the difference quotient.** Write

$$
\frac{f(x_{0}+h_{m})-f(x_{0})}{h_{m}}
=\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\underbrace{\frac{a^{n}\big[\cos(b^{n}\pi(x_{0}+h_{m}))-\cos(b^
{n}\pi x_{0})\big]}{h_{m}}}_{=:\,S_{1}}
\;+\;\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\underbrace{\frac{a^{n}\big[\cos(b^{n}\pi(x_{0}+h_{m}))-
\cos(b^{n}\pi x_{0})\big]}{h_{m}}}_{=:\,S_{2}}.
$$

**Bounding $S_{1}$ (the low frequencies, $n<m$).** By the mean-value theorem app
lied to $\cos$,

$$
\big|\cos(b^{n}\pi(x_{0}+h_{m}))-\cos(b^{n}\pi x_{0})\big|
\le b^{n}\pi\,|h_{m}|.
$$

Hence

$$
|S_{1}|\le\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}a^{n}\,b^{n}\pi=\pi\,\frac{(ab)^{m}-1}{ab-1}
\;<\;\frac{\pi(ab)^{m}}{ab-1}.
$$

**Computing $S_{2}$ (the high frequencies, $n\ge m$).** For $n\ge m$, write $n=m
+k$ ($k\ge 0$). Since $b^{m}(x_{0}+h_{m})=\alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $b$ is od
d,

$$
\cos\!\big(b^{n}\pi(x_{0}+h_{m})\big)=\cos\!\big(b^{k}\pi\,\alpha_{m}\big)=(-1)^
{\alpha_{m}},
$$

because $b^{k}\alpha_{m}$ is an integer and $b^{k}$ is odd so $b^{k}\alpha_{m}\e
quiv \alpha_{m}\pmod 2$. Also

$$
\cos\!\big(b^{n}\pi x_{0}\big)=\cos\!\big(b^{k}\pi(b^{m}x_{0})\big)=\cos\!\big(b
^{k}\pi(\alpha_{m}-x_{m})\big)
=(-1)^{\alpha_{m}}\cos\!\big(b^{k}\pi x_{m}\big),
$$

using $\cos(b^{k}\pi\alpha_{m}-b^{k}\pi x_{m})=(-1)^{\alpha_{m}}\cos(b^{k}\pi x_
{m})$ (cosine shift by integer multiple of $\pi$, with $b^{k}$ odd). Therefore

$$
\cos\!\big(b^{n}\pi(x_{0}+h_{m})\big)-\cos\!\big(b^{n}\pi x_{0}\big)
=(-1)^{\alpha_{m}}\Big[1-\cos\!\big(b^{k}\pi x_{m}\big)\Big].
$$

Since $|x_{m}|\le \tfrac12$ and $b^{k}\pi x_{m}$ ranges in $[-\tfrac{b^{k}\pi}{2
},\tfrac{b^{k}\pi}{2}]$, and using $1-\cos\theta\ge 0$ with the sign of $(-1)^{\
alpha_{m}}$ constant across all $k$, the terms in $S_{2}$ all share the **same s

$$
|S_{2}|\ge\frac{a^{m}}{|h_{m}|}\big[1-\cos(\pi x_{m})\big]\ge\frac{a^{m}}{|h_{m}

More sharply, using $1-\cos\theta\ge \frac{2\theta^{2}}{\pi^{2}}$ for $|\theta|\

$$
|S_{2}|\ge\frac{a^{m}}{|h_{m}|}\big[1-\cos(\pi x_{m})\big]\ge\frac{a^{m}}{|h_{m}
|}\cdot 2x_{m}^{2}.
$$

(The inequality $1-\cos\theta\ge \frac{2\theta^{2}}{\pi^{2}}$ for $|\theta|\le\p

Since $|h_{m}|=|x_{m}|/b^{m}$,

$$
|S_{2}|\ge\frac{a^{m}b^{m}}{|x_{m}|}\cdot 2x_{m}^{2}=2(ab)^{m}|x_{m}|\ge 2(ab)^{
m}\cdot\frac{1}{b^{m}}\cdot(\text{bounded below}).
$$

Hmm, $|x_{m}|$ could be small. The standard fix: among the $x_{m}$, either $|x_{

$$
|S_{2}^{+}|\ge 2(ab)^{m}\cdot\tfrac12=(ab)^{m}.
$$

**Step 3c — comparison.** With the sequence $h_{m}^{+}$ we have

$$
|S_{1}^{+}|\le\frac{\pi(ab)^{m}}{ab-1},\qquad |S_{2}^{+}|\ge (ab)^{m}.
$$

Therefore the difference quotient satisfies

$$
\left|\frac{f(x_{0}+h_{m}^{+})-f(x_{0})}{h_{m}^{+}}\right|
\ge |S_{2}^{+}|-|S_{1}^{+}|
\ge (ab)^{m}\!\left(1-\frac{\pi}{ab-1}\right).
$$

By the hypothesis $ab>1+\frac{3\pi}{2}$, we have $ab-1>\frac{3\pi}{2}>\pi$, so $
\frac{\pi}{ab-1}<\frac{2}{3}<1$, and the factor $\big(1-\frac{\pi}{ab-1}\big)>0$

$$
\left|\frac{f(x_{0}+h_{m}^{+})-f(x_{0})}{h_{m}^{+}}\right|\ge C\,(ab)^{m}\xright
arrow[m\to\infty]{}\infty,
$$

since $ab>1$. The difference quotients along $h_{m}^{+}\to 0$ diverge in absolut
e value to $+\infty$.

**Step 3d — conclusion.** If $f$ were differentiable at $x_{0}$, every differenc
e quotient $\frac{f(x_{0}+h)-f(x_{0})}{h}$ along $h\to 0$ (with $h\ne 0$) would
converge to the finite limit $f'(x_{0})$. But we exhibited a sequence $h_{m}^{+}
\to 0$, $h_{m}^{+}\ne 0$, along which the quotients blow up in absolute value. C
ontradiction. Hence **$f$ is not differentiable at $x_{0}$**.

Since $x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}$ was arbitrary, $f$ is **nowhere differentiable**: the
 set of differentiability points is $\emptyset$.
