## Solution

**Claim:** No such polynomials exist.

**Proof.** Suppose for contradiction that there exist polynomials $a(x), b(x), c
(y), d(y)$ with
$$1 + xy + x^2 y^2 = a(x)\,c(y) + b(x)\,d(y) \quad \text{identically.}$$

Write the coefficient expansions:
$$a(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i x^i, \quad b(x) = \sum_i \beta_i x^i, \quad c(y) = \sum
_j \gamma_j y^j, \quad d(y) = \sum_j \delta_j y^j.$$

Comparing the coefficient of $x^i y^j$ on both sides gives
$$M_{ij} \;=\; \alpha_i\,\gamma_j + \beta_i\,\delta_j,$$
where $M_{ij}$ is the coefficient of $x^i y^j$ in $1 + xy + x^2 y^2$.

In matrix form, the full coefficient matrix is
$$M = \boldsymbol{\alpha}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\!\top} + \boldsymbol{\beta}\,\b
oldsymbol{\delta}^{\!\top},$$
a sum of two outer products. Each outer product is a matrix of rank $\le 1$, so
$M$ has **rank $\le 2$**.

Now consider the $3 \times 3$ submatrix of $M$ indexed by rows $\{0,1,2\}$ and c
olumns $\{0,1,2\}$. Since the only nonzero coefficients of $1 + xy + x^2 y^2$ oc
cur at $(i,j) \in \{(0,0),(1,1),(2,2)\}$, this submatrix is
$$M' = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = I_3,$
$
which has **rank $3$**.

But $M'$ is the corresponding $3\times 3$ submatrix of $\boldsymbol{\alpha}\,\bo
ldsymbol{\gamma}^{\!\top} + \boldsymbol{\beta}\,\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\!\top}$, i
.e.,
$$M' = \boldsymbol{\alpha}'\,(\boldsymbol{\gamma}')^{\!\top} + \boldsymbol{\beta
}'\,(\boldsymbol{\delta}')^{\!\top},$$
where $\boldsymbol{\alpha}' = (\alpha_0,\alpha_1,\alpha_2)^{\top}$, etc. This is
 again a sum of two rank-$\le 1$ matrices, so $\operatorname{rank}(M') \le 2$.

This contradicts $\operatorname{rank}(M') = \operatorname{rank}(I_3) = 3$.

Therefore, no polynomials $a(x), b(x), c(y), d(y)$ can satisfy the identity $1 +
 xy + x^2 y^2 = a(x)c(y) + b(x)d(y)$. $\blacksquare$
