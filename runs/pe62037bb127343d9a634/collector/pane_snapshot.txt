# Proof: $V(t)$ is dense in $H^1(0,1)$

**Claim.** $V(t) = H^1(0,1)$ for every $t \in [0,T]$. In particular, $V(t)$ is d
ense in $H^1(0,1)$ (indeed, equal to it).

## Setup and notation

Fix $t \in [0,T]$. Since $\alpha$ is continuous on the compact set $[0,T]\times[
0,1]$, the function $x \mapsto \alpha(t,x)$ is continuous on $[0,1]$, hence boun
ded: there exists $M(t) > 0$ such that
$$|\alpha(t,x)| \le M(t), \quad \forall\, x \in [0,1].$$

The space $V(t)$ is defined as
$$V(t) = \bigl\{v \in L^2(0,1) : v' + \alpha(t,x)\,v \in L^2(0,1)\bigr\},$$
where $v'$ denotes the derivative in the sense of distributions on $(0,1)$, and
the condition $v' + \alpha(t,x)v \in L^2(0,1)$ means that the distribution $v' +
 \alpha(t,\cdot)\,v$ is represented by an $L^2$ function.

## Step 1: $V(t) \subseteq H^1(0,1)$

Let $v \in V(t)$. Then $v \in L^2(0,1)$ and $w := v' + \alpha(t,\cdot)\,v \in L^
2(0,1)$ (as distributions). Since $\alpha(t,\cdot) \in C([0,1]) \subset L^\infty
(0,1)$ with $\|\alpha(t,\cdot)\|_{L^\infty} \le M(t)$, and $v \in L^2(0,1)$, we
have
$$\alpha(t,\cdot)\,v \in L^2(0,1), \quad \text{with } \|\alpha\,v\|_{L^2} \le M(
t)\,\|v\|_{L^2}.$$

Now, in the sense of distributions:
$$v' = w - \alpha(t,\cdot)\,v.$$

Both $w$ and $\alpha(t,\cdot)\,v$ belong to $L^2(0,1)$, so their difference $v'
\in L^2(0,1)$. Since $v \in L^2(0,1)$ and $v' \in L^2(0,1)$, we conclude $v \in
H^1(0,1)$.

## Step 2: $H^1(0,1) \subseteq V(t)$

Let $v \in H^1(0,1)$. Then $v \in L^2(0,1)$ and $v' \in L^2(0,1)$. As above, $\a
lpha(t,\cdot)\,v \in L^2(0,1)$ because $\alpha(t,\cdot)$ is bounded and $v \in L
^2(0,1)$. Therefore
$$v' + \alpha(t,\cdot)\,v \in L^2(0,1),$$
being the sum of two $L^2$ functions. Hence $v \in V(t)$.

## Conclusion

From Steps 1 and 2, $V(t) = H^1(0,1)$ for every $t \in [0,T]$.

The crucial point is that the continuity of $\alpha$ on the compact set $[0,T]\t
imes[0,1]$ implies $\alpha(t,\cdot)$ is **bounded** for each fixed $t$. This mak
es multiplication by $\alpha(t,\cdot)$ a bounded operator on $L^2(0,1)$, so the
perturbation $\alpha(t,\cdot)\,v$ is always in $L^2$ whenever $v \in L^2$. Conse
quently, the condition $v' + \alpha(t,\cdot)\,v \in L^2$ is equivalent to $v' \i
n L^2$ (given $v \in L^2$), which is precisely the defining condition of $H^1(0,
1)$.

Therefore, **$V(t)$ is dense in $H^1(0,1)$** — in fact, $V(t)$ coincides exactly
 with $H^1(0,1)$.
