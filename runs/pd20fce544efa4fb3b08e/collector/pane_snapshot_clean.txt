## Proof

We evaluate $I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos x}{x}\,dx$ (understood as a C
auchy principal value, since the integrand has a non-integrable singularity at $
x=0$ and is not absolutely integrable at infinity).

### Strategy

Consider the auxiliary complex integral
$$J = \text{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ix}}{x}\,dx,$$
so that $\Re(J) = \text{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos x}{x}\,dx = I$ and
$\Im(J) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx$.

### Contour

The function $f(z)=\dfrac{e^{iz}}{z}$ has a simple pole at $z=0$ lying on the re
al axis. We use the standard indented contour in the **upper half-plane**:

- $\Gamma_R$: the large semicircle $|z|=R$, $\Im z\ge 0$, traversed counterclock
wise;
- $\gamma_\varepsilon$: the small semicircle $|z|=\varepsilon$, $\Im z\ge 0$, tr
aversed **clockwise** (to avoid the pole);
- the two real-axis segments $[-R,-\varepsilon]$ and $[\varepsilon,R]$.

Let $C$ denote the closed positively-oriented boundary of the resulting domain (
large arc $+$ real segments $+$ small arc). Since $f$ is holomorphic inside and
on $C$, by Cauchy's theorem
$$\oint_C f(z)\,dz = 0.$$

### Large arc $\Gamma_R$

On $\Gamma_R$, write $z=Re^{i\theta}$, $0\le\theta\le\pi$. Then $|e^{iz}|=e^{-R\
sin\theta}\le 1$ and $|f(z)|=\dfrac{e^{-R\sin\theta}}{R}$. By **Jordan's lemma**
 (applied to $e^{iz}/z$, with $g(z)=1/z\to 0$ as $|z|\to\infty$),
$$\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{\Gamma_R}\frac{e^{iz}}{z}\,dz = 0.$$

### Small arc $\gamma_\varepsilon$

On $\gamma_\varepsilon$, write $z=\varepsilon e^{i\theta}$ with $\theta$ going f
rom $\pi$ to $0$ (clockwise). Near $z=0$, $f(z)=\dfrac{e^{iz}}{z}=\dfrac{1}{z}+1
+\cdots$, so
$$\int_{\gamma_\varepsilon}\frac{e^{iz}}{z}\,dz = \int_{\gamma_\varepsilon}\frac
{1}{z}\,dz + \int_{\gamma_\varepsilon}\bigl(e^{iz}-1\bigr)\frac{dz}{z}.$$
The second term vanishes as $\varepsilon\to 0$ since $|e^{iz}-1|=O(\varepsilon)$
 and the arc length is $O(\varepsilon)$. For the first term,
$$\int_{\gamma_\varepsilon}\frac{dz}{z}=\int_{\pi}^{0}\frac{i\varepsilon e^{i\th
eta}}{\varepsilon e^{i\theta}}\,d\theta = i\int_{\pi}^{0}d\theta = -i\pi.$$
Hence
$$\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\gamma_\varepsilon}\frac{e^{iz}}{z}\,dz = -i\pi.$
$

### Assembling

From $\oint_C f=0$,
$$\int_{-R}^{-\varepsilon}\frac{e^{ix}}{x}\,dx + \int_{\gamma_\varepsilon}f\,dz
+ \int_{\varepsilon}^{R}\frac{e^{ix}}{x}\,dx + \int_{\Gamma_R}f\,dz = 0.$$
Letting $R\to\infty$ and $\varepsilon\to 0$,
$$\text{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ix}}{x}\,dx + (-i\pi) + 0 = 0,$$
so
$$\text{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ix}}{x}\,dx = i\pi.$$

### Extracting the real part

Taking real parts of both sides:
$$\text{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos x}{x}\,dx = \Re(i\pi) = 0.$$

(For completeness, taking imaginary parts gives the classical Dirichlet integral
 $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx=\pi$.)

### Consistency check (parity)

The integrand $\dfrac{\cos x}{x}$ is odd: $\dfrac{\cos(-x)}{-x}=-\dfrac{\cos x}{
x}$. Therefore its Cauchy principal value over the symmetric interval $(-\infty,
\infty)$ vanishes, in agreement with the complex-integral computation above.

## Result

$$\boxed{\,\text{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos x}{x}\,dx = 0\,}$$
