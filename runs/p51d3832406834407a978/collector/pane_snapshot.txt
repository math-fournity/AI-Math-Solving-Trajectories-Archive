**Answer: Yes.**

**Proof:**

We construct the sequence inductively. Set $S_0 = 0$. At each step $n \geq 1$, g

n$ is not a perfect power.

**Key observation:** Any integer $m \equiv 2 \pmod{4}$ is not a perfect power.

*Proof of observation:* Suppose $m = a^b$ with $b \geq 2$.
- If $a$ is even, then $4 \mid a^b$ (since $b \geq 2$), so $m \equiv 0 \pmod{4}$
.
- If $a$ is odd, then $a^b$ is odd, so $m$ is odd.

In neither case is $m \equiv 2 \pmod{4}$. ∎

**Construction:** At step $n$, among the four consecutive integers $S_{n-1}+1, S
_{n-1}+2, S_{n-1}+3, S_{n-1}+4$, at least one is congruent to $2 \pmod{4}$ (sinc
e any four consecutive integers cover all residues mod 4). By the observation ab
ove, that integer is not a perfect power. Let $a_n$ be the smallest $k \in \{1,2
,3,4\}$ such that $S_{n-1}+k$ is not a perfect power, and set $S_n = S_{n-1} + a
_n$.

**Verification:**
- Each $a_n$ is a natural number (in $\{1,2,3,4\}$), so the sequence is well-def
ined and infinite.
- Each partial sum $S_n$ is not a perfect power by construction.
- The induction never gets stuck, since among any four consecutive integers, at
least one is $\equiv 2 \pmod{4}$ and hence not a perfect power.

Thus there exists an infinite sequence of natural numbers such that every finite
