## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, $X$ must be a Hilbert space.

Let $X$ be an inner product space (over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$; the argume
nt is identical in both cases, and we write the real case for clarity). Let $X'$
 denote the continuous (topological) dual of $X$. The hypothesis is that the Rie
sz map
$$\Phi: X \to X', \qquad \Phi(z) = f_z, \quad f_z(x) = \langle x, z\rangle$$
is surjective. We show $X$ is complete.

### Step 1. Embed $X$ into its completion.

Every inner product space $X$ has a completion $H$: a Hilbert space $H$ together
 with a linear isometry $i: X \to H$ such that $i(X)$ is dense in $H$. We identi
fy $X$ with $i(X) \subseteq H$, so $X$ is a dense subspace of the Hilbert space
$H$, and the inner product on $X$ is the restriction of the inner product on $H$
.

### Step 2. Construct a continuous functional from a vector in $H \setminus X$.

Suppose, for contradiction, that $X$ is **not** complete. Then $H \setminus X \n
eq \varnothing$; pick $y \in H \setminus X$.

Define $g: X \to \mathbb{R}$ by
$$g(x) = \langle x, y \rangle_H, \qquad x \in X.$$
By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in $H$,
$$|g(x)| = |\langle x, y\rangle_H| \le \|x\|_H \, \|y\|_H = \|x\| \, \|y\|,$$
so $g$ is a bounded (continuous) linear functional on $X$, i.e. $g \in X'$.

### Step 3. Apply surjectivity of $\Phi$.

By the hypothesis that $\Phi$ is surjective, there exists $z \in X$ such that $\
Phi(z) = g$, meaning
$$\langle x, z\rangle = \langle x, y\rangle_H \qquad \text{for all } x \in X.$$
(The left side is the inner product in $X$, which equals the inner product in $H
$ since $X \subseteq H$.) Hence
$$\langle x,\; z - y\rangle_H = 0 \qquad \text{for all } x \in X.$$

### Step 4. Use density to force $z = y$.

Set $w = z - y \in H$. We have $w \perp X$ (orthogonal in $H$). Since $X$ is den
se in $H$ and the map $v \mapsto \langle v, w\rangle_H$ is continuous on $H$, th
e set
$$\{v \in H : \langle v, w\rangle_H = 0\}$$
is a closed subspace of $H$ containing the dense set $X$, so it equals $H$. In p
articular $\langle w, w\rangle_H = 0$, giving $w = 0$.

Therefore $z - y = 0$, i.e. $z = y$.

### Step 5. Contradiction.

But $z \in X$ while $y \in H \setminus X$, so $z = y$ is impossible. This contra
diction shows that $H \setminus X = \varnothing$, i.e. $X = H$.

### Conclusion.

Since $X$ equals its completion $H$ and $H$ is complete, $X$ is complete. A comp
lete inner product space is by definition a Hilbert space. $\blacksquare$
