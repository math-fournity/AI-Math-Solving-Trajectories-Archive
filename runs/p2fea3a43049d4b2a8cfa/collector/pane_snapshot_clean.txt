# Proof

**Claim.** There exists a sequence whose set of partial limits (subsequential li
mits) is exactly the interval $[0,1]$.

**Construction.** The set $\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$ is countable, so it can be enume
rated. Let $(a_n)_{n\geq 1}$ be any enumeration of $\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$, i.e.,
a sequence in which every rational number in $[0,1]$ appears exactly once.

We show that the set $L$ of partial limits of $(a_n)$ equals $[0,1]$.

---

**Part 1: $[0,1]\subseteq L$.**

Let $x\in[0,1]$. Since $\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$ is dense in $[0,1]$, for every $k\g
eq 1$ there exists a rational $r_k\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$ with $|r_k - x|<\tfrac
{1}{k}$.

We build a subsequence inductively. Set $n_0=0$. Having chosen $n_0<n_1<\cdots<n
_{k-1}$, pick $n_k>n_{k-1}$ such that $a_{n_k}=r_k$. This is possible because $r
_k$ appears exactly once in the enumeration, at some index $m_k$; since only fin
itely many indices $\leq n_{k-1}$ have been used, for all but finitely many of t
he $r_k$ we have $m_k>n_{k-1}$. To handle the finitely many cases where $m_k\leq
 n_{k-1}$, simply choose a *fresh* rational $r_k'\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$ with $|
r_k'-x|<\tfrac{1}{k}$ that has not yet appeared among $a_1,\dots,a_{n_{k-1}}$ —
such a rational exists because infinitely many rationals lie within $\tfrac{1}{k
}$ of $x$, while only finitely many have been used.

Thus we obtain a subsequence $(a_{n_k})$ with $|a_{n_k}-x|<\tfrac{1}{k}\to 0$, s
o $a_{n_k}\to x$. Hence $x\in L$.

---

**Part 2: $L\subseteq[0,1]$.**

Every term $a_n$ lies in $[0,1]$, which is a closed set. Any convergent subseque
nce of $(a_n)$ has its limit in the closure of $\{a_n:n\geq 1\}\subseteq[0,1]$,
and since $[0,1]$ is closed, the limit belongs to $[0,1]$. Therefore every parti
al limit lies in $[0,1]$.

---

**Conclusion.** Combining Parts 1 and 2, $L=[0,1]$. An enumeration of $\mathbb{Q
}\cap[0,1]$ is the desired sequence. $\blacksquare$
