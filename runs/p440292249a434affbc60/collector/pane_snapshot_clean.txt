The space described is the 2-sphere $S^2$ with antipodal points identified, $x \
sim -x$. This is precisely the **real projective plane** $\mathbb{RP}^2$.

## Proof

**Step 1: Identify the space.**

The real projective plane is defined as the quotient of $S^2$ by the equivalence
 relation $x \sim -x$ (identifying antipodal points). The antipodal map $a: S^2
\to S^2$, $a(x) = -x$, is a free action of $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ on $S^2$ (fr
ee because $a(x) = x$ has no solution on $S^2$). The quotient $S^2/\langle a \ra
ngle$ is exactly $\mathbb{RP}^2$.

**Step 2: Use the covering space structure.**

The quotient map $p: S^2 \to \mathbb{RP}^2$ is a 2-sheeted covering map. Since $
S^2$ is simply connected ($\pi_1(S^2) = 0$), $p: S^2 \to \mathbb{RP}^2$ is the *
*universal cover** of $\mathbb{RP}^2$.

**Step 3: Apply the theory of covering spaces.**

For a universal cover $p: \tilde{X} \to X$ with $\tilde{X}$ simply connected, th
e fundamental group of the base is isomorphic to the group of deck transformatio
ns of the cover:

$$\pi_1(X) \cong \text{Deck}(p)$$

The deck transformations of $p: S^2 \to \mathbb{RP}^2$ are the homeomorphisms $f
: S^2 \to S^2$ satisfying $p \circ f = p$. These are exactly the maps that permu
te points within each fiber $\{x, -x\}$. The two deck transformations are:
- The identity: $x \mapsto x$
- The antipodal map: $x \mapsto -x$

These form a group isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, since the antipodal m
ap has order 2 ($a \circ a = \text{id}$).

**Step 4: Conclusion.**

Therefore:

$$\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^2) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$$

**Verification via van Kampen (alternative approach).** We can also see this by
representing $\mathbb{RP}^2$ as a disk $D^2$ with antipodal boundary points iden
tified. The boundary circle becomes a single loop $\gamma$ in the quotient. The
disk is simply connected, and the identification on the boundary means that trav
ersing the boundary of the disk once in the quotient corresponds to going around
 $\gamma$ twice in a way that contracts (since the boundary of the disk is contr
actible through the disk). This gives the relation $\gamma^2 = 1$, yielding $\pi
_1(\mathbb{RP}^2) = \langle \gamma \mid \gamma^2 = 1 \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\
mathbb{Z}$, confirming the result.

## Answer

The fundamental group of the 2-sphere with the identification $x = -x$ (i.e., $\
mathbb{RP}^2$) is:

$$\boxed{\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^2) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}}$$
