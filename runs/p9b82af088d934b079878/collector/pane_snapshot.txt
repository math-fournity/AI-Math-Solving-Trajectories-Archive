## Proof

**Claim:** Not every symmetric matrix can be decomposed into a sum of two positi
ve definite matrices. The answer is **No**.

**Key fact:** The sum of two positive definite matrices is positive definite.

*Proof of key fact.* Let $P, Q$ be positive definite. For any nonzero vector $v$
,
$$v^{\top}(P+Q)v = v^{\top}Pv + v^{\top}Qv > 0 + 0 = 0,$$
since each term is strictly positive by positive definiteness. Hence $P+Q$ is po
sitive definite. $\square$

**Counterexample.** Consider the symmetric matrix $A = -I$ (the negative identit
y matrix, in any dimension $n \ge 1$). Its eigenvalues are all $-1 < 0$, so $A$
is **not** positive definite — in fact it is negative definite.

Suppose for contradiction that $A = P + Q$ with $P, Q$ positive definite. By the
 key fact, $P + Q$ is positive definite, so $A = -I$ would have to be positive d
efinite. But for any unit vector $e_1$,
$$e_1^{\top}(-I)e_1 = -1 < 0,$$
contradicting positive definiteness. Therefore no such decomposition exists for
$A = -I$.

**Conclusion.** Since there exists a symmetric matrix (e.g., $-I$) that cannot b
e written as a sum of two positive definite matrices, it is **not** the case tha
t *any* symmetric matrix admits such a decomposition. In fact, a necessary condi
tion is that the matrix itself be positive definite (since the sum of two positi
ve definite matrices is positive definite), so the class of decomposable symmetr
ic matrices is exactly the positive definite ones — a strict subset of all symme
tric matrices.
