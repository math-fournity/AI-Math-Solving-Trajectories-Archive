## Proof

We apply the **Seifert–van Kampen theorem** to the space $X = U \cup V$, where:
- $U$ is the torus, with $\pi_1(U) = \langle \alpha, \beta \mid \alpha\beta = \b
eta\alpha \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$,
- $V$ is the sphere, with $\pi_1(V) = \langle \emptyset \mid \emptyset \rangle =
 0$ (the sphere is simply connected),
- $U \cap V$ is a circle, with $\pi_1(U \cap V) = \langle \gamma \mid \emptyset
\rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}$.

### Step 1: Identify the inclusion homomorphisms

The Seifert–van Kampen theorem requires two inclusion-induced homomorphisms:

- $i_1: \pi_1(U \cap V) \to \pi_1(U)$, induced by the inclusion $U \cap V \hookr
ightarrow U$,
- $i_2: \pi_1(U \cap V) \to \pi_1(V)$, induced by the inclusion $U \cap V \hookr
ightarrow V$.

**Map $i_2$:** Since $\pi_1(V) = 0$ (the sphere is simply connected), every loop
 in $V$ is null-homotopic. Therefore:
$$i_2(\gamma) = e \quad \text{(the identity element)}.$$

**Map $i_1$:** The circle $U \cap V$ lies on the torus as a simple closed curve.
 In the standard geometric picture of a sphere placed inside a torus, the inters
ection circle is a **meridian** of the torus, which corresponds to one of the tw
o generators of $\pi_1(U)$. Taking this to be $\alpha$, we have:
$$i_1(\gamma) = \alpha.$$

### Step 2: Apply Seifert–van Kampen

The theorem states that $\pi_1(X)$ is the **amalgamated free product**:
$$\pi_1(X) = \pi_1(U) *_{\pi_1(U \cap V)} \pi_1(V).$$

In terms of group presentations, this is obtained by:
1. Taking all generators from $\pi_1(U)$ and $\pi_1(V)$,
2. Taking all relations from both groups,
3. Adding the **amalgamation relations** $i_1(g) = i_2(g)$ for every generator $
g$ of $\pi_1(U \cap V)$.

Applying this:

$$\pi_1(X) = \langle \alpha, \beta \mid \alpha\beta = \beta\alpha, \; i_1(\gamma
) = i_2(\gamma) \rangle.$$

Substituting $i_1(\gamma) = \alpha$ and $i_2(\gamma) = e$:

$$\boxed{\pi_1(X) = \langle \alpha, \beta \mid \alpha\beta = \beta\alpha, \; \al
pha = 1 \rangle.}$$

### Step 3: Simplify the presentation

The relation $\alpha = 1$ allows us to eliminate the generator $\alpha$. Substit
uting $\alpha = 1$ into the commutativity relation $\alpha\beta = \beta\alpha$ g
ives $1 \cdot \beta = \beta \cdot 1$, which is trivially satisfied and imposes n
o further constraint on $\beta$.

Thus:
$$\pi_1(X) = \langle \beta \mid \emptyset \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}.$$

### Conclusion

The resulting presentation of $\pi_1(X)$ is:
$$\pi_1(X) = \langle \alpha, \beta \mid \alpha\beta = \beta\alpha,\; \alpha = 1
\rangle,$$
and the fundamental group of this space is:
$$\pi_1(X) \cong \mathbb{Z}.$$

**Geometric interpretation:** Attaching a simply-connected sphere to the torus a
long a meridian circle kills the meridian generator $\alpha$ of the torus, while
 the longitude generator $\beta$ survives. The fundamental group reduces from $\
mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$ to $\mathbb{Z}$.
