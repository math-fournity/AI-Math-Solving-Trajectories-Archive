Looking at this problem, I need to find $D(n)$, the minimum number of colors to
color $n$ keys on a ring (with rotational and reflectional symmetry — the dihedr
al group) so that every key is uniquely identifiable. This is the **distinguishi
ng number** of the cycle graph $C_n$.

## Setup

The keys sit on a ring that can be **rotated** and **turned over** (reflected),
so the symmetry group is the dihedral group $D_n$ of order $2n$. A coloring "dis
tinguishes" all keys iff the only symmetry preserving the coloring is the identi
ty — i.e., the coloring has **trivial stabilizer**.

## Computing $D(n)$ for each $n$

**$n = 1$:** Single key, trivial symmetry group. $D(1) = 1$.

**$n = 2$:** Two keys that can be swapped (by rotation or flip). One color leave
s them indistinguishable; two colors break the swap. $D(2) = 2$.

**$n = 3$:** Dihedral group $D_3$, order 6. Every 2-coloring has a nontrivial sy
mmetry:
- One red, two blue: reflection through the red vertex preserves it.
- Two red, one blue: reflection through the blue vertex preserves it.
- All same: full symmetry.
So $D(3) = 3$ (three distinct colors trivially breaks all symmetry).

**$n = 4$:** Dihedral group $D_4$, order 8. I checked all $2^4 = 16$ two-colorin
gs (up to rotation: 0000, 0001, 0011, 0101, 0111, 1111 and their rotations). Eve
ry case has a nontrivial symmetry:
- 0001: reflection through the lone colored vertex.
- 0011: reflection through the midpoint of the two colored vertices.
- 0101: rotation by 2.
- 0111: reflection through the lone uncolored vertex.
So $D(4) = 3$.

**$n = 5$:** Dihedral group $D_5$, order 10. Two-colorings up to rotation (by nu
mber of red keys):
- 1 red: reflection through that vertex.
- 2 reds at distance 1 (adjacent): reflection through the midpoint of the pair.
- 2 reds at distance 2: e.g., positions 3,5 red — reflection through vertex 4 ma
ps $3\leftrightarrow 5$, preserving the coloring.
- 3+ reds: complements of the above, same conclusion.
Every 2-coloring has a nontrivial symmetry. So $D(5) = 3$.

**$n = 6$:** Dihedral group $D_6$, order 12. The coloring $(0,0,1,0,1,1)$ (posit
ions 3,5,6 red) has **trivial stabilizer**:
- **Rotations:** Each non-identity rotation produces a different pattern (verifi
ed all 5).
- **Reflections through opposite vertices** (axes 1–4, 2–5, 3–6): Each maps some
 0-position to a 1-position, breaking the coloring.
- **Reflections through edge midpoints** (axes 1₂–4₅, 2₃–5₆, 3₄–6₁): Each maps s
ome 0 to a 1, breaking the coloring.
So $D(6) = 2$.

**$n = 7$:** The coloring $(0,0,0,1,0,1,1)$ has trivial stabilizer (verified all
 7 reflections map a 0 to a 1; all 6 non-trivial rotations produce distinct patt
erns). So $D(7) = 2$.

**$n = 8, 9, 10$:** For $n \geq 6$, there are always enough positions to place c
olored keys asymmetrically. A coloring like $(0,\ldots,0,1,0,1,1)$ with the 1's
clustered asymmetrically at the end always has trivial stabilizer — no rotation
maps the cluster to itself, and no reflection preserves the asymmetric pattern.
So $D(n) = 2$ for all $n \geq 6$.

|-----|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $D(n)$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 |

## Computing $S$

$$S = \sum_{n=1}^{10} D(n) = 1 + 2 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 22$$

The key structural insight: for small cycles ($n = 3, 4, 5$), the dihedral group
 is "too symmetric" relative to the number of positions — every 2-coloring admit
s a nontrivial symmetry. Starting at $n = 6$, there is enough room to place a mi
nority color asymmetrically so that no rotation or reflection preserves the patt
ern, making 2 colors sufficient.

$$\boxed{S = 22}$$
