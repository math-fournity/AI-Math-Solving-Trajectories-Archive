**Theorem.** If a field $F$ has a cyclic multiplicative group $F^\times$, then $
F$ is finite.

**Proof.** We split into two cases by characteristic.

---

**Case 1: $\operatorname{char}(F) = 0$.**

The prime subfield of $F$ is $\mathbb{Q}$, so $\mathbb{Q}^\times \subseteq F^\ti
mes$. Since every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic, $\mathbb{Q}^\times$ woul
d have to be cyclic. But $\mathbb{Q}^\times$ is not cyclic: if $\mathbb{Q}^\time
s = \langle q \rangle$ for some $q \in \mathbb{Q}^\times$, then for two distinct
 primes $p_1, p_2$ we would have $p_1 = q^a$ and $p_2 = q^b$ for some integers $
a, b$, giving $p_1^b = q^{ab} = p_2^a$. By unique factorization this forces $a =
 b = 0$, hence $p_1 = p_2 = 1$, a contradiction. So this case cannot occur.

---

**Case 2: $\operatorname{char}(F) = p > 0$.**

The prime subfield is $\mathbb{F}_p$. Let $g$ be a generator of the cyclic group
 $F^\times$, so every nonzero element of $F$ is $g^n$ for some $n \in \mathbb{Z}
$.

*Subcase 2a: $g$ has finite order $n$.* Then $|F^\times| = n$, so $|F| = n+1$ is
 finite. Done.

*Subcase 2b: $g$ has infinite order.* Consider the element $1 + g$.

- If $1 + g = 0$, then $g = -1$. In characteristic $2$, this gives $g = 1$, whic
h has order $1$; in characteristic $p > 2$, this gives $g^2 = 1$, so $g$ has ord
er $2$. Either way this contradicts $g$ having infinite order.

- So $1 + g \neq 0$, and since $F^\times = \langle g \rangle$, there exists an i
nteger $a$ with $1 + g = g^a$.

  - $a \neq 0$: otherwise $g^0 = 1 = 1 + g$, giving $g = 0$, impossible.
  - $a \neq 1$: otherwise $g = 1 + g$, giving $1 = 0$, impossible.

  If $a > 0$, the relation $g^a = 1 + g$ gives $g^a - g - 1 = 0$, a nontrivial p
olynomial equation over $\mathbb{F}_p$.

  If $a < 0$, write $a = -m$ with $m > 0$. Then $g^{-m} = 1 + g$, so $1 = g^m +
g^{m+1}$, i.e., $g^{m+1} + g^m - 1 = 0$, again a nontrivial polynomial equation
over $\mathbb{F}_p$.

  In either subcase, $g$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{F}_p$, so $\mathbb{F}_p(g)$
is a finite extension of $\mathbb{F}_p$, hence a finite field. But every element
 of $F^\times$ is a power of $g$, and $g \in \mathbb{F}_p(g)$, so $F^\times \sub
seteq \mathbb{F}_p(g)^\times$. This makes $F$ finite, contradicting $g$ having i
nfinite order.

So Subcase 2b is impossible, and $g$ must have finite order. Therefore $F$ is fi
nite.

---

Both cases lead to the conclusion that $F$ is finite (Case 1 is outright impossi
ble, Case 2 forces finiteness). $\blacksquare$
