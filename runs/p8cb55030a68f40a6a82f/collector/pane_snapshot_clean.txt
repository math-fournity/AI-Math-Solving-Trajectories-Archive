## Proof

**Example:** The field of real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ is not algebraically closed,
 yet its algebraic closure is a finite extension.

**Claim.** $\mathbb{R}$ is not algebraically closed, and $\overline{\mathbb{R}}
= \mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}(i)$ with $[\overline{\mathbb{R}}:\mathbb{R}]=2$.

**Proof.**

**Step 1: $\mathbb{R}$ is not algebraically closed.**

The polynomial $f(x) = x^2 + 1 \in \mathbb{R}[x]$ has no root in $\mathbb{R}$, s
ince $x^2 \geq 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, so $x^2 + 1 \geq 1 > 0$. Hence $\m
athbb{R}$ is not algebraically closed.

**Step 2: $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}(i)$ is an algebraic extension of $\mathbb{R}$
 of degree 2.**

Let $i$ denote a root of $x^2+1$. Then $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}(i)$, and $i$ sat
isfies the irreducible polynomial $x^2+1$ over $\mathbb{R}$, so $[\mathbb{C}:\ma
thbb{R}] = 2$. In particular, $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$ is a finite (hence algebra
ic) extension.

**Step 3: $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed (Fundamental Theorem of Algebra).
**

We recall the standard proof. Let $f \in \mathbb{C}[x]$ be a non-constant polyno
mial. We show $f$ has a root in $\mathbb{C}$.

Write $f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n a_k x^k$ with $a_n \neq 0$, $n \geq 1$. Consider $|f(
z)|^2 = f(z)\overline{f(z)}$ as a continuous function $\mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{R}
_{\geq 0}$. Since $|f(z)| \to \infty$ as $|z| \to \infty$ (the leading term domi
nates), $|f|$ attains a global minimum at some point $z_0 \in \mathbb{C}$.

Suppose for contradiction that $f(z_0) \neq 0$. Write $f(z_0 + w) = f(z_0) \cdot
 g(w)$ where $g(w) = 1 + c_k w^k + \text{(higher order terms)}$ for some $k \geq
 1$ and $c_k \neq 0$ (after factoring out $f(z_0)$ and the lowest-order non-cons
tant term). Choose $w$ with $|w|$ small and $c_k w^k$ pointing in the direction
of $-1$ (i.e., $c_k w^k = -|c_k||w|^k$). Then $|g(w)| \leq |1 - |c_k||w|^k| + O(
|w|^{k+1}) < 1$ for $|w|$ sufficiently small. This gives $|f(z_0+w)| < |f(z_0)|$
, contradicting the minimality of $|f(z_0)|$.

Hence $f(z_0) = 0$, and $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed.

**Step 4: $\mathbb{C}$ is the algebraic closure of $\mathbb{R}$.**

Since $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed and $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$ is algebr
aic (Step 2), $\mathbb{C}$ is an algebraic extension of $\mathbb{R}$ that is alg
ebraically closed. By definition, $\mathbb{C}$ is an algebraic closure of $\math
bb{R}$, i.e., $\overline{\mathbb{R}} = \mathbb{C}$.

**Conclusion.**

$\mathbb{R}$ is a field that is not algebraically closed (Step 1), and its algeb
raic closure $\overline{\mathbb{R}} = \mathbb{C}$ is a finite extension with $[\
overline{\mathbb{R}}:\mathbb{R}] = 2$ (Steps 2–4). This provides the desired exa
mple. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** By the Artin–Schreier theorem, the only possibilities for a field $K
$ with $[\overline{K}:K] < \infty$ are: $K$ is algebraically closed ($[\overline
{K}:K]=1$), or $K$ is real closed ($[\overline{K}:K]=2$, with $\overline{K}=K(i)
$). Thus $\mathbb{R}$ is in fact a prototypical example of the non-trivial case.
