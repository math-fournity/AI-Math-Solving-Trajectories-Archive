## Proof

**Yes, such a sequence exists.**

**Construction.** Enumerate all integers as $n_1, n_2, n_3, \ldots$ (any bijecti
on $\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Z}$), and let $p_1 < p_2 < \cdots$ be the primes in inc
reasing order. Set $P_k = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k$.

For each $k\ge 1$, by the Chinese Remainder Theorem there is a unique residue $R
_k \pmod{P_k}$ satisfying
$$R_k \equiv -n_j \pmod{p_j}, \qquad j=1,\ldots,k.$$
Since the congruences for $j\le k$ are unchanged when passing from $k$ to $k+1$,
 we have $R_{k+1}\equiv R_k \pmod{P_k}$; the residue classes are nested.

Choose $a_k$ inductively (with $a_0:=0$): pick any element of the class $R_k \pm
od{P_k}$ such that
$$a_k > a_{k-1},\qquad a_k > \max_{1\le j\le k}(p_j - n_j),\qquad a_k\ge 1.$$
This is possible because the arithmetic progression $R_k \pmod{P_k}$ is infinite
. Then $1\le a_1<a_2<\cdots$ is a strictly increasing sequence of positive integ
ers.

**Verification.** Fix any integer $n$; write $n=n_j$ for some $j$. For every $k\
ge j$,
$$a_k \equiv R_k \equiv -n_j \pmod{p_j},$$
so $p_j\mid(a_k+n_j)$. By construction $a_k>p_j-n_j$, i.e. $a_k+n_j>p_j\ge 2$, h
ence $a_k+n_j$ is a positive integer strictly larger than $p_j$ and divisible by
 $p_j$, so it is **composite**. Therefore among the indices $k\ge j$, none yield
s a prime. Only the finitely many indices $k<j$ can contribute primes, so the se
t $\{a_k+n:k\ge 1\}$ contains at most finitely many primes.

Since $n$ was arbitrary, the sequence $(a_k)$ has the required property. $\black
square$
