Looking at this problem, I need to find the supremum of $\frac{a_{n+1}(F)}{a_n(F
)}$ over all distributions $F$ on positive reals.

## Proof

**Setup.** Let $F$ be a CDF on $(0,\infty)$ with $a_n(F)>0$. Using the tail-inte
gral formula $E[Y]=\int_0^\infty P(Y>t)\,dt$ for non-negative $Y$, and $P(M_n\le
 t)=F(t)^n$:

$$a_n(F)=\int_0^\infty\bigl(1-F(t)^n\bigr)\,dt,\qquad a_{n+1}(F)=\int_0^\infty\b
igl(1-F(t)^{n+1}\bigr)\,dt.$$

**Key function.** Define $\phi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ by
$$\phi(u)=\frac{1-u^{n+1}}{1-u^n}\ \ (u<1),\qquad \phi(1)=\tfrac{n+1}{n}\ \text{
(continuous extension)}.$$
Note $\phi(0)=1$, $\phi(1)=\frac{n+1}{n}$.

**Claim:** $\phi$ is increasing on $[0,1]$.

*Proof.* For $u\in(0,1)$,
$$\phi'(u)=\frac{u^{n-1}\,h(u)}{(1-u^n)^2},\qquad h(u)=n-(n+1)u+u^{n+1}.$$
Now $h(1)=0$ and $h'(u)=(n+1)(u^n-1)\le 0$ on $[0,1]$, so $h$ is decreasing, hen
ce $h(u)\ge h(1)=0$. Thus $\phi'(u)\ge 0$. $\square$

**Upper bound.** Since $1-u^{n+1}=(1-u^n)\,\phi(u)$, we can write
$$\frac{a_{n+1}(F)}{a_n(F)}=\frac{\int_0^\infty\bigl(1-F(t)^n\bigr)\,\phi\bigl(F
(t)\bigr)\,dt}{\int_0^\infty\bigl(1-F(t)^n\bigr)\,dt}.$$
This is a weighted average of $\phi(F(t))$ with non-negative weights $w(t)=1-F(t
)^n\ge 0$. Since $\phi(u)\le\frac{n+1}{n}$ for all $u\in[0,1]$:
$$\boxed{\frac{a_{n+1}(F)}{a_n(F)}\le\frac{n+1}{n}.}$$

**The bound is strict (not attained).** Equality requires $\phi(F(t))=\frac{n+1}
{n}$ for a.e. $t$ with $w(t)>0$. But $\phi(u)=\frac{n+1}{n}$ only at $u=1$, and
$F(t)=1$ forces $w(t)=1-F(t)^n=0$—a contradiction. So $\{t:w(t)>0\}$ must have m
easure $0$, giving $a_n(F)=0$, which is excluded. Hence the ratio is **strictly*
* less than $\frac{n+1}{n}$ for every admissible $F$.

**The supremum is approached.** Consider a two-point distribution: mass $1-p$ at
 $\varepsilon>0$ and mass $p$ at $1$. Then
$$a_n = 1-(1-\varepsilon)(1-p)^n,\qquad a_{n+1}=1-(1-\varepsilon)(1-p)^{n+1},$$
$$\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=\frac{1-(1-\varepsilon)(1-p)^{n+1}}{1-(1-\varepsilon)(1-p)
^n}.$$
As $\varepsilon\to 0^+$ this tends to $\frac{1-(1-p)^{n+1}}{1-(1-p)^n}$, and as
$p\to 0^+$ (using $(1-p)^k\approx 1-kp$) this tends to $\frac{(n+1)p}{np}=\frac{
n+1}{n}$.

Concretely: for any $\delta>0$, choose $p$ small enough that $\frac{1-(1-p)^{n+1
}}{1-(1-p)^n}>\frac{n+1}{n}-\delta/2$ (possible since $g(q)=\frac{1-q^{n+1}}{1-q
^n}$ is continuous and increasing on $(0,1)$ with $g(q)\to\frac{n+1}{n}$ as $q\t
o 1^-$), then choose $\varepsilon$ small enough that the two-point ratio exceeds
 $\frac{n+1}{n}-\delta$.

**Conclusion.** The supremum of $\frac{a_{n+1}(F)}{a_n(F)}$ over all distributio
ns $F$ on positive reals is exactly $\frac{n+1}{n}$. This value is **not attaine
d** by any distribution (the ratio is always strictly below $\frac{n+1}{n}$), so
 strictly speaking there is no maximum—only a supremum. The answer to the questi
on "Is the maximum value $\frac{n+1}{n}$?" is: **yes, $\frac{n+1}{n}$ is the sup
remum**, but it is a least upper bound, not a value achieved by any specific $F$
.
