## Proof

**Interpretation.** Since $f \ge 0$ is continuous with $\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}
 f(x)\,dx = 1$, it is a probability density function. Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ be
i.i.d. random variables with density $f$. Then

$$I_n(r) = \mathbb{P}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n} X_k^2 \le r^2\right).$$

We claim $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} I_n(r) = 0$ for every fixed $r > 0$.

---

**Key observation.** Since $f$ is continuous and $\int f = 1$, the distribution
is not a point mass at $0$. Therefore $\mathbb{P}(|X| > 0) > 0$, which means the
re exists $\epsilon > 0$ such that

$$q := \mathbb{P}(|X| \ge \epsilon) > 0.$$

(Indeed, $\{|X| \ge 1/m\}_{m \ge 1}$ is an increasing sequence of events whose u
nion is $\{|X| > 0\}$, which has positive probability, so some member has positi
ve probability.)

---

**Bounding $I_n(r)$.** On the event $\left\{\sum_{k=1}^n X_k^2 \le r^2\right\}$,
 the number of indices $k$ with $|X_k| \ge \epsilon$ is at most

$$m := \left\lfloor \frac{r^2}{\epsilon^2} \right\rfloor,$$

since each such index contributes at least $\epsilon^2$ to the sum. Hence, letti
ng $N_n = \#\{k \in \{1,\ldots,n\} : |X_k| \ge \epsilon\}$:

$$I_n(r) \le \mathbb{P}(N_n \le m).$$

---

**The binomial tail vanishes.** The random variable $N_n$ is Binomial$(n, q)$ wi
th $q > 0$. For $j \le m$ (where $m$ is a fixed constant independent of $n$):

$$\mathbb{P}(N_n = j) = \binom{n}{j} q^j (1-q)^{n-j} \le n^j (1-q)^{n-j} \le n^m
 (1-q)^{n-m}.$$

Therefore

$$\mathbb{P}(N_n \le m) = \sum_{j=0}^{m} \binom{n}{j} q^j (1-q)^{n-j} \le (m+1)\
, n^m\, (1-q)^{n-m}.$$

Since $0 < 1 - q < 1$, the exponential decay of $(1-q)^n$ dominates the polynomi
al growth of $n^m$:

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} (m+1)\, n^m\, (1-q)^{n-m} = 0.$$

---

**Conclusion.** Combining the above,

$$0 \le I_n(r) \le \mathbb{P}(N_n \le m) \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 0.$$

By the squeeze theorem,

$$\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty} I_n(r) = 0.}$$

**Remark.** The argument uses only that $f$ is a genuine (non-degenerate) probab
ility density — specifically that $f$ is not a Dirac mass at the origin. Continu
ity of $f$ guarantees this. No moment assumptions are needed: the exponential de
cay of the binomial tail $(1-q)^n$ overwhelms any polynomial factor, regardless
of the tail behavior of $f$.
