Yes, $S^2 \setminus \{p\}$ is homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Proof.**

Let $S^2 = \{(x,y,z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 : x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\}$, and let $p = (0,
0,1)$ be the north pole. We construct an explicit homeomorphism via stereographi
c projection from $p$ onto the equatorial plane $\mathbb{R}^2 \cong \{(x,y,0)\}$
.

Define $\varphi : S^2 \setminus \{p\} \to \mathbb{R}^2$ by
$$\varphi(x,y,z) = \left(\frac{x}{1-z},\, \frac{y}{1-z}\right).$$
This is the intersection of the line through $p = (0,0,1)$ and the point $(x,y,z
) \in S^2 \setminus \{p\}$ with the plane $z = 0$. Since $(x,y,z) \neq p$, we ha
ve $1 - z \neq 0$ (because $z = 1$ on $S^2$ forces $(x,y,z) = p$), so $\varphi$
is well-defined and continuous.

Define the inverse $\psi : \mathbb{R}^2 \to S^2 \setminus \{p\}$ by
$$\psi(u,v) = \left(\frac{2u}{u^2+v^2+1},\, \frac{2v}{u^2+v^2+1},\, \frac{u^2+v^
2-1}{u^2+v^2+1}\right).$$
A direct computation verifies $\varphi \circ \psi = \mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}^2}$
and $\psi \circ \varphi = \mathrm{id}_{S^2 \setminus \{p\}}$:

For $(u,v) \in \mathbb{R}^2$, with $r^2 = u^2 + v^2$, the image $\psi(u,v)$ has
$1 - z = 1 - \frac{r^2-1}{r^2+1} = \frac{2}{r^2+1}$, so
$$\varphi(\psi(u,v)) = \left(\frac{2u/(r^2+1)}{2/(r^2+1)},\, \frac{2v/(r^2+1)}{2
/(r^2+1)}\right) = (u,v).$$

Conversely, for $(x,y,z) \in S^2 \setminus \{p\}$, set $(u,v) = \varphi(x,y,z) =
 \left(\frac{x}{1-z}, \frac{y}{1-z}\right)$. Then $u^2 + v^2 = \frac{x^2+y^2}{(1
-z)^2} = \frac{1-z^2}{(1-z)^2} = \frac{1+z}{1-z}$, so $u^2+v^2+1 = \frac{2}{1-z}
$, and
$$\psi(u,v) = \left(\frac{2 \cdot x/(1-z)}{2/(1-z)},\, \frac{2 \cdot y/(1-z)}{2/
(1-z)},\, \frac{(1+z)/(1-z) - 1}{(1+z)/(1-z)+1}\right) = (x,y,z).$$

Both $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are continuous (they are rational functions with nonva
nishing denominators on their respective domains), and they are mutual inverses.
 Hence $\varphi$ is a homeomorphism.

Therefore $S^2 \setminus \{p\} \cong \mathbb{R}^2$.
