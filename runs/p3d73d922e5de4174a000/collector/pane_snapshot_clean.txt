## Proof

We work on the Banach space $X = C[a,b]$ equipped with the supremum norm $\|x\|_
\infty = \max_{t\in[a,b]} |x(t)|$. The operator $A:X\to X$ is defined by
$$[A(x)](s) = \int_a^b K(s,t)\,x(t)\,dt, \qquad s\in[a,b],$$
where $K:[a,b]\times[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ is continuous.

**Claim.** $A$ is Fréchet differentiable at every point $x_0\in C[a,b]$, and its
 Fréchet derivative at $x_0$ is the bounded linear operator $A$ itself, i.e. $DA
(x_0) = A$ for all $x_0$.

---

### Step 1. $A$ is well defined and maps $C[a,b]$ into $C[a,b]$.

Since $K$ is continuous on the compact set $[a,b]\times[a,b]$, it is bounded: th
ere exists $M = \max_{(s,t)}|K(s,t)|$. For any $x\in C[a,b]$ and any $s\in[a,b]$
,
$$|[A(x)](s)| \le \int_a^b |K(s,t)|\,|x(t)|\,dt \le M\|x\|_\infty (b-a),$$
so $A(x)$ is bounded. Continuity of $A(x)$ in $s$ follows from the uniform conti
nuity of $K$ on the compact square: given $\varepsilon>0$, there is $\delta>0$ s
uch that $|s_1-s_2|<\delta \Rightarrow |K(s_1,t)-K(s_2,t)|<\varepsilon$ for all
$t$, hence
$$|[A(x)](s_1)-[A(x)](s_2)| \le \varepsilon\|x\|_\infty(b-a).$$
Thus $A(x)\in C[a,b]$.

### Step 2. $A$ is a bounded (continuous) linear operator.

**Linearity.** For $x,y\in C[a,b]$ and $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}$:
$$[A(\alpha x+\beta y)](s)=\int_a^b K(s,t)(\alpha x(t)+\beta y(t))\,dt = \alpha[
A(x)](s)+\beta[A(y)](s),$$
by linearity of the integral. So $A(\alpha x+\beta y)=\alpha A(x)+\beta A(y)$.

**Boundedness.** From Step 1,
$$\|A(x)\|_\infty = \max_s\left|\int_a^b K(s,t)x(t)\,dt\right| \le M(b-a)\,\|x\|
_\infty.$$
Hence $\|A\|\le M(b-a)<\infty$; $A$ is bounded and therefore continuous.

### Step 3. A bounded linear operator between Banach spaces is Fréchet different
iable everywhere, with derivative equal to itself.

Let $x_0\in C[a,b]$ be arbitrary. We claim that $A$ is Fréchet differentiable at
 $x_0$ with $DA(x_0)=A$.

By definition, $A$ is Fréchet differentiable at $x_0$ if there exists a bounded
linear operator $L:X\to X$ such that
$$\lim_{\|h\|_\infty\to 0}\frac{\|A(x_0+h)-A(x_0)-L(h)\|_\infty}{\|h\|_\infty}=0
.$$

Take $L = A$. Then by linearity of $A$:
$$A(x_0+h)-A(x_0)-A(h) = A(x_0)+A(h)-A(x_0)-A(h) = 0.$$

Therefore
$$\frac{\|A(x_0+h)-A(x_0)-A(h)\|_\infty}{\|h\|_\infty} = \frac{0}{\|h\|_\infty}
= 0 \longrightarrow 0$$
as $\|h\|_\infty\to 0$.

The limit is exactly $0$, so the Fréchet derivative exists at $x_0$ and equals $
A$. Since $x_0$ was arbitrary, $DA(x_0)=A$ for every $x_0\in C[a,b]$.

### Conclusion

The Fréchet derivative of $A$ exists at every point of $C[a,b]$. Because $A$ is
linear and bounded (continuous) on the Banach space $(C[a,b],\|\cdot\|_\infty)$,
 it is Fréchet differentiable everywhere, and its Fréchet derivative at any $x_0
\in C[a,b]$ is the operator $A$ itself:
$$DA(x_0) = A, \qquad \forall\, x_0\in C[a,b].$$
