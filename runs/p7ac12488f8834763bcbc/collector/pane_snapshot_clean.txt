## Proof

**Step 1: Compute σ(2^x).**

The divisors of 2^x are 1, 2, 4, …, 2^x, so:
$$\sigma(2^x) = 1 + 2 + 4 + \cdots + 2^x = 2^{x+1} - 1.$$

Let $n = 2^{x+1} - 1$. The identity becomes:
$$\varphi(n) = 2^x = \frac{n+1}{2}.$$

**Step 2: Characterize when φ(n) is a power of 2, with n odd.**

Since $n = 2^{x+1}-1$ is odd, and $\varphi(n) = 2^x$ is a power of 2, we use the
 classical fact:

> *If n is odd and φ(n) is a power of 2, then n is a product of distinct Fermat
primes.*

**Proof of fact:** Write $n = \prod p_i^{a_i}$. Then $\varphi(n) = \prod p_i^{a_
i - 1}(p_i - 1)$. For this to be a power of 2 with each $p_i$ odd: each $a_i = 1
$ (else $p_i$ divides φ(n)), and each $p_i - 1$ is a power of 2. A prime $p$ wit
h $p - 1 = 2^m$ must have $m$ a power of 2 (if $m$ has an odd factor $d > 1$, th
en $2^m + 1 = (2^{m/d})^d + 1$ is divisible by $2^{m/d}+1$). So $p_i = 2^{2^{k_i
}} + 1 = F_{k_i}$, a Fermat number, and it must be prime. ∎

**Step 3: Reduce to a product identity.**

So $n = \prod_{i \in S} F_i$ where $S$ is a set of indices of Fermat primes, and
:
$$\varphi(n) = \prod_{i \in S}(F_i - 1) = \prod_{i \in S} 2^{2^i} = 2^{\sum_{i \
in S} 2^i} = 2^x,$$
so $x = \sum_{i \in S} 2^i$. We also need $n = 2^{x+1} - 1$, i.e.:
$$\prod_{i \in S} F_i = 2^{x+1} - 1, \quad \text{equivalently} \quad \prod_{i \i
n S} F_i + 1 = 2^{x+1}.$$

**Step 4: The Fermat product identity.**

The Fermat numbers satisfy $F_0 \cdot F_1 \cdots F_{k-1} = F_k - 2 = 2^{2^k} - 1
$ (proved by induction: base $F_0 = 3 = 2^2 - 1$; step $\prod_{i=0}^{k} F_i = (2
^{2^k}-1)(2^{2^k}+1) = 2^{2^{k+1}}-1$).

So for the **initial segment** $S = \{0, 1, \ldots, k-1\}$:
$$\prod_{i=0}^{k-1} F_i = 2^{2^k} - 1, \qquad x = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} 2^i = 2^k - 1
, \qquad x + 1 = 2^k. \quad \checkmark$$

This works **provided** $F_0, F_1, \ldots, F_{k-1}$ are all prime.

**Step 5: No other subsets work.**

We show $\prod_{i \in S} F_i + 1$ is a power of 2 **only** when $S$ is an initia
l segment $\{0, \ldots, k-1\}$ (or empty).

- **Case $0 \notin S$, $S \neq \emptyset$:** For $i \geq 1$, $F_i = 2^{2^i}+1 \e
quiv 1 \pmod{4}$. So $P = \prod_{i \in S} F_i \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, giving $P + 1
\equiv 2 \pmod{4}$. The only power of 2 that is $\equiv 2 \pmod{4}$ is 2 itself,
 requiring $P = 1$, i.e., $S = \emptyset$. Contradiction.

- **Case $0 \in S$ but $S$ is not an initial segment:** Let $j \geq 1$ be the sm
allest index missing from $S$, and let $S$ contain some index $> j$. Set $Q = \p
rod_{i \in S,\, i > j} F_i > 1$. Then:
$$P = \left(\prod_{i=0}^{j-1} F_i\right) \cdot Q = (2^{2^j} - 1) \cdot Q.$$
For $i > j$: $2^i > 2^j$, so $2^{2^i} \equiv 0 \pmod{2^{2^j+1}}$, hence $F_i \eq
uiv 1 \pmod{2^{2^j+1}}$, so $Q \equiv 1 \pmod{2^{2^j+1}}$. Writing $Q = 1 + c \c
dot 2^{2^j}$, we get $c = (Q-1)/2^{2^j} \equiv 0 \pmod{2}$, so **c is even**.

Now:
$$P + 1 = (2^{2^j}-1)Q + 1 = 2^{2^j}(1 + c(2^{2^j}-1)).$$
Since $c$ is even and $2^{2^j}-1$ is odd, $c(2^{2^j}-1)$ is even, so $1 + c(2^{2
^j}-1)$ is **odd** and $> 1$. An odd number $> 1$ cannot be a power of 2. So $P
+ 1$ is not a power of 2. ✗

Therefore only initial segments (including the empty set) yield solutions.

The known Fermat primes are $F_0 = 3$, $F_1 = 5$, $F_2 = 17$, $F_3 = 257$, $F_4
