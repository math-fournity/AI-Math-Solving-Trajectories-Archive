Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether there exists a primitive-re
cursive function whose graph cannot be represented as the zero set of a pair of
polynomials with positive integer coefficients.

**Answer: Yes.** $f(n) = 2^n$ is such a function.

---

## Proof

**Step 1: Reformulation.**

The condition $f(n) = m \Leftrightarrow P(n,m) = Q(n,m)$ with $P, Q \in \mathbb{
Z}_+[x,y]$ means the graph of $f$ equals the set $\{(n,m) \in \mathbb{N}^2 : R(n
,m) = 0\}$ where $R := P - Q \in \mathbb{Z}[x,y]$.

Conversely, any $R \in \mathbb{Z}[x,y]$ can be decomposed as $R = P - Q$ with $P
, Q \in \mathbb{Z}_+[x,y]$: split the monomials of $R$ into those with positive
coefficients (forming $P$) and those with negative coefficients (forming $Q$ wit
h negated signs). If one side is empty, add the constant $1$ to both $P$ and $Q$
. Hence the representation is **equivalent** to:

> The graph of $f$ is the zero set of some $R \in \mathbb{Z}[x,y]$ on $\mathbb{N
}^2$.

**Step 2: $f(n) = 2^n$ is primitive recursive.**

Exponentiation is primitive recursive: $2^0 = 1$ and $2^{n+1} = 2 \cdot 2^n$, wi
th multiplication being primitive recursive.

**Step 3: Key Lemma — No nonzero integer polynomial vanishes on $\{(n, 2^n) : n
\in \mathbb{N}\}$.**

*Lemma.* If $R(x,y) \in \mathbb{Z}[x,y]$ satisfies $R(n, 2^n) = 0$ for all $n \i
n \mathbb{N}$, then $R \equiv 0$.

*Proof of Lemma.* Write $R(x,y) = \sum_{j=0}^{d} q_j(x)\, y^j$ where $q_j(x) \in
 \mathbb{Z}[x]$ and $d = \deg_y R$. Substituting:

$$0 = R(n, 2^n) = \sum_{j=0}^{d} q_j(n)\, 2^{jn}, \quad \forall\, n \in \mathbb{
N}.$$

Suppose for contradiction that $R \not\equiv 0$. Let $J = \max\{j : q_j \not\equ
iv 0\}$. Then:

$$q_J(n)\, 2^{Jn} = -\sum_{j=0}^{J-1} q_j(n)\, 2^{jn}.$$

For all sufficiently large $n$, $q_J(n) \neq 0$ (a nonzero polynomial has finite
ly many roots). Taking absolute values:

$$|q_J(n)|\, 2^{Jn} \leq \sum_{j=0}^{J-1} |q_j(n)|\, 2^{jn} \leq C\, n^D\, 2^{(J
-1)n}$$

for constants $C, D$ (where $D = \max_j \deg q_j$). Since $q_J$ is a nonzero pol
ynomial of degree $d_J$, we have $|q_J(n)| \geq c\, n^{d_J}$ for some $c > 0$ an
d all large $n$. Thus:

$$c\, n^{d_J}\, 2^{Jn} \leq C\, n^D\, 2^{(J-1)n} \implies c\, 2^n \leq C\, n^{D
- d_J}.$$

The left side grows exponentially; the right side grows at most polynomially. Th
is is a contradiction for large $n$. Therefore $R \equiv 0$. $\square$

**Step 4: The graph of $f(n)=2^n$ is not the zero set of any $R \in \mathbb{Z}[x
,y]$.**

Suppose the graph of $f$ equals $\{(n,m) \in \mathbb{N}^2 : R(n,m) = 0\}$ for so
me $R \in \mathbb{Z}[x,y]$. Then in particular $R(n, 2^n) = 0$ for all $n \in \m
athbb{N}$. By the Lemma, $R \equiv 0$. But then the zero set of $R$ is all of $\
mathbb{N}^2$, which is strictly larger than the graph of $f$ (e.g., $(0, 2) \in
\mathbb{N}^2$ but $2 \neq 2^0 = 1$). Contradiction.

**Step 5: Conclusion.**

By the equivalence in Step 1, there exist no $P, Q \in \mathbb{Z}_+[x,y]$ such t
hat $f(n) = m \Leftrightarrow P(n,m) = Q(n,m)$ for $f(n) = 2^n$. Since $f(n) = 2
^n$ is primitive recursive, the answer is **yes**: such a primitive-recursive fu
nction exists.
