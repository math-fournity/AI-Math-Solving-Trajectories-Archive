## Proof

**Claim:** For every $\lambda \in [2, +\infty)$, there exists a real number $x$
with irrationality measure $\mu(x) = \lambda$.

### Preliminaries

Recall the irrationality measure:
$$\mu(x) = \sup\left\{\mu > 0 : \left|x - \tfrac{p}{q}\right| < \tfrac{1}{q^\mu}
 \text{ for infinitely many } \tfrac{p}{q}\in\mathbb{Q}\right\}.$$

For an irrational $x=[a_0;a_1,a_2,\dots]$ with convergents $p_n/q_n$, the **best
 approximation property** of convergents ensures that the irrationality measure
is determined entirely by the convergents. Using the classical bound
$$\frac{1}{2\,q_n\,q_{n+1}} < \left|x-\frac{p_n}{q_n}\right| < \frac{1}{q_n\,q_{
n+1}},$$
we obtain the standard formula:
$$\mu(x) = 1 + \limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log q_{n+1}}{\log q_n}. \tag{$\star$}$
$

Also, by **Dirichlet's theorem**, every irrational $x$ satisfies $\mu(x)\ge 2$,
so the range $[2,+\infty)$ is the natural target.

---

### Case $\lambda = 2$

Take $x=\sqrt{2}=[1;2,2,2,\dots]$. The denominators satisfy $q_{n+1}=2q_n+q_{n-1
}$, so $q_n$ grows exponentially: $q_n\sim C(1+\sqrt{2})^n$. Hence $\frac{\log q
_{n+1}}{\log q_n}\to 1$, and by $(\star)$:
$$\mu(\sqrt{2})=1+1=2.$$

*(Alternatively, Roth's theorem gives $\mu(x)=2$ for every irrational algebraic
$x$.)*

---

### Case $\lambda > 2$ (finite)

We construct $x$ explicitly via its continued fraction. Define $x=[0;a_1,a_2,a_3
,\dots]$ with:
- $a_1 = 1$,
- $a_{n+1} = \left\lfloor q_n^{\,\lambda-2}\right\rfloor$ for $n\ge 1$,

where $q_n$ is the denominator of the $n$-th convergent (with $q_{-1}=0,\; q_0=1
,\; q_n=a_n q_{n-1}+q_{n-2}$).

**Validity:** Since $q_n\ge 1$ and $\lambda-2>0$, we have $a_{n+1}=\lfloor q_n^{
\lambda-2}\rfloor\ge 1$, so this is a well-defined infinite continued fraction (
yielding an irrational $x$).

**Growth analysis:** We have
$$q_{n+1}=a_{n+1}\,q_n+q_{n-1}=\lfloor q_n^{\lambda-2}\rfloor\,q_n+q_{n-1}.$$

Using $\lfloor q_n^{\lambda-2}\rfloor = q_n^{\lambda-2}+O(1)$ and $q_{n-1}\le q_
n$:
$$q_n^{\lambda-1}-q_n \;\le\; q_{n+1} \;\le\; q_n^{\lambda-1}+2q_n.$$

Since $\lambda>2$ implies $\lambda-1>1$, the term $q_n^{\lambda-1}$ dominates $q
_n$ as $q_n\to\infty$. Therefore:
$$q_{n+1}=q_n^{\lambda-1}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr).$$

**$q_n\to\infty$:** Since $a_{n+1}\ge 1$, we have $q_{n+1}\ge q_n+q_{n-1}>q_n$ f
or $n\ge 1$, so $q_n$ is strictly increasing and unbounded.

Taking logarithms:
$$\log q_{n+1}=(\lambda-1)\log q_n + \log(1+o(1))=(\lambda-1)\log q_n+o(1).$$

Dividing by $\log q_n\to\infty$:
$$\frac{\log q_{n+1}}{\log q_n}=(\lambda-1)+\frac{o(1)}{\log q_n}\;\longrightarr
ow\;\lambda-1.$$

Applying $(\star)$:
$$\mu(x)=1+\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log q_{n+1}}{\log q_n}=1+(\lambda-1)=\lambda.
 \qquad\checkmark$$

---

### Conclusion

For every $\lambda\in[2,+\infty)$:

| $\lambda$ | Construction | $\mu(x)$ |
|---|---|---|
| $\lambda=2$ | $x=\sqrt{2}$ (or any irrational algebraic number) | $2$ |
| $\lambda>2$ | $x=[0;a_1,a_2,\dots]$ with $a_{n+1}=\lfloor q_n^{\lambda-2}\rflo
or$ | $\lambda$ |

Hence **yes**, for every $\lambda\in[2,+\infty)$ there exists a real number $x$
with $\mu(x)=\lambda$.
