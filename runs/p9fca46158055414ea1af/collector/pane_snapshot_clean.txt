**Reformulation.** Adam's claim for a fixed $k$ is: for every natural $n$, if $k
 \mid (n-1)(n+1)$, then $k \mid (n-1)$ or $k \mid (n+1)$. We seek all $k \in [1,
50]$ for which this holds, then sum them.

**Key observation.** Since $\gcd(n-1,\,n+1) \mid 2$, any odd prime power dividin
g $(n-1)(n+1)$ must divide one factor entirely. The only prime that can be "shar
ed" between $n-1$ and $n+1$ is $2$ (when $n$ is odd, both are even). This drives
 the entire classification.

---

### Lemma 1 (Odd prime powers are in $S$)

If $k = p^a$ with $p$ an odd prime, then $k \in S$.

*Proof.* If $p^a \mid (n-1)(n+1)$, since $\gcd(n-1,n+1) \mid 2$ and $p$ is odd,
$p \nmid \gcd(n-1,n+1)$, so $p^a \mid (n-1)$ or $p^a \mid (n+1)$. $\square$

### Lemma 2 ($k = 2p^a$ with $p$ odd prime is in $S$)

*Proof.* Since $2 \mid k$, the hypothesis $k \mid (n-1)(n+1)$ forces $n$ odd. Wr
ite $n-1 = 2s$, $n+1 = 2(s+1)$. Then $k \mid 4s(s+1)$ gives $p^a \mid 2s(s+1)$,
hence $p^a \mid s(s+1)$ (as $p$ is odd). Since $\gcd(s, s+1)=1$, either $p^a \mi
d s$ or $p^a \mid (s+1)$. In the first case $2p^a \mid 2s = n-1$; in the second
$2p^a \mid 2(s+1) = n+1$. $\square$

### Lemma 3 ($k = 2^a$ is in $S$ iff $a \le 2$)

*Proof.* For $a \le 2$: $k \mid (n-1)(n+1)$ requires $n$ odd (when $a \ge 1$). W
ith $n-1=2s$, $n+1=2(s+1)$: $2 \mid 4s(s+1)$ always, and $4 \mid 4s(s+1)$ always
. For $k=2$: $2 \mid 2s = n-1$ always. For $k=4$: $4 \mid 2s$ or $4 \mid 2(s+1)$
, i.e., $2 \mid s$ or $2 \mid (s+1)$, which always holds since $s, s+1$ are cons
ecutive.

For $a \ge 3$: take $n = 2^{a-1}+1$ (odd). Then $n-1 = 2^{a-1}$ and $n+1 = 2(2^{
a-2}+1)$, so $v_2((n-1)(n+1)) = (a-1)+1 = a$, giving $2^a \mid (n-1)(n+1)$. But
$v_2(n-1) = a-1 < a$ and $v_2(n+1) = 1 < a$, so $2^a \nmid (n-1)$ and $2^a \nmid
 (n+1)$. $\square$

### Lemma 4 (Odd $k$ with $\ge 2$ distinct prime factors is not in $S$)

*Proof.* Let $k = p^a \cdot m$ with $p$ odd prime, $m > 1$ odd, $\gcd(p^a, m) =
1$. By CRT choose $n \equiv 1 \pmod{p^a}$, $n \equiv -1 \pmod{m}$. Then $p^a \mi
d (n-1)$ and $m \mid (n+1)$, so $k \mid (n-1)(n+1)$. But $m \mid (n-1)$ would re
quire $m \mid (-2)$, impossible since $m$ is odd and $m > 1$. So $k \nmid (n-1)$
. Similarly $p^a \mid (n+1)$ would require $p^a \mid 2$, impossible. So $k \nmid
 (n+1)$. $\square$

### Lemma 5 ($k = 2^a \cdot q$, $a \ge 2$, $q > 1$ odd, is not in $S$)

*Proof.* By CRT choose $n \equiv 1 \pmod{2^a}$, $n \equiv -1 \pmod{q}$. Then $2^
a \mid (n-1)$ and $q \mid (n+1)$, so $k \mid (n-1)(n+1)$. But $q \mid (n-1)$ req
uires $q \mid 2$, impossible ($q$ odd, $q > 1$). And $2^a \mid (n+1)$ requires $
2^a \mid 2$, impossible for $a \ge 2$. So $k \nmid (n-1)$ and $k \nmid (n+1)$. $
\square$

### Lemma 6 ($k = 2q$ with $q$ having $\ge 2$ distinct odd prime factors is not
in $S$)

*Proof.* Write $q = p^a \cdot m$ with $p$ odd prime, $m > 1$ odd, $\gcd(p^a, m)
= 1$. By CRT choose $n \equiv 1 \pmod{2}$, $n \equiv 1 \pmod{p^a}$, $n \equiv -1
 \pmod{m}$. Then $n$ is odd, $p^a \mid (n-1)$, $m \mid (n+1)$, so $k = 2q \mid (
n-1)(n+1)$. Now $k \mid (n-1)$ requires $q \mid (n-1)$, hence $m \mid (n-1)$, bu
t $n-1 \equiv -2 \pmod{m}$ and $m$ is odd $> 1$, so $m \nmid (n-1)$. And $k \mid
 (n+1)$ requires $q \mid (n+1)$, hence $p^a \mid (n+1)$, but $n+1 \equiv 2 \pmod
{p^a}$ and $p$ is odd, so $p^a \nmid (n+1)$. $\square$

### Classification

Combining Lemmas 1–6, writing $k = 2^a \cdot q$ with $q$ odd:

$$k \in S \iff k = 1,\quad \text{or}\quad k = 2^a\ (a \in \{1,2\}),\quad \text{o
r}\quad k = p^b\ (p \text{ odd prime}),\quad \text{or}\quad k = 2p^b\ (p \text{
odd prime}).$$

Equivalently: **$k \in S$ iff $k$ is $1$, a prime, $4$, an odd prime power, or t
wice an odd prime power.**
