# Proof

**Answer: Yes, the hyperspace is a compact metric space.**

Let me establish this in three steps.

---

## Step 1: Setup and notation

Let $(E, d)$ be the metric linear space, and $X \subseteq E$ a nonempty compact
convex subset. Since $E$ is metrizable (hence Hausdorff) and $X$ is compact, $X$
 is closed in $E$. Endowing $X$ with the restricted metric, $(X, d)$ is a compac
t metric space.

Denote:
- $\mathcal{H}(X) = \{A \subseteq X : A \neq \emptyset,\ A \text{ closed}\}$ — t
he hyperspace of all nonempty closed subsets of $X$, equipped with the Hausdorff
 metric $d_H$.
- $\mathcal{C}(X) = \{A \in \mathcal{H}(X) : A \text{ is convex}\}$ — the subfam
ily of nonempty closed convex subsets.

Since $X$ is compact, every closed subset of $X$ is compact, so $d_H$ is a well-
defined finite metric on $\mathcal{H}(X)$.

---

## Step 2: $\mathcal{H}(X)$ is compact

This is the classical theorem that the hyperspace of nonempty closed subsets of
a compact metric space is compact under the Hausdorff metric. For completeness,
I sketch the proof via total boundedness and completeness.

**Total boundedness.** Fix $\varepsilon > 0$. Since $X$ is compact, cover it by
finitely many open balls $B(x_1, \varepsilon), \dots, B(x_N, \varepsilon)$. For
each $A \in \mathcal{H}(X)$, define $S(A) = \{i \in \{1,\dots,N\} : A \cap B(x_i
, \varepsilon) \neq \emptyset\}$. There are at most $2^N - 1$ nonempty subsets $
S \subseteq \{1,\dots,N\}$. For each nonempty $S$, pick a representative $A_S =
\{x_i : i \in S\} \in \mathcal{H}(X)$. If $S(A) = S$, then every point of $A$ is
 within $\varepsilon$ of some $x_i$ with $i \in S$ (so $A \subseteq A_S + B(0,\v
arepsilon)$), and every $x_i$ with $i \in S$ is within $\varepsilon$ of some poi
nt of $A$ (so $A_S \subseteq A + B(0,\varepsilon)$). Thus $d_H(A, A_S) \leq \var
epsilon$. The finitely many $\varepsilon$-balls around the $A_S$ cover $\mathcal
{H}(X)$.

**Completeness.** Let $(A_n)$ be a $d_H$-Cauchy sequence in $\mathcal{H}(X)$. De
fine
$$A = \{x \in X : \exists\, x_{n_k} \in A_{n_k} \text{ with } x_{n_k} \to x\}$$
(the Kuratowski upper limit). Using the Cauchy property, one shows $A$ is nonemp
ty, closed, and $d_H(A_n, A) \to 0$. (Nonemptiness: pick any $a_n \in A_n$; by c
ompactness of $X$ a subsequence converges to some $a \in A$. Convergence $d_H(A_
n,A)\to 0$: the Cauchy property gives $\sup_{a \in A_n} d(a, A) \to 0$, and the
definition of $A$ gives $\sup_{a \in A} d(a, A_n) \to 0$.)

Hence $\mathcal{H}(X)$ is a compact metric space.

---

## Step 3: $\mathcal{C}(X)$ is closed in $\mathcal{H}(X)$

Let $(C_n)$ be a sequence in $\mathcal{C}(X)$ with $C_n \to C$ in $(\mathcal{H}(
X), d_H)$. We show $C$ is convex.

Take $x, y \in C$ and $t \in [0,1]$. Since $d_H(C_n, C) \to 0$, for each $n$ the
re exist $x_n, y_n \in C_n$ with $d(x_n, x) \to 0$ and $d(y_n, y) \to 0$. By con
vexity of $C_n$,
$$z_n := t\, x_n + (1-t)\, y_n \in C_n.$$
Since $E$ is a metric linear space (a topological vector space with compatible m
etric), scalar multiplication and addition are continuous, so
$$z_n \to z := t\, x + (1-t)\, y.$$
Now $z_n \in C_n$ and $C_n \to C$ in Hausdorff metric, so:
$$d(z, C) \leq d(z, z_n) + d(z_n, C) \leq d(z, z_n) + d_H(C_n, C) \xrightarrow{n
\to\infty} 0.$$
Thus $d(z, C) = 0$, and since $C$ is closed, $z \in C$.

Therefore $tx + (1-t)y \in C$ for all $x, y \in C$ and $t \in [0,1]$, proving $C
$ is convex. Hence $C \in \mathcal{C}(X)$, and $\mathcal{C}(X)$ is closed in $\m
athcal{H}(X)$.

---

## Conclusion

$\mathcal{C}(X)$ is a closed subset of the compact metric space $\mathcal{H}(X)$
. A closed subset of a compact space is compact. Therefore $\mathcal{C}(X)$ — th
e hyperspace of nonempty closed convex subsets of $X$ endowed with the Hausdorff
 metric topology — **is a compact metric space**.
