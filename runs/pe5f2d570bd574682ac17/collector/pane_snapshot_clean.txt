**Claim:** Yes. If $k$ is an infinite field and $f(X,Y)\in k[X,Y]$ satisfies $f(
x,y)=0$ for all $(x,y)\in k^2$, then $f=0$ in $k[X,Y]$. Equivalently, the natura
l evaluation map $\varepsilon:k[X,Y]\to k[X,Y]^*$ is an isomorphism.

**Proof.**

Write $f$ as a polynomial in $Y$ with coefficients in $k[X]$:
$$f(X,Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{n} g_i(X)\,Y^i,\qquad g_i(X)\in k[X].$$

**Step 1 — Fix $x\in k$ and use the $Y$-variable.** For every fixed $x\in k$, th
e specialization $f(x,Y)=\sum_{i=0}^n g_i(x)Y^i$ is a univariate polynomial in $
k[Y]$. By hypothesis $f(x,y)=0$ for all $y\in k$, so $f(x,Y)$ vanishes on every
element of $k$.

A nonzero univariate polynomial over a field has at most as many roots as its de
gree. Since $k$ is infinite, a polynomial vanishing on all of $k$ must be the ze
ro polynomial. Hence
$$f(x,Y)=0\in k[Y]\quad\text{for every }x\in k.$$
Comparing coefficients, this gives
$$g_i(x)=0\quad\text{for every }i\in\{0,\dots,n\}\text{ and every }x\in k.\tag{$
\ast$}$$

**Step 2 — Use the $X$-variable.** By $(\ast)$, each $g_i(X)\in k[X]$ vanishes o
n every $x\in k$. Applying the same univariate fact (a nonzero polynomial over a
n infinite field cannot vanish identically on the field), we conclude
$$g_i(X)=0\quad\text{for every }i.$$

Therefore $f(X,Y)=\sum_{i=0}^n 0\cdot Y^i = 0$ in $k[X,Y]$.

**Step 3 — Isomorphism.** The evaluation map $\varepsilon:k[X,Y]\to k[X,Y]^*$ se
nding a polynomial to its induced function $k^2\to k$ is a surjective ring homom
orphism by definition of $k[X,Y]^*$. We have just shown $\ker\varepsilon=\{0\}$.
 Hence $\varepsilon$ is an isomorphism, and
$$k[X,Y]^*\cong k[X,Y].$$

**Remark on the hypothesis.** The infinitude of $k$ is essential. For a finite f
ield $\mathbb{F}_q$, the polynomial $X^q-X$ vanishes on every element of $\mathb
b{F}_q$ but is nonzero, so the kernel of $\varepsilon$ is the ideal $\langle X^q
-X,\,Y^q-Y\rangle$ and $k[X,Y]^*\cong k[X,Y]/\langle X^q-X,Y^q-Y\rangle\not\cong
 k[X,Y]$. The argument above breaks down precisely because over a finite field a
 nonzero univariate polynomial *can* vanish on the whole field. ∎
