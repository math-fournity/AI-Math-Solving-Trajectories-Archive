Theorem. A group $G$ with no proper nontrivial subgroups is cyclic.

Proof.

Let $G$ be a group whose only subgroups are ${e}$ (the trivial subgroup) and $G$
itself. We show $G$ is cyclic.

Case 1: $G = {e}$. Then $G$ is the trivial group, generated by $e$, hence
cyclic.

Case 2: $G \neq {e}$. Pick any element $g \in G$ with $g \neq e$. Consider the
cyclic subgroup generated by $g$:

$$\langle g \rangle = { g^n : n \in \mathbb{Z} }.$$

This is a subgroup of $G$. Since $g \neq e$, the subgroup $\langle g \rangle$
contains $g$ (and $e$), so $\langle g \rangle \neq {e}$; it is nontrivial.

By hypothesis, $G$ has no proper nontrivial subgroups. Therefore $\langle g
\rangle$, being a nontrivial subgroup of $G$, cannot be proper. Hence

$$\langle g \rangle = G.$$

This means every element of $G$ is a power of $g$, so $G$ is cyclic (generated
by $g$). $\blacksquare$

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Remark. The argument is sharp: the hypothesis forces every non-identity element
to be a generator. In fact one can further classify such groups — they are
precisely the cyclic groups of prime order, together with the trivial group —
but the statement requested only asks to show cyclicity, which the single
argument above establishes.
