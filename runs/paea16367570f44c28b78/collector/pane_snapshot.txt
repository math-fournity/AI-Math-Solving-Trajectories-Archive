**Claim.** Yes. If $f \in L_1(-\pi,\pi)$ satisfies $\hat{f}(n)=0$ for every $n\i
n\mathbb{Z}$, then $f=0$ almost everywhere on $(-\pi,\pi)$.

**Proof.**

We use the fact that trigonometric polynomials are dense in $C([-\pi,\pi])$ (in
the uniform norm) among continuous $2\pi$-periodic functions, and combined with
a standard approximation argument this yields the uniqueness of Fourier coeffici
ents for $L_1$ functions.

**Step 1. Reduction to a continuous test.** Let $g \in C([-\pi,\pi])$ be $2\pi$-
periodic. By the Weierstrass approximation theorem for trigonometric polynomials
, for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists a trigonometric polynomial
$$T(\theta)=\sum_{|n|\le N} c_n e^{in\theta}$$
such that $\|g-T\|_\infty < \varepsilon$.

**Step 2. The integral against $f$ vanishes for every trigonometric polynomial.*
* Since $\hat f(n)=0$ for all $n$, by linearity and the relation $\hat f(-n)=\ov
erline{\hat f(n)}$ (which also vanishes),
$$\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(\theta)\,T(\theta)\,d\theta = \sum_{|n|\le N} c_n \int_{-\
pi}^{\pi} f(\theta) e^{in\theta}\,d\theta = \sum_{|n|\le N} c_n\,\hat f(-n)=0.$$

**Step 3. Pass to continuous periodic $g$.** Using $|f|\in L_1$ and $\|g-T\|_\in
fty<\varepsilon$,
$$\left|\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(\theta)\,g(\theta)\,d\theta\right| = \left|\int_{-\p
i}^{\pi} f(\theta)\bigl(g(\theta)-T(\theta)\bigr)\,d\theta\right| \le \varepsilo
n\,\|f\|_1.$$
Since $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary,
$$\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(\theta)\,g(\theta)\,d\theta = 0 \qquad \text{for every con
tinuous } 2\pi\text{-periodic } g. \tag{$\ast$}$$

**Step 4. Extend to bounded measurable test functions.** Let $h$ be any bounded
measurable $2\pi$-periodic function. By Lusin's theorem (applied on the compact
group $\mathbb{T}$ with its Haar measure), for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a
continuous $2\pi$-periodic $g$ with $\|g\|_\infty\le\|h\|_\infty$ such that $g=h
$ except on a set of measure $<\varepsilon$. Then
$$\left|\int f\,h - \int f\,g\right| \le \int |f|\,|h-g| \le 2\|h\|_\infty\!\int
_{\{h\ne g\}} |f|.$$
Since $|\{h\ne g\}|<\varepsilon$ and $|f|\in L_1$, the integral $\int_{\{h\ne g\
}}|f|\to 0$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$ (absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral)
. By $(\ast)$, $\int f\,g=0$, hence $\int f\,h = 0$ for every bounded measurable
 $2\pi$-periodic $h$.

**Step 5. Conclude $f=0$ a.e.** Take $h(\theta)=\operatorname{sgn} f(\theta)$, e
xtended $2\pi$-periodically; this is bounded and measurable. By Step 4,
$$0=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(\theta)\,\operatorname{sgn}f(\theta)\,d\theta = \int_{-\
pi}^{\pi}|f(\theta)|\,d\theta = \|f\|_1.$$
Therefore $\|f\|_1=0$, which forces $f=0$ almost everywhere on $(-\pi,\pi)$.

**Conclusion.** The vanishing of all Fourier coefficients of an $L_1(-\pi,\pi)$
function implies the function is zero almost everywhere. This is the *uniqueness
 theorem for Fourier coefficients* (Fejér's theorem / the Fejér–Lebesgue uniquen
ess theorem), and the argument above is its standard proof via density of trigon
ometric polynomials and absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral.
