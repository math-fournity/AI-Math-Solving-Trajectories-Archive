## Proof

We seek the maximum size of a set $X \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ on which the Euclidea
n metric induces the discrete metric, i.e., there is a constant $c>0$ with $\|x-
y\|=c$ for all distinct $x,y\in X$ (scaling makes $c=1$). This is the problem of
 the maximum number of **equidistant points** in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

**Claim.** The maximum is $n+1$.

### Upper bound: $m \le n+1$

Let $x_1,\dots,x_m\in\mathbb{R}^n$ satisfy $\|x_i-x_j\|^2=c^2$ for all $i\ne j$.
 Translate so the centroid is at the origin: $\sum_{j=1}^m x_j=0$.

For fixed $i$, summing $\|x_i-x_j\|^2=c^2$ over $j\ne i$:
$$\sum_{j\ne i}\bigl(\|x_i\|^2+\|x_j\|^2-2\,x_i\cdot x_j\bigr)=(m-1)c^2.$$
Since $\sum_{j\ne i}x_j=-x_i$, the cross term gives $-2\,x_i\cdot\!\sum_{j\ne i}
x_j=2\|x_i\|^2$. Writing $S=\sum_j\|x_j\|^2$, the left side is
$$(m-1)\|x_i\|^2+(S-\|x_i\|^2)+2\|x_i\|^2=m\|x_i\|^2+S.$$
So $m\|x_i\|^2+S=(m-1)c^2$ for every $i$; hence all $\|x_i\|^2$ are equal. Set $
\|x_i\|^2=r^2$. Then $S=mr^2$ and $2mr^2=(m-1)c^2$, giving $r^2=\frac{(m-1)c^2}{
2m}$.

For $i\ne j$, $\|x_i-x_j\|^2=2r^2-2\,x_i\cdot x_j=c^2$, so
$$x_i\cdot x_j=r^2-\frac{c^2}{2}=\frac{(m-1)c^2}{2m}-\frac{c^2}{2}=-\frac{c^2}{2
m}.$$

The Gram matrix $G=(x_i\cdot x_j)$ is therefore
$$G=\frac{c^2}{2m}\bigl(mI-J\bigr),$$
where $J$ is the all-ones matrix. Its eigenvalues are $\frac{c^2}{2m}\cdot m=\fr
ac{c^2}{2}$ with multiplicity $m-1$ (vectors orthogonal to $\mathbf{1}$), and $0
$ with multiplicity $1$ (the vector $\mathbf{1}$). Thus $G$ is positive semidefi
nite of rank $m-1$.

Since the $x_i$ lie in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the Gram matrix has rank $\le n$. Hence $
m-1\le n$, i.e.,
$$m\le n+1.$$

### Lower bound: $n+1$ is achieved

The vertices of a regular simplex in $\mathbb{R}^n$ provide $n+1$ equidistant po
ints. Explicitly, take the standard basis vectors $e_1,\dots,e_{n+1}\in\mathbb{R
}^{n+1}$; they satisfy $\|e_i-e_j\|=\sqrt{2}$ for $i\ne j$ and all lie in the hy
perplane $\sum x_i=1$, an affine $n$-dimensional subspace, which is isometric to
 $\mathbb{R}^n$. Scaling by $1/\sqrt{2}$ gives $n+1$ points in $\mathbb{R}^n$ wi
th pairwise distance $1$.

### Conclusion

The maximum number of elements is $\boxed{n+1}$.
