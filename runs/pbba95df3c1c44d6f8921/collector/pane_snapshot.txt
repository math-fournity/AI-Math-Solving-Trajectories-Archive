## Proof

**Claim:** The Cantor–Lebesgue function can be approximated by a sequence of smo
oth functions in the $L^1$ norm. The answer is **yes**.

---

### Setup

Let $f:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ denote the Cantor–Lebesgue function (the Devil's staircase
). Recall its key properties:

- $f$ is **continuous** on $[0,1]$ (it is the uniform limit of the piecewise-lin
ear approximants $f_n$ constructed on the $n$-th stage of the Cantor set constru
ction).
- $f$ is **monotone non-decreasing** with $f(0)=0$, $f(1)=1$.
- $f$ is **bounded**: $0\le f(x)\le 1$ for all $x\in[0,1]$.

Since $f$ is continuous on the compact interval $[0,1]$, it is bounded and measu
rable, hence $f\in L^1([0,1])$.

---

### Key argument

**Step 1 (Weierstrass approximation).** Since $f$ is continuous on the compact s
et $[0,1]$, the **Weierstrass approximation theorem** applies: for every $\varep
silon>0$ there exists a polynomial $p_\varepsilon$ such that

$$
\sup_{x\in[0,1]} |f(x)-p_\varepsilon(x)| < \varepsilon.
$$

**Step 2 (Uniform convergence implies $L^1$ convergence).** On the finite-measur
e space $([0,1],\lambda)$ with $\lambda([0,1])=1$, uniform convergence controls
the $L^1$ norm directly:

$$
\|f - p_\varepsilon\|_{L^1} = \int_0^1 |f(x)-p_\varepsilon(x)|\,dx \le \int_0^1
\varepsilon\,dx = \varepsilon.
$$

**Step 3 (Polynomials are smooth).** Every polynomial is $C^\infty$ (infinitely
differentiable) on all of $\mathbb{R}$, hence in particular smooth on $[0,1]$.

---

### Conclusion

Taking $\varepsilon = 1/n$ for $n=1,2,3,\dots$, the Weierstrass theorem produces
 polynomials $p_n$ with

$$
\|f - p_n\|_{L^1([0,1])} \le \frac{1}{n} \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 0.
$$

Thus $\{p_n\}$ is a sequence of smooth functions converging to the Cantor–Lebesg
ue function $f$ in the $L^1$ norm.

**Remark.** This is consistent with the general principle that $C^\infty$ (indee
d, polynomials) are dense in $L^1([0,1])$. The singular nature of the Cantor–Leb
esgue function — it has derivative $0$ almost everywhere yet is non-constant, an
d its distributional derivative is a singular measure supported on the Cantor se
t — does **not** obstruct $L^1$ approximation. The obstruction would arise only
for approximation in a norm sensitive to derivatives (e.g., $W^{1,1}$ or $C^1$),
 where the singular part of the derivative cannot be matched by smooth functions
. In the bare $L^1$ norm, only the function values matter, and continuity alone
suffices to guarantee smooth approximability.
