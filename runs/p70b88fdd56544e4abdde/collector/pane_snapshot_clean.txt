## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. If $f \in L^1(0,1)$ and $\int_0^t f(s)\,ds = 0$ for every $t \in
 [0,1]$, then $f = 0$ almost everywhere.

**Proof.**

Define the cumulative function
$$F(t) := \int_0^t f(s)\,ds, \qquad t \in [0,1].$$

Since $f \in L^1(0,1)$, the function $F$ is **absolutely continuous** on $[0,1]$
 (this is the fundamental theorem of Lebesgue integration: the indefinite integr
al of an $L^1$ function is absolutely continuous).

A standard theorem of Lebesgue integration theory states that an absolutely cont
inuous function is differentiable almost everywhere, and its derivative satisfie
s
$$F'(t) = f(t) \quad \text{for almost every } t \in [0,1].$$

By hypothesis, $F(t) = 0$ for every $t \in [0,1]$, i.e. $F$ is identically zero.
 Therefore its derivative exists and equals zero everywhere it exists:
$$F'(t) = 0 \quad \text{a.e.}$$

Combining the two displayed relations,
$$f(t) = F'(t) = 0 \quad \text{for almost every } t \in [0,1].$$

Hence $f = 0$ almost everywhere. $\blacksquare$

**Remark on the necessity of the $L^1$ hypothesis.** The argument relies crucial
ly on $f$ being integrable, which guarantees that $F$ is absolutely continuous a
nd that the differentiation identity $F' = f$ a.e. holds. Without integrability
(e.g. for a conditionally integrable or merely measurable $f$), the indefinite i
ntegral need not be absolutely continuous, and the conclusion can fail. Here the
 $L^1$ assumption is exactly what is needed.
