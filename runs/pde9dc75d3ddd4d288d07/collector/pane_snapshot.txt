## Proof

### Definitions

An **integral domain** is a commutative ring with identity $1 \neq 0$ and no zer
o divisors. A **UFD** is an integral domain $R$ in which every non-zero non-unit
 element can be written as a product of irreducible elements, and this factoriza

### Part 1: Definitional Incompatibility

By the standard definition, an integral domain is **commutative**. The phrase "i
ntegral domain with non-commutative multiplication" is therefore self-contradict
ory: a ring with non-commutative multiplication is, by definition, not an integr
al domain. Since a UFD is defined as an integral domain satisfying the unique fa
ctorization property, and an integral domain is necessarily commutative, a non-c
ommutative ring cannot be a UFD.

### Part 2: Structural Necessity of Commutativity

Even if we set aside the definitional issue and consider a **non-commutative dom
ain** (a ring with identity, no zero divisors, but non-commutative multiplicatio
n), the unique factorization property cannot hold in the UFD sense.

The uniqueness condition in a UFD is stated **"up to order of the factors,"** me
aning that permuting the irreducible factors does not change the product. Formal
ly, the condition requires:

> If $p_1 p_2 \cdots p_n = q_1 q_2 \cdots q_m$ where all $p_i, q_j$ are irreduci
ble, then $n = m$ and there exists a permutation $\sigma \in S_n$ such that $p_i
$ is associate to $q_{\sigma(i)}$ for all $i$.

This condition **implicitly assumes** that reordering the factors preserves the
product — i.e., that $p_{\sigma(1)} \cdots p_{\sigma(n)} = p_1 \cdots p_n$ for a
ny permutation $\sigma$. But this is precisely the statement that the irreducibl
e factors commute, which is a consequence of the commutativity of the ring.

In a non-commutative ring, reordering factors generally **changes the product**:
 $p_1 p_2 \neq p_2 p_1$ for non-commuting irreducibles $p_1, p_2$. Therefore, th
e notion of uniqueness "up to order" is not meaningful in a non-commutative sett
ing, because different orderings produce **different elements entirely**, not me
rely different representations of the same element.

### Part 3: Failure of the Irreducible-Implies-Prime Property

A cornerstone property of UFDs is that **every irreducible element is prime**. T
he standard proof uses commutativity in an essential way:

Suppose $p$ is irreducible and $p \mid ab$, meaning $ab = pc$ for some $c \in R$
. Factor $a, b, c$ into irreducibles:
$$a = a_1 \cdots a_r, \quad b = b_1 \cdots b_s, \quad c = c_1 \cdots c_t.$$
Then:
$$a_1 \cdots a_r \cdot b_1 \cdots b_s = p \cdot c_1 \cdots c_t.$$

In a **commutative** ring, the left-hand side can be treated as a single unorder
ed product $a_1 \cdots a_r b_1 \cdots b_s$, and by uniqueness of factorization u
p to reordering, $p$ must be associate to one of the factors $a_i$ or $b_j$. Hen
ce $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$, proving $p$ is prime.

In a **non-commutative** ring, the product $a_1 \cdots a_r \cdot b_1 \cdots b_s$
 **cannot be reordered** — we cannot interleave or rearrange the $a_i$'s and $b_
j$'s without changing the product. Therefore, we cannot conclude that $p$ matche
s one of the $a_i$ or $b_j$ factors. The proof breaks down completely.

Without the property that irreducibles are prime, the fundamental structure theo
rem of UFDs fails. The entire edifice of unique factorization — GCDs, coprimalit

1. **By definition**: A UFD is an integral domain, and an integral domain is com
