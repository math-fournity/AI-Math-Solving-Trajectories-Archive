Looking at the problem, I need to determine whether every compact, closed, non-c
onvex, hole-free body $K \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ can be decomposed into finitely m
any compact convex bodies $C_i \subset K$ with $K = \bigcup C_i$.

**Answer: No.** Such a finite decomposition does not always exist.

**Counterexample.** Define
$$K = \bigl\{(x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : -1 \le x \le 1,\;\sqrt{1-x^2} \le y \le 2\
bigr\}.$$

This is the closed region bounded below by the upper semicircle $y = \sqrt{1-x^2
}$ (radius 1) and above by the line $y = 2$, with vertical sides at $x = \pm 1$.

**Verification of properties:**

- *Compact and closed:* $K$ is bounded and defined by non-strict inequalities in
volving continuous functions, so it is closed and bounded, hence compact.
- *Non-convex:* The points $(-1, 0)$ and $(1, 0)$ both lie in $K$, but their mid
point $(0, 0)$ does not, since $\sqrt{1 - 0^2} = 1 > 0$. So $K$ is not convex.
- *No holes:* $K$ is homeomorphic to a closed disk (it is a topological ball in
$\mathbb{R}^2$). Its complement $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus K$ is connected, and $K$
 is simply connected. Thus $K$ has no holes.

**Key Lemma.** *Let $C$ be any convex set with $C \subseteq K$. Then $C$ contain
s at most one point of the open arc*
$$A = \bigl\{(x,\, \sqrt{1-x^2}) : -1 < x < 1\bigr\}.$$

*Proof of Lemma.* Suppose for contradiction that $C$ contains two distinct point
s $p = (x_1, \sqrt{1-x_1^2})$ and $q = (x_2, \sqrt{1-x_2^2})$ on $A$, with $x_1
< x_2$. Since $C$ is convex, the entire segment $[p, q]$ lies in $C \subseteq K$
.

The function $f(x) = \sqrt{1 - x^2}$ is **strictly concave** on $(-1, 1)$, since
$$f''(x) = -\frac{1}{(1-x^2)^{3/2}} < 0.$$

By strict concavity, for any $t \in (0,1)$:
$$f\bigl(tx_1 + (1-t)x_2\bigr) > t\,f(x_1) + (1-t)\,f(x_2).$$

The left side is the $y$-coordinate of the arc at $x = tx_1 + (1-t)x_2$, while t
he right side is the $y$-coordinate of the segment $[p,q]$ at the same $x$. Thus
 every interior point of $[p,q]$ lies **strictly below** the arc, i.e., has $y <
 \sqrt{1 - x^2}$, so it does not belong to $K$. This contradicts $[p,q] \subsete
q K$.

Therefore $C$ contains at most one point of $A$. $\square$

**Proof that no finite decomposition exists.** Suppose $K = \bigcup_{i=1}^{N} C_
i$ where each $C_i$ is compact, convex, and $C_i \subseteq K$. Since $A \subset
K$ (every point of the arc is on the boundary of $K$, hence in $K$), we have
$$A \subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^{N} C_i.$$

By the Lemma, each $C_i$ contains at most one point of $A$, so the union $\bigcu
p_{i=1}^{N} C_i$ contains at most $N$ points of $A$. But $A$ is an arc parametri
zed by $x \in (-1, 1)$, which contains **uncountably many** points. For any fini
te $N$, we have $|A| > N$, so $A \not\subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^{N} C_i$, contradic
ting $K = \bigcup_{i=1}^{N} C_i$.

Therefore, no finite collection of compact convex bodies $C_i \subseteq K$ can s
atisfy $K = \bigcup_{i=1}^{N} C_i$.

**Conclusion.** The answer is **no**: there does not always exist such a finite
collection. The essential obstruction is that any strictly concave portion of th
e boundary of $K$ (such as a circular arc) cannot be covered by finitely many co
nvex subsets of $K$, because a convex set contained in $K$ can touch a strictly
concave boundary arc in at most one point—any two distinct points on the arc hav
e their connecting segment lying strictly outside $K$ by strict concavity.
