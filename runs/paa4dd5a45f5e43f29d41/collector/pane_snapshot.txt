## Solution

**Setup.** Place the $n$ vertices at positions $0, 1, \dots, n-1$ around the cir
cle (angular spacing $2\pi/n$). A closed semicircle is an arc of length $\pi$. F
our chosen vertices lie in some semicircle iff they fit in an arc of length $\le
q \pi$.

**Gap characterization.** For a 4-subset, list its points in circular order $p_1
, p_2, p_3, p_4$ and let the four gaps be $g_1, g_2, g_3, g_4$ (positive integer
s summing to $n$). The minimal covering arc has length $(n - g_{\max})\cdot 2\pi
/n$, so the subset fits in a semicircle iff
$$n - g_{\max} \leq \frac{n}{2}, \quad\text{i.e.,}\quad g_{\max} \geq \frac{n}{2
}.$$
Since the $g_i$ are positive integers summing to $n$, the largest gap is always
**unique** (two gaps $\geq n/2$ would force the other two to be $\leq 0$). Hence
 each qualifying subset has a unique largest gap.

**Counting via the counterclockwise-most point.** Fix a vertex $i$ as the counte
rclockwise-most point of the subset, and require all other 3 points to lie clock
wise from $i$ within angular distance $\leq \pi$. The clockwise distances $j_1 <
 j_2 < j_3$ from $i$ must satisfy $j_3 \leq n/2$:
- $n$ even: $j_3 \leq n/2$, so $j \in \{1,\dots,n/2\}$, giving $\binom{n/2}{3}$
choices.
- $n$ odd: $j_3 \leq (n-1)/2$, so $j \in \{1,\dots,(n-1)/2\}$, giving $\binom{(n
-1)/2}{3}$ choices.

This counts exactly the subsets whose largest gap is the one "behind" $i$ (the g
ap $g_4$). By uniqueness of the largest gap, **each qualifying subset is counted
 exactly once**. Therefore

$$\#\{\text{qualifying 4-subsets}\} = n\binom{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{3}, \qquad P(n
) = \frac{n\binom{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{3}}{\binom{n}{4}}.$$

**Verification of the formula.** Asymptotically $P(n) \to \frac{n\cdot(n/2)^3/6}
{n^4/24} = \frac{1}{2}$, matching the continuous result $4/2^{3}=1/2$. Small cas
es: $P(4)=4\binom{2}{3}/\binom{4}{4}=0$ ✓ (a semicircle of a square hits $\leq 3
$ vertices); $P(6)=6\binom{3}{3}/\binom{6}{4}=6/15=2/5$ ✓ (only the 6 sets of 4
consecutive vertices qualify).

**Compute $P(10)$.** $\lfloor 10/2\rfloor = 5$:
$$P(10) = \frac{10\binom{5}{3}}{\binom{10}{4}} = \frac{10\cdot 10}{210} = \frac{
100}{210} = \frac{10}{21}.$$

**Compute $P(11)$.** $\lfloor 11/2\rfloor = 5$:
$$P(11) = \frac{11\binom{5}{3}}{\binom{11}{4}} = \frac{11\cdot 10}{330} = \frac{
110}{330} = \frac{1}{3}.$$

**Final answer.**
$$P(10) + P(11) = \frac{10}{21} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{10}{21} + \frac{7}{21} = \
boxed{\dfrac{17}{21}}.$$
