## Counterexample

**Conjecture (false):** The union of uncountably many sets of Lebesgue measure z
ero always has Lebesgue measure zero.

**Counterexample.** Consider the family of sets
$$\mathcal{F} = \big\{\{x\} : x \in [0,1]\big\}.$$

**Step 1: The family is uncountable.** The map $x \mapsto \{x\}$ is a bijection
from $[0,1]$ to $\mathcal{F}$. Since $[0,1]$ is uncountable, $\mathcal{F}$ is an
 uncountable family of sets.

**Step 2: Each member has Lebesgue measure zero.** For every $x \in [0,1]$, the
singleton $\{x\}$ is a Borel set, and its Lebesgue measure is
$$m(\{x\}) = \inf\big\{m(I) : I \supseteq \{x\},\ I \text{ an interval}\big\}.$$
For any $\varepsilon > 0$, the interval $(x - \varepsilon/2,\, x + \varepsilon/2
)$ contains $\{x\}$ and has measure $\varepsilon$. Hence $m(\{x\}) \leq \varepsi
lon$ for all $\varepsilon > 0$, so $m(\{x\}) = 0$.

**Step 3: The union has positive (in fact, full) measure.** We have
$$\bigcup_{x \in [0,1]} \{x\} = [0,1].$$
The Lebesgue measure of $[0,1]$ is $m([0,1]) = 1 \neq 0$.

**Conclusion.** We have exhibited an uncountable family $\mathcal{F}$ of Lebesgu
e-null sets whose union is $[0,1]$, a set of measure $1$. Therefore the union of
 uncountably many measure-zero sets need not have measure zero, and the conjectu
re is false. $\blacksquare$
