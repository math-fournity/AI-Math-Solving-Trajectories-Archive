## Solution

**Setting up coordinates.** Label the cells of the $3\times 3\times 3$ cube by $
(i,j,k)$ with $i,j,k \in \{0,1,2\}$. The $1\times 1\times 3$ lines come in three
 families:

- **$x$-lines:** fix $(j,k)$, vary $i$ — there are $9$.
- **$y$-lines:** fix $(i,k)$, vary $j$ — there are $9$.
- **$z$-lines:** fix $(i,j)$, vary $k$ — there are $9$.

Total: $27$ lines, each required to contain exactly one white cube.

**Consistency check.** Each white cube lies on exactly $3$ lines (one per direct
ion). With $9$ white cubes, the total line–white-cube incidences are $9 \times 3
 = 27$, matching $27$ lines $\times\, 1$. ✓

**Reduction to Latin squares.** The $z$-line condition says: for each $(i,j)$, t
here is exactly one $k$ with a white cube at $(i,j,k)$. Define
$$c(i,j) = \text{the unique } k \text{ such that cell } (i,j,k) \text{ is white.
}$$
Then the set of white cubes is exactly $\{(i,j,c(i,j)) : i,j \in \{0,1,2}\}$, gi
ving $9$ white cubes.

Now apply the other two conditions:

- **$x$-line condition** (fix $j,k$, vary $i$): exactly one $i$ satisfies $c(i,j
)=k$. This means each **row** $j$ of $c$ is a permutation of $\{0,1,2\}$.
- **$y$-line condition** (fix $i,k$, vary $j$): exactly one $j$ satisfies $c(i,j
)=k$. This means each **column** $i$ of $c$ is a permutation of $\{0,1,2\}$.

Therefore $c$ is a **Latin square of order $3$**, and conversely every Latin squ
are of order $3$ yields a valid arrangement. The correspondence is bijective.

**Counting Latin squares of order $3$.** Choose the first row: any permutation o
f $\{0,1,2\}$, giving $3! = 6$ choices. Given the first row, the second row must
 be a **derangement** of $\{0,1,2\}$ relative to the first row (differing in eve
ry position). For $n=3$, there are exactly $2$ derangements. Once the first two
rows are fixed, the third row is uniquely determined (each column must contain a
ll three symbols).

$$\text{Total} = 6 \times 2 = 12.$$

**Verification via reduced squares.** The number of reduced Latin squares of ord
er $3$ (first row and first column both $0,1,2$) is $R(3)=1$. The general formul
a gives $L(3) = 3!\cdot 2!\cdot R(3) = 6\cdot 2\cdot 1 = 12$. ✓

**Rotations are distinct.** The problem states rotations are considered distinct
. Since we count all Latin squares without quotienting by any symmetry, rotation
s are already counted separately — no division is needed.

**Answer:** $\boxed{12}$
