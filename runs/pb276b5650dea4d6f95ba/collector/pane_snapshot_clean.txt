## Proof

**Claim:** $S_n = C^n([0,1], \mathbb{R})$ fails to be an integral domain for **e
very** non-negative integer $n$.

An integral domain is a commutative ring with unity and no zero divisors. $S_n$
is a commutative ring with unity (the constant function $1$) under pointwise add
ition and multiplication. Thus $S_n$ fails to be an integral domain precisely wh
en there exist nonzero $f, g \in S_n$ with $f \cdot g \equiv 0$.

We construct such a pair for each $n \geq 0$.

---

### Case $n = 0$ (continuous functions)

Define
$$f(x) = \max(0,\; x - \tfrac{1}{2}), \qquad g(x) = \max(0,\; \tfrac{1}{2} - x).
$$

Both $f$ and $g$ are continuous on $[0,1]$. We have $f(x) = 0$ for $x \in [0, \t
frac{1}{2}]$ and $g(x) = 0$ for $x \in [\tfrac{1}{2}, 1]$, while $f$ is positive
 on $(\tfrac{1}{2}, 1]$ and $g$ is positive on $[0, \tfrac{1}{2})$. Hence $f \cd
ot g \equiv 0$, yet neither $f$ nor $g$ is the zero function. So $S_0$ is not an
 integral domain.

---

### Case $n \geq 1$

Define
$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & x \leq \tfrac{1}{2}, \\[4pt] \bigl(x - \tfrac{1}{2}\b
igr)^{n+1} & x > \tfrac{1}{2}, \end{cases} \qquad g(x) = \begin{cases} \bigl(\tf
rac{1}{2} - x\bigr)^{n+1} & x < \tfrac{1}{2}, \\[4pt] 0 & x \geq \tfrac{1}{2}. \
end{cases}$$

**$f$ and $g$ are nonzero:** $f(1) = (\tfrac{1}{2})^{n+1} > 0$ and $g(0) = (\tfr
ac{1}{2})^{n+1} > 0$.

**$f \cdot g \equiv 0$:** For $x \leq \tfrac{1}{2}$, $f(x) = 0$; for $x \geq \tf
rac{1}{2}$, $g(x) = 0$. At $x = \tfrac{1}{2}$ both vanish. So the product is ide
ntically zero.

**$f \in C^n([0,1])$:** On $(\tfrac{1}{2}, 1]$, $f$ is a polynomial, hence smoot
h. On $[0, \tfrac{1}{2})$, $f \equiv 0$, hence smooth. It remains to check smoot
hness at $x_0 = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

For $k \leq n$, the $k$-th derivative of $f$ from the right at $x_0$ is:
$$f^{(k)}(x) = \frac{(n+1)!}{(n+1-k)!}\bigl(x - \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)^{n+1-k}, \qqu
ad x > \tfrac{1}{2}.$$

Since $n + 1 - k \geq 1$, we have $\lim_{x \to \tfrac{1}{2}^+} f^{(k)}(x) = 0$.

From the left, $f^{(k)} \equiv 0$ on $[0, \tfrac{1}{2})$, so $\lim_{x \to \tfrac
{1}{2}^-} f^{(k)}(x) = 0$.

The left and right limits of $f^{(k)}$ agree (both equal $0$) for every $0 \leq
k \leq n$, and the one-sided derivatives at $x_0$ also both equal $0$. Therefore
 $f^{(k)}$ is continuous at $x_0$ for all $0 \leq k \leq n$, i.e., $f \in C^n([0
,1])$.

By the symmetric argument (reflecting $x \mapsto 1 - x$), $g \in C^n([0,1])$ as
well.

---

### Conclusion

For every $n \geq 0$, we have exhibited nonzero functions $f, g \in C^n([0,1], \
mathbb{R})$ with $f \cdot g = 0$. Therefore $S_n$ has zero divisors and fails to
 be an integral domain for **all** non-negative integers $n$.
