## Proof

We seek all matrices $P$ near the identity $I$ satisfying $P^2 = I$, using the e
xponential parametrization.

**Step 1: Parametrize near $I$ via the logarithm.**

The matrix exponential $\exp\colon \mathfrak{gl}_n(\mathbb{C}) \to GL_n(\mathbb{
C})$ is a local diffeomorphism near $0$, with inverse given by the matrix logari
thm. Hence every $P$ sufficiently close to $I$ can be written uniquely as
$$P = e^{A}, \qquad A \text{ small (near } 0\text{)}.$$

**Step 2: Translate $P^2 = I$ into a condition on $A$.**

Since $A$ commutes with itself,
$$P^2 = e^{A}e^{A} = e^{2A}.$$
So $P^2 = I$ becomes
$$e^{2A} = I.$$
Setting $B = 2A$ (still near $0$), we must solve
$$e^{B} = I, \qquad B \text{ near } 0.$$

**Step 3: Show $e^{B} = I$ with $B$ near $0$ forces $B = 0$.**

Let $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ be the eigenvalues of $B$ (over $\mathbb{C}$).
 The eigenvalues of $e^{B}$ are $e^{\lambda_i}$, so $e^{B} = I$ gives
$$e^{\lambda_i} = 1 \implies \lambda_i \in 2\pi i\,\mathbb{Z}.$$

Since $B$ is near $0$, every $|\lambda_i|$ is small. The only element of $2\pi i
\,\mathbb{Z}$ with small modulus is $0$, so
$$\lambda_1 = \cdots = \lambda_n = 0.$$

Thus $B$ is nilpotent. Write $B^m = 0$ with $B^{m-1} \neq 0$ (if $B \neq 0$). Th
en
$$e^{B} - I = B + \frac{B^2}{2!} + \cdots + \frac{B^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}.$$
The lowest-order nonzero term is $B$ itself. Multiplying, $e^B - I = B\bigl(I +
\tfrac{B}{2} + \cdots\bigr)$, where the factor in parentheses is invertible (it
is $I$ plus a nilpotent). Hence $e^B - I \neq 0$ whenever $B \neq 0$. This contr
adicts $e^B = I$.

Therefore $B = 0$, i.e. $A = 0$.

**Step 4: Conclude.**

$A = 0$ gives $P = e^{0} = I$. Conversely $P = I$ clearly satisfies $P^2 = I$.

Hence the **only** solution of $P^2 = I$ near the identity is

$$\boxed{P = I.}$$
