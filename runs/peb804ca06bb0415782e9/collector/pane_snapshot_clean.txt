## Proof

**Step 1: Identify the field.** Let $\alpha = \sqrt{2}+\sqrt{5}$. Then $\alpha^2
 = 7+2\sqrt{10}$, so $\sqrt{10} = \frac{\alpha^2-7}{2} \in \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$.
Furthermore, $\alpha\sqrt{10} = (\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{5})\sqrt{10} = 2\sqrt{5}+5\sqrt{
2}$, so $5\sqrt{2}+2\sqrt{5} \in \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$. Solving the system $\{\sqr
t{2}+\sqrt{5} = \alpha,\; 5\sqrt{2}+2\sqrt{5} = \alpha\sqrt{10}\}$ gives $\sqrt{
2} = \frac{\alpha(\sqrt{10}-2)}{3} \in \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ and $\sqrt{5} = \alph
a - \sqrt{2} \in \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$. Hence
$$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{5}) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{5}),$$
a biquadratic (degree-4) field with $\mathbb{Q}$-basis $\{1, \sqrt{2}, \sqrt{5},
 \sqrt{10}\}$.

**Step 2: Discriminants of the quadratic subfields.** The three quadratic subfie
lds are $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{10})$,
 with squarefree radicands $2, 5, 10$.

- $2 \equiv 2 \pmod{4}$, so $d\bigl(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})\bigr) = 4\cdot 2 = 8$.
- $5 \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, so $d\bigl(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})\bigr) = 5$, with integr
al basis $\{1, \omega_5\}$ where $\omega_5 = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}$.
- $10 \equiv 2 \pmod{4}$, so $d\bigl(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{10})\bigr) = 4\cdot 10 = 4
0$.

The discriminant of the biquadratic field is the product of the three quadratic
discriminants:
$$d_K = 8 \cdot 5 \cdot 40 = 1600.$$

**Step 3: Apply the coprime-discriminant compositum theorem.** Set $K_1 = \mathb
b{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ and $K_2 = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$, so $K = K_1 K_2$. Their discri
minants are $d_{K_1} = 8 = 2^3$ and $d_{K_2} = 5$, which are **coprime**: $\gcd(
8,5)=1$.

By the standard theorem on compositums with coprime discriminant:

> If $K_1, K_2$ are number fields with $\gcd(d_{K_1}, d_{K_2}) = 1$, then $\math
cal{O}_{K_1 K_2} = \mathcal{O}_{K_1}\,\mathcal{O}_{K_2}$, and an integral basis
is the "tensor product" of the integral bases of $K_1$ and $K_2$.

The integral basis of $K_1 = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ is $\{1, \sqrt{2}\}$ (since $
2 \equiv 2 \pmod 4$), and the integral basis of $K_2 = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$ is
$\{1, \omega_5\} = \{1, \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\}$ (since $5 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$).

Taking all products gives the integral basis of $K$:
$$\{1\cdot 1,\; \sqrt{2}\cdot 1,\; 1\cdot \omega_5,\; \sqrt{2}\cdot \omega_5\} =
 \left\{1,\; \sqrt{2},\; \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2},\; \frac{\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{10}}{2}\ri
ght\}.$$

**Step 4: Verify the discriminant.** The compositum theorem also gives:
$$d_K = d_{K_1}^{_2:\mathbb{Q}]} \cdot d_{K_2}^{_1:\mathbb{Q}]} = 8^2 \cdot
5^2 = 64 \cdot 25 = 1600.$$

This matches the value $8 \cdot 5 \cdot 40 = 1600$ computed independently from t
he three quadratic subfields, confirming consistency. (The match is expected sin
ce $d_{K_3} = d_{K_1}\cdot d_{K_2} = 40$ when the discriminants are coprime.)

**Step 5: Verify each basis element is integral.**
- $1$: trivially integral.
- $\sqrt{2}$: root of $x^2 - 2$, monic over $\mathbb{Z}$. ✓
- $\omega_5 = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}$: root of $x^2 - x - 1$, monic over $\mathbb{
Z}$. ✓
- $\frac{\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{10}}{2} = \frac{\sqrt{2}(1+\sqrt{5})}{2} = \sqrt{2}\cdot
\omega_5$: this is a product of two algebraic integers, hence integral. ✓ (Expli
citly, it is a root of $x^4 - 6x^2 + 4 = 0$: setting $\beta = \frac{\sqrt{2}+\sq
rt{10}}{2}$, we get $\beta^2 = \frac{2+10+2\sqrt{20}}{4} = \frac{12+4\sqrt{5}}{4
} = 3+\sqrt{5}$, so $\beta^2 - 3 = \sqrt{5}$, and $(\beta^2-3)^2 = 5$, giving $\
beta^4 - 6\beta^2 + 9 = 5$, i.e., $\beta^4 - 6\beta^2 + 4 = 0$.)

**Conclusion.** An integral basis for the ring of integers $D$ of $\mathbb{Q}(\s
qrt{2}+\sqrt{5})$ is:

$$\boxed{\left\{1,\; \sqrt{2},\; \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2},\; \frac{\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{10

with field discriminant $d_K = 1600$.
