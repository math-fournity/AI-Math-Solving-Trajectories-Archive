**Theorem.** There exists an additive function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ that
is unbounded on every interval (equivalently, unbounded in every neighborhood of
 every point).

**Proof.** We work in ZFC.

**Step 1. If an additive function is bounded on some interval, then it is linear
.**

Let $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be additive and suppose $|f(x)|\le M$ for all $x
\in(a,b)$, $a<b$.

*Bounding near 0.* For any $h$ with $|h|<b-a$, choose $x\in(a,b)\cap(a-h,b-h)$ (
non-empty since $|h|<b-a$). Then $x,x+h\in(a,b)$, so
$$f(h)=f(x+h)-f(x),\qquad |f(h)|\le 2M.$$
Thus $f$ is bounded by $2M$ on $(-(b-a),\,b-a)$.

*Continuity at 0.* Given $\varepsilon>0$, pick $n\in\mathbb{N}$ with $2M/n<\vare
psilon$. For $|x|<(b-a)/n$, we have $|nx|<b-a$, so $|f(nx)|\le 2M$. Since $f(nx)
=nf(x)$, we get $|f(x)|\le 2M/n<\varepsilon$.

*Continuity everywhere and linearity.* Continuity at $0$ gives continuity everyw
here: $f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)=f(h)\to 0$. A continuous additive function satisfies $f(q
)=qf(1)$ for all $q\in\mathbb{Q}$ (by additivity), and for any $x\in\mathbb{R}$,
 taking $q_n\to x$ with $q_n\in\mathbb{Q}$ gives $f(x)=\lim f(q_n)=f(1)\cdot x$.
 So $f(x)=cx$ with $c=f(1)$.

**Step 2. A non-linear additive function is unbounded on every interval.**

By contrapositive of Step 1: if $f$ is additive and not of the form $f(x)=cx$, t
hen $f$ is not bounded on any interval $(a,b)$, $a<b$. Being "unbounded on every
 interval" is equivalent to being "unbounded in every neighborhood of every poin
t" (every neighborhood contains an interval, and every interval contains a neigh
borhood of each of its points).

**Step 3. A non-linear additive function exists (using the Axiom of Choice).**

View $\mathbb{R}$ as a vector space over $\mathbb{Q}$. By Zorn's Lemma, $\mathbb
{R}$ admits a Hamel basis $B$ over $\mathbb{Q}$: every real number is a unique f
inite $\mathbb{Q}$-linear combination of elements of $B$. Since $\dim_{\mathbb{Q
}}\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable while $\{1\}$ spans only $\mathbb{Q}$, the basis $B
$ (which we may take to contain $1$) also contains an irrational number $\alpha$
.

Define $f$ on $B$ by:
$$f(1)=1,\qquad f(\alpha)=0,\qquad f(b)=0\ \text{for all other }b\in B,$$
and extend $\mathbb{Q}$-linearly: if $x=q_1 b_1+\cdots+q_n b_n$ with $q_i\in\mat
hbb{Q}$, $b_i\in B$, set $f(x)=q_1 f(b_1)+\cdots+q_n f(b_n)$.

Then $f$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-linear, hence additive. But $f$ is not $\mathbb{R}$-lin
ear: if $f(x)=cx$ for all $x$, then $c=f(1)=1$, giving $f(\alpha)=\alpha\neq 0$,
 contradicting $f(\alpha)=0$ (since $\alpha$ is irrational, $\alpha\neq 0$).

**Conclusion.**

The function $f$ from Step 3 is additive and non-linear. By Step 2, it is unboun
ded on every interval, hence unbounded in every neighborhood of every point. The
refore, **there exists an additive function on $\mathbb{R}$ that is unbounded ev
erywhere**. $\blacksquare$
