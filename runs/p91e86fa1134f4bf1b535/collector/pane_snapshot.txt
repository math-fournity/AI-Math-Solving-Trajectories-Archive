## Solution

We evaluate
$$I = \int_0^{2\pi} e^{-i\theta}\, e^{e^{i\theta}}\, d\theta.$$

**Step 1: Expand the inner exponential as a Taylor series.**

For all $z \in \mathbb{C}$, $e^z = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{z^n}{n!}$, and the
series converges absolutely and uniformly on compact sets. Setting $z = e^{i\the
ta}$ (which lies on the unit circle, a compact set), we may interchange summatio
n and integration:

$$e^{e^{i\theta}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(e^{i\theta})^n}{n!} = \sum_{n=0}^
{\infty} \frac{e^{in\theta}}{n!}.$$

**Step 2: Multiply by $e^{-i\theta}$ and integrate term-by-term.**

$$I = \int_0^{2\pi} e^{-i\theta} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{in\theta}}{n!}\, d
\theta = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n!} \int_0^{2\pi} e^{i(n-1)\theta}\, d\the
ta.$$

The interchange is justified by uniform convergence of the Taylor series on $[0,
 2\pi]$.

**Step 3: Evaluate the Fourier-type integrals.**

For integer $k = n - 1$:
$$\int_0^{2\pi} e^{ik\theta}\, d\theta = \begin{cases} 2\pi & k = 0, \\[4pt] \df
rac{e^{ik\theta}}{ik}\Big|_0^{2\pi} = \dfrac{e^{2\pi i k} - 1}{ik} = 0 & k \neq
0, \end{cases}$$
since $e^{2\pi i k} = 1$ for every integer $k$.

Thus only the term with $n - 1 = 0$, i.e. $n = 1$, survives.

$$I = \frac{1}{1!}\cdot 2\pi = 2\pi.$$
