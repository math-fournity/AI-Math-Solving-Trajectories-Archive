Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether for every positive integer
$n$, there exists a polynomial $P$ with integer coefficients such that $\{P(1),
P(2), \dots, P(n)\}$ consists of distinct primes.

**Answer: Yes.**

## Proof

**Key idea:** Choose $n$ distinct primes all congruent to the same residue $r$ m
odulo $n!$, then use Newton interpolation to build a polynomial with integer coe
fficients.

### Step 1: Choosing the primes

Let $n$ be a fixed positive integer. Choose $r = 1$ (so $\gcd(r, n!) = 1$). By *
*Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions**, there are infinitel
y many primes $p \equiv 1 \pmod{n!}$. Select $n$ distinct such primes and denote
 them $p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n$.

### Step 2: Newton interpolation setup

Define the **Newton forward difference** operator $\Delta$ on the sequence $(p_1
, p_2, \dots, p_n)$:
$$\Delta^0 p_1 = p_1, \qquad \Delta^k p_1 = \sum_{j=0}^{k} (-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j
} p_{j+1} \quad (k \geq 1).$$

The **Newton interpolation polynomial** through the points $(1, p_1), (2, p_2),
\dots, (n, p_n)$ is:
$$P(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \frac{\Delta^k p_1}{k!} \prod_{j=1}^{k}(x - j).$$

By construction, $P(i) = p_i$ for $i = 1, 2, \dots, n$.

### Step 3: The coefficients are integers

Each factor $\prod_{j=1}^{k}(x - j)$ is a monic polynomial of degree $k$ with **
integer coefficients**. Therefore, $P \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ if and only if each coe
fficient $a_k = \frac{\Delta^k p_1}{k!}$ is an integer, i.e., $k! \mid \Delta^k
p_1$ for all $k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1$.

**Claim:** $n! \mid \Delta^k p_1$ for every $k \geq 1$.

*Proof of claim.* Since each $p_i \equiv 1 \pmod{n!}$, we have:
$$\Delta^k p_1 = \sum_{j=0}^{k} (-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j} p_{j+1} \equiv \sum_{j=0}
^{k} (-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j} \cdot 1 \pmod{n!}.$$

The sum $\sum_{j=0}^{k} (-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}$ equals $(1 - 1)^k = 0$ for all $
k \geq 1$ (by the binomial theorem). Therefore:
$$\Delta^k p_1 \equiv 0 \pmod{n!} \quad \text{for all } k \geq 1.$$

Since $k! \mid n!$ for $k \leq n$, we conclude $k! \mid \Delta^k p_1$ for all $k
 = 1, \dots, n-1$. For $k = 0$, $a_0 = p_1$ is a prime (hence an integer). $\squ
are$

### Step 4: Verification

- **Integer coefficients:** Each $a_k \in \mathbb{Z}$ by Step 3, and each $\prod
_{j=1}^k(x-j) \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$, so $P \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$.
- **Prime values:** $P(i) = p_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$, and each $p_i$ is prime
by construction.
- **Distinctness:** The primes $p_1, \dots, p_n$ were chosen to be distinct, so
$P(1), \dots, P(n)$ are distinct.

### Concrete illustration

For $n = 3$: primes $\equiv 1 \pmod{6}$ include $7, 13, 19$. Newton interpolatio
n gives $P(x) = 6x + 1$, and indeed $P(1) = 7$, $P(2) = 13$, $P(3) = 19$ — all d
istinct primes, with integer coefficients.

### Conclusion

For every positive integer $n$, by Dirichlet's theorem we can select $n$ distinc
t primes all congruent to $1 \pmod{n!}$, and the Newton interpolation polynomial
 through the points $(i, p_i)$ has integer coefficients (since $n!$ divides ever
y forward difference $\Delta^k p_1$ for $k \geq 1$). This polynomial $P \in \mat
hbb{Z}[x]$ satisfies the requirement that $\{P(1), \dots, P(n)\}$ consists of di
stinct primes.
