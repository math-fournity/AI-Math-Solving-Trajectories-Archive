## Solution

We prove $T(n) = \Theta(2^n)$, assuming standard non-negative finite base cases
for $n \le n_0$ and $\log$ to any fixed base $\ge 2$.

### Lower Bound: $T(n) = \Omega(2^n)$

For $n \ge 1$, $\log n \ge 0$. With non-negative base cases, $T(m) \ge 0$ for al
l $m$ by induction (every term on the RHS is non-negative). Hence:

$$T(n) = 2T(n-1) + \underbrace{(\log n)\,T(\lfloor\log n\rfloor)}_{\ge\, 0} + \u
nderbrace{1}_{>\,0} \;\ge\; 2\,T(n-1).$$

Unrolling from $n_0$:

$$T(n) \ge 2^{\,n-n_0}\,T(n_0) = \Omega(2^n). \qquad\checkmark$$

### Upper Bound — Step 1: $T(n) = O(2^n \cdot n)$

**Claim.** There exist constants $C, N$ such that $T(n) \le C\cdot 2^n\cdot n$ f
or all $n \ge N$.

**Proof** by strong induction. Choose $N$ large and $C$ large enough to cover al
l $n \in [N, M]$ for some finite $M > N$ (base cases). Assume the bound holds fo
r all $m$ with $N \le m < n$.

**Case (a):** $\lfloor \log n \rfloor \ge N$. Then by the inductive hypothesis a
pplied to both recursive calls:

$$T(n) \le 2\cdot C\cdot 2^{n-1}(n-1) + (\log n)\cdot C\cdot 2^{\lfloor\log n\rf
loor}\cdot\lfloor\log n\rfloor + 1.$$

$$= C\cdot 2^n\cdot n \;-\; C\cdot 2^n \;+\; (\log n)\cdot C\cdot 2^{\lfloor\log
 n\rfloor}\cdot\lfloor\log n\rfloor \;+\; 1.$$

To close the induction we need the last three terms $\le 0$, i.e.:

$$(\log n)\cdot 2^{\lfloor\log n\rfloor}\cdot\lfloor\log n\rfloor + \tfrac{1}{C}
 \;\le\; 2^n.$$

Since $2^{\lfloor\log n\rfloor} \le n$ and $\lfloor\log n\rfloor \le \log n$, th
e left side is at most $(\log n)^2\cdot n + 1/C$. For all sufficiently large $n$
, $(\log n)^2\cdot n \le 2^{n-1}$ and $1/C \le 2^{n-1}$, so the inequality holds
. $\checkmark$

**Case (b):** $\lfloor \log n \rfloor < N$. Then $T(\lfloor\log n\rfloor)$ is a
fixed constant (base case), so $(\log n)\,T(\lfloor\log n\rfloor) = O(\log n)$.
Thus:

$$T(n) \le C\cdot 2^n\cdot(n-1) + O(\log n) + 1 \le C\cdot 2^n\cdot n$$

for all sufficiently large $n$. $\checkmark$

This establishes $T(n) = O(2^n \cdot n)$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

### Upper Bound — Step 2: $T(n) = O(2^n)$

Unroll the recurrence from $N$ to $n$:

$$T(n) = 2^{n-N}\,T(N) \;+\; \sum_{k=N+1}^{n} 2^{n-k}\bigl[(\log k)\,T(\lfloor\l
og k\rfloor) + 1\bigr].$$

Split into three terms:

$$T(n) = \underbrace{2^{n-N}\,T(N)}_{(I)} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{k=N+1}^{n} 2^{
n-k}\,(\log k)\,T(\lfloor\log k\rfloor)}_{(II)} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{k=N+1}^{
n} 2^{n-k}}_{(III)}.$$

- **(I)** $= O(2^n)$. $\checkmark$
- **(III)** $= 2^n\sum_{k=N+1}^{n} 2^{-k} = O(2^n)$. $\checkmark$
- **(II)** $= 2^n\displaystyle\sum_{k=N+1}^{n} \frac{(\log k)\,T(\lfloor\log k\r
floor)}{2^k}$.

  From Step 1, $T(\lfloor\log k\rfloor) \le C\cdot 2^{\lfloor\log k\rfloor}\cdot
\lfloor\log k\rfloor \le C\cdot k\cdot\log k$. Therefore:

  $$\frac{(\log k)\,T(\lfloor\log k\rfloor)}{2^k} \;\le\; \frac{C\cdot k\cdot(\l
og k)^2}{2^k}.$$

  The series $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k\,(\log k)^2}{2^k}$ **conv
erges** (the ratio test gives limit $1/2 < 1$; equivalently, $2^k$ dominates any
 polynomial in $k$). Hence:

  $$\sum_{k=N+1}^{n} \frac{(\log k)\,T(\lfloor\log k\rfloor)}{2^k} \;\le\; C\sum
_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k\,(\log k)^2}{2^k} \;=\; O(1).$$

  So $(II) = 2^n \cdot O(1) = O(2^n)$. $\checkmark$

Combining: $T(n) = O(2^n) + O(2^n) + O(2^n) = O(2^n)$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

### Conclusion

$$\boxed{T(n) = \Theta(2^n)}$$

The term $2\,T(n-1)$ dominates: it alone produces $\Theta(2^n)$ growth. The "sel
f-referential" perturbation $(\log n)\,T(\lfloor\log n\rfloor)$ feeds back only
through the *doubly-logarithmically* smaller argument $\lfloor\log n\rfloor$, ma
king its total contribution (after weighting by $2^{-k}$ in the unrolled sum) a
convergent series — a bounded constant multiple of $2^n$, not a larger order.
