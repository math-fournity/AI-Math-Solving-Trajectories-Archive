## Proof

**Answer-leak check:** The problem statement contains no solutions, answer value
s, or proof sketches. Proceeding.

**Martingale condition.** For $g(S_n)$ to be a martingale, we need
$$\mathbb{E}[g(S_{n+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_n]=g(S_n)\quad\text{for all }n.$$
Since $S_{n+1}=S_n+X_{n+1}$ and $X_{n+1}$ is independent of $\mathcal{F}_n$ with
 $\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}=1)=\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}=-1)=\tfrac12$,
$$\mathbb{E}[g(S_{n+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_n]=\tfrac12\,g(S_n+1)+\tfrac12\,g(S_n-1).
$$
Thus the condition is
$$g(x+1)-2g(x)+g(x-1)=0 \qquad (\star)$$
for every value $x$ that $S_n$ can attain.

**The equation must hold for all integers.** At time $n$, $S_n$ takes values in
$\{-n,-n+2,\dots,n-2,n\}$, and $(\star)$ is needed at each $x\in\{-n,-n+2,\dots,
n-2\}$. As $n$ ranges over all non-negative integers, the union of these sets is
 all of $\mathbb{Z}$. Hence $(\star)$ must hold for every integer $x$.

**Polynomial identity.** Let $g$ be a polynomial and define the second-differenc
e operator
$$\Delta^2 g(x)=g(x+1)-2g(x)+g(x-1).$$
This is again a polynomial. For a monomial $x^k$:
- $k=0$: $\Delta^2(1)=1-2+1=0$.
- $k=1$: $\Delta^2(x)=(x+1)-2x+(x-1)=0$.
- $k\ge 2$: the leading term of $(x+1)^k-2x^k+(x-1)^k$ is $k(k-1)x^{k-2}\neq 0$,
 so $\Delta^2(x^k)$ is a nonzero polynomial of degree $k-2$.

By linearity, if $g(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}a_k x^k$ with $d\ge 2$ and $a_d\neq 0$, the
n $\Delta^2 g$ has leading term $d(d-1)a_d\,x^{d-2}\neq 0$, so $\Delta^2 g$ is a
 nonzero polynomial of degree $d-2$.

A nonzero polynomial has only finitely many roots, so it cannot vanish on all of
 $\mathbb{Z}$. Therefore $(\star)$ holding for all integers forces $\Delta^2 g\e
quiv 0$, which requires $d\le 1$.

**Conclusion.** The only polynomials satisfying $(\star)$ are the affine ones:
$$\boxed{g(x)=a+bx,\qquad a,b\in\mathbb{R}.}$$

**Verification.** For $g(x)=a+bx$,
$$\mathbb{E}[g(S_{n+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_n]=a+b\,\mathbb{E}[S_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{F}
_n]=a+b\,S_n=g(S_n),$$
since $S_n$ itself is a martingale ($\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1}]=0$). So every affine po
lynomial works, and no polynomial of degree $\ge 2$ does.
