No, it does not follow. Here is a counterexample.

**Counterexample.** Take the finite measure space $(X, F, \mu) = ([0,1], \mathca
l{B}, \lambda)$ where $\lambda$ is Lebesgue measure (so $\mu(X) = 1 < \infty$).
Define the "typewriter" sequence as follows. For each $n \geq 1$, write uniquely
$$n = 2^k + j, \qquad k \geq 0,\quad 0 \leq j < 2^k,$$
and set
$$f_n = \mathbf{1}_{\left[\,j/2^k,\; (j+1)/2^k\,\right]}.$$

Each $f_n$ is a nonnegative measurable function (a characteristic function of a
measurable interval), so $f_n \geq 0$ everywhere.

**The integrals tend to 0.** For $n = 2^k + j$ with $0 \leq j < 2^k$,
$$\int_{[0,1]} f_n\,d\lambda = \lambda\!\left(\left[\tfrac{j}{2^k}, \tfrac{j+1}{
2^k}\right]\right) = \frac{1}{2^k}.$$
As $n \to \infty$ we have $k \to \infty$, hence $\frac{1}{2^k} \to 0$. Therefore
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_X f_n\,d\mu = 0.$$

**But $f_n$ does not converge to $0$ at any point.** Fix any $x \in [0,1]$. For
each level $k \geq 0$, among the $2^k$ intervals $\left[\frac{j}{2^k}, \frac{j+1
}{2^k}\right]$, $j = 0, \dots, 2^k - 1$, at least one of them contains $x$ (the
intervals tile $[0,1)$, and $x=1$ is contained in the last interval of each leve
l). Hence for each $k$ there exists some $j_k$ with $f_{2^k + j_k}(x) = 1$. Thus
 $f_n(x) = 1$ for infinitely many $n$. On the other hand, for each $k$ there are
 also values of $j$ for which $x \notin \left[\frac{j}{2^k}, \frac{j+1}{2^k}\rig
ht]$, giving $f_{2^k+j}(x) = 0$. So $f_n(x) = 0$ for infinitely many $n$ as well
.

Consequently, for every $x \in [0,1]$, the sequence $(f_n(x))$ has a subsequence
 equal to $1$ and a subsequence equal to $0$, so it does **not** converge to $0$
. In particular $f_n \not\to 0$ almost everywhere (in fact $f_n$ fails to conver
ge to $0$ at *every* point).

**Conclusion.** The condition $\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_X f_n\,d\mu = 0$ with $f_n
\geq 0$ a.e. on a finite measure space does **not** imply $f_n \to 0$ almost eve
rywhere. The implication fails in general; convergence of integrals to zero only
 guarantees convergence to $0$ in measure (by Markov's inequality), not almost-e
verywhere convergence.
