## Solution

**Statement P is true; Statement Q is false.**

---

### Proof that P holds

**Existence.** The function $f(z) = \sin z$ is analytic on $D(0,1)$ (it is entir
e, in fact) and satisfies $f(z_n) = \sin(z_n)$ for every $n$. Hence at least one
 analytic function with the required property exists.

**Uniqueness.** Suppose $f$ and $g$ are both analytic on $D(0,1)$ with $f(z_n) =
 \sin(z_n)$ and $g(z_n) = \sin(z_n)$ for all $n$. Then $h := f - g$ is analytic
on $D(0,1)$ and $h(z_n) = 0$ for every $n$.

Since the $z_n$ are distinct and $z_n \to 0$, the set $\{z_n : n \geq 1\}$ has a
n accumulation point, namely $0$, which lies in the interior of $D(0,1)$. By the
 **identity theorem** for analytic functions, an analytic function that vanishes
 on a set with an accumulation point inside its domain must vanish identically.
Therefore $h \equiv 0$, i.e. $f = g$.

Combining existence and uniqueness, **P is true**: $f(z) = \sin z$ is the unique
 analytic function on $D(0,1)$ interpolating the prescribed values.

---

### Proof that Q fails

Suppose, for contradiction, that an analytic function $f$ on $D(0,1)$ satisfies
$f(z_n) = 0$ for even $n$ and $f(z_n) = 1$ for odd $n$.

Consider the subsequence $(z_{2k})$. Since $z_n \to 0$, we have $z_{2k} \to 0$ a
s $k \to \infty$. The points $z_{2k}$ are distinct (being a subsequence of disti
nct points), and $f(z_{2k}) = 0$ for every $k$. Thus $f$ vanishes on the set $\{
z_{2k} : k \geq 1\}$, which has accumulation point $0 \in D(0,1)$. By the identi
ty theorem, $f \equiv 0$ on $D(0,1)$.

But then $f(z_n) = 0$ for **all** $n$, contradicting the requirement $f(z_n) = 1
$ for odd $n$.

Therefore no such analytic function exists, and **Q is false**.

---

### Summary

| Statement | Verdict | Reason |
|-----------|---------|--------|
| P | **True** | $\sin z$ works; uniqueness follows from the identity theorem si
nce $\{z_n\}$ accumulates at $0 \in D(0,1)$. |
| Q | **False** | The even-indexed subsequence $\{z_{2k}\}$ accumulates at $0$,
forcing $f \equiv 0$ by the identity theorem, contradicting $f(z_n)=1$ for odd $
n$. |

The key distinction is that P prescribes values consistent with a single analyti
c function ($\sin z$), so the identity theorem yields uniqueness rather than a c
ontradiction; whereas Q prescribes two different limiting values ($0$ and $1$) a
long two subsequences both accumulating at the same interior point $0$, which no
 analytic function can satisfy.
