## Proof

**Key Observation:** For a non-abelian simple group $S$, the center $Z(S) = 1$.

**Step 1: Intersection with each factor.**

Let $N \trianglelefteq G$. For each $i$, since $N \trianglelefteq G$ and $S_i \t
rianglelefteq G$, we have $N \cap S_i \trianglelefteq S_i$. Since $S_i$ is simpl
e, either $N \cap S_i = 1$ or $N \cap S_i = S_i$.

Define:
- $A = \{i : N \cap S_i = S_i\}$ (factors fully contained in $N$),
- $B = \{i : N \cap S_i = 1\}$ (factors intersecting $N$ trivially).

Then $\{1,\ldots,k\} = A \sqcup B$, and clearly $\prod_{i \in A} S_i \leq N$.

**Step 2: $N$ is trivial on all factors in $B$.**

Take $(n_1, \ldots, n_k) \in N$ and any $i \in B$. For any $g \in S_i$, conjugat
ing by $e_i = (1,\ldots,g,\ldots,1) \in S_i \trianglelefteq G$ gives:
$$(n_1, \ldots, g\, n_i\, g^{-1}, \ldots, n_k) \in N.$$

Multiplying by the inverse of the original element:
$$(1, \ldots, n_i\, g\, n_i^{-1} g^{-1}, \ldots, 1) \in N.$$

This element lies in $S_i \cap N = 1$ (since $i \in B$), so $n_i\, g\, n_i^{-1}
g^{-1} = 1$ for all $g \in S_i$. This means $n_i \in Z(S_i)$. Since $S_i$ is **n
on-abelian simple**, $Z(S_i) = 1$, so $n_i = 1$.

**Step 3: Conclusion.**

Since $n_i = 1$ for every $i \in B$, every element of $N$ is supported only on $
A$, so $N \leq \prod_{i \in A} S_i$. Combined with $\prod_{i \in A} S_i \leq N$
from Step 1, we get:
$$N = \prod_{i \in A} S_i.$$

**Step 4: Counting.**

Every normal subgroup is uniquely determined by the subset $A \subseteq \{1, \ld
ots, k\}$, and conversely, every subset $A$ gives a normal subgroup $\prod_{i \i
n A} S_i$ (which is normal as a product of normal factors). The number of subset
s of a $k$-element set is $2^k$.

**Remark on why diagonal subgroups are excluded:** The "diagonal" subgroup $D =
\{(s, \alpha(s)) : s \in S\} \leq S \times S$ is **not** normal when $S$ is non-
abelian. Conjugating $(s, \alpha(s)) \in D$ by $(g_1, g_2) \in S \times S$ yield
s $(g_1 s g_1^{-1},\, g_2 \alpha(s) g_2^{-1})$. For this to lie in $D$, we'd nee
d $g_2 \alpha(s) g_2^{-1} = \alpha(g_1 s g_1^{-1})$ for **all** $g_1, g_2 \in S$
, i.e., $\operatorname{Inn}(g_2) = \alpha \circ \operatorname{Inn}(g_1) \circ \a
lpha^{-1}$ for all $g_1, g_2$. Since $\alpha$ normalizes $\operatorname{Inn}(S)$
, this forces $g_2 = \alpha(g_1)$ for all pairs — impossible for independent $g_
1, g_2$. The trivial center of $S$ is precisely what rules out such diagonals, l
eaving only the $2^k$ "coordinate" normal subgroups.

$$\boxed{2^k}$$
