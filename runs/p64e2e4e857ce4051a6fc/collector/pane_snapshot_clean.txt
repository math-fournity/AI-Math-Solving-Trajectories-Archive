## Proof

**Claim:** $f'$ is dominant.

**Setup and dimensions.** Since $f$ is finite and surjective, $\dim X = \dim Y$.
 Since $g$ and $h$ are surjective birational, $\dim X' = \dim X$ and $\dim Y' =
\dim Y$. Hence $\dim X' = \dim Y'$.

**Step 1: $h \circ f'$ is surjective.** The composition $f \circ g : X' \to Y$ i
s surjective, since $g$ is surjective ($g(X') = X$) and $f$ is surjective ($f(X)
 = Y$). By the hypothesis $h \circ f' = f \circ g$, the map $h \circ f' : X' \to
 Y$ is surjective, i.e., $h(f'(X')) = Y$.

**Step 2: The image of $f'$ has full $h$-shadow.** Let $Z := \overline{f'(X')} \
subseteq Y'$, a closed subset. Since $X', Y'$ are projective, the morphism $h :
Y' \to Y$ is proper, hence a closed map. Therefore $h(Z)$ is closed in $Y$. Sinc
e $f'(X') \subseteq Z$, we have
$$Y = h(f'(X')) \subseteq h(Z) \subseteq Y,$$
so $h(Z) = Y$.

**Step 3: $Z$ contains the isomorphism locus of $h$.** Since $h$ is birational a
nd $Y$ is integral (irreducible), there exists a nonempty (hence dense) open sub
set $V \subseteq Y$ such that $h^{-1}(V) \xrightarrow{\sim} V$ is an isomorphism
. For any $y \in V$, since $y \in h(Z)$, there is a point $z \in Z$ with $h(z) =
 y$. Over $V$ the map $h$ is injective, so $z$ is the unique point of $h^{-1}(y)
 \cap h^{-1}(V)$; in particular $z \in h^{-1}(V)$. This shows $h^{-1}(V) \subset
eq Z$.

**Step 4: $Z = Y'$.** The set $h^{-1}(V)$ is a nonempty open subset of $Y'$ (non
empty because $h$ is surjective and $V$ is nonempty; open because $h$ is continu
ous). Since $Y'$ is irreducible, every nonempty open subset is dense. Thus $h^{-
1}(V)$ is dense in $Y'$. As $Z$ is closed and contains the dense subset $h^{-1}(
V)$, we conclude $Z = Y'$.

**Conclusion.** $Z = \overline{f'(X')} = Y'$, which is precisely the statement t
hat $f' : X' \to Y'$ is dominant. $\blacksquare$
