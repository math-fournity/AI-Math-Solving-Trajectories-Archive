**Claim:** Yes, $g_1$ and $g_2$ are necessarily conjugate in $G$.

**Proof.**

Since every finite-dimensional representation of a finite group over $\mathbb{C}
$ decomposes as a direct sum of irreducible representations, the hypothesis "$\c
hi_\rho(g_1) = \chi_\rho(g_2)$ for every representation $\rho$" is equivalent to

$$\chi_i(g_1) = \chi_i(g_2) \quad \text{for every irreducible character } \chi_i
,\ i=1,\dots,r,$$

where $r$ is the number of conjugacy classes of $G$ (equivalently, the number of
 irreducible representations).

We use the **column orthogonality relation** of the character table. Let $g, h \
in G$. Then

$$\sum_{i=1}^{r} \chi_i(g)\,\overline{\chi_i(h)} \;=\; \begin{cases} |C_G(g)| &
\text{if } g \text{ and } h \text{ are conjugate in } G, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.
} \end{cases}$$

(This is a standard consequence of the orthogonality of the character table view
ed as a unitary matrix up to scaling; it follows from the Schur orthogonality re
lations applied to matrix coefficients.)

Now apply this with $g = g_1$ and $h = g_2$. By our hypothesis, $\chi_i(g_1) = \
chi_i(g_2)$ for all $i$, so:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{r} \chi_i(g_1)\,\overline{\chi_i(g_2)} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{r} \chi_i
(g_1)\,\overline{\chi_i(g_1)}.$$

By column orthogonality (the case $g = h = g_1$), the right-hand side equals $|C
_G(g_1)|$.

By column orthogonality (the case $g = g_1, h = g_2$), the left-hand side equals
 $|C_G(g_1)|$ if $g_1 \sim g_2$, and $0$ otherwise.

Therefore:

$$|C_G(g_1)| = |C_G(g_1)| \cdot \mathbf{1}_{g_1 \sim g_2}.$$

Since $|C_G(g_1)| \geq 1$ (the identity centralizes every element), we can divid
e both sides by $|C_G(g_1)|$ to obtain:

$$\mathbf{1}_{g_1 \sim g_2} = 1.$$

Hence $g_1$ and $g_2$ are conjugate in $G$. $\blacksquare$
