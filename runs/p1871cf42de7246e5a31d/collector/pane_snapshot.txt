## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, there exists an uncountable ring with a countable (in fact, nont
rivial and countably infinite) quotient ring.

**Construction.** Let
$$R = \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{Z}$$
be the direct product ring, with componentwise addition and multiplication. Let
$$I = \mathbb{R} \times \{0\}.$$

**Step 1: $R$ is uncountable.** The projection $R \to \mathbb{R}$, $(a,n)\mapsto
 a$, is surjective, so $|R| \ge |\mathbb{R}|$. Hence $R$ is uncountable. (In fac
t $|R| = |\mathbb{R}|\cdot|\mathbb{Z}| = |\mathbb{R}|$.)

**Step 2: $I$ is a (two-sided) ideal of $R$.** $I$ is clearly an additive subgro
up of $R$. For absorption: take $(r,0)\in I$ and $(a,n)\in R$. Then
$$(a,n)\cdot(r,0) = (ar,\, n\cdot 0) = (ar,0)\in I, \qquad (r,0)\cdot(a,n) = (ra
,\,0\cdot n) = (ra,0)\in I.$$
So $I$ is a two-sided ideal.

**Step 3: The quotient $R/I$ is countably infinite.** Define
$$\varphi: R \to \mathbb{Z}, \qquad \varphi(a,n) = n.$$
This is a surjective ring homomorphism (it preserves addition and multiplication
 since both are componentwise, and $\varphi(1,1)=1$). Its kernel is
$$\ker\varphi = \{(a,n)\in R : n=0\} = \mathbb{R}\times\{0\} = I.$$
By the First Isomorphism Theorem,
$$R/I \;\cong\; \mathbb{Z}.$$
Since $\mathbb{Z}$ is countably infinite, $R/I$ is a countable (nontrivial) ring
.

**Conclusion.** $R = \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}$ is an uncountable ring, and its
 quotient $R/(\mathbb{R}\times\{0\})\cong\mathbb{Z}$ is countable. Therefore suc
h a ring exists. $\blacksquare$
