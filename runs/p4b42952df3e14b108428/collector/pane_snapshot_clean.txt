## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, $\mu_1 = \mu_2$.

**Setup.** Since $\mu_2 \ge \mu_1 \ge 0$, both $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ are positive
Radon measures on $\Omega$. For a positive (finite) measure $\nu$, the total var
iation norm coincides with the total mass:
$$\|\nu\| = |\nu|(\Omega) = \nu(\Omega),$$
because the total variation of a positive measure is the measure itself.

**Key step.** The hypothesis $\mu_2 \ge \mu_1 \ge 0$ lets us define the differen
ce
$$\nu := \mu_2 - \mu_1,$$
which is a well-defined positive Radon measure (positivity follows from $\mu_2 \
ge \mu_1$). By linearity and the identification of the norm with total mass for
positive measures,
$$\|\mu_2\| - \|\mu_1\| = \mu_2(\Omega) - \mu_1(\Omega) = (\mu_2 - \mu_1)(\Omega
) = \nu(\Omega).$$
The hypothesis $\|\mu_2\| = \|\mu_1\|$ gives $\|\mu_2\| - \|\mu_1\| = 0$, hence
$$\nu(\Omega) = 0.$$

**Conclusion.** A positive measure whose total mass is zero must be the zero mea
sure: for every Borel set $E \subseteq \Omega$, $0 \le \nu(E) \le \nu(\Omega) =
0$, so $\nu(E) = 0$. Therefore $\nu = 0$, i.e.
$$\mu_2 - \mu_1 = 0 \implies \mu_1 = \mu_2.$$

Thus the answer is **yes**: under the assumptions $0 \le \mu_1 \le \mu_2$ and $\
|\mu_1\| = \|\mu_2\|$, we necessarily have $\mu_1 = \mu_2$.
