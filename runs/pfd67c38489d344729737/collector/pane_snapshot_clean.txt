## Proof

Let $(S_n)_{n \geq 0}$ be the symmetric simple random walk with $S_0 = 0$, $S_n
= \sum_{k=1}^n X_k$ where $X_k$ are i.i.d. with $\mathbb{P}(X_k = \pm 1) = 1/2$.

**Key observation.** Since $|S_i|$ is typically of order $\sqrt{i}$, the sum $\s
um_{i=1}^n |S_i|$ is of order $\sum_{i=1}^n \sqrt{i} \sim \frac{2}{3}n^{3/2}$, s
o $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n |S_i|$ grows like $\sqrt{n}$ and diverges. We make th
is rigorous via Donsker's invariance principle.

**Step 1: Functional CLT.** By Donsker's theorem, the process $\{S_{\lfloor nt \
rfloor}/\sqrt{n}\}_{0 \le t \le 1}$ converges in distribution to standard Browni
an motion $\{B_t\}_{0 \le t \le 1}$ in the Skorokhod space $D[0,1]$.

**Step 2: Continuous mapping.** Define the functional $\Phi: D[0,1] \to \mathbb{
R}$ by $\Phi(f) = \int_0^1 |f(t)|\,dt$. This functional is continuous at every c
ontinuous limit (since for continuous $g$, $|\Phi(f) - \Phi(g)| \le \|f - g\|_\i
nfty$). Since Brownian motion has continuous paths a.s., by the continuous mappi
ng theorem:

$$\int_0^1 \frac{|S_{\lfloor nt \rfloor}|}{\sqrt{n}}\,dt \;\Rightarrow\; \int_0^
1 |B_t|\,dt =: Y.$$

**Step 3: Riemann sum identification.** The left-hand side is a Riemann sum:

$$\int_0^1 \frac{|S_{\lfloor nt \rfloor}|}{\sqrt{n}}\,dt = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}
^{n-1} \frac{|S_i|}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{1}{n\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} |S_i|.$$

The difference between $\frac{1}{n\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}|S_i|$ and $\frac{1}{
n\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S_i|$ is $\frac{|S_n| - |S_0|}{n\sqrt{n}} = \frac{|S_n|
}{n\sqrt{n}} = \frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{|S_n|}{\sqrt{n}}$. Since $|S_n|/\sqrt{n}$ i
s tight (it converges in distribution to $|B_1|$), this difference vanishes in p
robability. Therefore:

$$\frac{1}{n\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n} |S_i| \;\Rightarrow\; Y = \int_0^1 |B_t|\,dt
.$$

**Step 4: The limit $Y$ is a.s. positive.** Brownian motion $B_t$ is not identic
ally zero on $[0,1]$: indeed $\mathbb{P}(B_t = 0\ \forall\, t \in [0,1]) = 0$. S
ince $|B_t| \ge 0$ and $|B_t| > 0$ on a set of positive Lebesgue measure a.s., w
e have $Y = \int_0^1 |B_t|\,dt > 0$ almost surely.

**Step 5: Conclusion.** We write

$$\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n |S_i| = \sqrt{n}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{n\sqrt{n}}\s
um_{i=1}^n |S_i|}_{=: Y_n},$$

so

$$\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n |S_i| > a\right) = \mathbb{P}\!\left
(Y_n > \frac{a}{\sqrt{n}}\right).$$

We have $Y_n \Rightarrow Y$ with $Y > 0$ a.s., and $a/\sqrt{n} \to 0$.

- **If $a \le 0$:** Since $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n |S_i| \ge 0$ and (for $n \ge
1$) $S_1 = \pm 1$ so the sum is strictly positive a.s., the probability equals $
1$ for all $n \ge 1$.

- **If $a > 0$:** Fix $\varepsilon > 0$. Since $Y > 0$ a.s., there exists $\delt
a > 0$ (a continuity point of the distribution of $Y$, which has a continuous CD
F) such that $\mathbb{P}(Y > \delta) > 1 - \varepsilon$. For all sufficiently la
rge $n$, $a/\sqrt{n} < \delta$, so:

$$\mathbb{P}(Y_n > a/\sqrt{n}) \ge \mathbb{P}(Y_n > \delta) \to \mathbb{P}(Y > \
delta) > 1 - \varepsilon.$$

Since $\varepsilon > 0$ is arbitrary, $\mathbb{P}(Y_n > a/\sqrt{n}) \to 1$.

**Result.** For every $a \in \mathbb{R}$,

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n |S_i| > a\right) = \b
oxed{1}.$$

The intuition is that $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n |S_i| \sim \sqrt{n}\cdot\int_0^1|
B_t|\,dt \to \infty$ in probability, since the integral $\int_0^1|B_t|\,dt$ is s
trictly positive almost surely.
