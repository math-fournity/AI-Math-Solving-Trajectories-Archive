## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, such a non-constant entire function exists.

**Setup.** Consider the sequence of points
$$a_n = n + \frac{1}{n}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$
We have $a_1 = 2$, $a_2 = \frac{5}{2}$, $a_3 = \frac{10}{3}$, and in general $a_
n \to \infty$ as $n \to \infty$. These are the required zeros: $f(a_n) = 0$ for
every $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

**Weierstrass factorization theorem.** Given any sequence $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty
}$ of distinct complex numbers with $|a_n| \to \infty$, there exists an entire f
unction whose zero set is exactly $\{a_n : n \geq 1\}$ (with any prescribed mult
iplicities). The function is constructed as a *canonical product*:
$$f(z) = \prod_{n=1}^{\infty} E_p\!\left(\frac{z}{a_n}\right), \qquad E_p(w) = (
1-w)\exp\!\left(w + \frac{w^2}{2} + \cdots + \frac{w^p}{p}\right),$$
where the genus $p$ is chosen so that $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |a_n|^{-(p+1)} < \inf
ty$. Under this condition the product converges absolutely and uniformly on ever
y compact subset of $\mathbb{C}$, hence defines an entire function, and its zero
s are precisely $\{a_n\}$.

**Choosing the genus.** Since $|a_n| = n + \frac{1}{n} \geq n$, we have
$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{|a_n|^2} \leq \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} <
 \infty.$$
Thus genus $p = 1$ suffices (we need $\sum |a_n|^{-(p+1)} = \sum |a_n|^{-2} < \i
nfty$). The elementary factor of genus 1 is
$$E_1(w) = (1-w)\,e^{w}.$$

**Construction.** Define
$$\boxed{\,f(z) = \prod_{n=1}^{\infty} \left(1 - \frac{z}{n + 1/n}\right) \exp\!
\left(\frac{z}{n + 1/n}\right).\,}$$

- **Entireness:** The estimate $|a_n| \geq n$ gives $\sum_{n} |a_n|^{-2} < \inft
y$, so by the standard convergence criterion for canonical products, the infinit
e product converges uniformly on compact subsets of $\mathbb{C}$. Each factor is
 entire, so the limit is entire.
- **Zeros:** Each factor $E_1(z/a_n)$ vanishes exactly at $z = a_n$ (a simple ze
ro), and no factor vanishes at $z = a_m$ for $m \neq n$. The exponential factors
 $\exp(z/a_n)$ never vanish, so they do not introduce or remove zeros. Hence the
 zero set of $f$ is exactly $\{a_n : n \geq 1\} = \left\{n + \tfrac{1}{n} : n \i
n \mathbb{N}\right\}$.
- **Non-constant:** $f$ has infinitely many distinct zeros. A non-zero constant
function has no zeros, and the zero function is constant. Therefore $f$ is non-c
onstant.

**Verification of the required property.** For every $n \in \mathbb{N}$,
$$f\!\left(n + \tfrac{1}{n}\right) = 0,$$
by construction.

**Conclusion.** The canonical product above is a non-constant entire function va
nishing at every $n + \frac{1}{n}$, $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Therefore such a functio
n exists.
