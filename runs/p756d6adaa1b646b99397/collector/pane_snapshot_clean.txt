**Answer: The statement is FALSE.**

**Counterexample.** Take $X = \ell^1$ and $Y = \ell^2$, both with their standard
 norms. These are Banach spaces, and $X \subset Y$: indeed, if $(x_n) \in \ell^1
$, then
$$\sum_{n} |x_n|^2 \le \left(\sum_n |x_n|\right)^2 < \infty,$$
so $(x_n) \in \ell^2$. Thus $\ell^1 \subset \ell^2$.

Now compute the duals. It is standard that
$$\left(\ell^1\right)^* \cong \ell^\infty \quad\text{and}\quad \left(\ell^2\righ
t)^* \cong \ell^2.$$

If the claimed statement were true, we would have $\ell^\infty = (\ell^1)^* \sub
set (\ell^2)^* = \ell^2$. But this is false: the constant sequence $(1, 1, 1, \d
ots)$ belongs to $\ell^\infty$, yet
$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty 1^2 = \infty,$$
so $(1,1,1,\dots) \notin \ell^2$. Hence $\ell^\infty \not\subset \ell^2$.

**Conceptual reason.** When $X \subset Y$ with continuous inclusion, the natural
 map on duals goes the *other* direction: restriction $R: Y^* \to X^*$, $R(f) =
f|_X$. By Hahn–Banach, $R$ is surjective, so $X^* \cong Y^*/X^\perp$ where $X^\p
erp = \{f \in Y^* : f|_X = 0\}$. Thus $X^*$ is a **quotient** of $Y^*$, not a su
bspace; there is no natural inclusion $X^* \subset Y^*$, and as the counterexamp
le shows, such an inclusion generally fails.
