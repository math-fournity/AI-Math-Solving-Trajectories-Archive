## Solution

**Yes**, such a formula exists.

Consider the first-order language with a single binary relation symbol $R$ and *
*no equality**. Let $\varphi$ be the sentence:

$$\varphi \;:=\; \forall x\,\neg R(x,x)\;\wedge\;\forall x\forall y\forall z\big
l((R(x,y)\wedge R(y,z))\to R(x,z)\bigr)\;\wedge\;\forall x\,\exists y\,R(x,y).$$

This sentence uses only $R$, the connectives $\neg,\wedge,\to$, and the quantifi
ers $\forall,\exists$ — **no identity/equality symbol**.

### $\varphi$ is satisfiable (by an infinite structure)

Take $\mathcal M = (\mathbb N, <)$. The usual order $<$ on $\mathbb N$ is irrefl
exive, transitive, and every natural number has a strictly larger one. Hence $\m
athcal M \models \varphi$, and $\mathcal M$ is infinite.

### No finite (non-empty) structure satisfies $\varphi$

Suppose for contradiction that $\mathcal M = (M, R^{\mathcal M})$ is a **finite,
 non-empty** structure with $\mathcal M \models \varphi$. Then $R^{\mathcal M}$
is:

- **irreflexive**: $\forall x\,\neg R(x,x)$, and
- **transitive**: $\forall x\forall y\forall z\,((R(x,y)\wedge R(y,z))\to R(x,z)
)$.

Irreflexivity + transitivity imply **asymmetry**: if $R(a,b)$ and $R(b,a)$ held,
 transitivity would give $R(a,a)$, contradicting irreflexivity. So $R^{\mathcal
M}$ is a **strict partial order** on $M$.

**Claim:** Every finite non-empty strict partial order has a maximal element (an
 element $m$ with no $n$ such that $R(m,n)$).

*Proof of claim.* Start with any element $a_0\in M$. If $a_0$ is maximal, done.
Otherwise pick $a_1$ with $R(a_0,a_1)$. If $a_1$ is maximal, done. Otherwise con
tinue, producing a chain $a_0, a_1, a_2,\dots$ with $R(a_i,a_{i+1})$ for each $i
$. By asymmetry, all $a_i$ are distinct (any repeat $a_i = a_j$ with $i<j$ would
, via transitivity along the chain, yield $R(a_i,a_i)$, contradicting irreflexiv
ity). Since $M$ is **finite**, the chain must terminate, reaching a maximal elem
ent. $\square$

So $M$ contains a maximal element $m$: there is no $n\in M$ with $R^{\mathcal M}
(m,n)$. This directly contradicts the third conjunct $\forall x\,\exists y\,R(x,
y)$ of $\varphi$.

Therefore **no finite non-empty structure satisfies $\varphi$**.

### Conclusion

The sentence $\varphi$ — a first-order formula **without the identity operator**
 — is satisfiable (e.g. by $(\mathbb N,<)$) but **only** by infinite structures.
 Hence such a formula exists.
