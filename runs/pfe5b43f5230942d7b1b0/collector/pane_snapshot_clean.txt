## Proof

The answer is **YES** for $1 < p \leq \infty$ (equivalently $1 \leq q < \infty$)
, and this holds **by the very definition** of weak-$\star$ convergence. The cas
e $p = 1$ requires separate commentary since $L^1$ is not a dual space in the $L
^p$ scale.

---

### Setup: When does $L^p$ carry a weak-$\star$ topology?

A weak-$\star$ topology is defined on a dual space $X^*$. To speak of weak-$\sta
r$ convergence in $L^p(\mathbb{R}^d)$, we need $L^p$ to be realizable as a dual
space. By the **Riesz Representation Theorem**:

$$(L^q(\mathbb{R}^d))^* \cong L^p(\mathbb{R}^d), \quad \text{whenever } 1 \leq q
 < \infty,\; 1/p + 1/q = 1,$$

i.e., for $1 < p \leq \infty$. The duality pairing is:

$$\langle f, g \rangle = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(x)\,g(x)\,dx, \quad f \in L^p,\;
g \in L^q.$$

---

### Case 1: $1 < p < \infty$ (so $1 < q < \infty$)

Here $L^p = (L^q)^*$ with $1 < q < \infty$. The **weak-$\star$ topology** on $L^
p = (L^q)^*$ is, by definition, the coarsest topology making all the evaluation
maps $f \mapsto \langle f, g \rangle$ continuous for $g \in L^q$.

Therefore, $f_n \stackrel{*}{\rightharpoonup} f$ in $L^p = (L^q)^*$ **means prec
isely**:

$$\langle f_n, g \rangle \to \langle f, g \rangle \quad \text{for all } g \in L^
q,$$

that is,

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f_n\, g \to \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f\, g \quad \text{for all
} g \in L^q(\mathbb{R}^d).$$

This is true by definition. $\checkmark$

*(Remark: since $1 < p < \infty$ makes $L^p$ reflexive, the weak-$\star$ topolog
y on $L^p = (L^q)^*$ coincides with the weak topology on $L^p$, so weak and weak
-$\star$ convergence are the same here.)*

---

### Case 2: $p = \infty$ (so $q = 1$)

Here $L^\infty = (L^1)^*$. The weak-$\star$ topology on $L^\infty = (L^1)^*$ is,
 by definition, the topology of pointwise convergence on $L^1$. Thus $f_n \stack
rel{*}{\rightharpoonup} f$ in $L^\infty$ **means precisely**:

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f_n\, g \to \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f\, g \quad \text{for all
} g \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^d).$$

This is true by definition. $\checkmark$

*(Note: here weak-$\star$ convergence is strictly weaker than weak convergence i
n $L^\infty$, since $(L^\infty)^* = \mathrm{ba}(\mathbb{R}^d) \supsetneq L^1(\ma
thbb{R}^d)$. But the question asks about weak-$\star$ convergence, which tests a
gainst $L^1$ exactly.)*

---

### Case 3: $p = 1$ (so $q = \infty$)

Here $L^1$ is **not** isomorphic to $(L^\infty)^*$, since $(L^\infty(\mathbb{R}^
d))^* = \mathrm{ba}(\mathbb{R}^d)$ (the space of bounded finitely-additive signe
d measures absolutely continuous w.r.t. Lebesgue measure), which is **strictly l
arger** than $L^1(\mathbb{R}^d)$. Therefore $L^1$ does not arise as the dual of
$L^\infty$ in the $L^p$ scale, and the notion of "weak-$\star$ convergence in $L
^1$" is **not well-defined** in the standard $L^p$ duality framework. The questi
on is ill-posed for $p = 1$.

---

### Conclusion

For every $p$ in the range $1 < p \leq \infty$ (equivalently, $1 \leq q < \infty
$), $L^p(\mathbb{R}^d) \cong (L^q(\mathbb{R}^d))^*$, and **weak-$\star$ converge
nce of $(f_n)$ to $f$ in $L^p$ is, by definition, the statement that**

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f_n\, g \;\longrightarrow\; \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f\, g \qua
d \text{for all } g \in L^q(\mathbb{R}^d).$$

Thus the implication holds **tautologically**: the integral convergence against
all $L^q$ test functions is not a consequence of weak-$\star$ convergence but ra
ther its **defining property**. The only caveat is the endpoint $p = 1$, where $
L^1$ is not a dual space in the $L^p$ scale, so weak-$\star$ convergence is not
a meaningful notion there.
