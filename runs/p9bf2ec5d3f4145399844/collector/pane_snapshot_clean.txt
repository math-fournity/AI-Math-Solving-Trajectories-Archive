Proof

Claim. Let $(A, \lambda_A)$ and $(B, \lambda_B)$ be principally polarized
abelian varieties over a field, and let $f: A \to B$ be an isogeny that respects
the polarization, i.e.

$$f^\vee \circ \lambda_B \circ f = \lambda_A.$$

Then $f$ is an isomorphism.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Proof.

Recall that a principal polarization is an isomorphism $\lambda: X \to X^\vee$
that arises from an ample line bundle; in particular $\deg(\lambda) = 1$.

Step 1: Degree of the dual isogeny. For any isogeny $f: A \to B$ of abelian
varieties, the dual isogeny $f^\vee: B^\vee \to A^\vee$ satisfies

$$\deg(f^\vee) = \deg(f).$$

This is standard: the dual isogeny fits into the exact sequence defining the
kernel duality, and $|\ker f^\vee| = |\ker f|$.

Step 2: Degree of a composition. For composable isogenies $g, h$,

$$\deg(g \circ h) = \deg(g)\cdot \deg(h),$$

since $|\ker(g\circ h)| = |\ker g|\cdot|\ker h|$ when the composition is defined
(the kernel of $h$ maps into the kernel of $g$).

Step 3: Apply to the polarization-pulling-back identity. The hypothesis $f^\vee
\circ \lambda_B \circ f = \lambda_A$ gives, by taking degrees:

$$\deg(\lambda_A) = \deg(f^\vee \circ \lambda_B \circ f) = \deg(f^\vee)\cdot
\deg(\lambda_B)\cdot \deg(f).$$

Step 4: Substitute the principal conditions. Since $\lambda_A$ and $\lambda_B$
are principal, $\deg(\lambda_A) = \deg(\lambda_B) = 1$. Using Step 1 ($
\deg(f^\vee) = \deg(f)$):

$$1 = \deg(f)\cdot 1 \cdot \deg(f) = \deg(f)^2.$$

Step 5: Conclude. Since $\deg(f)$ is a positive integer (the order of the finite
kernel $\ker f$), $\deg(f)^2 = 1$ forces $\deg(f) = 1$, i.e. $\ker f = {0}$. An
isogeny with trivial kernel is an isomorphism. $\blacksquare$

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Remark. The key point is multiplicativity of degree under composition together
with the equality $\deg(f^\vee)=\deg(f)$: pulling back a polarization of degree
$d_B$ via an isogeny of degree $n$ produces a polarization of degree $n^2 d_B$.
When both polarizations are principal ($d_A = d_B = 1$), this forces $n=1$.
