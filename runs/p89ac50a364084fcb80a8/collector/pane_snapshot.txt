**Answer: Yes, a continuous open surjection $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is neces
sarily a homeomorphism.**

**Proof.**

We show that continuity + openness forces $f$ to be strictly monotone, which tog
ether with surjectivity gives bijectivity, and then openness of a bijection give
s continuity of the inverse.

---

**Step 1. $f$ has no local extremum and is not constant on any open interval.**

Suppose $f$ has a local maximum at $x_0$: there is $\delta>0$ with $f(x)\le f(x_
0)$ for all $x\in(x_0-\delta,x_0+\delta)$. Set $S=f((x_0-\delta,x_0+\delta))$. T
hen $f(x_0)\in S$ and $f(x_0)=\max S$, so $f(x_0)$ is not an interior point of $
S$ (every neighborhood of $f(x_0)$ contains values $>f(x_0)$, none of which lie
in $S$). Hence $S$ is not open, contradicting that $f$ is an open map. The case
of a local minimum is identical.

If $f$ is constant on some open interval $I$, then $f(I)$ is a single point, whi
ch is not open — again a contradiction.

---

**Step 2. On every closed interval $[a,b]$, the extrema of $f$ are attained only
 at the endpoints.**

Since $f$ is continuous, it attains its maximum $M$ and minimum $m$ on $[a,b]$.
If the maximum is attained at some $x_M\in(a,b)$, then $x_M$ is a local maximum
of $f$ (take a small neighborhood inside $(a,b)$), contradicting Step 1. Likewis
e the minimum is attained only at $\{a,b\}$.

Consequently, for every $x\in[a,b]$:
$$\min(f(a),f(b))\le f(x)\le \max(f(a),f(b)).$$

---

**Step 3. For all $x<y<z$, $f(y)$ lies strictly between $f(x)$ and $f(z)$.**

Applying Step 2 to $[x,z]$: $f(y)\in[\min(f(x),f(z)),\,\max(f(x),f(z))]$. If $f(
y)=f(x)$, then $f$ is constant on $[x,y]$ (its max and min on $[x,y]$ are both $
f(x)=f(y)$, both at endpoints), so $f$ is constant on the open interval $(x,y)$,
 contradicting Step 1. Similarly $f(y)\ne f(z)$. Hence $f(y)$ is **strictly** be
tween $f(x)$ and $f(z)$.

---

**Step 4. $f$ is strictly monotone.**

Condition (Step 3) implies that for every triple $x<y<z$, either $f(x)<f(y)<f(z)
$ or $f(x)>f(y)>f(z)$. The choice of direction is **globally consistent**: given
 $x<y<z<w$, the triples $(x,y,z)$ and $(y,z,w)$ must agree on the order of $f(y)
$ versus $f(z)$. Therefore either $f$ is strictly increasing on all of $\mathbb{
R}$, or strictly decreasing on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

---

**Step 5. $f$ is a homeomorphism.**

A strictly monotone continuous function is injective. Since $f$ is also surjecti
ve, it is a continuous bijection $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$. Being open and bijec
tive, the inverse $f^{-1}$ maps open sets to open sets — equivalently, $f^{-1}$
is continuous. (Equivalently, a strictly monotone continuous bijection on $\math
bb{R}$ has a continuous inverse by the standard intermediate-value argument.)

Therefore $f$ is a homeomorphism. $\blacksquare$
