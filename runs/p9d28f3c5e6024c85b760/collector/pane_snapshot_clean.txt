## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. If $f: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ is Lipschitz continuous on a bound
ed star-shaped domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n$, then there exists $M > 0$ s
uch that $\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\right| < M$ almost everywhere in
 $\Omega$ for every $i = 1, \dots, n$.

**Proof.**

Since $f$ is Lipschitz continuous on $\Omega$, there exists a constant $L > 0$ s
uch that
$$|f(x) - f(y)| \le L\,|x - y| \quad \text{for all } x, y \in \Omega.$$

A *domain* is by definition an open connected set, so $\Omega$ is open in $\math
bb{R}^n$. (The boundedness and star-shapedness hypotheses are not needed for the
 core argument; they are consistent with the setting but the conclusion follows
from Lipschitz continuity alone.)

By **Rademacher's theorem**, a Lipschitz function on an open subset of $\mathbb{
R}^n$ is (Fréchet) differentiable almost everywhere in $\Omega$. At every point
$x \in \Omega$ where $f$ is differentiable, the directional derivative of $f$ in
 any direction $v$ with $|v| = 1$ satisfies
$$\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}(x)\right| = \left|Df(x)\,v\right| \le L,$$
because the directional derivative is the limit of difference quotients $\frac{f
(x+tv) - f(x)}{t}$, each of which is bounded in absolute value by $L$ (using the
 Lipschitz condition with $y = x + tv$, valid for $t$ small enough since $\Omega
$ is open).

In particular, taking $v = e_i$ (the standard basis vector), at every point of d
ifferentiability we have
$$\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x)\right| \le L.$$

Since $f$ is differentiable a.e. in $\Omega$, this bound holds almost everywhere
.

Now choose any $M > L$ (for instance $M = L + 1$). Then at every point of differ
entiability,
$$\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x)\right| \le L < M,$$
so $\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\right| < M$ holds almost everywhere in
 $\Omega$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The Lipschitz constant $L$ itself serves as an a.e. upper bound on e
very partial derivative; the strict inequality is obtained by taking any $M > L$
. The boundedness and star-shapedness of $\Omega$ are not essential for this con
clusion—Rademacher's theorem requires only that $\Omega$ be open (which is part
of the definition of a domain) and that $f$ be Lipschitz.
