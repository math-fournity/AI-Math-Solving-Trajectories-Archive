## Proof

**The statement is true.**

Let $p$ be an odd prime. Then $p - 1$ is even, so $(p-1)/2$ is a positive intege
r that divides $p-1$.

**Key fact.** The multiplicative group $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^*$ is cyclic of
 order $p-1$. A cyclic group of order $n$ contains an element of order $d$ for e
very divisor $d \mid n$. Since $(p-1)/2 \mid (p-1)$, there exists $a \in (\mathb
b{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^*$ with $\operatorname{ord}_p(a) = (p-1)/2$.

**Cycle structure of the permutation.** Define $\sigma : \Phi(p) \to \Phi(p)$ by
 $\sigma(x) = ax \bmod p$. This is a bijection since $\gcd(a, p) = 1$. For any $
x \in \Phi(p)$, the cycle containing $x$ is
$$x,\; ax,\; a^2 x,\; \ldots$$
and its length is the smallest positive $k$ such that $a^k x \equiv x \pmod{p}$.
 Since $x$ is invertible mod $p$, this is equivalent to $a^k \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$,
 giving $k = \operatorname{ord}_p(a) = (p-1)/2$.

Thus **every** cycle has the same length $(p-1)/2$. Since $|\Phi(p)| = p - 1$, t
he number of cycles is:
$$\frac{p-1}{(p-1)/2} = 2.$$

**Verification with small primes:**
- $p = 3$: $a = 1$ (order 1), identity on $\{1,2\}$ → two fixed points. ✓
- $p = 5$: $a = 4$ (order 2), cycles $(1,4)$ and $(2,3)$. ✓
- $p = 7$: $a = 2$ (order 3), cycles $(1,2,4)$ and $(3,6,5)$. ✓
- $p = 11$: $a = 3$ (order 5), cycles $(1,3,9,5,4)$ and $(2,6,7,10,8)$. ✓

**Conclusion.** For every odd prime $p$, choosing $a$ to be any element of order
 $(p-1)/2$ in the cyclic group $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^*$ yields a permutation
 of $\Phi(p)$ consisting of exactly two cycles. $\blacksquare$
