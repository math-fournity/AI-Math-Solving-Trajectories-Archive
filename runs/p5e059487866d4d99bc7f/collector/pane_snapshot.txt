## Solution

**Answer: No.** The hypothesis does not imply uniform convergence almost everywh
ere. We give a counterexample.

### Counterexample

Let $E = [0,1] \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ (so $l=1$, $m(E)=1>0$). Define
$$f_k(x) = x^k, \qquad x \in [0,1].$$
Each $f_k$ is continuous (hence measurable). The pointwise limit is
$$f(x) = \lim_{k\to\infty} x^k = \begin{cases} 0 & x \in [0,1), \\ 1 & x = 1. \e
nd{cases}$$

### The hypothesis is satisfied

Since $m(E)=1<\infty$ and $f_k \to f$ pointwise on $[0,1]$ (everywhere, in fact)
, **Egorov's theorem** applies: for every $\epsilon>0$ there exists a measurable
 set $A_\epsilon \subseteq E$ with $m(A_\epsilon) > m(E)-\epsilon$ such that $f_
k\to f$ uniformly on $A_\epsilon$. Taking $F = E \setminus A_\epsilon$, we have
$m(F) < \epsilon$; by inner regularity we may shrink $A_\epsilon$ slightly to ma
ke $F$ closed while keeping $m(F)\le\epsilon$. Thus for every $\epsilon>0$ there
 is a **closed** $F\subseteq E$ with $m(F)\le\epsilon$ and $f_k\to f$ uniformly
on $E\setminus F$. The hypothesis holds.

### Uniform convergence a.e. fails

Suppose, for contradiction, that $f_k\to f$ uniformly a.e. on $E$. Then there is
 a null set $N\subseteq[0,1]$ with $m(N)=0$ such that $f_k\to f$ uniformly on $[
0,1]\setminus N$.

Consider the set $[0,1)\setminus N$. Since $m(N)=0$, this set has measure $1$ an
d in particular is **dense near $1$**: for every $\delta>0$, the interval $(1-\d
elta,1)$ is not contained in $N$, so $[0,1)\setminus N$ contains points arbitrar
ily close to $1$.

On $[0,1)\setminus N$ the limit function is $f\equiv 0$. Hence uniform convergen
ce would require
$$\sup_{x\in[0,1)\setminus N} |x^k - 0| = \sup_{x\in[0,1)\setminus N} x^k \xrigh
tarrow{k\to\infty} 0.$$

But for every $k$, since $[0,1)\setminus N$ accumulates at $1$,
$$\sup_{x\in[0,1)\setminus N} x^k = 1.$$
Indeed, for any $\eta>0$ we can find $x\in[0,1)\setminus N$ with $x>2^{-1/k}$, g
iving $x^k>1/2$; more directly, taking $x$ arbitrarily close to $1$ makes $x^k$
arbitrarily close to $1$.

Therefore $\sup_{x\in[0,1]\setminus N}|f_k(x)-f(x)|\ge \sup_{x\in[0,1)\setminus
N}x^k = 1$ for all $k$, which does **not** tend to $0$. This contradicts uniform
 convergence on $[0,1]\setminus N$.

Since $N$ was an arbitrary null set, no such $N$ exists; i.e., $f_k\not\to f$ un
iformly almost everywhere on $E$.

### Conclusion

We have exhibited measurable functions $f_k(x)=x^k$ on $E=[0,1]$ satisfying the
stated hypothesis (via Egorov's theorem) for which $f_k$ does **not** converge u
niformly almost everywhere. Hence the implication is **false**.
