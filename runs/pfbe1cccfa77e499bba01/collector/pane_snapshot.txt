## Proof

We prove the answer is **yes**: for any nonconstant $P \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ and an
y positive integer $n$, there exists $a \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $|P(a+1)|, \do
ts, |P(a+n)|$ are all composite.

We use two ingredients: **Schur's theorem** and the **Chinese Remainder Theorem*
*.

---

**Schur's Theorem (1912).** *If $P \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ is nonconstant, then infin
itely many primes divide some value $P(k)$, $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.*

*Proof.* If $P(0)=0$, then $P(x)=x\,Q(x)$, and every prime $p$ divides $P(p)$, g
iving infinitely many primes.

If $P(0)\neq 0$, let $c=P(0)$. Suppose only $p_1,\dots,p_m$ divide values of $P$
. Set $M = c\,p_1\cdots p_m$ and $Q(x)=P(Mx)/c$. Since $Mx\equiv 0\pmod{c}$, we
get $P(Mx)\equiv P(0)=c\pmod{c}$, so $c\mid P(Mx)$ and $Q(x)\in\mathbb{Z}$ for a
ll $x\in\mathbb{Z}$. Then $Q$ is nonconstant with $Q(0)=1$, and every prime fact
or of any $Q(t)$ lies in $\{p_1,\dots,p_m\}$.

For any $k\in\mathbb{Z}$, since $k\,p_1\cdots p_m\equiv 0\pmod{p_i}$, we have $Q
(k\,p_1\cdots p_m)\equiv Q(0)=1\pmod{p_i}$ for each $i$. So no $p_i$ divides $Q(
k\,p_1\cdots p_m)$, yet all its prime factors must be among $p_1,\dots,p_m$. Thi
s forces $|Q(k\,p_1\cdots p_m)|=1$ for all $k$, contradicting $Q$ nonconstant (s
o $|Q(t)|\to\infty$). $\square$

---

**Main proof.**

By Schur's theorem, select $n$ **distinct** primes $p_1,\dots,p_n$ each dividing
 some value of $P$, and pick $b_i\in\mathbb{Z}$ with $P(b_i)\equiv 0\pmod{p_i}$.

Since the $p_i$ are pairwise coprime, the Chinese Remainder Theorem gives $a_0\i
n\mathbb{Z}$ with
$$a_0 \equiv b_i - i \pmod{p_i}, \qquad i=1,\dots,n.$$

Let $M = p_1\cdots p_n$. For any $k\in\mathbb{Z}$, set $a = a_0 + kM$. Then $a+i
\equiv b_i\pmod{p_i}$, so (since $P$ has integer coefficients):
$$P(a+i)\equiv P(b_i)\equiv 0\pmod{p_i}.$$

Thus $p_i\mid P(a+i)$ for every $i$.

Now we choose $k$ to ensure compositeness. Since $P$ is nonconstant of degree $d
\ge 1$:
- $|P(a_0+kM+i)|\to\infty$ as $|k|\to\infty$ for each $i$, so for large enough $
|k|$, $|P(a+i)|>p_i$ for all $i$ (ruling out $|P(a+i)|=p_i$, which would be prim
e).
- $P(a_0+kM+i)=0$ for at most $d$ values of $k$ (since $P$ has $\le d$ integer r
oots and $a_0+kM+i$ is injective in $k$), so we avoid those finitely many $k$ (r
uling out $|P(a+i)|=0$).

For such $k$, each $|P(a+i)|$ is a positive integer divisible by $p_i$ with $|P(
a+i)|>p_i$, hence $|P(a+i)|=p_i\cdot m_i$ with $m_i\ge 2$, making $|P(a+i)|$ com
posite.

This holds simultaneously for all $i=1,\dots,n$. $\blacksquare$
