Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether there exists a positive con
tinuous function $f$ on $\mathbb{R}$ with $f(x) \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$, yet
 $f^p$ is not (Lebesgue) integrable for any $p > 0$.

**Answer: Yes, such a function exists.**

## Construction

Let $\varphi: \mathbb{R} \to [0,1]$ be the triangular bump $\varphi(t) = \max(0,
\, 1-|t|)$, which is continuous, satisfies $\varphi(0)=1$ and $\varphi(t)=0$ for
 $|t|\ge 1$.

For each integer $n \ge 2$, define:
- **Center:** $x_n = 2^{2^n}$
- **Height:** $h_n = \dfrac{1}{n}$
- **Half-width:** $w_n = n^n$

Set $\psi_n(x) = h_n\,\varphi\!\left(\dfrac{x - x_n}{w_n}\right)$, so $\psi_n$ i
s supported on $[x_n - w_n,\; x_n + w_n]$, is continuous, and peaks at $\psi_n(x
_n) = h_n$.

**The bumps have disjoint supports.** The right endpoint of bump $n$ is $x_n + w
_n = 2^{2^n} + n^n$, and the left endpoint of bump $n+1$ is $x_{n+1} - w_{n+1} =
 2^{2^{n+1}} - (n+1)^{n+1}$. Their gap is
$$2^{2^{n+1}} - 2^{2^n} - n^n - (n+1)^{n+1} = 2^{2^n}\!\left(2^{2^n}-1\right) -
n^n - (n+1)^{n+1}.$$
For $n=2$: $16\cdot 15 - 4 - 27 = 209 > 0$. For $n\ge 3$, $2^{2^n}$ grows super-
exponentially while $n^n$ grows much slower, so the gap is positive. Hence suppo
rts are disjoint for all $n\ge 2$. ✓

**Define:**
$$f(x) = e^{-|x|} + \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} \psi_n(x).$$

Since the supports are disjoint and march off to $+\infty$, on any bounded inter
val only finitely many $\psi_n$ are nonzero, so $f$ is well-defined and continuo
us.

## Verification of the three required properties

**1. $f > 0$ everywhere.** Since $e^{-|x|} > 0$ for all $x$ and each $\psi_n \ge
 0$, we have $f(x) \ge e^{-|x|} > 0$. ✓

**2. $f(x) \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$.**

- *As $x \to -\infty$:* All bumps are centered at $x_n \ge 16 > 0$, so for $x <
0$, $f(x) = e^{-|x|} = e^x \to 0$. ✓

- *As $x \to +\infty$:* If $x$ is not in any bump's support, $f(x) = e^{-x} \to
0$. If $x$ lies in the support of $\psi_n$, then $x \ge x_n - w_n \ge 2^{2^n} -
n^n \to \infty$, and
$$f(x) = e^{-x} + \psi_n(x) \le e^{-x} + h_n = e^{-x} + \frac{1}{n} \xrightarrow
{n\to\infty} 0.$$
Since every $x$ large enough falls into one of these two cases, $f(x)\to 0$. ✓

**3. $f^p$ is not integrable for any $p > 0$.**

On the support of $\psi_n$, we have $f(x) \ge \psi_n(x)$, so:
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)^p\,dx \;\ge\; \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} \int_{x_n - w_n
}^{x_n + w_n} \psi_n(x)^p\,dx \;=\; \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} h_n^p \cdot w_n \int_{-1
}^{1} \varphi(t)^p\,dt.$$

Set $C_p = \int_{-1}^{1}\varphi(t)^p\,dt$. Since $\varphi$ is continuous with $\
varphi(0)=1$, we have $\varphi(t) > 0$ on $(-1,1)$, so $C_p > 0$.

Substituting $h_n = 1/n$ and $w_n = n^n$:
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)^p\,dx \;\ge\; C_p \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} \frac{n^n}{
n^p} \;=\; C_p \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} n^{\,n-p}.$$

For any fixed $p > 0$, choose $N > p + 1$. Then for all $n \ge N$, the exponent
$n - p > 1$, so $n^{n-p} \ge n$. Therefore:
$$\sum_{n=2}^{\infty} n^{n-p} \;\ge\; \sum_{n=N}^{\infty} n \;=\; +\infty.$$

Hence $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)^p\,dx = +\infty$ for every $p > 0$. ✓

## Intuition

The function $f$ consists of a rapidly decaying baseline $e^{-|x|}$ (which alone
 would make every $f^p$ integrable) plus a sequence of bumps whose **heights** $
1/n$ tend to $0$ (ensuring $f\to 0$) but whose **widths** $n^n$ grow super-expon
entially. For any fixed power $p$, the term contributed by the $n$-th bump is $n
^{n-p}$, which diverges because the super-exponential width $n^n$ overwhelms the
 polynomial suppression $n^{-p}$ from the height. This works for *every* $p>0$ s
imultaneously because $n-p \to \infty$ regardless of how large $p$ is.
