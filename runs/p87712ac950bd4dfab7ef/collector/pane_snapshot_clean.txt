Proof: Multiplication of Infinite Cardinals in ZF without AC

Answer: Yes. The multiplication of infinite cardinals is well-defined in
Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory without the Axiom of Choice.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Setup and Definitions

We work throughout in ZF (Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory without the Axiom of
Choice).

Cardinals in ZF. Without AC, not every set is well-orderable, so the von Neumann
cardinal assignment (identifying a cardinal with its initial ordinal) does not
assign a cardinal to every set. However, there are two standard ways to define
cardinals in ZF:

  1. Scott's trick: For any set $A$, define its cardinal $|A|$ as the set of all
  sets equipotent to $A$ that have minimal rank among all such sets. This is a
  set (by the foundation axiom and separation) and is provably an equivalence-
  class representative for the relation "equipotent to $A$." Every set has a
  Scott cardinal in ZF.
  2. Alephs (well-ordered cardinals): For well-orderable sets, the cardinal is
  the unique initial ordinal (aleph) equipotent to the set.

Either way, every set has a well-defined cardinality in ZF.

Cardinal multiplication. For cardinals $\kappa = |A|$ and $\lambda = |B|$,
define: $$\kappa \cdot \lambda := |A \times B|.$$

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Step 1: The Cartesian Product $A \times B$ Exists in ZF

For any sets $A$ and $B$, the Cartesian product $A \times B = {(a, b) : a \in
A,, b \in B}$ is a set in ZF. Using the Kuratowski definition $(a, b) = {{a},
{a, b}}$, each ordered pair is an element of $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(A \cup
B))$. By the axioms of Pairing, Union, Power Set, and Separation: $$A \times B
\subseteq \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(A \cup B)),$$ so $A \times B$ is a set. No
choice is involved.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Step 2: The Definition is Well-Defined (Independent of Representatives)

Suppose $|A| = |A'|$ and $|B| = |B'|$, witnessed by bijections $f: A \to A'$ and
$g: B \to B'$. Define: $$h: A \times B \to A' \times B', \quad h(a, b) = (f(a),
g(b)).$$

  • Injectivity: If $h(a_1, b_1) = h(a_2, b_2)$, then $f(a_1) = f(a_2)$ and
  $g(b_1) = g(b_2)$. Since $f, g$ are injective, $a_1 = a_2$ and $b_1 = b_2$.
  • Surjectivity: For any $(a', b') \in A' \times B'$, there exist $a \in A, b
  \in B$ with $f(a) = a', g(b) = b'$ (since $f, g$ are surjective), so $h(a, b)
  = (a', b')$.

Thus $h$ is a bijection, proving $|A \times B| = |A' \times B'|$. This argument
uses only ZF — no choice is needed to compose two given bijections.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Step 3: The Product of Two Infinite Cardinals is a Well-Defined Cardinal

Since $A \times B$ is a set (Step 1), it has a well-defined cardinality $|A
\times B|$ in ZF (via Scott's trick or, for well-orderable $A, B$, via the aleph
assignment). By Step 2, this cardinality depends only on $|A|$ and $|B|$, not on
the particular representatives. Therefore:

$$\kappa \cdot \lambda := |A \times B|$$

is a well-defined cardinal for any cardinals $\kappa, \lambda$, including
infinite ones.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Step 4: For Alephs (Well-Ordered Cardinals), the Product is Also an Aleph

If $\kappa$ and $\lambda$ are alephs (i.e., $A$ and $B$ are well-orderable),
then $A \times B$ is well-orderable in ZF: the lexicographic order on $A \times
B$ is a well-ordering. (Proof: any non-empty $S \subseteq A \times B$ has a
least element — take the least first coordinate $a_0$ appearing in $S$, then the
least second coordinate $b_0$ among pairs in $S$ with first coordinate $a_0$;
then $(a_0, b_0)$ is the least element of $S$.)

Hence $|A \times B|$ is again an aleph, so the product of two alephs is an aleph
— all provable in ZF.

Moreover, for infinite alephs, ZF proves $\kappa \cdot \lambda = \max(\kappa,
\lambda)$:

  • $\max(\kappa, \lambda) \leq \kappa \cdot \lambda$ (since $\kappa, \lambda
  \geq 1$).
  • $\kappa \cdot \lambda \leq \mu \cdot \mu = \mu$ where $\mu = \max(\kappa,
  \lambda)$, using the ZF-provable fact that $\mu \cdot \mu = \mu$ for infinite
  well-ordered cardinals (established by transfinite induction on $\mu$, using
  the well-ordering of $\mu \times \mu$).

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Step 5: What AC is Actually Needed For

The Axiom of Choice is not needed to define cardinal multiplication. What AC
provides are additional properties:

  • Trichotomy/comparability: Without AC, not all cardinals are comparable ($
  \kappa \leq \lambda$ or $\lambda \leq \kappa$ may fail for non-well-orderable
  cardinals).
  • $\kappa \cdot \kappa = \kappa$ for all infinite $\kappa$: This holds for
  alephs in ZF, but may fail for non-well-orderable infinite cardinals without
  AC.
  • Every set is well-orderable: Without AC, some sets have no aleph cardinal,
  but they still have Scott cardinals, and multiplication is still defined.

None of these affect the definition of the multiplication operation itself.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Choice. For any two sets $A$ and $B$ (including infinite ones), the Cartesian
product $A \times B$ exists by the axioms of ZF, and the cardinal $|A \times B|$
is well-defined (via Scott's trick for arbitrary sets, or as an aleph for well-
orderable sets). The operation $\kappa \cdot \lambda = |A \times B|$ is well-
defined independent of the choice of representatives, as shown by the explicit
construction of a bijection from given bijections — all within ZF. The Axiom of
Choice is needed only for additional properties of cardinal arithmetic

the definition of multiplication itself.
