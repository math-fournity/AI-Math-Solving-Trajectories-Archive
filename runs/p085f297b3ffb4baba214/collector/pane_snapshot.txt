## Proof

**Answer: Yes. The discriminant of ω is also D.**

Let θ be a quadratic number with minimal polynomial $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c \in \m
athbb{Z}[x]$, where $a > 0$, $\gcd(a,b,c)=1$, and discriminant $D = b^2 - 4ac$.
Let $\sigma = \begin{pmatrix} p & q \\ r & s \end{pmatrix} \in GL_2(\mathbb{Z})$
, so $\delta := \det\sigma = ps - qr = \pm 1$. Define
$$\omega = \frac{p\theta + q}{r\theta + s}.$$

**Step 1: ω is a quadratic number (degree 2, irrational).**

From $\omega(r\theta + s) = p\theta + q$ we get $(p - r\omega)\theta = \omega s
- q$.
- If $p - r\omega \neq 0$, then $\theta = \frac{\omega s - q}{p - r\omega}$; if
$\omega \in \mathbb{Q}$ then $\theta \in \mathbb{Q}$, contradicting that θ is qu
adratic (irrational).
- If $p - r\omega = 0$, then $\omega s - q = 0$ too (else no solution), giving $
\omega = p/r$ and $ps = qr$, i.e. $\det\sigma = 0$, contradicting $\sigma \in GL
_2(\mathbb{Z})$.

Hence $\omega \notin \mathbb{Q}$, so $\omega$ has degree 2 over $\mathbb{Q}$.

**Step 2: Derive the polynomial satisfied by ω.**

Solving for θ: $\theta = \frac{s\omega - q}{p - r\omega}$. Substituting into $a\
theta^2 + b\theta + c = 0$ and multiplying by $(p - r\omega)^2$:
$$a(s\omega - q)^2 + b(s\omega - q)(p - r\omega) + c(p - r\omega)^2 = 0.$$

Expanding and collecting powers of ω gives $A\omega^2 + B\omega + C = 0$ where
$$A = as^2 - bsr + cr^2, \quad B = -2asq + b(sp+qr) - 2cpr, \quad C = aq^2 - bpq
 + cp^2.$$

**Step 3: Recognize this as a change of variables on the binary quadratic form.*
*

Homogenize: $f(x,y) = ax^2 + bxy + cy^2$ (so $f(\theta,1) = 0$ and $\operatornam
e{disc}(f) = b^2 - 4ac = D$). Setting $\omega = X/Y$ and $\theta = x/y$, the rel
ation $\theta = \frac{s\omega - q}{p - r\omega} = \frac{sX - qY}{pY - rX}$ gives
 the substitution
$$\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} s & -q \\ -
r & p \end{pmatrix}}_{M} \begin{pmatrix} X \\ Y \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det M = s
p - qr = \delta = \pm 1.$$

The transformed form $g(X,Y) = f(sX - qY,\; -rX + pY) = AX^2 + BXY + CY^2$ satis
fies $g(\omega, 1) = A\omega^2 + B\omega + C$.

**Step 4: Discriminant transformation.**

The symmetric matrix of $f$ is $F = \begin{pmatrix} a & b/2 \\ b/2 & c \end{pmat
rix}$, with $\operatorname{disc}(f) = -4\det F = b^2 - 4ac$. The matrix of $g$ i
s $G = M^T F M$, so
$$\operatorname{disc}(g) = -4\det G = -4(\det M)^2 \det F = (\det M)^2 \operator
name{disc}(f) = \delta^2 D.$$
Since $\delta = \pm 1$, $\delta^2 = 1$, hence
$$B^2 - 4AC = D.$$

**Step 5: $A\omega^2 + B\omega + C$ is the minimal polynomial of ω (up to sign o
f leading coefficient).**

- **$A \neq 0$:** $A = f(s, -r)$. Since $\det\sigma = \pm 1$, the pair $(s, -r)
\neq (0,0)$. If $f(s,-r) = 0$ with a nonzero integer argument, then $f$ represen
ts 0 nontrivially over $\mathbb{Q}$, which happens iff $\operatorname{disc}(f) =
 D$ is a perfect square — but then $\theta = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{D}}{2a} \in \mat
hbb{Q}$, contradicting θ being quadratic. So $A \neq 0$.

- **Primitivity ($\gcd(A,B,C) = 1$):** Suppose a prime $\ell$ divides $A, B, C$.
 Then $g \equiv 0 \pmod{\ell}$. Since $\det M = \pm 1$, $M$ is invertible over $
\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}$, so the substitution $(x,y) = M(X,Y)$ is invertible m
od $\ell$. Thus $f \equiv 0 \pmod{\ell}$, meaning $\ell \mid a, b, c$, contradic
ting $\gcd(a,b,c) = 1$. Hence $\gcd(A,B,C) = 1$.

Since $A\omega^2 + B\omega + C$ is a primitive degree-2 polynomial with rational
 (integer) coefficients having ω as a root, and ω has degree 2, this is (up to m
aking the leading coefficient positive) exactly the minimal polynomial of ω. The
 discriminant of the minimal polynomial is $B^2 - 4AC = D$ (changing the sign of
 all coefficients leaves $B^2 - 4AC$ invariant).

**Conclusion.** The discriminant of ω equals $D$. $\blacksquare$
