## Answer

**No.** Pointwise convergence of step functions to an $L^1$ limit does **not** i
n general imply convergence in the $L^1$ norm. A single explicit counterexample
suffices.

### Counterexample

Work on the measure space $([0,1], \mathcal{L}, m)$ with Lebesgue measure. Defin
e the step functions

$$
f_n(x) = n \cdot \mathbf{1}_{[0,\,1/n]}(x), \qquad n = 1, 2, \dots
$$

Each $f_n$ is a step function (constant on $[0,1/n]$ and on $(1/n,1]$).

**Pointwise limit.** For every $x \in (0,1]$, once $n > 1/x$ we have $1/n < x$,
so $x \notin [0,1/n]$ and $f_n(x) = 0$. Hence $f_n(x) \to 0$ for all $x \in (0,1
]$. At $x = 0$, $f_n(0) = n \to \infty$, but a single point has measure zero. Th
erefore $f_n \to 0$ a.e., and the pointwise-a.e. limit is

$$
f(x) = 0,
$$

which is certainly in $L^1([0,1])$ with $\|f\|_1 = 0$.

**$L^1$ norm of the difference.** Compute

$$
\|f_n - f\|_1 = \int_0^1 |f_n(x) - 0|\, dx = \int_0^1 n\,\mathbf{1}_{[0,1/n]}(x)
\, dx = n \cdot \frac{1}{n} = 1.
$$

So $\|f_n - f\|_1 = 1$ for every $n$, and consequently

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty} \|f_n - f\|_1 = 1 \neq 0.
$$

The sequence does **not** converge to $f$ in $L^1$.

### Justification / Remark

The obstruction is the lack of a uniform integrable dominating function. The mas
s of $f_n$ stays equal to $1$ at every step, but it concentrates onto the shrink
ing interval $[0,1/n]$; pointwise it "escapes to a point," while the $L^1$ norm
— which integrates absolute value — still sees the full mass. This is precisely
the situation the **Dominated Convergence Theorem** rules out: if one had $|f_n|
 \le g$ for some $g \in L^1$, then a.e. pointwise convergence *would* force $L^1
$ convergence. Without such domination (or monotonicity, or uniform integrabilit
y), pointwise convergence alone is too weak to control the integral, even when t
he limit is itself integrable.
