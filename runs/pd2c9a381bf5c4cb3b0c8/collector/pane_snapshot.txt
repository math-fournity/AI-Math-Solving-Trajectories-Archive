## Proof

We show that for every prime $p$, there exists $n \geq 1$ with $p \nmid u_n$. Th
is implies no prime divides all $u_n$, hence $\gcd(u_1, u_2, \ldots) = 1$.

Since $\gcd(a_0, a_1) = 1$, for any prime $p$, at least one of $a_0, a_1$ is not
 divisible by $p$. We split into two cases.

**Key lemma.** If $q$ is a prime with $q \mid m$, then $(q-1) \mid \varphi(m)$.
*Proof:* Write $m = q^a \cdot s$ with $\gcd(s, q) = 1$, $a \geq 1$. Then $\varph
i(m) = q^{a-1}(q-1)\varphi(s)$, so $(q-1) \mid \varphi(m)$. $\square$

**Case 1: $p = 2$.**

For $m \geq 3$, $\varphi(m)$ is even (since $m$ has an odd prime factor $q$ with
 $(q-1) \mid \varphi(m)$, or $m = 2^a$ with $a \geq 2$ giving $2 \mid \varphi(m)
$). Also $\varphi(2) = 1$.

- **If $a_0$ is odd:** Take $n = 2$. Then $\varphi(2) = 1$ and $\varphi(2+k)$ is
 even for all $k \geq 1$ (since $2+k \geq 3$). So $u_2 \equiv a_0 \cdot 1 \equiv
 1 \pmod{2}$.
- **If $a_0$ is even (hence $a_1$ is odd):** Take $n = 1$. Then $\varphi(2) = 1$
 and $\varphi(1+k)$ is even for all $k \geq 2$ (since $1+k \geq 3$). So $u_1 \eq
uiv a_0\varphi(1) + a_1 \cdot 1 \pmod{2}$. Since $a_0$ is even, $a_0\varphi(1) \
equiv 0$, giving $u_1 \equiv a_1 \equiv 1 \pmod{2}$.

In both subcases, $2 \nmid u_n$. $\checkmark$

**Case 2: $p \geq 3$.**

**Subcase 2a: $p \nmid a_0$.**

Choose distinct primes $q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_d$ with $q_k \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$, $q_
k > d$, and $q_k \neq p$ for all $k$. Such primes exist by Dirichlet's theorem (
infinitely many primes $\equiv 1 \pmod{p}$).

By the Chinese Remainder Theorem, find $r$ modulo $M = p \cdot q_1 \cdots q_d$ s
atisfying:
- $r \equiv -k \pmod{q_k}$ for $k = 1, \ldots, d$,
- $r \equiv 2 \pmod{p}$.

We verify $\gcd(r, M) = 1$: For each $q_k$, since $r \equiv -k \pmod{q_k}$ and $
1 \leq k \leq d < q_k$, we have $q_k \nmid r$. Since $r \equiv 2 \pmod{p}$ and $
p \geq 3$, we have $p \nmid r$. $\checkmark$

By Dirichlet's theorem, there are infinitely many primes $n \equiv r \pmod{M}$.
Take such $n$ with $n > \max(d, p)$. Then:

- $n$ is prime, so $\varphi(n) = n - 1 \equiv r - 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$, hence $p
 \nmid \varphi(n)$.
- For each $k = 1, \ldots, d$: $n + k \equiv r + k \equiv 0 \pmod{q_k}$, so $q_k
 \mid (n+k)$. By the lemma, $(q_k - 1) \mid \varphi(n+k)$, and since $p \mid (q_
k - 1)$, we get $p \mid \varphi(n+k)$.

Therefore:
$$u_n \equiv a_0 \varphi(n) + \sum_{k=1}^d a_k \varphi(n+k) \equiv a_0 \cdot 1 +
 0 \equiv a_0 \pmod{p}.$$
Since $p \nmid a_0$, we conclude $p \nmid u_n$. $\checkmark$

**Subcase 2b: $p \mid a_0$ (hence $p \nmid a_1$).**

Choose distinct primes $q_0, q_2, q_3, \ldots, q_d$ with $q_k \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$
, $q_k > d$, and $q_k \neq p$ for all $k \in \{0, 2, \ldots, d\}$.

We seek $n$ such that $n+1$ is prime with $p \nmid \varphi(n+1)$, and $p \mid \v
arphi(n+k)$ for $k \in \{0, 2, \ldots, d\}$.

Set $m = n + 1$. By CRT, find $r'$ modulo $M' = p \cdot q_0 \cdot q_2 \cdots q_d

- $r' \equiv 1 - k \pmod{q_k}$ for $k \in \{0, 2, \ldots, d\}$ (i.e., $r' \equiv
 1 \pmod{q_0}$, $r' \equiv -1 \pmod{q_2}$, $r' \equiv -2 \pmod{q_3}$, etc.),
- $r' \equiv 2 \pmod{p}$.

We verify $\gcd(r', M') = 1$: For $q_0$: $r' \equiv 1 \pmod{q_0}$, so $q_0 \nmid
 r'$. For $q_k$ with $k \geq 2$: $r' \equiv 1-k \pmod{q_k}$ and $|1-k| = k-1 < q
_k$ with $1-k \neq 0$, so $q_k \nmid r'$. Since $r' \equiv 2 \pmod{p}$ and $p \g
eq 3$, $p \nmid r'$. $\checkmark$

By Dirichlet's theorem, there are infinitely many primes $m \equiv r' \pmod{M'}$
. Take such $m$ large enough, and set $n = m - 1 \geq 1$. Then:

- $\varphi(n+1) = \varphi(m) = m - 1 \equiv r' - 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$, so $p \nm
id \varphi(n+1)$.
- For $k = 0$: $n = m - 1 \equiv r' - 1 \equiv 0 \pmod{q_0}$, so $q_0 \mid n$, h
ence $p \mid \varphi(n)$.
- For $k = 2, \ldots, d$: $n + k = m - 1 + k \equiv r' - 1 + k \equiv (1-k) - 1
+ k \equiv 0 \pmod{q_k}$, so $q_k \mid (n+k)$, hence $p \mid \varphi(n+k)$.

Therefore:
$$u_n \equiv a_0 \varphi(n) + a_1 \varphi(n+1) + \sum_{k=2}^d a_k \varphi(n+k) \
equiv 0 + a_1 \cdot 1 + 0 \equiv a_1 \pmod{p}.$$
Since $p \nmid a_1$, we conclude $p \nmid u_n$. $\checkmark$

**Conclusion.** For every prime $p$, we have exhibited $n \geq 1$ with $p \nmid
u_n$. Therefore no prime divides all $u_n$, which means $\gcd(u_1, u_2, u_3, \ld

acksquare$
