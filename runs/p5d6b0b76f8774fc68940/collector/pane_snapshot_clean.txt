**Proof.**

Let $M := \sup_{(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^2} |f_{xy}(s,t)|$, which is finite since the
second partial derivatives of $f$ are globally bounded. We claim $C = M$ satisfi
es the required inequality.

**Step 1.** Since $f(0,y) = 0$ for all $y$, differentiating with respect to $y$
gives
$$f_y(0, t) = 0 \quad \text{for all } t \in \mathbb{R}.$$

**Step 2.** Fix $(x,t) \in \mathbb{R}^2$. By the fundamental theorem of calculus
 applied to the function $s \mapsto f_y(s,t)$ on the segment from $0$ to $x$,
$$f_y(x,t) - f_y(0,t) = \int_0^x f_{xy}(s,t)\,ds.$$
Using Step 1,
$$f_y(x,t) = \int_0^x f_{xy}(s,t)\,ds.$$
Therefore
$$|f_y(x,t)| \le \int_0^{|x|} |f_{xy}(s,t)|\,ds \le M|x|.$$

**Step 3.** Since $f(x,0) = 0$ for all $x$, applying the fundamental theorem of
calculus to $t \mapsto f(x,t)$ on the segment from $0$ to $y$,
$$f(x,y) = \int_0^y f_y(x,t)\,dt.$$
Hence
$$|f(x,y)| \le \int_0^{|y|} |f_y(x,t)|\,dt \le \int_0^{|y|} M|x|\,dt = M|x||y|.$

**Conclusion.** Taking $C = M = \sup_{\mathbb{R}^2} |f_{xy}| > 0$ (well-defined
and finite by hypothesis), we have for every $(x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$,
$$|f(x,y)| \le C\,|x\,y|.$$

Thus such a constant $C > 0$ exists. $\blacksquare$
