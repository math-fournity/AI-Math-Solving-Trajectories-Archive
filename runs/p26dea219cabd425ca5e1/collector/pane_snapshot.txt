ynomial $P'$ (after some values are corrupted) still has $\beta$ as a root.

## Proof

**Setup.** Let $P(x) = g(x)(x - \beta)$ with $\beta \in \mathbb{F}_p$. We evalua
te at $n$ distinct nonzero points $x_1, \ldots, x_n \in \mathbb{F}_p$ to get $y_
i = P(x_i)$. Assume $n$ is sufficient to determine $P$ (i.e., $\deg P \leq n-1$)
, so interpolating the correct points recovers $P$ exactly, and $P(\beta) = 0$.

Some subset $S \subseteq \{1,\ldots,n\}$ of values are replaced by uniformly ran
dom $y'_i \in \mathbb{F}_p$. Let $\tilde{y}_i = y'_i$ if $i \in S$, and $\tilde{
y}_i = y_i$ if $i \notin S$.

**Lagrange interpolation.** The interpolated polynomial is:
$$P'(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \tilde{y}_i \, L_i(x), \quad \text{where } L_i(x) = \pr
od_{j \neq i} \frac{x - x_j}{x_i - x_j}.$$

**Evaluating at $\beta$.** Since interpolating the *original* (uncorrupted) poin
ts recovers $P$:
$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i \, L_i(\beta) = P(\beta) = 0.$$

Therefore:
$$P'(\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \tilde{y}_i \, L_i(\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i \, L
_i(\beta) + \sum_{i \in S} (y'_i - y_i)\, L_i(\beta) = \sum_{i \in S} (y'_i - y_
i)\, L_i(\beta).$$

**Key claim.** In the generic case (at least one changed index $i \in S$ has $L_
i(\beta) \neq 0$), the probability that $P'(\beta) = 0$ is exactly $\frac{1}{p}$
.

**Proof of claim.** The value $L_i(\beta) = \prod_{j \neq i} \frac{\beta - x_j}{
x_i - x_j}$ is a fixed nonzero constant for each $i$ (nonzero when $\beta \neq x
_j$ for all $j \neq i$, which is the generic situation when $\beta \notin \{x_1,
 \ldots, x_n\}$).

Pick any $i^* \in S$ with $L_{i^*}(\beta) \neq 0$. Then:
$$P'(\beta) = (y'_{i^*} - y_{i^*})\, L_{i^*}(\beta) + \underbrace{\sum_{i \in S
\setminus \{i^*\}} (y'_i - y_i)\, L_i(\beta)}_{C \text{ (fixed once other } y'_i
 \text{ are chosen)}}.$$

Since $L_{i^*}(\beta) \neq 0$, as $y'_{i^*}$ ranges uniformly over $\mathbb{F}_p
$, the expression $(y'_{i^*} - y_{i^*})\, L_{i^*}(\beta) + C$ takes each value i
n $\mathbb{F}_p$ exactly once. Hence:
$$\Pr\!\big[P'(\beta) = 0\big] = \frac{1}{p}.$$

This holds regardless of how many values are corrupted (as long as at least one
corrupted index has $L_i(\beta) \neq 0$), and regardless of the degree of $g$ or
 the specific choice of $x_i$ (in the generic case).

**Conclusion.** For a large prime $p$, the probability that $P'$ has $\beta$ as
a root is:

$$\boxed{\dfrac{1}{p}}$$
