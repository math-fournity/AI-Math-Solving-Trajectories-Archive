## Proof

We want to show $\displaystyle\int_0^1 x^x\,dx = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n+1}\,
n^{-n}$.

**Step 1: Series expansion of the integrand.**

For $x \in (0,1]$, write $x^x = e^{x\ln x}$. Using the Taylor series of the expo
nential,

$$x^x = e^{x\ln x} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(x\ln x)^n}{n!} = \sum_{n=0}^{\inf
ty}\frac{x^n(\ln x)^n}{n!}.$$

**Step 2: Justifying term-by-term integration.**

Consider $f(x) = x\ln x$ on $(0,1]$, extended by $f(0)=0$ (since $\lim_{x\to 0^+
}x\ln x = 0$). Then $f'(x) = \ln x + 1 = 0$ at $x = 1/e$, where $f(1/e) = -1/e$.
 Hence $|f(x)| \le 1/e$ on $[0,1]$. Therefore

$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{|x\ln x|^n}{n!} \le \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(1/e)^n}
{n!} = e^{1/e} < \infty,$$

uniformly on $[0,1]$. By the Weierstrass $M$-test, the series converges uniforml
y, so we may integrate term by term:

$$\int_0^1 x^x\,dx = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int_0^1 x^n(\ln x)^n\,dx.$$

**Step 3: Evaluating $\displaystyle\int_0^1 x^n(\ln x)^n\,dx$.**

For $a > -1$ and integer $k \ge 0$, differentiating the identity $\displaystyle\
int_0^1 x^a\,dx = \frac{1}{a+1}$ $k$ times with respect to $a$ gives

$$\int_0^1 x^a(\ln x)^k\,dx = \frac{(-1)^k\,k!}{(a+1)^{k+1}}.$$

(This is justified since $x^a(\ln x)^k$ is dominated by an integrable function n
ear $x=0$ and the differentiation under the integral is valid.) Setting $a = n$
and $k = n$:

$$\int_0^1 x^n(\ln x)^n\,dx = \frac{(-1)^n\,n!}{(n+1)^{n+1}}.$$

**Step 4: Assembling the series.**

Substituting back,

$$\int_0^1 x^x\,dx = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\cdot\frac{(-1)^n\,n!}{(n+1)
^{n+1}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{(n+1)^{n+1}}.$$

Re-indexing with $m = n+1$ (so $m$ runs from $1$ to $\infty$):

$$\int_0^1 x^x\,dx = \sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{m^m} = \sum_{m=1}^{\in
fty}(-1)^{m+1}\,m^{-m},$$

where the last equality uses $(-1)^{m-1} = (-1)^{m+1}$. This is precisely the de
sired series. $\blacksquare$
