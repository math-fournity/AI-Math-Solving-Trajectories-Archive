## Proof

We work under the path measure $Q$ induced by the SDE
$$dX_t = g(X_t,t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t, \qquad X_0 = x_0.$$
By definition of $Q$, the process $W_t$ is a standard $Q$-Brownian motion on $[0
,T]$, and $X_t$ is the (strong) solution of the above SDE driven by this $Q$-Bro
wnian motion. In particular, $X_t$ is adapted to the natural filtration $\mathca
l{F}_t = \sigma(W_s : 0\le s\le t)$ (equivalently $\sigma(X_s:0\le s\le t)$, sin
ce $X$ is a strong solution of an SDE driven solely by $W$).

Consider the process
$$M_T \;=\; \int_0^T \frac{f(X_t)-g(X_t,t)}{\sigma^2}\,dW_t.$$

**Step 1: The integrand is adapted.** Since $f$ is a (deterministic, bounded) fu
nction and $X_t$ is $\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable, $f(X_t)$ is $\mathcal{F}_t$-meas
urable. Likewise $g(X_t,t)$ is $\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable. Hence
$$H_t \;:=\; \frac{f(X_t)-g(X_t,t)}{\sigma^2}$$
is $\mathcal{F}_t$-adapted (and progressively measurable, being a continuous fun
ctional of the continuous process $X$).

**Step 2: Square-integrability.** $f$ is bounded, so $|f(X_t)|\le \|f\|_\infty$.
 Under the standard hypotheses guaranteeing existence/uniqueness of the SDE solu
tion (e.g. $g$ Lipschitz with linear growth), $X_t$ has finite moments of every
order on $[0,T]$, so $g(X_t,t)$ is square-integrable over $[0,T]$ under $Q$. Con
sequently
$$\mathbb{E}_Q\!\left[\int_0^T H_t^2\,dt\right] \;<\;\infty.$$

**Step 3: Itô integral is a martingale with zero mean.** With $H_t$ adapted and
square-integrable, the Itô integral
$$M_t \;=\; \int_0^t H_s\,dW_s, \qquad 0\le t\le T,$$
is a (true) $Q$-martingale with $M_0 = 0$. The defining property of a martingale
 gives
$$\mathbb{E}_Q[M_t] \;=\; \mathbb{E}_Q[M_0] \;=\; 0 \qquad\text{for all } t\in[0
,T].$$
In particular,
$$\left\langle \int_0^T \frac{f(X_t)-g(X_t,t)}{\sigma^2}\,dW_t \right\rangle_Q \
;=\; \mathbb{E}_Q[M_T] \;=\; 0.$$

**Step 4: Why it is not merely "constant" but specifically zero.** A general Itô
 integral $\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t$ with adapted, square-integrable $H$ always has ex
pectation equal to its initial value $0$; it is not merely some unspecified cons
tant but exactly $0$. The only way a nonzero constant could arise would be if th
e integrand had a singular/non-adapted component or if $W$ were not a $Q$-martin
gale — neither holds here, since $W$ is by construction a $Q$-Brownian motion an
d $H_t$ is $\mathcal{F}_t$-adapted. The boundedness of $f$ and the standard assu
mptions on $g$ ensure the integral is a true (not merely local) martingale, ruli
ng out any strict-local-martingale subtlety that could produce a nonzero "consta
nt" expectation.

Therefore the expectation is **zero** (and hence trivially also constant, with t
he constant being $0$).

### PROOF COMPLETE
