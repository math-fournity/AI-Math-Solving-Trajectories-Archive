Proof: $\ell^2$ and $L^2$ are Isometrically Isomorphic

Setup and Definitions

Let $\ell^2$ denote the space of all square-summable sequences $x = (x_1, x_2,
\dots)$ with $$|x|{\ell^2} = \left(\sum{n=1}^\infty |x_n|^2\right)^{1/2} <
\infty,$$ equipped with the inner product $\langle x, y\rangle = \sum_{n=1}
^\infty x_n \overline{y_n}$.

Let $L^2$ denote the space of (equivalence classes of) square-integrable
functions on a $\sigma$-finite measure space (the standard cases being
$L^2[0,1]$ or $L^2(\mathbb{R})$), with $$|f|_{L^2} = \left(\int |f|^2,
d\mu\right)^{1/2} < \infty,$$ equipped with the inner product $\langle f,
g\rangle = \int f,\overline{g}, d\mu$.

Both are Hilbert spaces (complete inner product spaces). We prove they are
isometrically isomorphic — i.e., there exists a linear bijection $U: \ell^2 \to
L^2$ with $|Ux|{L^2} = |x|{\ell^2}$ for all $x \in \ell^2$.

Key Theorem

Theorem. Any two separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces are isometrically
isomorphic.

Proof of the theorem. Let $H_1, H_2$ be separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert
spaces. By the Gram–Schmidt process applied to any countable dense subset, each
admits a countable orthonormal basis (ONB): ${e_n}{n=1}^\infty$ for $H_1$ and
${f_n}{n=1}^\infty$ for $H_2$.

Define $U: H_1 \to H_2$ by $$U!\left(\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n e_n\right) =
\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n f_n.$$

  • Well-defined: For $x = \sum a_n e_n \in H_1$, Parseval's identity gives $
  \sum |a_n|^2 = |x|^2 < \infty$. Since ${f_n}$ is an ONB of $H_2$, the series $
  \sum a_n f_n$ converges in $H_2$ (by the Riesz–Fischer theorem), so $Ux \in
  H_2$.
  • Linear: Immediate from linearity of the coefficient map.
  • Isometric: By Parseval's identity applied in both spaces, $$|Ux|{H_2}^2 =
  \sum{n=1}^\infty |a_n|^2 = |x|{H_1}^2.$$ Hence $|Ux|{H_2} = |x|_{H_1}$.
  • Surjective: For any $y = \sum b_n f_n \in H_2$, we have $\sum |b_n|^2 = |y|
  ^2 < \infty$, so $x = \sum b_n e_n \in H_1$ and $Ux = y$.
  • Injective: $|Ux| = 0 \implies |x| = 0 \implies x = 0$.

Thus $U$ is a linear bijective isometry, i.e., an isometric isomorphism. $
\square$

Applying the Theorem

We verify that $\ell^2$ and $L^2$ are both separable and infinite-dimensional
Hilbert spaces.

$\ell^2$ is separable and infinite-dimensional

  • Infinite-dimensional: The standard unit vectors $e^{(k)} = (0,\dots,0,1,0,
  \dots)$ (with $1$ in position $k$) are countably many linearly independent
  elements.
  • Separable: The set of sequences with rational (or rational-complex) entries
  that are eventually zero is countable and dense in $\ell^2$.
  • Hilbert space: $\ell^2$ is complete (a standard result: every absolutely
  summable series in $\ell^2$ converges).

$L^2$ is separable and infinite-dimensional

We treat the two standard cases:

Case $L^2[0,1]$:

  • Infinite-dimensional: The monomials ${1, x, x^2, \dots}$ are linearly
  independent and lie in $L^2[0,1]$.
  • Separable: The trigonometric system ${e^{2\pi i n x}}_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}$ is
  a countable orthonormal basis (by the Stone–Weierstrass theorem and density of
  continuous functions), so $L^2[0,1]$ is separable. Equivalently, step
  functions with rational values on rational-partition intervals are countable
  and dense.
  • Hilbert space: $L^2[0,1]$ is complete (Riesz–Fischer theorem).

Case $L^2(\mathbb{R})$:

  • Infinite-dimensional: The Hermite functions $h_n(x) = H_n(x)e^{-x^2/2}$
  (with $H_n$ the Hermite polynomials) are countably many linearly independent
  elements.
  • Separable: The Hermite functions form a countable orthonormal basis of
  $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ (a classical result), so $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ is separable.
  Alternatively, step functions with rational coefficients on intervals with
  rational endpoints are countable and dense.
  • Hilbert space: $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ is complete (Riesz–Fischer theorem).

Conclusion

Since $\ell^2$ and $L^2$ are both separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces,
the Theorem applies: there exists a linear bijection $U: \ell^2 \to L^2$
preserving the norm (and, by the polarization identity, preserving the inner
product — i.e., $U$ is a unitary).

Explicit construction (for $L^2[0,1]$): Take the Fourier ONB ${e^{2\pi i n x}}{n
\in \mathbb{Z}}$ of $L^2[0,1]$ and the standard ONB ${\delta^{(k)}}{k=1}^\infty$
of $\ell^2$. Enumerate $\mathbb{Z}$ as ${n_1, n_2, \dots}$ and define $$U!
\left(\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k, \delta^{(k)}\right) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k,
e^{2\pi i n_k x}.$$ By Parseval's identity on both sides, $|Ux|{L^2} = |x|
{\ell^2}$, and $U$ is a surjective linear isometry.

Therefore, the space of infinite sequences with the Euclidean-type norm ($
\ell^2$) and the space of square-integrable functions ($L^2$) are isometrically
isomorphic.
