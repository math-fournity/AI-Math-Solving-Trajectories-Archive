Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether a sphere $S$ passing throug
h a set of points, with its center in the convex hull of those points, must nece
ssarily be the unique circumsphere.

**Answer: No, $S$ need not be the circumsphere.**

## Proof

### Key Observation

Two distinct spheres in $\mathbb{R}^d$ can share the same set of points only if
those points all lie on a common hyperplane. Specifically, if spheres $S$ (cente
r $c$, radius $r$) and $S'$ (center $c'$, radius $r'$) both pass through all poi
nts $p_i$, then subtracting their equations $\|p_i - c\|^2 = r^2$ and $\|p_i - c
'\|^2 = r'^2$ yields:

$$(c - c') \cdot p_i = \frac{(\|c\|^2 - r^2) - (\|c'\|^2 - r'^2)}{2} \quad \text
{for all } i.$$

If $c \neq c'$, all $p_i$ lie on the hyperplane $H = \{x : (c-c') \cdot x = \tex
t{const}\}$.

The condition $c \in \operatorname{conv}\{p_i\}$ forces $c \in H$ (since $\opera
torname{conv}\{p_i\} \subset H$), but it does **not** prevent the points from be
ing coplanar in the first place. When the points are coplanar and $c \in H$, the
 sphere $S$ intersects $H$ in a $(d{-}2)$-sphere, and infinitely many other sphe
res (obtained by shifting the center along the normal to $H$) also pass through
all the points.

### Explicit Counterexample

Take $d = 3$, $n = 4$, with:
$$p_1 = (1,0,0),\quad p_2 = (-1,0,0),\quad p_3 = (0,1,0),\quad p_4 = (0,-1,0).$$

Let $S$ be the unit sphere centered at the origin: $S = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^3 : \
|x\| = 1\}$.

**All points lie on $S$:** Each $p_i$ has $\|p_i\| = 1$. ✓

**Center is in the convex hull:** The origin equals $\frac{1}{4}(p_1 + p_2 + p_3
 + p_4)$, a convex combination with all weights $= 1/4 \geq 0$ summing to $1$. ✓

**$S$ is not the unique sphere through the points:** Define $S'$ centered at $c'
 = (0,0,1)$ with radius $r' = \sqrt{2}$:
$$S' = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^3 : \|x - (0,0,1)\| = \sqrt{2}\}.$$

Verification that all $p_i$ lie on $S'$:
- $\|p_1 - (0,0,1)\|^2 = \|(1,0,-1)\|^2 = 1 + 0 + 1 = 2 = (r')^2$ ✓
- $\|p_2 - (0,0,1)\|^2 = \|(-1,0,-1)\|^2 = 1 + 0 + 1 = 2$ ✓
- $\|p_3 - (0,0,1)\|^2 = \|(0,1,-1)\|^2 = 0 + 1 + 1 = 2$ ✓
- $\|p_4 - (0,0,1)\|^2 = \|(0,-1,-1)\|^2 = 0 + 1 + 1 = 2$ ✓

Since $S \neq S'$ (different centers and radii) yet both pass through all four p
oints, $S$ is **not** the unique sphere through the points. Therefore $S$ is not
 the circumsphere.

### Why the Condition Fails to Guarantee Uniqueness

The four points are coplanar (all lie in the $xy$-plane $H = \{z=0\}$). The cent
er of $S$ (the origin) lies in $H$, consistent with $c \in \operatorname{conv}\{
p_i\} \subset H$. The intersection $S \cap H$ is the unit circle in the $xy$-pla
ne. Any sphere whose center lies on the $z$-axis at $(0,0,t)$ with radius $\sqrt
{1+t^2}$ intersects $H$ in this same unit circle, hence passes through all four
points. There are infinitely many such spheres (one for each $t \in \mathbb{R}$)
, so the circumsphere is not uniquely determined.

The condition $c \in \operatorname{conv}\{p_1, \ldots, p_n\}$ constrains the cen
ter to lie in the affine span of the points, but it does not require the points
to affinely span $\mathbb{R}^d$. Uniqueness of the circumsphere requires exactly
 this: the points must not lie in any hyperplane. The convex-hull condition is o
rthogonal to this requirement.

### Conclusion

$S$ need not be the circumsphere of the points. The condition that the center of
 $S$ lies in the convex hull of the points is insufficient to guarantee that $S$
 is the unique sphere passing through all points.
