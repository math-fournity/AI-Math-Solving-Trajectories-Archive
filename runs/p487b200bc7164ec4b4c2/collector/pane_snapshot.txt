## Proof

**Claim:** The abelian hypothesis is **not necessary**. Any group with a totally
 ordered subgroup lattice is automatically abelian, and consequently is isomorph

\}$ (assuming $G$ is nontrivial).

### Step 1: A totally ordered subgroup lattice forces $G$ to be abelian.

Let $G$ be a group whose subgroup lattice is totally ordered (i.e., for any subg
roups $H, K \leq G$, either $H \subseteq K$ or $K \subseteq H$).

Take any two elements $x, y \in G$. The cyclic subgroups $\langle x \rangle$ and
 $\langle y \rangle$ are subgroups of $G$, so by total ordering either $\langle
x \rangle \subseteq \langle y \rangle$ or $\langle y \rangle \subseteq \langle x
 \rangle$.

- **Case 1:** $\langle x \rangle \subseteq \langle y \rangle$. Then $x \in \lang
le y \rangle$, so $x = y^m$ for some integer $m$. Since $x$ and $y$ both lie in
the cyclic (hence abelian) group $\langle y \rangle$, they commute: $xy = yx$.

- **Case 2:** $\langle y \rangle \subseteq \langle x \rangle$. Then $y \in \lang
le x \rangle$, so $y = x^n$ for some integer $n$. Since both lie in the cyclic g
roup $\langle x \rangle$, they commute: $xy = yx$.

In either case $xy = yx$. Since $x, y$ were arbitrary, **$G$ is abelian**. $\squ
are$

This immediately shows the abelian hypothesis is redundant — it is a *consequenc
e* of the totally ordered subgroup lattice condition, not an independent assumpt
ion.

### Step 2: $G$ is a $p$-group for some prime $p$ (or trivial).

Suppose $G$ is nontrivial. If $G$ had elements $x, y$ of orders $p^a$ and $q^b$
respectively, where $p \neq q$ are primes, then $|\langle x \rangle| = p^a$ and
$|\langle y \rangle| = q^b$. By Lagrange's theorem, $\langle x \rangle \subseteq
 \langle y \rangle$ would require $p^a \mid q^b$ (impossible since $p \neq q$),
and similarly $\langle y \rangle \subseteq \langle x \rangle$ is impossible. Thi
s contradicts total ordering. So all elements have order a power of a single pri
me $p$; $G$ is a $p$-group.

Similarly, $G$ cannot have both an element of infinite order and an element of f
inite order: if $x$ has infinite order and $y$ has finite order, then $\langle x
 \rangle$ is infinite and $\langle y \rangle$ is finite, so neither can contain
the other, contradicting total ordering.

### Step 3: $G$ cannot be torsion-free (unless trivial).

If $G$ is torsion-free and nontrivial, pick $x \neq e$. Then $\langle x \rangle
\cong \mathbb{Z}$. For any $y \in G$, either $\langle y \rangle \subseteq \langl
e x \rangle$ (so $y \in \langle x \rangle$) or $\langle x \rangle \subseteq \lan
gle y \rangle$ (so $x \in \langle y \rangle$, and $\langle y \rangle$ is also in
finite cyclic). In either case, $x$ and $y$ are rationally dependent, so $G$ has
 rank $1$ as an abelian group, hence $G$ embeds into $\mathbb{Q}$.

But no nontrivial subgroup of $\mathbb{Q}$ has a totally ordered subgroup lattic
e:
- If $G \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$, then $G \cong n\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}$ for s
ome $n \geq 1$, and $\mathbb{Z}$ has incomparable subgroups $2\mathbb{Z}$ and $3
\mathbb{Z}$.
- If $G$ contains a non-integer $a/b$ (in lowest terms, $b > 1$), then $G$ conta
ins $1/p$ for some prime $p \mid b$ (since $G$ is a group). With $\mathbb{Z} \su
bseteq G$ and $1/p \in G$, the elements $2/p$ and $3/p$ (choosing primes $2, 3 \
neq p$, or adjusting) both lie in $G$, but $\langle 2/p \rangle$ and $\langle 3/
p \rangle$ are incomparable: $(2/p)/(3/p) = 2/3 \notin \mathbb{Z}$ and $(3/p)/(2
/p) = 3/2 \notin \mathbb{Z}$.

This contradiction shows $G$ must be a torsion group, i.e., a $p$-group.

### Step 4: If $G$ is finite, then $G \cong \mathbb{Z}/p^k\mathbb{Z}$.

A finite abelian $p$-group decomposes as $\mathbb{Z}/p^{a_1}\mathbb{Z} \times \c
dots \times \mathbb{Z}/p^{a_r}\mathbb{Z}$ with $a_1 \geq \cdots \geq a_r \geq 1$
. If $r \geq 2$, then each factor contributes a distinct subgroup of order $p$,
giving at least two incomparable subgroups of order $p$. This contradicts total
ordering, so $r = 1$ and $G$ is cyclic of order $p^k$.

### Step 5: If $G$ is infinite, then $G \cong \mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$.

$G$ is an infinite abelian $p$-group with totally ordered subgroup lattice. Ever
y finitely generated subgroup is finite (being a finitely generated abelian $p$-
group, hence finite) and has a totally ordered subgroup lattice, so by Step 4 ea
ch is cyclic of order $p^n$ for some $n$.

For each $n \geq 1$, $G$ has at most one subgroup of order $p^n$ (by total order
ing). Since $G$ is infinite, it has elements of arbitrarily large order (otherwi
se $G$ would be a finite union of finite sets). So for each $n$, $G$ has a uniqu
e subgroup $G_n$ of order $p^n$, with $G_1 \subset G_2 \subset \cdots$, and $G =
 \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} G_n$.

Each $G_n \cong \mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ with the natural inclusions $G_n \hook
rightarrow G_{n+1}$, so:
$$G \cong \varinjlim \left( \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Z}/p^
2\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \cdots \right) = \mathbb{Z}(p^\infty).$$

### Conclusion

We have shown that **any** group $G$ with a totally ordered subgroup lattice is
automatically abelian (Step 1), and (if nontrivial) is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}
(p^k)$ for some prime $p$ and $k \in \{1,2,\ldots\} \cup \{\infty\}$ (Steps 2–5)
. The abelian hypothesis is therefore **not necessary** — it is a logical conseq
uence of the totally ordered subgroup lattice condition, not an independent assu
mption that needs to be imposed.
