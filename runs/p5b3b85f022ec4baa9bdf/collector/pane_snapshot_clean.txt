## Solution

We seek $\operatorname{Res}_{z=0} f(z)$ where $f(z) = e^{e^{1/z}}$, i.e. the coe
fficient of $z^{-1}$ in the Laurent expansion of $f$ about $z=0$.

**Substitution.** Let $w = 1/z$, so $z^{-1} = w$. Then

$$f(z) = e^{e^{1/z}} = e^{e^{w}}.$$

The function $g(w) = e^{e^{w}}$ is entire (a composition of entire functions), s
o it admits a Taylor expansion valid for all $w \in \mathbb{C}$:

$$g(w) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n w^n, \qquad a_n = \frac{1}{n!}\left.\frac{d^{n}
}{dw^{n}} e^{e^{w}}\right|_{w=0}.$$

**Rewriting in $z$.** Substituting $w = 1/z$,

$$f(z) = e^{e^{1/z}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n\, z^{-n}.$$

This is precisely the Laurent expansion of $f$ about $z = 0$. The residue is the
 coefficient of $z^{-1}$, namely $a_1$.

**Computing $a_1$.**

$$a_1 = \left.\frac{d}{dw} e^{e^{w}}\right|_{w=0} = \left. e^{e^{w}} \cdot e^{w}
\right|_{w=0} = e^{e^{0}} \cdot e^{0} = e^{1}\cdot 1 = e.$$

**Verification via Bell numbers.** Writing $e^{e^{w}} = e\cdot e^{e^{w}-1}$ and
using the classical identity $e^{e^{w}-1} = \sum_{n\ge 0} B_n \frac{w^n}{n!}$ (w
here $B_n$ are the Bell numbers), we get $a_n = e\,B_n/n!$. Since $B_1 = 1$, thi
s gives $a_1 = e\cdot 1/1! = e$, confirming the direct computation.

**Conclusion.**

$$\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_{z=0}\, e^{e^{1/z}} = e}$$
