Problem. If $G$ is a group and $H \triangleleft G$, show that if $a \in G$ has
finite order $o(a)$, then $Ha$ in $G/H$ has finite order $m$, where $m \mid
o(a)$.

Proof.

Let $n = o(a)$, so $a^n = e$ (the identity of $G$), and $n$ is the least
positive integer with this property.

Since $H \triangleleft G$, the quotient group $G/H$ is well-defined, with
identity coset $He = H$ and group operation $(Hx)(Hy) = Hxy$.

Consider the coset $Ha \in G/H$. Compute its $n$-th power in the quotient:

$$(Ha)^n = \underbrace{(Ha)(Ha)\cdots(Ha)}_{n \text{ factors}} = H a^n = He = H.
$$

So $(Ha)^n = H$, the identity of $G/H$. This shows that $Ha$ has finite order in
$G/H$; let $m = o(Ha)$ be the order of $Ha$ in $G/H$.

By definition, $m$ is the least positive integer such that $(Ha)^m = H$, i.e.,
$Ha^m = H$.

Now we use the division algorithm: write $n = qm + r$ with $0 \le r < m$. Then

$$(Ha)^n = (Ha)^{qm+r} = \bigl((Ha)^m\bigr)^q (Ha)^r = H^q (Ha)^r = (Ha)^r.$$

But $(Ha)^n = H$, so $(Ha)^r = H$. Since $m$ is the least positive integer with
$(Ha)^m = H$, and $0 \le r < m$, we must have $r = 0$ (otherwise $r$ would be a
smaller positive exponent giving the identity, contradicting the minimality of
$m$).

Therefore $n = qm$, i.e., $m \mid n = o(a)$. $\blacksquare$
