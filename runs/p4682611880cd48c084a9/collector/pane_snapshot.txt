## Proof

We determine convergence based on the sign of $p$.

### Case 1: $p < 0$ — Converges if and only if $p < -1$

When $p < 0$, we have $n^p \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$. Since $\sin(x)/x \to 1$ as
$x \to 0$, we get
$$\frac{\sin(n^p)}{n^p} \to 1 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$
For sufficiently large $n$, $n^p \in (0, \pi/2)$, so both $\sin(n^p)$ and $n^p$
are positive. By the **limit comparison test**, $\sum \sin(n^p)$ converges if an
d only if $\sum n^p$ converges. The $p$-series $\sum n^p$ converges if and only
if $p < -1$.

Therefore, for $p < 0$: the series **converges** $\iff$ $p < -1$.

### Case 2: $p = 0$ — Diverges

For $p = 0$, $n^0 = 1$ for all $n \geq 1$, so $\sin(n^0) = \sin(1) \neq 0$. The
terms do not tend to zero, so the series **diverges**.

### Case 3: $p > 0$ — Diverges

We show that $\sin(n^p) \not\to 0$ for every $p > 0$, which implies divergence.

**Key tool — Equidistribution.** We use the following classical results:

- **Weyl's theorem (1916):** If $P(x)$ is a polynomial with at least one non-con
stant irrational coefficient, then $\{P(n)\}$ is equidistributed mod $1$.

- **Fejér's theorem:** If $f:[1,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}$ is differentiable with $f'
(x)$ eventually monotone, $f'(x)\to 0$, and $x|f'(x)|\to\infty$, then $\{f(n)\}$
 is equidistributed mod $1$.

- **van der Corput's difference theorem:** If for every $h\geq 1$ the sequence $
\{x_{n+h}-x_n\}$ is equidistributed mod $1$, then $\{x_n\}$ is equidistributed m
od $1$.

**Claim:** For every $p > 0$, the sequence $\left\{\dfrac{n^p}{2\pi}\right\}$ is
 equidistributed mod $1$.

*Proof of Claim.* Set $\alpha = \frac{1}{2\pi}$, which is irrational, and $f(n)
= \alpha n^p$.

**Subcase (a): $p$ is a positive integer.** Then $f(x) = \alpha x^p$ is a polyno
mial in $x$ with leading coefficient $\alpha$ irrational. By Weyl's theorem, $\{
f(n)\}$ is equidistributed mod $1$. ✓

**Subcase (b): $0 < p < 1$.** Apply Fejér's theorem to $f(x) = \alpha x^p$:
- $f'(x) = \alpha p\, x^{p-1} \to 0$ since $p-1<0$. ✓
- $x|f'(x)| = |\alpha|\,p\,x^p \to \infty$. ✓
- $f'(x)$ is monotone for large $x$. ✓

So $\{f(n)\}$ is equidistributed mod $1$. ✓

**Subcase (c): $p > 1$, $p$ not an integer.** Let $k = \lfloor p \rfloor \geq 1$
, so $0 < p - k < 1$. We apply van der Corput's difference theorem iteratively $
k$ times.

The $k$-th order finite difference of $f$ at step sizes $h_1, \ldots, h_k \geq 1
$ is:
$$\Delta^k_{h_1,\ldots,h_k} f(n) = \alpha \sum_{S \subseteq \{1,\ldots,k\}} (-1)
^{k-|S|} \left(n + \sum_{i \in S} h_i\right)^p.$$

By Taylor expansion, as $n \to \infty$:
$$\Delta^k_{h_1,\ldots,h_k} f(n) = \alpha \cdot p(p-1)\cdots(p-k+1) \cdot h_1 h_
2 \cdots h_k \cdot n^{p-k} + O(n^{p-k-1}).$$

Since $p$ is not an integer, none of $p, p-1, \ldots, p-k+1$ equals zero (as $p
- k + 1 > 1 > 0$ and $p - j > 0$ for $j \leq k-1$). Set:
$$C = \alpha \cdot p(p-1)\cdots(p-k+1) \cdot h_1 \cdots h_k.$$
Since $\alpha$ is irrational and $p(p-1)\cdots(p-k+1) \cdot h_1\cdots h_k$ is a
nonzero real, $C$ is **irrational**.

Define $g(x) = \Delta^k_{h_1,\ldots,h_k} f(x)$. Then $g(x) = C\, x^{p-k} + O(x^{
p-k-1})$ with $0 < p-k < 1$ and $C$ irrational. We verify Fejér's conditions for
 $g$:
- $g'(x) = C(p-k)\,x^{p-k-1} + O(x^{p-k-2}) \to 0$ since $p-k-1 < 0$. ✓
- $x|g'(x)| \sim |C|(p-k)\,x^{p-k} \to \infty$ since $p-k > 0$. ✓
- $g'(x)$ is eventually monotone. ✓

By Fejér's theorem, $\{g(n)\} = \{\Delta^k f(n)\}$ is equidistributed mod $1$ fo
r every choice of $h_1, \ldots, h_k \geq 1$.

Now we lift back via van der Corput. At the $k$-th level, equidistribution of $\
{\Delta^k f(n)\}$ for all step sizes implies (by van der Corput) equidistributio
n of $\{\Delta^{k-1} f(n)\}$ for all step sizes. Repeating $k$ times, we conclud
e $\{f(n)\} = \{\alpha n^p\}$ is equidistributed mod $1$. ✓

This completes the proof of the Claim. $\square$

**Consequence:** Since $\left\{\frac{n^p}{2\pi}\right\}$ is equidistributed mod
$1$, the values $\frac{n^p}{2\pi} \pmod{1}$ are dense in $[0,1)$. Therefore:
$$\sin(n^p) = \sin\!\left(2\pi \cdot \left\{\tfrac{n^p}{2\pi}\right\}\right)$$
takes values dense in $[-1, 1]$. In particular, $\sin(n^p) \not\to 0$, so the se
ries **diverges** by the divergence test.

### Conclusion

| Range of $p$ | Behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| $p < -1$ | **Converges** | $\sin(n^p) \sim n^p$, $\sum n^p$ converges |
| $p = -1$ | Diverges | $\sin(1/n) \sim 1/n$, harmonic series |
| $-1 < p < 0$ | Diverges | $\sin(n^p) \sim n^p$, $\sum n^p$ diverges |
| $p = 0$ | Diverges | $\sin(1) \neq 0$, terms don't vanish |
| $p > 0$ | Diverges | Equidistribution $\Rightarrow$ $\sin(n^p) \not\to 0$ |

The series $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty \sin(n^p)$ converges **if and only if
 $p < -1$**.
