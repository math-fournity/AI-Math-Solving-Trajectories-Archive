## Proof

**Setup.** Fix a "previous arrangement" of the $n$ guests around a round table.
We count the number of distinct circular arrangements in which **at least one**
guest has a different pair of neighbors than they had in this reference arrangem
ent.

**Counting total circular arrangements.** Two arrangements related by a rotation
 of the table are identified, so the total number of distinct circular arrangeme
nts of $n$ guests is
$$T = (n-1)!\,.$$

**Counting the complement.** We count arrangements in which **every** guest has
the *same* (unordered) pair of neighbors as in the reference arrangement.

In the reference arrangement, each guest $g$ has two neighbors; call this unorde
red pair $N(g)$. Suppose a new arrangement also gives every guest $g$ the pair $
N(g)$. Pick any guest $g_0$; one of its two neighbors, say $g_1$, sits to its ri
ght in the reference arrangement. In the new arrangement $g_1$ must sit either t
o the right or to the left of $g_0$:

- If $g_1$ sits to the **right** of $g_0$, then $g_1$'s other neighbor must be t
he same as in the reference arrangement, forcing the next guest, and so on. Indu
ctively the entire sequence reproduces the reference arrangement.
- If $g_1$ sits to the **left** of $g_0$, the same inductive argument reproduces
 the **reversal** (mirror image) of the reference arrangement.

Thus exactly **two** circular arrangements preserve every guest's neighbor set:
the reference arrangement and its reversal. These two are distinct precisely whe
n $n > 2$ (for $n=2$ the two guests are always mutual neighbors; for $n>2$ no ro
tation turns an arrangement into its reversal).

**Applying inclusion–exclusion (complement).** The number of arrangements where
at least one guest has different neighbors is the total minus the complement:

$$T - 2 = (n-1)! - 2.$$

**Verification for small cases.**

- $n=3$: $(3-1)!-2 = 0$. With three people every guest is adjacent to both other
s in every arrangement, so no arrangement can change anyone's neighbors. ✓
- $n=4$: $(4-1)!-2 = 4$. Labeling guests $1,2,3,4$ with reference $1\text{-}2\te
xt{-}3\text{-}4$, the six circular arrangements (fixing guest 1) are $1234, 1243
, 1324, 1342, 1423, 1432$. Only $1234$ (the reference) and $1432$ (its reversal)
 preserve all neighbor sets; the remaining four each alter at least one guest's
neighbors. ✓

**Conclusion.** The number of arrangements of $n$ guests ($n>2$) around a round
table in which at least one guest has different neighbors than in a given previo
us arrangement is

$$\boxed{(n-1)! - 2}.$$
