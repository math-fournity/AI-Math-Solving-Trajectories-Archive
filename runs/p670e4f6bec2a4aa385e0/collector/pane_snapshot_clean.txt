## Solution

Let $f(n) = n!$ denote the factorial operation. Then:

- $A = f^{99}(100)$ — factorial applied 99 times to 100
- $B = f^{100}(99)$ — factorial applied 100 times to 99

**Key step: reduce both to the same number of remaining factorial applications.*
*

Peel off one application of $f$ from $A$:

$$A = f^{99}(100) = f^{98}\!\big(f(100)\big) = f^{98}(100!)$$

Peel off two applications of $f$ from $B$:

$$B = f^{100}(99) = f^{98}\!\big(f^{2}(99)\big) = f^{98}\!\big((99!)!\big)$$

Now both $A$ and $B$ are $f^{98}$ applied to some argument. It remains to compar
e the arguments:

$$A = f^{98}(100!), \qquad B = f^{98}\!\big((99!)!\big)$$

**Compare $100!$ and $(99!)!$:**

$$100! = 100 \times 99!$$

while

$$(99!)! = \text{factorial of } 99!$$

Since $99!$ is astronomically larger than $100$ (indeed $99! \gg 100$), and the
factorial function is strictly increasing for all $n \geq 1$, we have:

$$99! > 100 \implies (99!)! > 100!$$

**Preserve the inequality through $f^{98}$:**

The factorial function $f(n) = n!$ is strictly increasing for $n \geq 1$. Theref
ore its 98-fold iterate $f^{98}$ is also strictly increasing on arguments $\geq
1$. Since both $100! \geq 1$ and $(99!)! \geq 1$, the inequality is preserved:

$$(99!)! > 100! \implies f^{98}\!\big((99!)!\big) > f^{98}(100!)$$

$$\implies B > A$$

**Conclusion:** $B$ is greater than $A$. Applying factorial 100 times to 99 yiel
ds a larger number than applying factorial 99 times to 100, because the "head st
art" of starting from 99 (giving $(99!)!$ after two steps) vastly outweighs the
single extra factorial application, since $(99!)! \gg 100!$.
