## Proof

**Yes**, such a function exists.

### Step 1: Construct a closed, uncountable set $C \subseteq [0,1]$ containing n
o rationals.

Enumerate $\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1] = \{q_1, q_2, q_3, \ldots\}$. We build a nested s
equence $C_0 \supseteq C_1 \supseteq C_2 \supseteq \cdots$, where each $C_n$ is
a finite union of pairwise disjoint closed intervals, satisfying:

- $C_0 = [0,1]$,
- $q_n \notin C_n$ for each $n \geq 1$,
- every interval of $C_{n-1}$ produces at least two intervals in $C_n$,
- the maximum interval length in $C_n$ is at most $3^{-n}$.

**Construction at step $n$.** Given $C_{n-1}$ as a finite union of disjoint clos
ed intervals. For each interval $I=[a,b]$ in $C_{n-1}$:

1. **Remove the open middle third:** replace $I$ with $L=[a,\, a+\tfrac{b-a}{3}]
$ and $R=[a+\tfrac{2(b-a)}{3},\, b]$, each of length $(b-a)/3$.

2. **Exclude $q_n$:** Since $L$ and $R$ are separated by a gap, $q_n$ lies in at
 most one of them. If $q_n \in L$ (resp. $R$), remove a small open interval arou
nd $q_n$ from that piece — choosing it small enough to leave at least one non-de
generate closed subinterval (if $q_n$ is an interior point, this yields two subi
ntervals; if $q_n$ is an endpoint, it yields one). The other piece ($R$ or $L$)
remains intact. If $q_n \notin L\cup R$, both survive unchanged.

In every case, $I$ produces **at least two** non-degenerate closed subintervals,
 $q_n$ is excluded, and each resulting interval has length $\leq (b-a)/3 \leq 3^
{-n}$.

**Properties of $C = \bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty} C_n$:**

- **Closed:** Intersection of closed sets. ✓
- **Nonempty:** Each $C_n$ is nonempty and compact, and the sequence is nested;
by Cantor's intersection theorem, $C \neq \emptyset$. ✓
- **Contains no rationals:** For each $n$, $q_n \notin C_n \supseteq C$, so $q_n
 \notin C$. ✓
- **Nowhere dense (empty interior):** Any nonempty open interval contains ration
als, but $C$ contains none, so no interval is contained in $C$. ✓
- **Perfect:** For any $x \in C$ and $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $n$ with $3^{-n}
< \varepsilon$. Then $x$ lies in some interval $I$ of $C_n$ with $|I|<\varepsilo
n$. Since $I$ produces at least two subintervals in $C_{n+1}$, at least one subi
nterval avoids $x$ yet remains within $I$; by nonemptiness it contains a point $
y \in C$ with $y \neq x$ and $|y-x|<\varepsilon$. So every point of $C$ is a lim
it point. ✓
- **Uncountable:** A nonempty perfect subset of $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable. ✓

### Step 2: Define $f$ and compute $D(f)$.

Define $f = \chi_C : [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$, the characteristic function of $C$.

- **At $x \in C$:** $f(x)=1$. Since $C$ has empty interior, every neighborhood o
f $x$ contains points outside $C$ where $f=0$. The oscillation at $x$ is $\geq 1
$, so **$f$ is discontinuous at $x$**.

- **At $x \notin C$:** Since $C$ is closed, there exists $\delta > 0$ with $(x-\
delta,\, x+\delta)\cap C = \emptyset$. On this neighborhood $f \equiv 0$, so **$
f$ is continuous at $x$**.

Therefore $D(f) = C$, which is **uncountable** and **contains no rational number
s**. $\blacksquare$
