## Theorem

A real matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ has a real orthonormal eigenvector
 basis if and only if $A$ is symmetric, i.e., $A = A^T$.

---

## Proof

We prove both directions.

### (⟹) Orthonormal eigenvector basis ⟹ $A$ symmetric

Suppose there exists an orthogonal matrix $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ (so $Q
^T Q = I$) whose columns $v_1, \dots, v_n$ are eigenvectors of $A$ with (real) e
igenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$. Setting $D = \operatorname{diag}(\lamb
da_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$, the eigenvector equations $A v_i = \lambda_i v_i$ asse
mble into

$$AQ = QD,$$

so $A = QDQ^T$. Since $D$ is diagonal, $D^T = D$, and therefore

$$A^T = (QDQ^T)^T = QD^T Q^T = QDQ^T = A.$$

Hence $A$ is symmetric. ∎

### (⟸) $A$ symmetric ⟹ real orthonormal eigenvector basis

This is the **Real Spectral Theorem**. We establish it through three lemmas and
an induction.

**Lemma 1 (Eigenvalues are real).** Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ be symme
tric and let $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ be an eigenvalue with eigenvector $v \in \
mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{0\}$, so $Av = \lambda v$. Then

$$\bar v^T A v = \lambda\, \bar v^T v. \tag{1}$$

On the other hand, using $A = A^T$ and $A$ real (so $\bar A = A$):

$$\bar v^T A v = \bar v^T A^T v = (A\bar v)^T v = \overline{(Av)}^T v = \overlin
e{\lambda v}^{\,T} v = \bar\lambda\, \bar v^T v. \tag{2}$$

Comparing (1) and (2): $(\lambda - \bar\lambda)\,\bar v^T v = 0$. Since $v \neq
0$, we have $\bar v^T v > 0$, so $\lambda = \bar\lambda$; the eigenvalue is real
. Because $A$ and $\lambda$ are both real, the null space of $A - \lambda I$ is
a real subspace, so a **real** eigenvector can always be chosen. ∎

**Lemma 2 (Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal).** Let $Av = \l
ambda v$ and $Aw = \mu w$ with $v, w \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $\lambda \neq \mu$. T
hen

$$w^T A v = \lambda\, w^T v, \qquad w^T A v = w^T A^T v = (Aw)^T v = \mu\, w^T v
.$$

Subtracting: $(\lambda - \mu)\, w^T v = 0$. Since $\lambda \neq \mu$, we get $w^
T v = 0$. ∎

**Lemma 3 (Inductive construction).** We prove by induction on $n$ that every re
al symmetric $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ admits an orthogonal diagonalizatio
n $A = QDQ^T$.

*Base case* ($n = 1$): trivial, $A = [a]$, $Q = [1]$, $D = [a]$.

*Inductive step* ($n \geq 2$): By Lemma 1, $A$ has a real eigenvalue $\lambda_1$
 with a real unit eigenvector $v_1 \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Extend $v_1$ to an orthono
rmal basis $\{v_1, u_2, \dots, u_n\}$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ (e.g., via Gram–Schmidt)
 and form the orthogonal matrix $Q_1 = [v_1 \mid u_2 \mid \cdots \mid u_n]$. Con
sider $B = Q_1^T A Q_1$. Its first column is

$$B e_1 = Q_1^T A Q_1 e_1 = Q_1^T A v_1 = \lambda_1\, Q_1^T v_1 = \lambda_1\, e_
1 = (\lambda_1, 0, \dots, 0)^T.$$

Since $B = Q_1^T A Q_1$ is symmetric ($B^T = Q_1^T A^T Q_1 = Q_1^T A Q_1 = B$),
the first row equals the first column's transpose, so

$$B = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & \mathbf{0}^T \\ \mathbf{0} & A_1 \end{pmatrix}
,$$

where $A_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}$ is symmetric. By the inductive hyp
othesis, $A_1 = Q_2 D_2 Q_2^T$ for some orthogonal $Q_2$ and diagonal $D_2$. Def
ine

$$\widetilde{Q}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \mathbf{0}^T \\ \mathbf{0} & Q_2 \end{pm
atrix}, \qquad D = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & \mathbf{0}^T \\ \mathbf{0} & D_2
\end{pmatrix}, \qquad Q = Q_1\, \widetilde{Q}_2.$$

Then $\widetilde{Q}_2$ is orthogonal (block diagonal of orthogonal blocks), so $
Q$ is orthogonal (product of orthogonal matrices), and

$$A = Q_1 B Q_1^T = Q_1\, \widetilde{Q}_2\, D\, \widetilde{Q}_2^T\, Q_1^T = Q D
Q^T.$$

The columns of $Q$ are a real orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $A$. ∎

### Conclusion

Combining both directions, a real matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ possess
es a real orthonormal eigenvector basis **if and only if $A$ is symmetric** ($A
= A^T$). Equivalently, this is the condition that $A$ be **orthogonally diagonal
izable**: there exists an orthogonal $Q$ and a real diagonal $D$ with $A = QDQ^T
$.
