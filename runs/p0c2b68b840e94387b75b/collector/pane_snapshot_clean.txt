## Solution

Let $I = (XY, (X-Y)Z) \subseteq K[X,Y,Z]$. I claim
$$\sqrt{I} = (XY,\, XZ,\, YZ).$$

The proof has three parts: (A) exhibit the candidate as an intersection of prime
s, (B) show the candidate contains $\sqrt{I}$, (C) show $\sqrt{I}$ contains the
candidate.

### (A) A useful decomposition

Set $P_1=(X,Y)$, $P_2=(X,Z)$, $P_3=(Y,Z)$, all prime in $K[X,Y,Z]$.

**Claim.** $(XY,XZ,YZ)=P_1\cap P_2\cap P_3$.

*Step 1:* $(X,Y)\cap(X,Z)=(X,YZ)$. The containment $(X,YZ)\subseteq(X,Y)\cap(X,Z
)$ is clear since $X\in P_1\cap P_2$ and $YZ\in P_1$ (via $Y$) and $YZ\in P_2$ (
via $Z$). Conversely, let $f\in (X,Y)\cap(X,Z)$, say $f=aX+bY=cX+dZ$. Then $bY-d
Z=(c-a)X$. Specializing $X\mapsto 0$ gives $b(0,Y,Z)\cdot Y=d(0,Y,Z)\cdot Z$ in
$K[Y,Z]$, so $b(0,Y,Z)$ is divisible by $Z$: write $b=eZ+X\tilde b$. Then
$$f=aX+(eZ+X\tilde b)Y=(a+\tilde b Y)X+e\cdot YZ\in(X,YZ).$$

*Step 2:* $(X,YZ)\cap(Y,Z)=(XY,XZ,YZ)$. Let $f=aX+bYZ\in(X,YZ)\cap(Y,Z)$. Since
$f\in(Y,Z)$, setting $Y=Z=0$ gives $a(X,0,0)\,X=0$, hence $a\in(Y,Z)$, say $a=pY
+qZ$. Then
$$f=pYX+qZX+bYZ\in(XY,XZ,YZ).$$
The reverse containment is immediate ($XY,XZ\in(X,YZ)$ via $X$, and all three li
e in $(Y,Z)$: $XY$ via $Y$, $XZ$ via $Z$, $YZ$ via both).

Combining: $P_1\cap P_2\cap P_3=(XY,XZ,YZ)$, an intersection of primes, hence **
radical**.

### (B) $(XY,XZ,YZ)\subseteq\sqrt{I}$

- $XY\in I$ directly, so $XY\in\sqrt{I}$.
- For $XZ$: $(X-Y)Z\in I$, so $XZ\cdot(X-Y)Z=X(X-Y)Z^{2}=X^{2}Z^{2}-XYZ^{2}\in I
$. Also $XY\in I\Rightarrow XYZ^{2}=XY\cdot Z^{2}\in I$. Adding,
  $$(XZ)^{2}=X^{2}Z^{2}=\bigl(X^{2}Z^{2}-XYZ^{2}\bigr)+XYZ^{2}\in I.$$
  Hence $XZ\in\sqrt{I}$.
- For $YZ$: $(X-Y)Z\in I$, so $YZ\cdot(X-Y)Z=Y(X-Y)Z^{2}=XYZ^{2}-Y^{2}Z^{2}\in I
$. Together with $XYZ^{2}\in I$,
  $$(YZ)^{2}=Y^{2}Z^{2}=XYZ^{2}-\bigl(XYZ^{2}-Y^{2}Z^{2}\bigr)\in I.$$
  Hence $YZ\in\sqrt{I}$.

Since $\sqrt{I}$ is an ideal, $(XY,XZ,YZ)\subseteq\sqrt{I}$.

### (C) $\sqrt{I}\subseteq(XY,XZ,YZ)$

It suffices to show $I\subseteq(XY,XZ,YZ)$, since by (A) the latter is radical a
nd therefore equals its own radical. Now $XY\in(XY,XZ,YZ)$ trivially, and
$$(X-Y)Z=XZ-YZ\in(XY,XZ,YZ).$$
Thus $I\subseteq(XY,XZ,YZ)$, and taking radicals (using that $(XY,XZ,YZ)$ is rad
ical):
$$\sqrt{I}\subseteq\sqrt{(XY,XZ,YZ)}=(XY,XZ,YZ).$$

### Conclusion

From (B) and (C),
$$\boxed{\sqrt{I}=(XY,\,XZ,\,YZ).}$$

Equivalently, $\sqrt{I}=(X,Y)\cap(X,Z)\cap(Y,Z)$, the ideal of the union of the
three coordinate axes. The argument is purely algebraic (no Nullstellensatz), so
 it holds over **any** field $K$.
