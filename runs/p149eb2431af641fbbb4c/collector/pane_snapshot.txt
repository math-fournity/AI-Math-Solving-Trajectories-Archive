## Proof

We analyze the convergence of
$$I(\alpha) = \int_{\mathbb{S}^n} \frac{d\theta}{|\theta - \theta_0|^\alpha},$$
where $\mathbb{S}^n \subset \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ is the unit $n$-sphere, $\theta_0
\in \mathbb{S}^n$ is fixed, and $|\theta-\theta_0|$ is the Euclidean distance in
 the ambient $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$.

**The only possible singularity is at $\theta = \theta_0$.** Away from $\theta_0
$, the integrand is continuous and bounded, so the integral over $\mathbb{S}^n \
setminus B(\theta_0, \varepsilon)$ is finite for any $\varepsilon > 0$. Converge
nce is therefore determined entirely by the behavior near $\theta_0$.

**Local analysis near $\theta_0$.** Choose geodesic normal coordinates centered
at $\theta_0$, with $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ denoting the local coordinate (so $x =
0$ corresponds to $\theta_0$). In these coordinates the volume form is smooth an
d equals $d\theta = J(x)\,dx$ with $J(0) = 1$ and $J$ smooth, bounded, and bound
ed away from $0$ near the origin.

The ambient Euclidean distance satisfies, for $\theta$ near $\theta_0$,
$$|\theta - \theta_0|^2 = 2 - 2\cos\,\rho = 4\sin^2(\rho/2),$$
where $\rho = |x|$ is the geodesic distance from $\theta_0$. Hence $|\theta - \t
heta_0| = 2\sin(\rho/2) \sim \rho = |x|$ as $x \to 0$. Therefore
$$\frac{1}{|\theta-\theta_0|^\alpha} \sim \frac{1}{|x|^\alpha} \quad (x \to 0).$
$

**Reduction to a radial integral in $\mathbb{R}^n$.** By the above, there exist
constants $c, C > 0$ and $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x$ with $|x| < \delta$,
$$\frac{c}{|x|^\alpha} \le \frac{1}{|\theta(x)-\theta_0|^\alpha} \le \frac{C}{|x
|^\alpha},$$
and $J(x)$ is bounded above and below by positive constants. Thus $I(\alpha)$ co
nverges if and only if
$$\int_{|x|<\delta} \frac{dx}{|x|^\alpha}$$
converges.

Passing to polar coordinates in $\mathbb{R}^n$ ($dx = r^{n-1}\,dr\,d\omega$):
$$\int_{|x|<\delta} \frac{dx}{|x|^\alpha} = |\mathbb{S}^{n-1}| \int_0^\delta r^{
n-1-\alpha}\,dr.$$

**Convergence criterion.** The integral $\int_0^\delta r^{n-1-\alpha}\,dr$ conve
rges at $r = 0$ if and only if the exponent satisfies $n - 1 - \alpha > -1$, i.e
.,
$$\alpha < n.$$

- If $\alpha < n$: the exponent $n-1-\alpha > -1$, the integral converges, so $I
(\alpha) < \infty$.
- If $\alpha \ge n$: the exponent $n-1-\alpha \le -1$, the integral diverges, so
 $I(\alpha) = +\infty$.
- If $\alpha \le 0$: the integrand has no singularity (it is bounded, even vanis
hing at $\theta_0$ when $\alpha < 0$), and convergence is trivial — consistent w
ith $\alpha \le 0 < n$.

**Conclusion.** The integral $\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^n} \frac{d\theta}{|\
theta-\theta_0|^\alpha}$ converges if and only if
$$\boxed{\alpha < n.}$$

The critical (threshold) value is $\alpha = n$, at which the integral diverges l
ogarithmically.
