## Solution

**Problem.** Determine all entire functions $f(z)$ satisfying $f(z+1) = (2z+1)\,
f(z)$.

---

### Step 1. A particular entire solution

Define
$$
h(z) \;=\; \frac{e^{i\pi z}\,2^{z}}{\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-z\right)}.
$$

Since $1/\Gamma$ is entire (with simple zeros at $0,-1,-2,\dots$), the compositi
on $1/\Gamma(\tfrac12 - z)$ is entire, and $e^{i\pi z}$, $2^z = e^{z\ln 2}$ are
entire and non-vanishing. Hence **$h$ is entire**.

**Verification of the functional equation.** Using $\Gamma(w) = (w-1)\,\Gamma(w-
1)$, i.e. $\Gamma(\tfrac12 - z) = (-\tfrac12 - z)\,\Gamma(-\tfrac12 - z)$, so $\
Gamma(-\tfrac12 - z) = \Gamma(\tfrac12 - z)/(-\tfrac12 - z)$:
$$
h(z+1) = \frac{e^{i\pi(z+1)}\,2^{z+1}}{\Gamma(-\tfrac12 - z)} = \frac{-e^{i\pi z
}\,2^{z+1}\cdot(-\tfrac12 - z)}{\Gamma(\tfrac12 - z)} = \frac{e^{i\pi z}\,2^{z+1
}(z+\tfrac12)}{\Gamma(\tfrac12 - z)} = (2z+1)\,h(z). \quad\checkmark
$$

### Step 2. Zeros of $h$

The factor $1/\Gamma(\tfrac12 - z)$ vanishes (simply) exactly where $\tfrac12 -
z \in \{0,-1,-2,\dots\}$, i.e. at
$$
z = \tfrac12,\;\tfrac32,\;\tfrac52,\;\dots
$$
Since $e^{i\pi z}\cdot 2^z$ never vanishes, **$h$ has simple zeros exactly at**
$z = n+\tfrac12$ for $n = 0,1,2,\dots$, and no other zeros.

### Step 3. Every entire solution vanishes at the positive half-integers

Set $z = -\tfrac12$ in the functional equation:
$$
f(\tfrac12) = (2\cdot(-\tfrac12)+1)\,f(-\tfrac12) = 0\cdot f(-\tfrac12) = 0.
$$
By induction: if $f(n+\tfrac12)=0$, then
$$
f\!\big((n{+}1)+\tfrac12\big) = f\!\big(n+\tfrac12+1\big) = \big(2(n+\tfrac12)+1
\big)\,f\!\big(n+\tfrac12\big) = (2n+2)\cdot 0 = 0.
$$
So **$f(n+\tfrac12) = 0$ for all $n \geq 0$**.

### Step 4. The quotient $f/h$ is entire and $1$-periodic

Let $f$ be any entire solution. Define $p(z) = f(z)/h(z)$ where $h(z)\neq 0$.

- **At $z = n+\tfrac12$ ($n\geq 0$):** $h$ has a simple zero and $f$ has a zero
(at least simple, by Step 3). Hence $f/h$ has a **removable** singularity.
- **Elsewhere:** $h\neq 0$, so $f/h$ is holomorphic.

Thus $p$ extends to an **entire** function. For $z$ with $h(z)\neq 0$:
$$
p(z+1) = \frac{f(z+1)}{h(z+1)} = \frac{(2z+1)\,f(z)}{(2z+1)\,h(z)} = \frac{f(z)}
{h(z)} = p(z).
$$
By the identity theorem, $p(z+1)=p(z)$ for all $z$, so **$p$ is entire and $1$-p
eriodic**.

### Step 5. Conclusion

Every entire solution has the form $f = h\cdot p$ with $p$ entire and $1$-period
ic. Conversely, for any entire $1$-periodic $p$, the function $f(z)=h(z)\,p(z)$
is entire (product of entire functions) and satisfies
$$
f(z+1) = h(z+1)\,p(z+1) = (2z+1)\,h(z)\,p(z) = (2z+1)\,f(z).
$$

---

**The set of all entire solutions is**
$$
\boxed{\,f(z) \;=\; \frac{(-2)^{z}}{\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-z\right)}\;P(z)\,
}
$$
**where $(-2)^z = e^{z(\ln 2 + i\pi)}$, and $P(z)$ is an arbitrary entire functi
on satisfying $P(z+1)=P(z)$ (i.e., entire and $1$-periodic).**

The case $P\equiv 0$ gives $f\equiv 0$; non-trivial choices of $P$ (e.g. $P\equi
v c\neq 0$, or $P(z)=e^{2\pi i k z}$ for $k\in\mathbb{Z}$) yield non-trivial sol
utions.
