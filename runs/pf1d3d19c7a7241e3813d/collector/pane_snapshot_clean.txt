# Mordell's Finite Basis Theorem for Nonsingular Cubic Curves over ℚ

## Statement

Let $C$ be a nonsingular cubic curve defined over $\mathbb{Q}$. We may write it
(after a suitable change of coordinates) in Weierstrass form

$$C: y^2 = x^3 + ax + b, \qquad a, b \in \mathbb{Q}, \qquad 4a^3 + 27b^2 \neq 0,
$$

where the nonsingularity condition $4a^3 + 27b^2 \neq 0$ ensures the curve has n
o cusps or nodes. The set $C(\mathbb{Q})$ of rational points — points $(x,y) \in
 \mathbb{Q}^2$ satisfying the equation, together with a "point at infinity" $\ma
thcal{O}$ serving as the identity — carries the structure of an abelian group un
der the geometric chord-and-tangent law.

**Mordell's Theorem (1922).** *The group $C(\mathbb{Q})$ is finitely generated.
Equivalently, there exist finitely many rational points $P_1, \dots, P_r \in C(\
mathbb{Q})$ such that*

$$C(\mathbb{Q}) = \mathbb{Z}P_1 \oplus \mathbb{Z}P_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathb
b{Z}P_r \oplus C(\mathbb{Q})_{\text{tors}}.$$

*Here $C(\mathbb{Q})_{\text{tors}}$ is the finite torsion subgroup (points of fi
nite order), and $r \geq 0$ is the **rank** of the curve.*

Thus every rational point on $C$ can be written as a $\mathbb{Z}$-linear combina
tion of the generators plus a torsion point, and the structure theorem for finit
ely generated abelian groups gives the displayed decomposition.

## Historical Context

Louis Mordell proved this result in 1922, resolving a question that had been imp
licit in the work of Poincaré. It was the first deep structural theorem about ra
tional points on algebraic varieties of genus $> 0$. André Weil generalized it i
n 1928 to abelian varieties over number fields (the *Mordell–Weil theorem*), and
 the group $C(\mathbb{Q})$ is accordingly called the **Mordell–Weil group**.

## Significance

### 1. Finiteness of generation, not just of solutions
For genus $0$ curves (conics), rational points are either empty or dense (parame
trized), so no finite-generation statement is meaningful. For genus $\geq 2$ cur
ves, Faltings' theorem (1983) later showed $C(\mathbb{Q})$ is *finite*. Mordell'
s theorem occupies the intermediate genus-1 case: the solution set is **not fini
te** in general, but it is *finitely generated* — a remarkable structural compro
mise between the parametric freedom of genus 0 and the rigidity of genus $\geq 2
$.

### 2. It makes the "arithmetic" of the curve computable in principle
Because $C(\mathbb{Q})$ is finitely generated, describing all rational points re
duces to:
- finding the (finite) torsion subgroup — completely classified by Mazur's theor
em (1977) into 15 possible group structures,
- finding a finite set of generators, i.e. determining the rank $r$ and explicit
 points $P_i$.

This converts an a priori infinite problem into a finite (though hard) computati
onal task, which is the foundation of the algorithmic theory of elliptic curves
used in modern number theory and cryptography.

### 3. The rank is the central open invariant
Mordell's theorem guarantees the rank $r$ is a well-defined finite integer, but
the proof is **ineffective**: it does not, by itself, produce an algorithm guara
nteed to compute $r$ or the generators. Determining $r$ for a given curve remain
s one of the great open problems. The **Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture** predi
cts $r$ in terms of the analytic behavior of the $L$-function $L(C,s)$ at $s=1$,
 and is one of the Clay Millennium Prize problems. Mordell's theorem is what giv
es $r$ its meaning and makes that conjecture well-posed.

### 4. It launched the descent machinery
Mordell's proof introduced the **descent argument** to the arithmetic of curves:
 one shows the quotient $C(\mathbb{Q})/nC(\mathbb{Q})$ is finite for some $n \ge
q 2$ (the *weak Mordell–Weil theorem*), and combines this with a **height functi
on** $h: C(\mathbb{Q}) \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ satisfying
- $h(P+Q) \leq 2h(P) + 2h(Q) + O(1)$ (quasi-quadratic growth),
- $h(nP) \approx n^2 h(P)$,
- for every bound $B$, the set $\{P : h(P) \leq B\}$ is finite (Northcott proper
ty).

The finiteness of $C(\mathbb{Q})/nC(\mathbb{Q})$ plus the Northcott property tog
ether force $C(\mathbb{Q})$ to be finitely generated. This **descent + height**
paradigm became the template for the entire cohomological descent and height-mac
hine machinery of modern Diophantine geometry (Weil, Néron, Tate, Arakelov, Falt
ings).

### 5. It underpins the Mordell conjecture
Mordell observed that for genus $\geq 2$ curves, finite generation of the Jacobi
an (which Weil later proved) combined with the embedding of the curve into its J
acobian should force $C(\mathbb{Q})$ itself to be finite — because a finitely ge
nerated subgroup of an abelian variety intersects a high-genus curve in only fin
itely many points. This insight, made precise by Raynaud, is exactly what Faltin
gs proved in 1983. So Mordell's finite basis theorem is the conceptual seed of F
altings' theorem.

## Sketch of the Proof Structure

For completeness, the argument has two pillars:

**(A) Weak Mordell–Weil.** For a fixed integer $n \geq 2$ (classically $n=2$), t
he quotient $C(\mathbb{Q})/nC(\mathbb{Q})$ is finite. For $n=2$ this is shown by
 associating to each coset a quartic (or cubic) covering whose rational points c
orrespond to elements of a finite 2-Selmer group, computable via the finiteness
of class groups and Dirichlet's unit theorem. Thus there are only finitely many
cosets.

**(B) Height descent.** Define the (naïve) height $h(P) = \log \max(|m|,|n|)$ wh
en $x(P) = m/n$ in lowest terms, and the canonical height $\hat h(P) = \lim_{k\t
o\infty} h(2^k P)/4^k$, which is a quadratic form on $C(\mathbb{Q})$. Using (A),
 pick representatives $Q_1,\dots,Q_s$ for the cosets of $nC(\mathbb{Q})$. Any $P
 \in C(\mathbb{Q})$ can be written $P = nP' + Q_i$ for some $i$ and some $P' \in
 C(\mathbb{Q})$, and the quadratic property of $\hat h$ gives $\hat h(P') \leq \
tfrac{1}{n^2}\hat h(P) + O(1) < \hat h(P)$ for $P$ of sufficiently large height.
 Iterating, every $P$ reduces to a point of bounded height, of which there are f
initely many by Northcott. Hence $C(\mathbb{Q})$ is generated by a finite set, n
amely those bounded-height points together with the coset representatives.

This is the canonical template: **weak Mordell–Weil (cohomological finiteness) +
 height machine (Northcott + quadratic growth) ⇒ finite generation.**

## Summary

Mordell's theorem states that the rational points on a nonsingular cubic over $\
mathbb{Q}$ form a finitely generated abelian group, decomposing as $\mathbb{Z}^r
 \oplus C(\mathbb{Q})_{\text{tors}}$. Its significance is fourfold: it gives the
 genus-1 case its precise structural form between the parametric genus-0 case an
d the finite genus-$\geq 2$ case; it reduces the infinite problem of listing rat
ional points to the finite problem of finding rank and generators; it defines th
e rank $r$ that the Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture seeks to explain; and it in
troduced the descent-plus-height paradigm that became the backbone of modern Dio
phantine geometry and ultimately led, via the Mordell conjecture, to Faltings' f
initeness theorem.
