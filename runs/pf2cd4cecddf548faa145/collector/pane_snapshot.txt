## Answer

**Yes.** A continuously differentiable function on an open subset of a Banach sp
ace is locally Lipschitz.

### Proof

Let $X, Y$ be Banach spaces, $U \subseteq X$ open, and $f: U \to Y$ continuously
 differentiable (i.e., $f \in C^1(U; Y)$, meaning the Fréchet derivative $Df: U
\to \mathcal{L}(X; Y)$ exists and is continuous). We show that $f$ is locally Li
pschitz on $U$: every point of $U$ has a neighborhood on which $f$ is Lipschitz.

**Step 1 — Local bound on the derivative.**

Fix $x_0 \in U$. Since $Df$ is continuous at $x_0$ and $U$ is open, there exists
 $r > 0$ with $\overline{B}(x_0, r) \subseteq U$ such that
$$
\|Df(x)\|_{\mathcal{L}(X;Y)} \le M := \|Df(x_0)\| + 1 \qquad \text{for all } x \
in \overline{B}(x_0, r).
$$
This uses only continuity of $Df$ at the single point $x_0$ — no compactness of
$\overline{B}(x_0,r)$ is required.

**Step 2 — Mean-value inequality via the fundamental theorem of calculus.**

Let $x, y \in B(x_0, r)$. Since the open ball is convex, the line segment $\{x +
 t(y - x) : t \in [0,1]\}$ lies entirely in $B(x_0, r) \subseteq U$. Define
$$
\varphi(t) := f\bigl(x + t(y - x)\bigr), \qquad t \in [0,1].
$$
Then $\varphi: [0,1] \to Y$ is $C^1$ with
$$
\varphi'(t) = Df\bigl(x + t(y-x)\bigr)(y - x).
$$
Applying the fundamental theorem of calculus for continuous $Y$-valued functions
 on $[0,1]$ (valid in any Banach space),
$$
f(y) - f(x) = \varphi(1) - \varphi(0) = \int_0^1 \varphi'(t)\,dt = \int_0^1 Df\b
igl(x + t(y-x)\bigr)(y-x)\,dt.
$$
Taking norms and using $\|Df(x + t(y-x))\| \le M$ for all $t \in [0,1]$,
$$
\|f(y) - f(x)\| \le \int_0^1 \bigl\|Df\bigl(x + t(y-x)\bigr)\bigr\|\,\|y - x\|\,
dt \le M\,\|y - x\|.
$$

**Step 3 — Conclusion.**

We have shown: for every $x_0 \in U$ there exist $r > 0$ and $M < \infty$ such t
hat
$$
\|f(y) - f(x)\| \le M\,\|y - x\| \qquad \text{for all } x, y \in B(x_0, r).
$$
Hence $f$ is Lipschitz on $B(x_0, r)$, a neighborhood of $x_0$. Since $x_0$ was
arbitrary, $f$ is locally Lipschitz on $U$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The argument uses only (i) continuity of $Df$ at each point to get a
 local bound, and (ii) convexity of balls to apply the one-dimensional fundament
al theorem of calculus along line segments. No compactness is invoked, so the re
sult holds in arbitrary (possibly infinite-dimensional) Banach spaces. The conve
rse is false: locally Lipschitz need not imply $C^1$ (e.g., $f(x) = |x|$ on $\ma
thbb{R}$).
