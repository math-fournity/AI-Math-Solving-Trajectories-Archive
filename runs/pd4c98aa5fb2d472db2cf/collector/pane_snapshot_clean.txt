Looking at this problem, I need to find all integers $k$ such that $\frac{1}{x}+
\frac{1}{y}+\frac{1}{z}=\frac{k}{xyz}$ has infinitely many integer solutions $(x
,y,z)$ with $\gcd(k,xyz)=1$.

**Reformulation.** Multiplying both sides by $xyz$ (with $x,y,z \neq 0$):
$$xy + yz + xz = k, \quad \gcd(k, xyz) = 1.$$

---

**Step 1: Even $k$ is impossible.**

Suppose $k$ is even and $\gcd(k, xyz) = 1$. Then $2 \nmid xyz$, so $x, y, z$ are
 all odd. But then $xy, yz, xz$ are each odd, so $xy + yz + xz$ is odd — contrad
icting $k$ being even. Therefore **no even $k$ admits even a single solution**,
let alone infinitely many.

---

**Step 2: Every odd $k$ works.**

Fix any odd integer $k$. I construct infinitely many solutions via the parametri
zation $x + y = 1$.

Set $y = 1 - x$ and solve for $z$:
$$z = \frac{k - xy}{x + y} = \frac{k - x(1-x)}{1} = x^2 - x + k.$$

Then $(x,\; 1-x,\; x^2 - x + k)$ satisfies $xy + yz + xz = k$ for every integer
$x$.

**Coprimality check.** Note $xy = x(1-x) = x - x^2 = -(x^2 - x) = -(z - k) = k -
 z$. So:
$$xyz = (k - z)\cdot z.$$
Thus $\gcd(k, xyz) = \gcd(k, z(k-z))$. Since $\gcd(k, k-z) = \gcd(k, z)$, this e
quals $\gcd(k, z)^{\,}$... more precisely:
$$\gcd(k,\, z(k-z)) = 1 \iff \gcd(k, z) = 1 \iff \gcd(k,\, x^2 - x + k) = 1 \iff
 \gcd(k,\, x(x-1)) = 1.$$
This holds iff $\gcd(k, x) = 1$ and $\gcd(k, x-1) = 1$.

**There are infinitely many such $x$.** For each prime $p \mid k$ (all odd, sinc
e $k$ is odd), we exclude $x \equiv 0$ and $x \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$, leaving $p - 2
 \geq 1$ allowed residue classes. By the Chinese Remainder Theorem, the allowed
$x$ form a nonempty union of arithmetic progressions — infinitely many integers.

**Non-vanishing check.** We need $x \neq 0$, $y = 1-x \neq 0$ (so $x \neq 1$), a
nd $z = x^2 - x + k \neq 0$. The equation $x^2 - x + k = 0$ has at most 2 intege
r solutions, so excluding finitely many values of $x$ still leaves infinitely ma
ny valid choices.

**Verification with examples.**
- $k = 1$, $x = 2$: $(2, -1, 3)$, check: $-2 - 3 + 6 = 1$ ✓, $\gcd(1, -6) = 1$ ✓
.
- $k = 3$, $x = 2$: $(2, -1, 5)$, check: $-2 - 5 + 10 = 3$ ✓, $\gcd(3, -10) = 1$
 ✓.
- $k = -1$, $x = 2$: $(2, -1, 1)$, check: $-2 - 1 + 2 = -1$ ✓, $\gcd(-1, -2) = 1
$ ✓.

---

**Conclusion.** Even $k$ admits no solutions at all (Step 1), while every odd $k
$ admits infinitely many solutions (Step 2). Therefore the integers $k$ are prec
isely **all odd integers**.
