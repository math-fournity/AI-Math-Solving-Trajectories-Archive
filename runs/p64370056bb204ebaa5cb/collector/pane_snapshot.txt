## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, the restriction $S|_{X_1}: X_1 \to X_2$ is invertible (as a boun
ded linear bijection with bounded inverse).

**Step 1: Injectivity.** Since $S$ is invertible on $X$, it is injective on all
of $X$. Therefore its restriction to any subspace $X_1$ is injective:
$$Sx_1 = Sx_2,\; x_1,x_2\in X_1 \implies x_1 = x_2.$$

**Step 2: Surjectivity onto $X_2$.** This is given: $S(X_1) = X_2$.

Combining Steps 1 and 2, $S|_{X_1}: X_1 \to X_2$ is an algebraic bijection.

**Step 3: The inverse is the restriction of $S^{-1}$.** For any $y \in X_2$, the
re exists (by surjectivity) a unique $x \in X_1$ with $Sx = y$. Then
$$(S|_{X_1})^{-1}(y) = x = S^{-1}(y).$$
Thus
$$(S|_{X_1})^{-1} = S^{-1}\big|_{X_2}.$$

**Step 4: Boundedness of the inverse.** Since $S \in \mathcal{L}(X)$ is invertib
le, $S^{-1} \in \mathcal{L}(X)$ is bounded with $\|S^{-1}\| < \infty$. The restr
iction of a bounded operator to any subspace is bounded with norm no larger:
$$\|(S|_{X_1})^{-1}\| = \|S^{-1}|_{X_2}\| \leq \|S^{-1}\| < \infty.$$

**Step 5: Boundedness of $S|_{X_1}$.** Likewise, $S|_{X_1}$ is the restriction o
f the bounded operator $S$, so $\|S|_{X_1}\| \leq \|S\| < \infty$.

**Conclusion.** $S|_{X_1}: X_1 \to X_2$ is a bounded linear bijection whose inve
rse $(S|_{X_1})^{-1} = S^{-1}|_{X_2}$ is also bounded. Hence the restriction of
$S$ to $X_1$ is invertible from $X_1$ to $X_2$.

**Remark on closedness.** Note that this argument does **not** require $X_1$ or
$X_2$ to be closed. The key point is that we already possess a *global* bounded
inverse $S^{-1}$, so the inverse of the restriction is automatically the restric
tion of the inverse — no appeal to the Bounded Inverse Theorem (which would requ
ire $X_1$ to be a Banach space, i.e., closed) is needed. The identity $(S|_{X_1}
)^{-1} = S^{-1}|_{X_2}$ holds purely algebraically, and boundedness follows from
 the global bound on $S^{-1}$.
