## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, such a function exists.

**Construction.** Enumerate the rationals in $[0,1]$ as $\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]=\{r
_1,r_2,r_3,\dots\}$. Define

$$f(x)=\sum_{\{n\,:\,r_n\le x\}} 2^{-n}, \qquad x\in[0,1].$$

This series converges absolutely since $\sum_{n=1}^\infty 2^{-n}=1<\infty$, so $
f$ is well-defined with $0\le f(x)\le 1$.

---

**Bounded variation.** For any partition $0=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_m=1$,

$$\sum_{i=1}^{m}|f(x_i)-f(x_{i-1})|=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\sum_{\{n:\,x_{i-1}<r_n\
le x_i\}}2^{-n}\right|\le\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{\{n:\,x_{i-1}<r_n\le x_i\}}2^{-n}=\
sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}=1.$$

Hence $V(f)\le 1<\infty$, so $f$ is of bounded variation on $[0,1]$.

---

**Continuity at every irrational.** Let $\alpha\in[0,1]\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ and
let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ so that $\sum_{n>N}2^{-n}<\varepsilon$. The fini
te set $\{r_1,\dots,r_N\}$ does not contain $\alpha$, so

$$\delta:=\min_{1\le n\le N}|r_n-\alpha|>0.$$

For any $x$ with $|x-\alpha|<\delta$, none of $r_1,\dots,r_N$ lies between $x$ a
nd $\alpha$, so

$$|f(x)-f(\alpha)|=\left|\sum_{\{n:\,\min(x,\alpha)<r_n\le\max(x,\alpha)\}}2^{-n
}\right|\le\sum_{n>N}2^{-n}<\varepsilon.$$

Thus $f$ is continuous at $\alpha$.

---

**Discontinuity at every rational.** Let $r_k$ be a rational in the enumeration.
 The left-hand limit is

$$f(r_k^-)=\lim_{x\to r_k^-}f(x)=\sum_{\{n:\,r_n<r_k\}}2^{-n}=f(r_k)-2^{-k},$$

since the only term distinguishing $f(r_k)$ from $f(r_k^-)$ is the term $2^{-k}$
 corresponding to $r_k$ itself (which satisfies $r_k\le r_k$ but not $r_k<r_k$).
 Since $2^{-k}\ne 0$, we have $f(r_k^-)\ne f(r_k)$, so $f$ is discontinuous at $
r_k$.

---

**Conclusion.** The function $f(x)=\sum_{\{n:\,r_n\le x\}}2^{-n}$ is continuous
at every irrational, discontinuous at every rational, and of bounded variation o
n $[0,1]$. Therefore such a function exists. $\blacksquare$
