## Solution

### (a) $\left(z^{2}+1\right)^{1/2}$

**Factoring.** Write $z^2 + 1 = (z - i)(z + i)$. The function is

$$w(z) = \bigl[(z-i)(z+i)\bigr]^{1/2}.$$

**Branch points (finite).** A branch point of $w = g(z)^{1/n}$ occurs at every z
ero of $g$ where the zero has order not divisible by $n$. Here $g(z) = (z-i)(z+i
)$ has **simple** zeros (order 1) at $z = i$ and $z = -i$, and $n = 2$. Since $1
 \nmid 2$ is not the right way to say it—rather, the order 1 is not a multiple o
f 2—each is a branch point.

Concretely: near $z = i$, write $z - i = re^{i\theta}$; then $w \approx (z-i)^{1
/2}(2i)^{1/2}$. Traversing a small loop around $z = i$ once ($\theta \to \theta
+ 2\pi$) gives $w \to w\, e^{i\pi} = -w \neq w$. So $z = i$ is a **branch point
of order 1** (square-root type). By the identical argument, $z = -i$ is also a *
*branch point of order 1**.

**Branch point at infinity.** Set $z = 1/t$:

$$\left(z^2+1\right)^{1/2} = \left(\frac{1+t^2}{t^2}\right)^{1/2} = \frac{(1+t^2
)^{1/2}}{t}.$$

The numerator $(1+t^2)^{1/2} \to 1$ as $t \to 0$ and is single-valued near $t=0$
. The denominator $t$ is single-valued. Hence traversing a large circle ($t$ cir
cles the origin once) returns $w$ to its original value: **infinity is NOT a bra
nch point**.

(Equivalently: $w = z\bigl(1 + 1/z^2\bigr)^{1/2}$ for large $|z|$, and $(1+1/z^2
)^{1/2}$ is analytic and single-valued at $\infty$.)

**Branch cut structure.** There are exactly two branch points, $z = i$ and $z =
-i$, and infinity is ordinary. A single branch cut **connecting the two branch p
oints** renders the function single-valued on the complement. The standard choic
e is the **straight-line segment on the imaginary axis from $-i$ to $i$**:

$$\text{Cut: } \{iy : -1 \le y \le 1\}.$$

Any closed loop avoiding this cut encircles either both branch points (net phase
 $e^{i\pi}\cdot e^{i\pi} = e^{2\pi i} = 1$, single-valued) or neither—so $w$ is
single-valued on $\mathbb{C} \setminus [-i, i]$.

*Alternative:* two rays $[i, i\infty)$ and $[-i, -i\infty)$ along the imaginary
axis also work, but since $\infty$ is not a branch point, the single segment $[-
i,i]$ is the most economical choice.

### (b) $\bigl((z+1)(z-2)\bigr)^{1/3}$

**Factoring.** The function is

$$w(z) = \bigl[(z+1)(z-2)\bigr]^{1/3}.$$

**Branch points (finite).** $g(z) = (z+1)(z-2)$ has **simple** zeros at $z = -1$
 and $z = 2$, and $n = 3$. Since order 1 is not a multiple of 3, both are branch
 points.

Near $z = -1$: $w \approx (z+1)^{1/3}(-3)^{1/3}$. A loop around $z = -1$ gives $
w \to w\, e^{2\pi i/3} \neq w$. So $z = -1$ is a **branch point of order 2** (cu
be-root type). Likewise $z = 2$ is a **branch point of order 2**.

**Branch point at infinity.** Set $z = 1/t$:

$$\bigl[(z+1)(z-2)\bigr]^{1/3} = \left[\frac{(1+t)(1-2t)}{t^2}\right]^{1/3} = \f
rac{\bigl[(1+t)(1-2t)\bigr]^{1/3}}{t^{2/3}}.$$

The numerator tends to $1$ and is single-valued near $t = 0$. The denominator $t
^{2/3}$: a single circuit of $t = 0$ sends $t^{2/3} \to t^{2/3} e^{4\pi i/3}$, s
o $w \to w\, e^{-4\pi i/3} = w\, e^{2\pi i/3} \neq w$. **Infinity IS a branch po
int** (of order 2, cube-root type).

*Consistency check:* a large loop encircling both $z=-1$ and $z=2$ picks up phas
e $e^{2\pi i/3}\cdot e^{2\pi i/3} = e^{4\pi i/3} = e^{-2\pi i/3}$, which is exac
tly the phase $e^{2\pi i/3}$ (mod $2\pi$: $e^{4\pi i/3}$ vs $e^{2\pi i/3}$—wait,
 $e^{-4\pi i/3} = e^{2\pi i/3}$, and the product of the two finite contributions
 is $e^{4\pi i/3}$; the phase at infinity is $e^{2\pi i/3}$, and $e^{4\pi i/3}\c
dot e^{2\pi i/3} = e^{2\pi i} = 1$). ✓ The total phase around all three branch p
oints is 1, as required.

**Branch cut structure.** There are **three** branch points: $z = -1$, $z = 2$,
and $z = \infty$. We need cuts that prevent any loop from encircling a single br
anch point (or any combination whose total phase $\neq 1$). A standard and econo
mical choice is **two cuts along the real axis**:

$$\text{Cuts: } (-\infty, -1] \;\cup\; [2, +\infty).$$

This connects $-\infty \to -1$ (i.e., $\infty$ to $-1$) and $2 \to +\infty$ (i.e
., $2$ to $\infty$), pairing all three branch points. On the slit plane $\mathbb
{C} \setminus \bigl((-\infty,-1]\cup[2,\infty)\bigr)$, any closed loop encircles
 a subset of branch points whose total phase is $e^{2k\pi i/3}$ with $k \equiv 0
 \pmod{3}$, so $w$ is single-valued.

*Alternative:* a cut from $-1$ to $2$ along $[-1,2]$ together with a ray from $2
$ to $+\infty$ (or from $-1$ to $-\infty$) also works. Any choice that pairs the
 three branch points with non-crossing cuts is valid.

### Summary

| Function | Finite branch points | $\infty$ a branch point? | Branch cut (stand
ard choice) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $(z^2+1)^{1/2}$ | $z = \pm i$ (order 1 each, $\sqrt{\;}$ type) | No | Segment
$[-i,\, i]$ on imaginary axis |
| $((z+1)(z-2))^{1/3}$ | $z = -1,\; z = 2$ (order 2 each, $\sqrt[3]{\;}$ type) |
 Yes (order 2) | Two rays: $(-\infty,-1]\cup[2,+\infty)$ on real axis |

The rule of thumb: for $g(z)^{1/n}$ with $g$ a polynomial of degree $d$, every s
imple zero of $g$ is a branch point of order $n-1$; infinity is a branch point i
f and only if $d$ is not divisible by $n$. In (a), $d=2$, $n=2$, $2\mid 2$ → $\i
nfty$ is ordinary. In (b), $d=2$, $n=3$, $2\nmid 3$ → $\infty$ is a branch point
.
