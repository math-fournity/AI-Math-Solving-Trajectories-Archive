**Claim:** Yes, it is possible. We give an explicit example with $n = 2$.

Define
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 5 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
B = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 5 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Step 1: $A$ is Hurwitz (all eigenvalues have negative real part).**

$A$ is upper triangular, so its eigenvalues are its diagonal entries: $\lambda_1
(A) = -1$, $\lambda_2(A) = -1$. Both have negative real part. ✓

**Step 2: $B$ is Hurwitz.**

$B$ is lower triangular, so its eigenvalues are its diagonal entries: $\lambda_1
(B) = -1$, $\lambda_2(B) = -1$. Both have negative real part. ✓

**Step 3: $A + B$ has an eigenvalue with non-negative real part.**

$$
A + B = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 5 \\ 5 & -2 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The characteristic polynomial is
$$
\det(A+B - \lambda I) = (-2-\lambda)^2 - 25 = \lambda^2 + 4\lambda - 21 = (\lamb
da - 3)(\lambda + 7).
$$

So the eigenvalues of $A+B$ are $\lambda = 3$ and $\lambda = -7$. The eigenvalue
 $3$ has positive (hence non-negative) real part. ✓

**Conclusion.** Both $A$ and $B$ have all eigenvalues with strictly negative rea
l parts, yet $A + B$ has an eigenvalue $3 \geq 0$. This shows that the set of Hu
rwitz matrices is **not** closed under addition, and the answer to the question
is **yes**.

**Remark on why this happens.** Hurwitz stability of a matrix $M$ is equivalent
to the existence of a (Lyapunov) positive-definite matrix $P_M$ such that $M^\to
p P_M + P_M M \prec 0$. For $A$ and $B$ individually, the witnessing Lyapunov ma
trices $P_A$ and $P_B$ need not coincide. The sum $A+B$ is Hurwitz only if there
 is a *single* $P$ serving both simultaneously — a much stronger requirement. In
 the example above, $A$ and $B$ are each stable (in fact, each is a Jordan block
 with eigenvalue $-1$), but their large symmetric off-diagonal entries $5$ and $
5$ combine constructively in $A+B$, pushing one eigenvalue up to $+3$ while the
other drops to $-7$. The trace is preserved ($\operatorname{tr}(A+B) = -4 = \ope
ratorname{tr}(A) + \operatorname{tr}(B)$), but the eigenvalues spread apart, wit
h one crossing into the right half-plane.
