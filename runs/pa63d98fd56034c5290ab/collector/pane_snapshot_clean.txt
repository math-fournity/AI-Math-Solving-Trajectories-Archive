**Answer: Yes, such sequences exist.**

**Construction.** Define
$$a_n = 1, \qquad b_n = -1 + \left(\tfrac{1}{3}\right)^n \quad (n \ge 1).$$

**Verification.** Recall (Cauchy–Hadamard) that the radius of convergence of $\s
um c_n z^n$ is $R = 1/\limsup_{n\to\infty}|c_n|^{1/n}$.

*Series $\sum a_n z^n$:* Since $a_n = 1$, we have $|a_n|^{1/n} = 1$ for all $n$,
 so $\limsup |a_n|^{1/n} = 1$, giving radius $R_a = 1$. ✓

*Series $\sum b_n z^n$:* We have $b_n = -1 + (1/3)^n \to -1$, so $|b_n| \to 1$.
Consequently $|b_n|^{1/n} \to 1$ (since for any sequence $x_n \to 1$ with $x_n >
 0$ eventually, $x_n^{1/n} \to 1$). Hence $\limsup |b_n|^{1/n} = 1$, giving radi
us $R_b = 1$. ✓

*Series $\sum (a_n+b_n) z^n$:* We have
$$a_n + b_n = 1 + \left(-1 + \left(\tfrac{1}{3}\right)^n\right) = \left(\tfrac{1
}{3}\right)^n.$$
Therefore
$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty (a_n+b_n) z^n = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \left(\tfrac{z}{3}\right)^
n = \frac{z/3}{1 - z/3},$$
which converges for $|z/3| < 1$, i.e. $|z| < 3$. The radius of convergence is $R
_{a+b} = 3$. ✓

**Why this works (intuition).** The radius of convergence of a sum is governed b
y $\limsup |a_n+b_n|^{1/n}$, which can be *strictly smaller* than $\max(\limsup|
a_n|^{1/n}, \limsup|b_n|^{1/n})$ when $a_n$ and $b_n$ cancel each other to expon
ential precision. Here $a_n = 1$ and $b_n = -1 + (1/3)^n$ agree to leading order
 (both tend to $\pm 1$ in magnitude), so their sum is exponentially small, of or
der $(1/3)^n$, producing a larger radius of 3. Each individual sequence still ha
s coefficients of order 1, so each has radius 1.

Thus the required complex sequences exist. $\blacksquare$
