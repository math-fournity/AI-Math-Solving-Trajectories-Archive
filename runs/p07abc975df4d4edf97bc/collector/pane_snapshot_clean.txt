## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, the sequence $\{f_n\}$ converges pointwise to a function $g: X \
to \mathbb{R}$.

**Construction of $g$.** For each $x \in X$, the sequence $\{f_n(x)\}_{n \ge 1}$
 is, by hypothesis, a Cauchy sequence in $\mathbb{R}$. Since $\mathbb{R}$ is com
plete (every Cauchy sequence in $\mathbb{R}$ converges to a limit in $\mathbb{R}
$), there exists a real number, which we denote $g(x) \in \mathbb{R}$, such that

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x) = g(x).$$

This defines a function $g: X \to \mathbb{R}$ pointwise. By construction, for ev
ery $x \in X$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $N(x, \varepsilon)$ such
 that for all $n \ge N(x,\varepsilon)$,

$$|f_n(x) - g(x)| < \varepsilon.$$

This is precisely the definition of pointwise convergence of $\{f_n\}$ to $g$.

**Justification summary.** The only ingredient needed is the completeness of the
 codomain $\mathbb{R}$. The hypothesis guarantees that each pointwise sequence $
\{f_n(x)\}$ is Cauchy; completeness of $\mathbb{R}$ upgrades each Cauchy sequenc
e to a convergent one, yielding the pointwise limit $g(x)$ at every $x \in X$.

**Remark (measurability is not needed for convergence).** The measurability of t
he $f_n$ and the measure $\mu$ play no role in establishing pointwise convergenc
e — the argument is purely pointwise and relies only on the completeness of $\ma
thbb{R}$. (Measurability would be relevant for subsequent questions, e.g., wheth
er $g$ itself is measurable — which it is, as a pointwise limit of measurable fu
nctions — but that is beyond what the question asks.)
