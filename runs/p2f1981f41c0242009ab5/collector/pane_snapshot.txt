## Proof

**The answer is yes: $F$ is surjective for all $n, m > 1$.**

The proof uses elementary matrices. For a commutative ring $R$ and $1 \le i \ne
j \le n$, let $E_{ij}(a)$ denote the matrix differing from $I_n$ only in the $(i
,j)$-entry, which is $a \in R$. Each $E_{ij}(a) \in \mathrm{SL}_n(R)$; the subgr
oup they generate is $E_n(R) \subseteq \mathrm{SL}_n(R)$.

The argument has three steps.

---

### Step 1: A key identity — $\mathrm{diag}(a, a^{-1}) \in E_2(R)$ for any commu
tative ring $R$ and unit $a \in R^\times$

We verify directly:

$$E_{12}(a)\,E_{21}(-a^{-1})\,E_{12}(a) = \begin{pmatrix}1&a\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\b
egin{pmatrix}1&0\\-a^{-1}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&a\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = \
begin{pmatrix}0&a\\-a^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix},$$

since the $(1,1)$-entry of the first product is $1 - a\cdot a^{-1} = 0$, and the
 final $(2,2)$-entry is $-a^{-1}\cdot a + 1 = 0$. Likewise:

$$E_{12}(-1)\,E_{21}(1)\,E_{12}(-1) = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.$$

Multiplying:

$$\begin{pmatrix}0&a\\-a^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix
} = \begin{pmatrix}a&0\\0&a^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\mathrm{diag}(a,\, a^{-1}) = E_{12}(a)\,E_{21}(-a^{-1})\,E_{12}(a)\,E_{
12}(-1)\,E_{21}(1)\,E_{12}(-1) \in E_2(R).}$$

All six factors are defined over $R$ since $a^{-1} \in R$. $\square$

---

### Step 2: $\mathrm{SL}_n(R) = E_n(R)$ for $n \ge 2$ when $R$ is a local ring

We proceed by induction on $n$.

**Base case $n=2$.** Let $A \in \mathrm{SL}_2(R)$. Since $\det A = 1$ is a unit,
 $A \in \mathrm{GL}_2(R)$, so the first column $(a_{11}, a_{21})^T$ is unimodula
r (its entries generate the unit ideal). In a local ring, the non-units form the
 unique maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$; since the entries generate $R$, at least o
ne is a unit. If $a_{21}$ is a unit, swap rows via $E_{12}(1)E_{21}(-1)E_{12}(1)
$ (a permutation matrix in $E_2(R)$). Now $a_{11}$ is a unit. Left-multiply by $
E_{21}(-a_{21}a_{11}^{-1})$ to zero out $a_{21}$, giving an upper triangular mat
rix $\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&b\\0&a_{11}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}$. Right-multiply by $E_
{12}(-ba_{11}^{-1})$ to zero out $b$, yielding $\mathrm{diag}(a_{11}, a_{11}^{-1
})$, which lies in $E_2(R)$ by Step 1. Hence $A \in E_2(R)$.

**Inductive step $n \ge 3$.** Let $A \in \mathrm{SL}_n(R)$. The first column is
unimodular; some entry $a_{i1}$ is a unit (local ring). Swap rows $1$ and $i$, t
hen use $E_{j1}(-a_{j1}a_{11}^{-1})$ for $j=2,\dots,n$ to zero out the rest of t
he first column. Use column operations $E_{j1}(-b_j a_{11}^{-1})$ (right multipl
ication) to zero out the first row. The result is $\mathrm{diag}(a_{11}, A')$ wi
th $A' \in \mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(R)$, $\det A' = a_{11}^{-1}$. Write $A' = \mathrm{d
iag}(a_{11}^{-1}, 1, \dots, 1)\cdot A''$ with $A'' \in \mathrm{SL}_{n-1}(R)$. Th
en:

$$\mathrm{diag}(a_{11}, A') = \underbrace{\mathrm{diag}(a_{11},\, a_{11}^{-1},\,
 1, \dots, 1)}_{\in\, E_n(R) \text{ by Step 1}} \cdot \underbrace{\mathrm{diag}(
1,\, A'')}_{\in\, E_n(R) \text{ by induction}}.$$

So $A \in E_n(R)$. $\square$

---

### Step 3: $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}) = E_n(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})
$ and $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{Z}) = E_n(\mathbb{Z})$ for $n \ge 2$

**For $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$:** Factor $m = p_1^{k_1}\cdots p_r^{k_r}$. By the
 Chinese Remainder Theorem, $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \cong \prod_i \mathbb{Z}/p_i
^{k_i}\mathbb{Z}$, so $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}) \cong \prod_i \math
rm{SL}_n(\mathbb{Z}/p_i^{k_i}\mathbb{Z})$ and $E_n(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}) \cong
 \prod_i E_n(\mathbb{Z}/p_i^{k_i}\mathbb{Z})$. Each $\mathbb{Z}/p_i^{k_i}\mathbb
{Z}$ is a **local ring** (unique maximal ideal $p_i\mathbb{Z}/p_i^{k_i}\mathbb{Z
}$), so Step 2 gives $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{Z}/p_i^{k_i}\mathbb{Z}) = E_n(\mathb
b{Z}/p_i^{k_i}\mathbb{Z})$. Taking the product: $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{Z}/m\math
bb{Z}) = E_n(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})$.

**For $\mathbb{Z}$:** $\mathbb{Z}$ is a Euclidean domain. The same Gaussian-elim
ination argument applies: the first column of $A \in \mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{Z})$
is unimodular (entries have gcd $1$), and the Euclidean algorithm (via elementar
y row operations $E_{ij}(q)$ with $q = \lfloor a_{j1}/a_{i1}\rfloor$) reduces it
 to $(\pm 1, 0, \dots, 0)^T$. If the sign is $-1$, left-multiply by $\mathrm{dia
g}(-1,-1,1,\dots,1) \in E_n(\mathbb{Z})$ (Step 1 with $a=-1$). The rest proceeds
 as in Step 2 by induction. Hence $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{Z}) = E_n(\mathbb{Z})$.

---

### Conclusion: $F$ is surjective

The reduction map $F: \mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{Z}) \to \mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{Z}/m\m
athbb{Z})$ sends $E_{ij}(a) \mapsto E_{ij}(\bar{a})$. Since every residue class
$\bar{a} \in \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ lifts to some $a \in \mathbb{Z}$, the image
 $F(\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{Z}))$ contains every elementary matrix $E_{ij}(\bar{a}
)$. By Step 3:

$$F(\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{Z})) \supseteq E_n(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}) = \mathrm{S
L}_n(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}).$$

Therefore $F$ is **surjective** for all $n, m > 1$. $\blacksquare$
