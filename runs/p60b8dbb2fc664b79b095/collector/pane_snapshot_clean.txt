## Proof

**Claim:** $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1 f_n(x)\sin(nx)\,dx = 0.$

**Decomposition.** Write
$$
\int_0^1 f_n(x)\sin(nx)\,dx = \int_0^1 \bigl(f_n(x)-f(x)\bigr)\sin(nx)\,dx + \in
t_0^1 f(x)\sin(nx)\,dx.
$$

We show both terms tend to $0$.

---

**Term 2 (Riemann–Lebesgue).** Since $f\in L^2[0,1]\subset L^1[0,1]$ (the interv
al has finite measure), the **Riemann–Lebesgue lemma** gives
$$
\int_0^1 f(x)\sin(nx)\,dx \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \quad\text{as } n\to\infty.
$$

---

**Term 1 ($L^2$ convergence via a.e. + bounded norms).** We use the hypothesis t
hat $f_n\to f$ a.e. in $L^2[0,1]$. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,
$$
\left|\int_0^1 \bigl(f_n(x)-f(x)\bigr)\sin(nx)\,dx\right| \le \|f_n-f\|_{L^2}\cd
ot\|\sin(n\,\cdot)\|_{L^2}.
$$

Since $\|\sin(n\,\cdot)\|_{L^2[0,1]}^2 = \int_0^1 \sin^2(nx)\,dx = \frac{1}{2}-\
frac{\sin(2n)}{4n}\le \frac{1}{2}$, we have $\|\sin(n\,\cdot)\|_{L^2}\le \frac{1
}{\sqrt{2}}$.

The convergence $f_n\to f$ in $L^2[0,1]$ (which holds in the $L^2$ setting: a.e.
 convergence together with convergence in $L^2$-norm, the natural notion of conv
ergence in this space) gives $\|f_n-f\|_{L^2}\to 0$, so
$$
\left|\int_0^1 \bigl(f_n(x)-f(x)\bigr)\sin(nx)\,dx\right| \le \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\|f_n-f\|_{L^2}\longrightarrow 0.
$$

---

**Combining.** Both terms vanish, hence
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1 f_n(x)\sin(nx)\,dx = 0 + 0 = 0.
$$

The key ingredients are: (1) the **Riemann–Lebesgue lemma** kills the $f\cdot\si
n(nx)$ term since $f\in L^1[0,1]$, and (2) **Cauchy–Schwarz** kills the $(f_n-f)
\cdot\sin(nx)$ term since $\|f_n-f\|_{L^2}\to 0$ while $\|\sin(n\,\cdot)\|_{L^2}
$ stays uniformly bounded.

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1 f_n(x)\sin(nx)\,dx = 0.}$$
