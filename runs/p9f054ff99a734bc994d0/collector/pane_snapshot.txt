Looking at this problem, I need to find a prime $p$ such that for every $k \in \
{1, 2, \ldots, 10\}$, there exist integers $a_k, b_k$ with $p = a_k^2 + k \cdot
b_k^2$.

## Deriving Necessary Congruence Conditions

**Layer k=1** ($p = a^2 + b^2$): By Fermat's theorem on sums of two squares, an
odd prime is representable iff $p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$.

**Layer k=2** ($p = a^2 + 2b^2$): A prime $p$ is representable iff $p \equiv 1,
3 \pmod{8}$. Combined with $p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, this forces $p \equiv 1 \pmod{
8}$.

**Layer k=3** ($p = a^2 + 3b^2$): Representable iff $p = 3$ or $p \equiv 1 \pmod
{3}$. Since $p=3$ fails k=1, we need $p \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$.

**Layer k=4** ($p = a^2 + 4b^2 = a^2 + (2b)^2$): Any $p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ works
 (in the two-square representation, one term is even). Already satisfied.

**Layer k=8** ($p = a^2 + 8b^2 = a^2 + 2(2b)^2$): Requires $p \equiv 1 \pmod{8}$
 (in the $x^2+2y^2$ representation, $y$ must be even, which happens iff $p \equi
v 1 \pmod 8$). Already satisfied.

**Layer k=9** ($p = a^2 + 9b^2 = a^2 + (3b)^2$): Requires $p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$
and $p \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$ (so that in the two-square representation, one term is
 divisible by 3). Already satisfied by $p \equiv 1 \pmod{24}$.

**Layer k=5** ($p = a^2 + 5b^2$): The form $x^2+5y^2$ has discriminant $-20$ and
 class number 2. By genus theory, primes represented by the principal form $x^2+
5y^2$ are exactly $p \equiv 1, 9 \pmod{20}$.

**Layer k=6** ($p = a^2 + 6b^2$): Discriminant $-24$, class number 2. Primes rep
resented by $x^2+6y^2$ are exactly $p \equiv 1, 7 \pmod{24}$. Since $p \equiv 1
\pmod{24}$, this is satisfied.

**Layer k=7** ($p = a^2 + 7b^2$): Discriminant $-28$, class number 1. All primes
 with $\left(\frac{-7}{p}\right) = 1$ are represented. Since $-7 \equiv 1 \pmod{
4}$, this gives $\left(\frac{p}{7}\right) = 1$, i.e., $p \equiv 1, 2, 4 \pmod{7}
$.

**Layer k=10** ($p = a^2 + 10b^2$): Discriminant $-40$, class number 2. Primes r
epresented by $x^2+10y^2$ are exactly $p \equiv 1, 9, 11, 19 \pmod{40}$. Since $
p \equiv 1 \pmod{8}$, this restricts to $p \equiv 1, 9 \pmod{40}$, which is alre
ady implied by $p \equiv 1, 9 \pmod{20}$.

## Combining All Conditions

The binding constraints reduce to:
- $p \equiv 1 \pmod{24}$ (from k=1,2,3,8)
- $p \equiv 1$ or $9 \pmod{20}$ (from k=5)
- $p \equiv 1, 2, 4 \pmod{7}$ (from k=7)

By CRT, $p \equiv 1 \pmod{24}$ and $p \equiv 1 \pmod{20}$ gives $p \equiv 1 \pmo
d{120}$; $p \equiv 1 \pmod{24}$ and $p \equiv 9 \pmod{20}$ gives $p \equiv 49 \p
mod{120}$.

Combining with $p \equiv 1, 2, 4 \pmod{7}$ yields six residue classes modulo $\t
ext{lcm}(120, 7) = 840$:

$$p \equiv 1,\; 121,\; 169,\; 289,\; 361,\; 529 \pmod{840}.$$
