# Solution

**Answer: Yes, such a Schwartz function $f$ exists.**

## Proof

The key fact is that the Fourier transform is a bijection on the Schwartz space.

**Definition.** The Schwartz space $\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$ consists of all smo
oth functions $\phi \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ such that for all multi-indices (h
ere, non-negative integers) $\alpha, \beta$:
$$\sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} \left| x^\alpha \phi^{(\beta)}(x) \right| < \infty.$$

**Fundamental Theorem (Fourier Inversion on $\mathcal{S}$).** The Fourier transf
orm
$$\mathcal{F} : \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}), \qquad \hat
{f}(\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\, e^{-2\pi i x \xi}\, dx$$
is a bijection (indeed, a linear homeomorphism) of $\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$ ont
o itself. Its inverse is given by
$$\mathcal{F}^{-1}(g)(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(\xi)\, e^{2\pi i x \xi}\, d
\xi,$$
and satisfies $\mathcal{F} \circ \mathcal{F}^{-1} = \mathcal{F}^{-1} \circ \math
cal{F} = \mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})}$.

*Sketch of the theorem:* One first shows that $\mathcal{F}$ maps $\mathcal{S}(\m
athbb{R})$ into $\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$ (using integration by parts: different
iation becomes multiplication by the variable and vice versa, both operations pr
eserving rapid decay). One then verifies the inversion formula on a dense subset
 (e.g., Gaussian functions, which are eigenfunctions of $\mathcal{F}$), and exte
nds by continuity. The map $\mathcal{F}^{-1}$ is seen to be a two-sided inverse,
 establishing bijectivity.

**Application to the problem.** We are given that $g$ is a Schwartz function (i.
e., $g \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$) with the additional properties that $g = 1$
 on $C$ and $g = 0$ outside $W$. These additional properties describe the *speci
fic values* $g$ takes, but they do not affect the abstract fact that $g \in \mat
hcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$.

Since $g \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$ and $\mathcal{F} : \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
 \to \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$ is a bijection, there exists a **unique** $f \in \
mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$ such that $\hat{f} = g$, namely:
$$f = \mathcal{F}^{-1}(g) \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}).$$

This $f$ is a Schwartz function (since $\mathcal{F}^{-1}$ maps $\mathcal{S}(\mat
hbb{R})$ into $\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$), and by construction $\hat{f} = g$.

**Remark on the hypotheses.** The conditions "$g = 1$ on $C$" and "$g = 0$ outsi
de $W$" (with $C$ compact, $W$ open, $C \subset W$) ensure that such a $g$ can b
e constructed—for instance, via a smooth partition of unity or a standard mollif
ier/bump-function argument yielding a $C_c^\infty$ function (which is automatica
lly Schwartz). However, for the question at hand, only the fact that $g \in \mat
hcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$ matters: the Fourier transform's bijectivity on $\mathcal{S
}(\mathbb{R})$ guarantees the existence of $f$ regardless of the particular shap
e of $g$.

**Conclusion.** Yes, there exists a Schwartz function $f$ whose Fourier transfor
m is $g$. This follows immediately from the bijectivity of the Fourier transform
 on the Schwartz space $\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$.
