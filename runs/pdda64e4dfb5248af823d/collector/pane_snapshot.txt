## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, $(x_n)$ is necessarily bounded.

**Proof by contrapositive.** Suppose $(x_n)$ is unbounded. We construct a sequen
ce $(y_n)$ with $\sum_n y_n$ convergent but $\sum_n x_n y_n$ divergent, contradi
cting the hypothesis.

**Constructing the subsequence.** Since $(x_n)$ is unbounded, every tail is unbo
unded: for any $N$, $\sup_{n \ge N}|x_n| = \infty$ (removing finitely many terms
 from an unbounded sequence leaves it unbounded). Hence we may pick indices $n_1
 < n_2 < n_3 < \cdots$ with
$$|x_{n_k}| > 2^k \qquad (k = 1, 2, \dots).$$
In particular $x_{n_k} \neq 0$ for all $k$.

**Defining $(y_n)$.** Set $y_n = 0$ for $n \notin \{n_k\}$, and
$$y_{n_k} = \frac{1}{k \cdot x_{n_k}}.$$

**$\sum_n y_n$ converges.** We have
$$|y_{n_k}| = \frac{1}{k\,|x_{n_k}|} < \frac{1}{k \cdot 2^k}.$$
Thus $\sum_n |y_n| = \sum_k |y_{n_k}| \le \sum_k \frac{1}{k\,2^k} < \infty$, so
$\sum_n y_n$ converges absolutely (hence converges in $\mathbb{C}$).

**$\sum_n x_n y_n$ diverges.** For the chosen indices,
$$x_{n_k}\,y_{n_k} = x_{n_k}\cdot\frac{1}{k\,x_{n_k}} = \frac{1}{k},$$
and all other terms vanish. Therefore
$$\sum_n x_n y_n = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k} = +\infty,$$
the harmonic series, which diverges. Hence $\sum_n x_n y_n$ does not converge in
 $\mathbb{C}$.

**Conclusion.** The existence of such $(y_n)$ contradicts the hypothesis that $\
sum_n x_n y_n$ converges for every convergent $\sum_n y_n$. Therefore the assump
tion that $(x_n)$ is unbounded is false, and $(x_n)$ must be bounded. $\blacksqu
are$
