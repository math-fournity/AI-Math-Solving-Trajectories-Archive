## Proof

We claim the limit exists and equals $0$.

**Key tool: translation invariance of Lebesgue measure.** For every $h \in \math
bb{R}$ and every integrable function $\varphi$,
$$\int_{\mathbb{R}} \varphi(x-h)\,dx = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \varphi(x)\,dx,$$
i.e. $\|\varphi(\cdot - h)\|_1 = \|\varphi\|_1$.

---

### Step 1. The result holds for $g \in C_c(\mathbb{R})$.

Let $g$ be continuous with compact support $K = \operatorname{supp} g$. Since $K
$ is compact, $g$ is uniformly continuous. Given $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $\del
ta > 0$ such that
$$|y - x| < \delta \implies |g(y) - g(x)| < \varepsilon.$$

For $|h| < \delta$ and every $x \in \mathbb{R}$, we have $|(x-h) - x| = |h| < \d
elta$, so
$$|g(x-h) - g(x)| < \varepsilon.$$

Moreover, $g(x-h) - g(x)$ vanishes outside $K + [-\delta, \delta] := \{k + t : k
 \in K,\ |t| \le \delta\}$, which is compact (hence of finite Lebesgue measure,
say $M$). Therefore, for $|h| < \delta$,
$$\int_{\mathbb{R}} |g(x-h) - g(x)|\,dx \le \varepsilon \cdot M.$$

Since $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|
g(x-h)-g(x)|\,dx = 0$.

---

### Step 2. Density of $C_c(\mathbb{R})$ in $L^1(\mathbb{R})$.

It is a standard fact that $C_c(\mathbb{R})$ is dense in $L^1(\mathbb{R})$: for
every $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $g \in C
_c(\mathbb{R})$ with
$$\|f - g\|_1 < \varepsilon.$$

---

### Step 3. Extension to all $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$.

Let $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ and $\varepsilon > 0$. Choose $g \in C_c(\mathbb{R})
$ with $\|f - g\|_1 < \varepsilon$. By the triangle inequality and translation i
nvariance (Step 1 tool),
$$\begin{aligned}
\int_{\mathbb{R}} |f(x-h) - f(x)|\,dx
&\le \int_{\mathbb{R}} |f(x-h) - g(x-h)|\,dx + \int_{\mathbb{R}} |g(x-h) - g(x)|
\,dx + \int_{\mathbb{R}} |g(x) - f(x)|\,dx \\
&= \|f - g\|_1 + \int_{\mathbb{R}} |g(x-h) - g(x)|\,dx + \|g - f\|_1 \\
&< 2\varepsilon + \int_{\mathbb{R}} |g(x-h) - g(x)|\,dx.
\end{aligned}$$

By Step 1, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $|h| < \delta$ implies $\int_{\ma
thbb{R}} |g(x-h) - g(x)|\,dx < \varepsilon$. Hence for $|h| < \delta$,
$$\int_{\mathbb{R}} |f(x-h) - f(x)|\,dx < 3\varepsilon.$$

Since $\varepsilon > 0$ was arbitrary,
$$\lim_{h \to 0} \int_{\mathbb{R}} |f(x-h) - f(x)|\,dx = 0.$$

---

### Conclusion

For every $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, the limit exists and
$$\boxed{\lim_{h\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x-h)-f(x)|\,dx = 0.}$$

This is the classical **continuity of translation in $L^1$** theorem.
