## Proof

**Claim:** No. In fact, *every* $(k+1)\times(k+1)$ principal submatrix must have
 a negative eigenvalue, so none of them can be positive semidefinite.

Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ real symmetric matrix with $k$ non-negative eigenvalue
s and $n-k$ negative eigenvalues. Order the eigenvalues
$$\lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \cdots \le \lambda_{n-k} < 0 \le \lambda_{n-k+1} \
le \cdots \le \lambda_n.$$
In particular $\lambda_{n-k}<0$.

Let $B$ be any $(k+1)\times(k+1)$ principal submatrix of $A$, with eigenvalues
$$\mu_1 \le \mu_2 \le \cdots \le \mu_{k+1}.$$

**Cauchy's Interlacing Theorem.** For an $m\times m$ principal submatrix of an $
n\times n$ symmetric matrix,
$$\lambda_i \;\le\; \mu_i \;\le\; \lambda_{i+n-m}, \qquad i=1,\dots,m.$$

Here $m=k+1$, so $n-m = n-k-1$. Applying the upper bound with $i=1$:
$$\mu_1 \;\le\; \lambda_{1+n-k-1} \;=\; \lambda_{n-k}.$$

Since $\lambda_{n-k}<0$, we obtain
$$\mu_1 \le \lambda_{n-k} < 0.$$

Thus every $(k+1)\times(k+1)$ principal submatrix $B$ has a strictly negative sm
allest eigenvalue $\mu_1$, and therefore $B$ is **not** positive semidefinite.

Since this argument applies to *every* choice of $(k+1)\times(k+1)$ principal su
bmatrix, it is impossible for all of them to be positive semidefinite. $\blacksq
