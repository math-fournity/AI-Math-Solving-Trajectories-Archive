## Proof

**Yes**, such a prime $t$ exists (indeed, infinitely many exist).

**Step 1: Convert Legendre symbol conditions to congruence conditions via quadra
tic reciprocity.**

Since $p \equiv 1 \pmod{8}$, we have $p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, so $\frac{p-1}{2}$ i
s even. By quadratic reciprocity, for any odd prime $t \neq p$:
$$\left(\frac{p}{t}\right) = \left(\frac{t}{p}\right) \cdot (-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}\
cdot\frac{t-1}{2}} = \left(\frac{t}{p}\right).$$

Since $q \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$, $\frac{q-1}{2}$ is odd. By quadratic reciprocity:
$$\left(\frac{q}{t}\right) = \left(\frac{t}{q}\right) \cdot (-1)^{\frac{q-1}{2}\
cdot\frac{t-1}{2}} = \left(\frac{t}{q}\right)(-1)^{\frac{t-1}{2}}.$$

Thus $\left(\frac{q}{t}\right) = 1$ if and only if:
- $t \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ and $\left(\frac{t}{q}\right) = 1$, **or**
- $t \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$ and $\left(\frac{t}{q}\right) = -1$.

**Step 2: Construct a valid residue class modulo $4pq$.**

We seek a residue class $a \bmod{4pq}$ with $\gcd(a, 4pq) = 1$ satisfying:
1. $\left(\frac{a}{p}\right) = 1$ (i.e., $a$ is a quadratic residue mod $p$),
2. $a \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ and $\left(\frac{a}{q}\right) = 1$ (i.e., $a$ is a quad
ratic residue mod $q$).

By the Chinese Remainder Theorem, the residue class mod $4pq$ is determined inde
pendently by residues mod $4$, mod $p$, and mod $q$. The number of valid classes
 is:
$$1 \cdot \frac{p-1}{2} \cdot \frac{q-1}{2} \geq 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 1,$$
since $p \geq 3$ and $q \geq 3$ (both are odd primes), so each has at least one
quadratic residue.

Such a class $a$ satisfies $\gcd(a, 4pq) = 1$: the condition $a \equiv 1 \pmod{4
}$ ensures $a$ is odd (coprime to $4$), and $a$ being a nonzero quadratic residu
e mod $p$ (resp. mod $q$) ensures $\gcd(a, p) = 1$ (resp. $\gcd(a, q) = 1$).

**Step 3: Apply Dirichlet's theorem.**

By Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions, there are infinitel
y many primes $t$ with $t \equiv a \pmod{4pq}$.

For any such prime $t \neq p, q$ (which holds for all but finitely many), we ver
ify:
- $\left(\frac{p}{t}\right) = \left(\frac{t}{p}\right) = \left(\frac{a}{p}\right
) = 1$. ✓
- Since $t \equiv a \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, we have $(-1)^{\frac{t-1}{2}} = 1$, so $
\left(\frac{q}{t}\right) = \left(\frac{t}{q}\right) = \left(\frac{a}{q}\right) =
 1$. ✓

**Conclusion.** There exists a prime $t \neq p, q$ such that both $\left(\frac{p
}{t}\right) = 1$ and $\left(\frac{q}{t}\right) = 1$. In fact, infinitely many su
ch primes exist.

**Remark.** The given conditions $p \equiv 1 \pmod{8}$, $q \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$, a
nd $\left(\frac{p}{q}\right) = 1$ are not needed for the existence result — the
argument works for any two distinct odd primes. The condition $p \equiv 1 \pmod{
4}$ simplifies the reciprocity step for $p$, but even without it, one would hand
le the additional sign factor by including it in the case analysis, yielding the
 same conclusion via Dirichlet's theorem.
