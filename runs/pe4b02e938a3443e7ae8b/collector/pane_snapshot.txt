## Proof

Let $K$ be an imaginary quadratic field with ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$. S
uppose, for contradiction, that there exists $\alpha \in K$ such that:

1. $\alpha$ is **not** a unit in $\mathcal{O}_K$, and
2. $\alpha$ has finite multiplicative order, i.e., $\alpha^n = 1$ for some $n \i
n \mathbb{N}$.

**Step 1: $\alpha$ is an algebraic integer.**

Since $\alpha^n = 1$, the element $\alpha$ is a root of the polynomial $x^n - 1
\in \mathbb{Z}[x]$. This is a monic polynomial with integer coefficients. By def
inition, any root of a monic polynomial with integer coefficients is an **algebr
aic integer**.

**Step 2: $\alpha \in \mathcal{O}_K$.**

The ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ is precisely the set of all algebraic integ
ers that lie in $K$:
$$\mathcal{O}_K = \{\beta \in K : \beta \text{ is an algebraic integer}\}.$$
Since $\alpha \in K$ (given) and $\alpha$ is an algebraic integer (Step 1), we c
onclude $\alpha \in \mathcal{O}_K$.

**Step 3: $\alpha$ is a unit in $\mathcal{O}_K$.**

From $\alpha^n = 1$, we can write:
$$\alpha \cdot \alpha^{n-1} = 1.$$
Now $\alpha^{n-1}$ is a power of $\alpha$. Since $\alpha \in \mathcal{O}_K$ and
$\mathcal{O}_K$ is a ring (closed under multiplication), every power of $\alpha$
 lies in $\mathcal{O}_K$. In particular, $\alpha^{n-1} \in \mathcal{O}_K$.

Thus $\alpha$ has a multiplicative inverse $\alpha^{-1} = \alpha^{n-1}$ that lie
s in $\mathcal{O}_K$. By definition, $\alpha$ is a **unit** in $\mathcal{O}_K$.

**Step 4: Contradiction.**

Step 3 shows $\alpha$ is a unit in $\mathcal{O}_K$, which directly contradicts c
ondition (1) that $\alpha$ is not a unit. Therefore, no such element exists.

**Remark.** This argument does not use any special property of imaginary quadrat
ic fields — it applies to **any** number field $K$. The essential point is that
finite multiplicative order forces an element to be both an algebraic integer (h
ence in $\mathcal{O}_K$) and invertible within $\mathcal{O}_K$ (hence a unit). T
he two conditions "not a unit in $\mathcal{O}_K$" and "finite multiplicative ord
er" are mutually exclusive for any element of $K$.

**Conclusion:** No, there does **not** exist an element $\alpha \in K$ that is n
ot a unit in $\mathcal{O}_K$ and has finite multiplicative order. Any element of
 $K$ satisfying $\alpha^n = 1$ is necessarily a unit in $\mathcal{O}_K$.
