# Solution

**Answer:** The largest such integer is $k = n$.

## Proof

We prove that $k = n$ is both achievable (lower bound) and optimal (upper bound)
.

### Upper Bound: $k \leq n$

We show that no $k \geq n+1$ can work. Suppose for contradiction that there exis
ts a finite collection $\mathcal{F}$ of closed convex subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$
with the $k$-intersection property but not the $(k+1)$-intersection property, wh
ere $k \geq n+1$.

Since $\mathcal{F}$ does not have the $(k+1)$-intersection property, it must con
tain at least $k+1$ sets (otherwise the $(k+1)$-intersection property would hold
 vacuously). Let $|\mathcal{F}| = m \geq k+1 \geq n+2$.

Since $\mathcal{F}$ has the $k$-intersection property and $m \geq k > n$, every
$k$-element subcollection has non-empty intersection. Because $n+1 \leq k$, any
$(n+1)$-element subcollection can be extended to a $k$-element subcollection (si
nce $m \geq k$), and the intersection of the $(n+1)$ sets contains the intersect
ion of the $k$ sets, hence is non-empty. Thus $\mathcal{F}$ has the $(n+1)$-inte
rsection property.

By **Helly's Theorem** (for finite collections of convex sets in $\mathbb{R}^n$)
, the $(n+1)$-intersection property implies that the entire collection $\mathcal
{F}$ has non-empty intersection. Since $m \geq k+1$, every $(k+1)$-element subco
llection has non-empty intersection — contradicting the assumption that $\mathca
l{F}$ does not have the $(k+1)$-intersection property. $\square$

### Lower Bound: $k = n$ is achievable

We construct a finite collection of $n+1$ closed convex sets in $\mathbb{R}^n$ s
uch that every $n$ of them intersect, but all $n+1$ do not.

Let $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_{n+1}$ be the vertices of a simplex in $\mathbb{R}^n$ (
i.e., $n+1$ points in general position: affinely independent). For each $i \in \
{1, \ldots, n+1\}$, define
$$E_i = \operatorname{conv}\bigl(\{p_1, \ldots, p_{n+1}\} \setminus \{p_i\}\bigr
),$$
the $(n-1)$-dimensional face of the simplex opposite to vertex $p_i$. Each $E_i$
 is a closed convex set (a simplex of dimension $n-1$).

**Every $n$ of the $E_i$ intersect:** Consider any $n$-element subcollection $\{
E_{i_1}, \ldots, E_{i_n}\}$. There is exactly one index $j \in \{1, \ldots, n+1\
}$ not among $\{i_1, \ldots, i_n\}$. The vertex $p_j$ belongs to every $E_{i_\el
l}$ (since $j \neq i_\ell$ for all $\ell$), so $p_j \in \bigcap_{\ell=1}^{n} E_{
i_\ell}$. In fact, this intersection equals $\{p_j\}$, which is non-empty.

**All $n+1$ do not intersect:** Suppose $x \in \bigcap_{i=1}^{n+1} E_i$. Since $
x \in E_i$, we can write $x = \sum_{j \neq i} \lambda^{(i)}_j p_j$ with $\lambda
^{(i)}_j \geq 0$ and $\sum_{j \neq i} \lambda^{(i)}_j = 1$. By the affine indepe
ndence of $\{p_1, \ldots, p_{n+1}\}$, the barycentric coordinates of $x$ with re
spect to the simplex are unique. The condition $x \in E_i$ forces the $i$-th bar
ycentric coordinate of $x$ to be $0$. Since this holds for every $i$, all baryce
ntric coordinates are $0$, which is impossible for a point (they must sum to $1$
). Therefore $\bigcap_{i=1}^{n+1} E_i = \emptyset$.

Thus the collection $\{E_1, \ldots, E_{n+1}\}$ has the $n$-intersection property
 but not the $(n+1)$-intersection property. $\square$

### Conclusion

Combining both parts, the largest integer $k$ such that there exists a finite co
llection of closed convex subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the $k$-intersection pr
operty but not the $(k+1)$-intersection property is:

$$\boxed{k = n}.$$
