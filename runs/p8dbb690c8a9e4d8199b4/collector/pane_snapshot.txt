## Proof

**Setup.** Let $X = \mathbb{R}$ equipped with the co-countable topology: $U \sub
seteq X$ is open iff $U = \varnothing$ or $X \setminus U$ is countable. A neighb
orhood of $x$ is any set $X \setminus C$ where $C$ is countable and $x \notin C$
.

---

### 1. Convergent sequences

**Claim.** A sequence $(x_n)$ in $X$ converges to $x$ if and only if it is event
ually constant equal to $x$ (i.e., $\exists N\ \forall n \geq N,\ x_n = x$).

*Proof of "$\Leftarrow$".* If $x_n = x$ for all $n \geq N$, then for every open
neighborhood $U \ni x$, all $n \geq N$ satisfy $x_n = x \in U$. So $x_n \to x$.

*Proof of "$\Rightarrow$".* Suppose $x_n \to x$ but $(x_n)$ is **not** eventuall
y equal to $x$. Then the set $S = \{n \in \mathbb{N} : x_n \neq x\}$ is infinite
. Define
$$C = \{x_n : n \in S\} = \{x_n : x_n \neq x\}.$$
This is a countable set (the image of a countable set). Moreover $x \notin C$, s
ince every element of $C$ is some $x_n \neq x$. Hence $U = X \setminus C$ is an
open neighborhood of $x$. By convergence, $\exists N\ \forall n \geq N,\ x_n \in
 U = X \setminus C$, i.e., $x_n \notin C$ for all $n \geq N$. But for every $n \
in S$ (of which there are infinitely many, in particular some $n \geq N$), $x_n
\in C$ by definition — contradiction. $\square$

So the convergent sequences are exactly the **eventually constant** sequences.

---

### 2. Sequential closure

The sequential closure of $A \subseteq X$ is
$$\operatorname{scl}(A) = \{x \in X : \exists (x_n) \subseteq A,\ x_n \to x\}.$$

By part 1, any convergent sequence $(x_n) \to x$ is eventually equal to $x$. Sin
ce $(x_n) \subseteq A$, the eventual value $x$ must belong to $A$. Conversely, e
very $x \in A$ is the limit of the constant sequence $x, x, \dots \subseteq A$.
Therefore
$$\boxed{\operatorname{scl}(A) = A \quad \text{for every } A \subseteq X.}$$

---

### 3. Closure

Recall $x \in \overline{A}$ iff every open neighborhood of $x$ meets $A$, i.e.,
for every countable $C$ with $x \notin C$, $(X \setminus C) \cap A \neq \varnoth
ing$, equivalently $A \not\subseteq C$.

**Case 1: $A$ is countable.** If $x \notin A$, take $C = A$: this is countable,
$x \notin C$, and $A \subseteq C$, so $(X\setminus C)\cap A = \varnothing$, henc
e $x \notin \overline{A}$. If $x \in A$ then trivially $x \in \overline{A}$. Thu
s $\overline{A} = A$.

**Case 2: $A$ is uncountable.** For any $x \in X$ and any countable $C$ with $x
\notin C$, we have $A \not\subseteq C$ (since $A$ is uncountable but $C$ is coun
table). Hence $(X \setminus C) \cap A \neq \varnothing$, so $x \in \overline{A}$
. Since $x$ was arbitrary, $\overline{A} = X$.

$$\boxed{\overline{A} = \begin{cases} A & \text{if } A \text{ is countable}, \\
X & \text{if } A \text{ is uncountable}. \end{cases}}$$

---

### 4. Example where $\operatorname{scl}(A) \neq \overline{A}$

Take $A = [0,1] \subset X$. This set is uncountable, so by part 3,
$$\overline{A} = X = \mathbb{R}.$$
But by part 2,
$$\operatorname{scl}(A) = A = [0,1].$$
Since $[0,1] \neq \mathbb{R}$ (e.g., $2 \in \overline{A} \setminus \operatorname
{scl}(A)$), we have
$$\operatorname{scl}(A) \neq \overline{A}.$$

This confirms that $X$ is **not sequential**: the topology cannot be recovered f
rom its convergent sequences, and sequential closure fails to capture the full c
losure. The obstruction is precisely that the co-countable topology has "too few
" convergent sequences (only eventually constant ones) relative to its rich clos
ure structure (uncountable sets are dense).
