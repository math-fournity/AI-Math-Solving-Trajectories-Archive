## Answer: No.

We provide a counterexample. Let $f(t) = \sin(\pi a(t - x))$, where $x$ is the r
eal number given in the problem.

**Step 1: $f$ is bandlimited with $\hat{f}$ supported in $[-a/2, a/2]$.**

Using the convention $\hat{f}(\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\, e^{-2\pi i \
xi t}\, dt$, we write

$$f(t) = \sin(\pi a(t-x)) = \frac{e^{-i\pi a x}\, e^{2\pi i (a/2)\, t} \;-\; e^{
i\pi a x}\, e^{2\pi i (-a/2)\, t}}{2i}.$$

Since $\widehat{e^{2\pi i \alpha t}} = \delta(\xi - \alpha)$,

$$\hat{f}(\xi) = \frac{e^{-i\pi a x}\,\delta(\xi - a/2) \;-\; e^{i\pi a x}\,\del
ta(\xi + a/2)}{2i},$$

which is supported at $\{-a/2,\, a/2\} \subset [-a/2,\, a/2]$. Hence $f$ is band
limited with the required support.

**Step 2: $f$ vanishes on the lattice.**

For every integer $z$,

$$f\!\left(x + \frac{z}{a}\right) = \sin\!\left(\pi a \cdot \frac{z}{a}\right) =
 \sin(\pi z) = 0.$$

**Step 3: $f$ is not identically zero.**

$f$ is a non-constant sinusoid; for instance $f\!\left(x + \frac{1}{2a}\right) =
 \sin(\pi/2) = 1 \neq 0$.

**Conclusion.** We have exhibited a bandlimited function $f$ with $\operatorname
{supp}(\hat{f}) \subseteq [-a/2, a/2]$ that vanishes at every point of the latti
ce $\{x + z/a : z \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ yet is not identically zero. Therefore it do
es **not** follow that $f \equiv 0$.

**Remark (Nyquist-rate interpretation).** Under the convention above, the lattic
e spacing $1/a$ is exactly the Nyquist sampling interval for a signal bandlimite
d to $[-a/2, a/2]$. At this critical rate, uniqueness of reconstruction can fail
: the frequency components sitting at the endpoints $\xi = \pm a/2$ of the suppo
rt are aliased, so a pure sinusoid at the Nyquist frequency can vanish at every
sample point without being zero. If one additionally requires $f \in L^2(\mathbb
{R})$ (equivalently $\hat{f} \in L^2$), uniqueness is restored: setting $g(u) =
f(x + u/a)$ gives $\hat{g}(\xi) = a\, e^{2\pi i a x \xi}\, \hat{f}(a\xi)$, suppo
rted in $[-1/2, 1/2]$, and the condition $g(n)=0$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ say
s $\hat{g}$ is orthogonal to every $e^{2\pi i n \xi}$ in $L^2([-1/2,1/2])$; sinc
e $\{e^{2\pi i n \xi}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}$ is a complete orthonormal system on an
 interval of length $1$, this forces $\hat{g}=0$ a.e., hence $f \equiv 0$. The c
ounterexample exploits precisely the fact that $\sin(\pi a\, t) \notin L^2(\math
bb{R})$, so its Fourier transform is a distribution (point masses at $\pm a/2$)
rather than an $L^2$ function.
