**Answer: Yes.** A nil ideal need not be nilpotent. Here is a standard counterex
ample.

---

**Construction.** Let $k$ be a field and consider the polynomial ring in countab
ly many variables
$$S = k[x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots].$$
Let $J = (x_1^2,\, x_2^2,\, x_3^2,\, \ldots) \subset S$ be the ideal generated b
y the squares of all the variables, and set
$$R = S/J, \qquad I = (x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots) \subset R$$
(the image of the ideal $(x_1, x_2, \ldots)$ in the quotient). We claim:

1. **$I$ is nil:** every $f \in I$ satisfies $f^N = 0$ for some $N$.
2. **$I$ is not nilpotent:** $I^n \neq 0$ for every $n \geq 1$.

---

**Proof that $I$ is nil.** Take any $f \in I$. Since $f$ is a polynomial in $R$,
 it involves only finitely many variables, say $f = \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_i\, x_i$ (p
lus higher-degree terms, but every monomial in $f$ involves only $x_1, \ldots, x
_m$ for some $m$). Consider $f^{2m}$. Every monomial of degree $2m$ in the varia
bles $x_1, \ldots, x_m$ must contain some $x_i$ to a power $\geq 2$ (by the pige
onhole principle: $2m$ factors distributed among $m$ variables forces some varia
ble to appear at least twice). But $x_i^2 = 0$ in $R$ for every $i$, so every su
ch monomial vanishes. Hence
$$f^{2m} = 0 \quad \text{in } R.$$
Therefore every element of $I$ is nilpotent, i.e., $I$ is a nil ideal.

---

**Proof that $I^n \neq 0$ for all $n$.** Consider the monomial
$$\mu_n = x_1 x_2 x_3 \cdots x_n \in R.$$
This monomial lies in $I^n$ (it is a product of $n$ generators of $I$). We claim
 $\mu_n \neq 0$ in $R$.

The quotient $R = S/J$ kills exactly those polynomials in the ideal $J = (x_i^2
: i \geq 1)$. A $k$-basis for $R$ is given by the **squarefree** monomials in $S
$ (monomials where each variable appears with exponent $0$ or $1$), since every
non-squarefree monomial is divisible by some $x_i^2$ and hence lies in $J$.

The monomial $\mu_n = x_1 x_2 \cdots x_n$ is squarefree (each $x_i$ for $1 \leq
i \leq n$ appears exactly once), so it is **not** in $J$, and therefore represen
ts a nonzero element of $R$.

Since $\mu_n \in I^n$ and $\mu_n \neq 0$, we conclude
$$I^n \neq 0 \quad \text{for every } n \geq 1.$$

---

**Conclusion.** The ideal $I = (x_1, x_2, \ldots)$ in $R = k[x_1, x_2, \ldots]/(
x_1^2, x_2^2, \ldots)$ is nil (every element is nilpotent) but is not nilpotent
($I^n \neq 0$ for all $n$). This shows that the notions of **nil ideal** and **n
ilpotent ideal** are genuinely distinct: nilpotency requires a *uniform* bound o
n the nilpotency index across all elements of the ideal, while nilpotency of ind
ividual elements allows the index to grow without bound.
