**Answer: Yes, $B$ is separable.**

**Proof.**

Let $A$ be a Banach algebra and $S \subset A$ a separable subset. Let $B$ denote
 the Banach subalgebra generated by $S$, i.e., the smallest closed subalgebra of
 $A$ containing $S$:

$$B = \overline{\operatorname{alg}(S)},$$

where $\operatorname{alg}(S)$ is the (algebraic) subalgebra consisting of all fi
nite linear combinations of finite products of elements of $S$ (if a unit is req
uired, adjoin $1_A$; this adds a single element and does not affect the argument

**Step 1: Reduce to a countable dense subset.**

Since $S$ is separable, there exists a countable set $D \subset S$ that is dense
 in $S$ (closure taken in $A$). We claim:

$$\overline{\operatorname{alg}(S)} = \overline{\operatorname{alg}(D)}.$$

The inclusion $\overline{\operatorname{alg}(D)} \subseteq \overline{\operatornam
e{alg}(S)}$ is immediate since $D \subseteq S$ implies $\operatorname{alg}(D) \s
ubseteq \operatorname{alg}(S)$.

For the reverse inclusion, it suffices to show $\operatorname{alg}(S) \subseteq
\overline{\operatorname{alg}(D)}$. A typical element of $\operatorname{alg}(S)$
has the form

$$x = \sum_{i=1}^{m} c_i \, s_{i,1} s_{i,2} \cdots s_{i,k_i}, \qquad c_i \in \ma
thbb{C},\ s_{i,j} \in S.$$

For each $s_{i,j} \in S$, since $D$ is dense in $S$, choose a sequence $(d_{i,j}
^{(n)})_{n \geq 1} \subset D$ with $d_{i,j}^{(n)} \to s_{i,j}$ in norm. By the B
anach algebra inequality $\|xy\| \leq \|x\|\|y\|$, multiplication is jointly con
tinuous. A standard telescoping estimate gives:

$$\left\| s_{i,1}\cdots s_{i,k_i} - d_{i,1}^{(n)}\cdots d_{i,k_i}^{(n)} \right\|
 \leq \sum_{j=1}^{k_i} \left(\prod_{\ell < j} \|d_{i,\ell}^{(n)}\|\right) \|s_{i
,j} - d_{i,j}^{(n)}\| \left(\prod_{\ell > j} \|s_{i,\ell}\|\right) \xrightarrow{
n\to\infty} 0,$$

since each $\|d_{i,\ell}^{(n)}\|$ is eventually bounded (convergent sequences ar
e bounded) and each $\|s_{i,j} - d_{i,j}^{(n)}\| \to 0$. By continuity of additi
on and scalar multiplication,

$$\sum_{i=1}^{m} c_i \, d_{i,1}^{(n)} \cdots d_{i,k_i}^{(n)} \;\xrightarrow{n\to
\infty}\; x.$$

Each term in the sum lies in $\operatorname{alg}(D)$, so $x \in \overline{\opera
torname{alg}(D)}$. Hence $\operatorname{alg}(S) \subseteq \overline{\operatornam
e{alg}(D)}$, and taking closures yields $\overline{\operatorname{alg}(S)} \subse
teq \overline{\operatorname{alg}(D)}$.

**Step 2: $\operatorname{alg}(D)$ is separable.**

Let $\mathcal{P}$ be the set of all finite products of elements of $D$ (includin
g the empty product $1_A$ if working unitally). Since $D$ is countable, the set
of all finite sequences from $D$ is countable, so $\mathcal{P} = \{p_1, p_2, p_3
, \ldots\}$ is countable.

Now $\operatorname{alg}(D) = \operatorname{span}(\mathcal{P})$. Define

$$Q = \left\{ \sum_{j=1}^{m} q_j \, p_{i_j} : m \in \mathbb{N},\ q_j \in \mathbb
{Q}+i\mathbb{Q},\ i_j \in \mathbb{N} \right\}.$$

This is a countable set (countable union of countable sets). We claim $Q$ is den
se in $\operatorname{alg}(D)$. Indeed, any $x = \sum_{j=1}^{m} c_j p_{i_j} \in \
operatorname{alg}(D)$ with $c_j \in \mathbb{C}$ can be approximated by choosing
$q_j \in \mathbb{Q}+i\mathbb{Q}$ with $|c_j - q_j| < \varepsilon / (m \cdot \max
_j \|p_{i_j}\|)$, giving

$$\left\| x - \sum_{j=1}^{m} q_j p_{i_j} \right\| \leq \sum_{j=1}^{m} |c_j - q_j
| \|p_{i_j}\| < \varepsilon.$$

Thus $Q$ is a countable dense subset of $\operatorname{alg}(D)$, so $\operatorna
me{alg}(D)$ is separable.

**Step 3: Conclude.**

Since $Q$ is dense in $\operatorname{alg}(D)$ and $\operatorname{alg}(D)$ is den
se in $B = \overline{\operatorname{alg}(D)}$, the countable set $Q$ is dense in
$B$. Therefore $B$ is separable. $\blacksquare$
