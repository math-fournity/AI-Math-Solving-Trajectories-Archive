## Proof

**Setup and notation.** Let $T>0$ be a common period so that $\mathbf{A}(t+T)=\m
athbf{A}(t)$ for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$. Each entry $a_{ij}(t)$ is real-analytic o
n $\mathbb{R}$ and $T$-periodic. $\mathbf{A}(t)$ is Hermitian positive semi-defi
nite and rank deficient. $\lambda(t)$ denotes the largest eigenvalue, assumed **
simple** for every $t$. $\mathbf{x}(t)$ is a unit-norm eigenvector for $\lambda(
t)$, and $\mathbf{B}(t)=\lambda(t)\,\mathbf{x}(t)\mathbf{x}(t)^{H}$.

We must decide whether the Fourier series of each $b_{ij}(t)$ converges pointwis
e to $b_{ij}(t)$.

---

### Step 1. $\mathbf{B}(t)$ is $T$-periodic.

Since $\mathbf{A}(t+T)=\mathbf{A}(t)$, the spectra coincide: the largest eigenva
lue satisfies $\lambda(t+T)=\lambda(t)$. Any unit eigenvector $\mathbf{x}(t+T)$
for $\lambda(t+T)=\lambda(t)$ lies in the one-dimensional eigenspace of $\mathbf
{A}(t)$ for $\lambda(t)$, so $\mathbf{x}(t+T)=e^{i\theta(t)}\mathbf{x}(t)$ for s
ome real phase. The rank-one projector $\mathbf{x}(t)\mathbf{x}(t)^{H}$ is gauge
-invariant under $\mathbf{x}\mapsto e^{i\theta}\mathbf{x}$, hence
$$\mathbf{B}(t+T)=\lambda(t+T)\,\mathbf{x}(t+T)\mathbf{x}(t+T)^{H}=\lambda(t)\,\
mathbf{x}(t)\mathbf{x}(t)^{H}=\mathbf{B}(t).$$
So every $b_{ij}$ is $T$-periodic.

---

### Step 2. The spectral projector onto the top eigenspace is real-analytic.

This is the classical analytic perturbation result (Kato–Rellich). Because $\lam
bda(t)$ is a **simple** eigenvalue for every $t$, it is uniformly separated from
 the rest of the spectrum on each compact interval. Concretely, on any compact $
K\subset\mathbb{R}$ the gap
$$g(t)=\lambda(t)-\mu_2(t),\qquad \mu_2(t)=\text{second-largest eigenvalue},$$
is continuous and strictly positive, hence $\min_K g>0$. Therefore one can draw
a fixed contour $\Gamma$ (a small circle in $\mathbb{C}$) enclosing $\lambda(t)$
 and no other eigenvalue, uniformly for $t\in K$. The Riesz spectral projector i
s
$$\mathbf{P}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}(z\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}(t))^{-1}\,
dz.$$
The resolvent $(z\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}(t))^{-1}$ is jointly real-analytic in $(z
,t)$ on a neighborhood of $\Gamma\times K$ (entries of $\mathbf{A}$ are real-ana
lytic, and inversion is analytic away from poles, which $\Gamma$ avoids by const
ruction). Integrating an analytic function over a fixed contour preserves analyt
icity, so $\mathbf{P}(t)$ is real-analytic on $K$. Since $K$ is arbitrary, $\mat
hbf{P}(t)$ is real-analytic on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

Note $\mathbf{P}(t)=\mathbf{x}(t)\mathbf{x}(t)^{H}$ is exactly the rank-one proj
ector onto the top eigenspace, independent of the phase choice for $\mathbf{x}$.

---

### Step 3. $\mathbf{B}(t)$ is real-analytic.

The largest eigenvalue is recovered from the projector by the trace formula
$$\lambda(t)=\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\mathbf{A}(t)\,\mathbf{P}(t)\big),$$
which is a product and trace of real-analytic matrix-valued functions, hence rea
l-analytic. Consequently
$$\mathbf{B}(t)=\lambda(t)\,\mathbf{P}(t)$$
is real-analytic as a matrix-valued function, and each entry $b_{ij}(t)$ is real
-analytic on $\mathbb{R}$.

---

### Step 4. Real-analytic + periodic $\Rightarrow$ absolute (hence pointwise) Fo
urier convergence.

A real-analytic $T$-periodic function $f$ extends holomorphically to a strip $\{
z:|\operatorname{Im}z|<\rho\}$ for some $\rho>0$ (real-analyticity on $\mathbb{R
}$ plus periodicity gives a uniform radius of analyticity on each period, and by
 compactness of a period this radius is bounded below). Its Fourier coefficients
 therefore decay exponentially:
$$|\hat{f}_k|\le C\,e^{-\rho'|k|}\quad\text{for some }\rho'>0,\ C<\infty.$$
Hence $\sum_k |\hat{f}_k|<\infty$: the Fourier series converges **absolutely and
 uniformly** on $\mathbb{R}$, and in particular **converges pointwise** to $f(t)
$ at every $t$.

Applying this to each $b_{ij}(t)$, which is real-analytic and $T$-periodic by St
eps 1–3, we obtain that the Fourier series of $b_{ij}(t)$ converges to $b_{ij}(t
)$ for every $t\in\mathbb{R}$.

---

### Remark on the role of the hypotheses.

- **Simplicity of $\lambda(t)$** is essential: it guarantees the spectral projec
tor is analytic (no eigenvalue crossing). Without it, $\mathbf{P}(t)$ could fail
 to be analytic even when $\mathbf{A}(t)$ is, e.g. at eigenvalue collisions wher
e the projector may be merely continuous or even discontinuous in a choice of br
anch.
- **Analyticity of $\mathbf{A}(t)$** propagates to $\mathbf{B}(t)$ through the a
nalytic functional calculus; mere $C^\infty$ would not suffice for the exponenti
al Fourier decay used above (though $C^2$ would already give uniform convergence
 by Dirichlet–Jordan-type results).
- **Rank deficiency** of $\mathbf{A}(t)$ is consistent with the setup but does n
ot obstruct the argument: it only concerns the null space, while the top eigenva
lue is simple and separated from the rest of the spectrum regardless of how many
 zero eigenvalues exist.

---

### Conclusion.

**Yes.** The Fourier series of each entry $b_{ij}(t)$ of $\mathbf{B}(t)$ converg
es pointwise to $b_{ij}(t)$ for every $t\in\mathbb{R}$. In fact the convergence
is absolute and uniform, because $\mathbf{B}(t)$ is real-analytic and periodic —
 analyticity following from the simplicity of the top eigenvalue via the analyti
c spectral projection theorem, and periodicity following from gauge invariance o
f the rank-one projector.
