Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether a $C^\infty$ function exist
s near $x=c$ that oscillates above and below $f(c)$ in every one-sided neighborh
ood of $c$.

**Answer: Yes, such a function exists.**

## Proof

Take $c = 0$ and define:
$$f(x) = \begin{cases} e^{-1/x^2}\sin(1/x), & x \neq 0 \\ 0, & x = 0 \end{cases}
$$

### Step 1: $f$ is $C^\infty$ at $0$ with $f^{(n)}(0)=0$ for all $n$.

The function $g(x) = e^{-1/x^2}$ (for $x\neq 0$), $g(0)=0$, is the classic smoot
h flat function: $g^{(n)}(0)=0$ for all $n$, and each $g^{(n)}(x) = Q_n(1/x)\,e^
{-1/x^2}$ for some polynomial $Q_n$.

The derivatives of $h(x)=\sin(1/x)$ (for $x\neq 0$) are of the form $R_n(1/x)\si
n(1/x)$ or $R_n(1/x)\cos(1/x)$ for polynomials $R_n$.

By the product rule, every derivative $f^{(n)}(x)$ for $x\neq 0$ is a finite sum
 of terms of the form $S(1/x)\,e^{-1/x^2}\sin(1/x)$ or $S(1/x)\,e^{-1/x^2}\cos(1
/x)$, where $S$ is a polynomial. Since $e^{-1/x^2}$ decays faster than any polyn
omial in $1/x$ grows, every such term $\to 0$ as $x\to 0$.

**Induction on $n$:** Base case $f(0)=0$. Suppose $f^{(k)}(0)=0$ and $f^{(k)}$ i
s continuous at $0$ for all $k\le n$. Then:
$$f^{(n+1)}(0) = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{f^{(n)}(x)-f^{(n)}(0)}{x} = \lim_{x\to 0}\fr
ac{f^{(n)}(x)}{x}.$$
Since $f^{(n)}(x)$ is a sum of terms $S(1/x)e^{-1/x^2}\sin(1/x)$ (or $\cos$), di
viding by $x$ gives terms $\frac{1}{x}S(1/x)e^{-1/x^2}\sin(1/x)$, which is again
 of the same form (a polynomial in $1/x$ times $e^{-1/x^2}$ times a bounded trig
 factor), hence $\to 0$. So $f^{(n+1)}(0)=0$, and $f^{(n+1)}$ is continuous at $
0$ since $f^{(n+1)}(x)\to 0$ as shown above.

Thus $f\in C^\infty$ in a neighborhood of $0$, with $f^{(n)}(0)=0$ for all $n\ge
 0$.

### Step 2: $f$ oscillates above and below $f(0)=0$ in every one-sided neighborh
ood.

For any $\delta>0$, consider the right side $(0,\delta)$. The function $\sin(1/x
)$ attains the value $+1$ at $x = 1/(2k\pi + \pi/2)$ and $-1$ at $x=1/(2k\pi+3\p
i/2)$ for all sufficiently large integers $k$. For large enough $k$, both points
 lie in $(0,\delta)$. At these points, $e^{-1/x^2}>0$, so:
- $f\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2k\pi+\pi/2}\right) = e^{-1/x^2}\cdot(+1) > 0 = f(0)$,
- $f\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2k\pi+3\pi/2}\right) = e^{-1/x^2}\cdot(-1) < 0 = f(0)$.

So there exist $x, x'\in(0,\delta)$ with $f(x)>f(0)$ and $f(x')<f(0)$.

The left side $(-\delta,0)$ is identical: $\sin(1/x)$ oscillates the same way fo
r $x<0$, and $e^{-1/x^2}>0$, giving points with $f>0$ and $f<0$.

### Conclusion

The function $f(x)=e^{-1/x^2}\sin(1/x)$ (with $f(0)=0$) is infinitely differenti
able in a neighborhood of $c=0$ and satisfies: for every $\delta>0$, there exist
 $x,x'$ in both $(c-\delta,c)$ and $(c,c+\delta)$ with $f(x)>f(c)$ and $f(x')<f(
c)$. $\blacksquare$
