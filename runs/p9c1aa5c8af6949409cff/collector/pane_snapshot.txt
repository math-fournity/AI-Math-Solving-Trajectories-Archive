Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the limit exists given the
limsup condition.

**Answer: No.** The limit need not exist. I'll construct a counterexample.

## Counterexample

Define a piecewise linear "sawtooth" function $f:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$. Set $
s_1=0$ and $s_{k+1}=s_k+k+1/k$ for $k\ge1$. On each tooth $k$:
- **Rise:** On $[s_k,\, s_k+k]$, $f$ increases linearly from $0$ to $1$ (slope $
1/k$).
- **Fall:** On $[s_k+k,\, s_{k+1}]=[s_k+k,\, s_k+k+1/k]$, $f$ decreases linearly
 from $1$ to $0$ (slope $-k$).

Then $f$ is continuous, piecewise linear, satisfies $0\le f\le 1$, and is absolu
tely continuous on $[0,\infty)$. By absolute continuity, $\int_x^{x+h}f'(y)\,dy
= f(x+h)-f(x)$.

## Verification that $\limsup_{x\to\infty}[f(x+h)-f(x)]=0$ for every $h>0$

Fix $h>0$. As $x\to\infty$, $x$ lies in tooth $k$ with $k\to\infty$. For large $
k$, each tooth has length $k+1/k > h$, so $[x,x+h]$ intersects at most two conse
cutive teeth.

The derivative $f'$ takes values $+1/k$ (rise of tooth $k$), $-k$ (fall of tooth
 $k$), $+1/(k+1)$ (rise of tooth $k+1$), etc. The **positive** slopes in this ra
nge are at most $1/k$. Therefore:

$$\int_x^{x+h} f'(t)\,dt \le h\cdot\frac{1}{k} = \frac{h}{k}\xrightarrow{k\to\in
fty} 0,$$

since any portion of $[x,x+h]$ lying in a fall phase contributes **negatively**
(slope $-k$), only decreasing the integral. This gives $\limsup \le 0$.

For the reverse inequality: when $x$ lies in the interior of the rise of tooth $
k$ with both $x, x+h$ in the rise (possible for large $k$ since $h < k$), we get
 $\int_x^{x+h}f'\,dt = h/k > 0 \to 0$. Hence $\limsup \ge 0$.

Combining: $\limsup_{x\to\infty}[f(x+h)-f(x)] = 0$ for every $h>0$. ✓

## The limit does NOT exist

Take $x_k = s_k + k$ (the peak of tooth $k$), so $f(x_k)=1$. For large $k$, the
fall has length $1/k < h$, so $x_k+h$ lies in the rise of tooth $k+1$. Specifica
lly:

$$x_k + h - s_{k+1} = h - 1/k,$$

so $f(x_k+h) = \frac{h-1/k}{k+1} \to 0$. Therefore:

$$f(x_k+h)-f(x_k) \to 0 - 1 = -1.$$

Since $0\le f\le 1$, we always have $f(x+h)-f(x)\ge -1$, so $\liminf_{x\to\infty
}[f(x+h)-f(x)] = -1$.

## Conclusion

For every $h>0$:
$$\limsup_{x\to\infty}\int_x^{x+h}f'(y)\,dy = 0, \qquad \liminf_{x\to\infty}\int
_x^{x+h}f'(y)\,dy = -1.$$

Since $\limsup = 0 \ne -1 = \liminf$, the limit $\lim_{x\to\infty}\int_x^{x+h}f'
(y)\,dy$ **does not exist** (and in particular does not equal zero).

The non-negativity of $f$ prevents the limsup from being positive (the function
cannot sustain increases), but it does **not** prevent sharp decreases—after whi
ch $f\ge0$ allows recovery, but the recovery is slow (slope $\to 0$), producing
persistent negative excursions in $f(x+h)-f(x)$.
