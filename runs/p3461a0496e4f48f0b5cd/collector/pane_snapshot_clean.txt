## Proof

We compute $\int_S \omega$ where $\omega = dx \wedge dy$ and $S$ is the surface
of the ellipsoid $\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}+\frac{z^2}{c^2}=1$.

### Method 1: Stokes' Theorem

The ellipsoid surface $S = \partial V$ is the boundary of the solid ellipsoid $V
 = \{\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}+\frac{z^2}{c^2} \le 1\}$. The 2-form $\omeg
a = dx \wedge dy$ is **closed**, since

$$d\omega = d(dx \wedge dy) = d^2x \wedge dy - dx \wedge d^2y = 0.$$

By the general Stokes' theorem,

$$\int_S \omega = \int_{\partial V} \omega = \int_V d\omega = \int_V 0 = 0.$$

### Method 2: Direct Computation (Verification)

Parametrize the ellipsoid:
$$x = a\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\quad y = b\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\quad z = c\cos\th
eta,$$
with $\theta \in [0,\pi]$, $\varphi \in [0,2\pi]$.

Computing the differentials:
$$dx = a\cos\theta\cos\varphi\,d\theta - a\sin\theta\sin\varphi\,d\varphi,$$
$$dy = b\cos\theta\sin\varphi\,d\theta + b\sin\theta\cos\varphi\,d\varphi.$$

Taking the wedge product:
$$dx \wedge dy = ab\cos\theta\cos\varphi\cdot\sin\theta\cos\varphi\,d\theta\wedg
e d\varphi - ab\sin\theta\sin\varphi\cdot\cos\theta\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\wedge d
\theta$$
$$= ab\sin\theta\cos\theta\cos^2\varphi\,d\theta\wedge d\varphi + ab\sin\theta\c
os\theta\sin^2\varphi\,d\theta\wedge d\varphi$$
$$= ab\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\varphi.$$

Integrating over the surface:
$$\int_S \omega = ab\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi\int_0^{\pi}\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\the
ta = ab\cdot 2\pi\cdot\left[\frac{\sin^2\theta}{2}\right]_0^{\pi} = ab\cdot 2\pi
\cdot\frac{0-0}{2} = 0.$$

The integrand $\sin\theta\cos\theta = \frac{1}{2}\sin 2\theta$ is **odd** about
$\theta = \pi/2$ on $[0,\pi]$, so the integral vanishes — confirming the Stokes'
 theorem result.

### Conclusion

$$\boxed{\int_S dx \wedge dy = 0}$$

The integral of the 2-form $dx \wedge dy$ over the closed ellipsoid surface is *
*zero**. This follows because $dx \wedge dy$ is a closed form ($d(dx\wedge dy)=0
$) and the ellipsoid is the boundary of a compact region, so Stokes' theorem giv
es the integral as the integral of $d\omega$ over the enclosed volume, which is
zero.
