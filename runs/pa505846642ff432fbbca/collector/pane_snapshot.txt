**Theorem.** The quotient space $\mathbb{D}^3/{\sim}$, where $\sim$ identifies a
ntipodal points on $\partial\mathbb{D}^3 = S^2$, is homeomorphic to real project
ive $3$-space $\mathbb{RP}^3$.

**Proof.**

Recall that $\mathbb{RP}^3$ is defined as the quotient $S^3/(x\sim -x)$, where $
S^3\subset\mathbb{R}^4$ is the unit sphere and the antipodal map $a(x)=-x$ ident
ifies each point with its opposite. Let $q:S^3\to\mathbb{RP}^3$ denote the quoti
ent map.

**Step 1: Decompose $S^3$ into two hemispheres.**

Write
$$S^3 = D^3_+ \cup D^3_-,$$
where
$$D^3_+ = \{(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)\in S^3 : x_4 \ge 0\}, \qquad D^3_- = \{(x_1,x_2,x_
3,x_4)\in S^3 : x_4 \le 0\}.$$
Their intersection is the equator
$$S^2 = \{(x_1,x_2,x_3,0)\in S^3\} = \partial D^3_+ = \partial D^3_-.$$

The projection $\pi:D^3_+\to\mathbb{D}^3$, $\pi(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)=(x_1,x_2,x_3)$,
 is a homeomorphism: it is continuous, bijective (since $x_4 = \sqrt{1-x_1^2-x_2
^2-x_3^2}$ on $D^3_+$), and its inverse is continuous. Thus $D^3_+\cong\mathbb{D
}^3$, and under this identification $\partial D^3_+$ corresponds to $S^2=\partia
l\mathbb{D}^3$.

**Step 2: The antipodal map exchanges the hemispheres.**

The antipodal map $a(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)=(-x_1,-x_2,-x_3,-x_4)$ sends $D^3_+$ onto
$D^3_-$ and $D^3_-$ onto $D^3_+$. On the equator $S^2$ (where $x_4=0$), it restr
icts to the antipodal map of $S^2\subset\mathbb{R}^3$.

**Step 3: $q|_{D^3_+}$ is surjective.**

For any $[p]\in\mathbb{RP}^3$ with representative $p\in S^3$: if $p\in D^3_+$, t
hen $[p]=q(p)$ with $p\in D^3_+$. If $p\in D^3_-$, then $-p\in D^3_+$ and $q(p)=
q(-p)$. Hence $q(D^3_+)=\mathbb{RP}^3$.

**Step 4: $q|_{D^3_+}$ identifies exactly the antipodal pairs on the boundary.**

Take $p,p'\in D^3_+$. Then $q(p)=q(p')$ iff $p'=\pm p$.

- If $p'=p$: trivial.
- If $p'=-p$: both $p$ and $-p$ lie in $D^3_+$, so $x_4(p)\ge 0$ and $x_4(-p)=-x
_4(p)\ge 0$, forcing $x_4(p)=0$. Thus $p\in S^2=\partial D^3_+$, and $p'=-p$ is
its antipodal point on $S^2$.

Conversely, if $p\in\partial D^3_+$ and $p'=-p\in\partial D^3_+$, then $q(p)=q(p
')$ by definition. Therefore the equivalence relation induced by $q$ on $D^3_+$
is *exactly* the identification of antipodal points on $\partial D^3_+$.

**Step 5: Conclude by uniqueness of the quotient.**

The map $q|_{D^3_+}:D^3_+\to\mathbb{RP}^3$ is a continuous surjection from a com
pact space to a Hausdorff space (since $S^3$ is compact and $\mathbb{RP}^3$ is H
ausdorff), hence it is a quotient map. By Step 4, the equivalence relation it in
duces on $D^3_+$ is precisely "antipodal identification on $\partial D^3_+$." By
 the uniqueness of the quotient topology, there is a homeomorphism
$$D^3_+/\!\sim\;\;\cong\;\mathbb{RP}^3.$$

Using the homeomorphism $\pi:D^3_+\cong\mathbb{D}^3$ from Step 1 (which carries
$\partial D^3_+$ to $\partial\mathbb{D}^3=S^2$ and carries the antipodal relatio
n on $\partial D^3_+$ to the antipodal relation on $S^2$), we obtain
$$\mathbb{D}^3/\!\sim\;\;\cong\;\;D^3_+/\!\sim\;\;\cong\;\;\mathbb{RP}^3.$$

Therefore the quotient space is $\mathbb{RP}^3$. $\blacksquare$
