**Claim:** Yes, the distribution $E = \mathrm{span}(Y_1, \dots, Y_n)$ is integra
ble.

---

**Step 1: $E$ is a well-defined smooth distribution of rank $n$.**

At each point $(p, \bar p) \in U \times \bar U$, we have the canonical splitting
$$T_{(p,\bar p)}(U \times \bar U) \cong T_p U \oplus T_{\bar p}\bar U.$$
Under this splitting, $Y_i = X_i + \bar X_i$ has components $(X_i|_p,\; \bar X_i
|_{\bar p})$. Since the $X_i$ are linearly independent at every point of $U$, th
e projections of $Y_1, \dots, Y_n$ onto the first summand are independent, hence
 the $Y_i$ themselves are linearly independent at every point of $U \times \bar
U$. Thus $E$ is a smooth rank-$n$ distribution.

---

**Step 2: The cross brackets vanish.**

The vector field $X_i$ depends only on the coordinates of the first factor $U$,
while $\bar X_j$ depends only on the coordinates of the second factor $\bar U$.
Writing them in coordinates $(x^1,\dots,x^n)$ on $U$ and $(\bar x^1,\dots,\bar x
^n)$ on $\bar U$:
$$X_i = \sum_a a_i^a(x)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x^a}, \qquad \bar X_j = \sum_b
 \bar a_j^b(\bar x)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial \bar x^b}.$$
Since the coefficients of $X_i$ depend only on $x$ and those of $\bar X_j$ depen
d only on $\bar x$, and the partial derivatives $\partial/\partial x^a$ and $\pa
rtial/\partial \bar x^b$ commute, we get
$$[X_i, \bar X_j] = 0 \quad \text{for all } i, j.$$

---

**Step 3: Compute $[Y_i, Y_j]$.**

Using bilinearity of the Lie bracket and Step 2:
$$[Y_i, Y_j] = [X_i + \bar X_i,\; X_j + \bar X_j] = [X_i, X_j] + [\bar X_i, \bar
 X_j].$$

By hypothesis $[X_i, X_j] = \sum_{k=1}^n c_{ij}^k X_k$ with **constant** $c_{ij}
^k$. Since $\bar X_i$ is another copy with the same structure (the $\bar X_i$ sa
tisfy the same bracket relations with the same constants),
$$[\bar X_i, \bar X_j] = \sum_{k=1}^n c_{ij}^k \bar X_k.$$

Therefore:
$$[Y_i, Y_j] = \sum_{k=1}^n c_{ij}^k X_k + \sum_{k=1}^n c_{ij}^k \bar X_k = \sum
_{k=1}^n c_{ij}^k\,(X_k + \bar X_k) = \sum_{k=1}^n c_{ij}^k\, Y_k.$$

---

**Step 4: Involutivity $\Rightarrow$ Integrability.**

Since the $c_{ij}^k$ are constants, they are in particular smooth functions on $
U \times \bar U$. Thus $[Y_i, Y_j] \in E$ for all $i, j$, which means $E$ is **i
nvolutive**. By the **Frobenius theorem**, an involutive smooth distribution is
integrable: through every point of $U \times \bar U$ there passes a maximal inte
gral manifold of $E$.

---

**Remark on the role of constant coefficients.** The constancy of $c_{ij}^k$ is
essential. If the $c_{ij}^k$ were functions on $U$, then $[\bar X_i, \bar X_j]$
would involve $\bar c_{ij}^k(\bar x)$ (the same *functions* evaluated at $\bar x
$), and we would obtain
$$[Y_i, Y_j] = \sum_k c_{ij}^k(x)\, X_k + \sum_k c_{ij}^k(\bar x)\,\bar X_k,$$
which is **not** generally expressible as $\sum_k f_k(x,\bar x)\, Y_k = \sum_k f
_k\,(X_k + \bar X_k)$, since the coefficients of the $X_k$-part and the $\bar X_
k$-part would need to be the same function $f_k$, but here they are $c_{ij}^k(x)
$ and $c_{ij}^k(\bar x)$ respectively — equal only when $c_{ij}^k$ is constant.
The constancy guarantees these coincide, making $E$ involutive. $\blacksquare$
