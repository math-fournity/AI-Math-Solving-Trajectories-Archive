## Answer

**Yes**, a commutative ring can have a polynomial with uncountably many roots ev
en when the polynomial has a nonzero constant term.

### Construction

Let $F$ be a field of characteristic $\neq 2$ (for instance $F = \mathbb{Q}$), a
nd let $I$ be an uncountable index set (for instance $I = \mathbb{R}$). Define

$$R = F^{I} = \prod_{i \in I} F,$$

the direct product of $|I|$ copies of $F$, with componentwise addition and multi
plication. This is a commutative ring with unity $1_R = (1)_{i \in I}$.

Consider the polynomial

$$p(x) = x^{2} - 1 \in R[x].$$

Its constant term is $-1_R = (-1)_{i \in I}$, which is **nonzero** in $R$ (every
 component is $-1 \neq 0$ in $F$).

### Verification of uncountably many roots

For every function $\varepsilon : I \to \{+1, -1\}$, define

$$r_{\varepsilon} = \bigl(\varepsilon(i)\bigr)_{i \in I} \in R.$$

Then

$$r_{\varepsilon}^{2} = \bigl(\varepsilon(i)^{2}\bigr)_{i \in I} = (1)_{i \in I}
 = 1_R,$$

so $p(r_{\varepsilon}) = r_{\varepsilon}^{2} - 1_R = 0$. Thus each $r_{\varepsil
on}$ is a root of $p$.

The map $\varepsilon \mapsto r_{\varepsilon}$ is injective (distinct functions g
ive distinct tuples), so $p$ has at least as many roots as there are functions $
I \to \{+1,-1\}$, namely

$$\left|\{+1,-1\}^{I}\right| = 2^{|I|}.$$

Since $I$ is uncountable, $|I| \geq \aleph_{0}$ and in fact $|I| > \aleph_{0}$,
so

$$2^{|I|} \geq 2^{\aleph_{0}} > \aleph_{0}.$$

Hence $p$ has **uncountably many** roots.

### Why the nonzero constant term does not prevent this

Over a field, a degree-$n$ polynomial has at most $n$ roots, and a nonzero const
ant term is irrelevant to that bound. The bound fails over general commutative r
ings because of zero divisors: the product ring $F^{I}$ contains $2^{|I|}$ idemp
otent-like "sign" elements, and the factorization $x^{2}-1 = (x-1)(x+1)$ splits
componentwise, allowing every independent componentwise choice of $+1$ or $-1$ t
o yield a distinct global root. The nonzero constant term $-1_R$ only rules out
$0$ being a root (indeed $p(0) = -1_R \neq 0$); it does not bound the number of
nonzero roots in the presence of many idempotents / zero divisors.

### Conclusion

A commutative ring **can** have a polynomial with uncountably many roots even wh
en that polynomial has a nonzero constant term. The ring $R = \mathbb{Q}^{\mathb
b{R}}$ with the polynomial $p(x) = x^{2} - 1$ (constant term $-1 \neq 0$) provid
es an explicit example with $2^{|\mathbb{R}|}$ roots.
