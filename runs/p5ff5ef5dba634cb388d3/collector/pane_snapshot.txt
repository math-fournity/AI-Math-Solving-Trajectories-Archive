## Proof

**Setup.** Parametrize the contour $C_r$ by $z = re^{i\theta}$, $0 \le \theta \l
e \pi$, so $dz = ire^{i\theta}\,d\theta$. Then

$$
f(r) = \int_{C_r} \frac{\sin z}{z}\,dz = \int_0^{\pi} \frac{\sin(re^{i\theta})}{
re^{i\theta}}\,ire^{i\theta}\,d\theta = i\int_0^{\pi} \sin(re^{i\theta})\,d\thet
a.
$$

**Power-series expansion.** Using the globally convergent expansion $\sin w = \s
um_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n w^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}$ with $w = re^{i\theta}$, and
noting the series converges uniformly on $[0,\pi]$ for each fixed $r$ (so termwi
se integration is valid):

$$
\int_0^{\pi} \sin(re^{i\theta})\,d\theta = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n r^{2
n+1}}{(2n+1)!}\int_0^{\pi} e^{i(2n+1)\theta}\,d\theta.
$$

**Computing the angular integral.** For every $n \ge 0$,

$$
\int_0^{\pi} e^{i(2n+1)\theta}\,d\theta = \frac{e^{i(2n+1)\pi}-1}{i(2n+1)}.
$$

Since $2n+1$ is odd, $e^{i(2n+1)\pi} = \cos((2n+1)\pi) + i\sin((2n+1)\pi) = -1$.
 Hence

$$
\int_0^{\pi} e^{i(2n+1)\theta}\,d\theta = \frac{-1-1}{i(2n+1)} = \frac{-2}{i(2n+
1)} = \frac{2i}{2n+1}.
$$

**Assembling $f(r)$.** Substituting back,

$$
\int_0^{\pi} \sin(re^{i\theta})\,d\theta = 2i\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n r^
{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!\,(2n+1)},
$$

and therefore

$$
f(r) = i\cdot 2i\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n r^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!\,(2n+1)} = -2
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n r^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!\,(2n+1)}.
$$

**Taking the limit.** Dividing by $r$ (for $r \neq 0$):

$$
\frac{f(r)}{r} = -2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n r^{2n}}{(2n+1)!\,(2n+1)}.
$$

This is a power series in $r$ (convergent for all $r$, since $\sin$ is entire) w
ith constant term (the $n=0$ term) equal to

$$
-2\cdot \frac{1}{1!\cdot 1} = -2.
$$

All higher terms ($n \ge 1$) carry a positive power of $r$ and vanish as $r \to
0$. By continuity of the power series,

$$
\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{f(r)}{r} = -2.
$$

**Remark on the singularity.** Although $\frac{\sin z}{z}$ has a singularity at
$0$ lying on $C_r$, the singularity is removable (the limit at $0$ is $1$). The
parametrization computation above bypasses the issue entirely: the factor $z = r
e^{i\theta}$ in the denominator cancels against $dz = ire^{i\theta}\,d\theta$, l
eaving the well-defined integral $i\int_0^\pi \sin(re^{i\theta})\,d\theta$, whic
h is manifestly finite for every $r > 0$.

$$
\boxed{\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{f(r)}{r} = -2.}
$$
