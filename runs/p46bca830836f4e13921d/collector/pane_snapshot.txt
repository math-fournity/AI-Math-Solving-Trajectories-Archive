## Solution

**Answer: No, the equality $v_P(Q \circ R) = v_P(Q) \cdot v_P(R)$ does not hold
in general.** Below are counterexamples covering both the case where $P$ is line
ar and where $\deg P \geq 2$, followed by analysis of when the formula does hold
.

---

### Counterexample 1: $P$ linear

Let $K = \mathbb{Q}$, $P = X$ (irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$), $Q(X) = X - 1$,
and $R(X) = X + 1$.

- $v_P(Q) = v_X(X-1) = 0$, since $X \nmid (X-1)$ (the constant term $-1 \neq 0$)
.
- $v_P(R) = v_X(X+1) = 0$, since $X \nmid (X+1)$ (the constant term $1 \neq 0$).
- $Q \circ R = Q(R(X)) = (X+1) - 1 = X$, so $v_P(Q \circ R) = v_X(X) = 1$.

Thus $v_P(Q \circ R) = 1$ but $v_P(Q) \cdot v_P(R) = 0 \cdot 0 = 0$. The equalit
y **fails**.

**Key insight:** When $v_P(R) = 0$ (i.e., $P \nmid R$), the right-hand side is $
0$ regardless of $Q$. But $Q \circ R$ can still be divisible by $P$ — compositio
n can "create" a $P$-factor even when neither $Q$ nor $R$ individually has one.
Here, $R$ shifts the input so that $R(0) = 1$ becomes a root of $Q$.

---

### Counterexample 2: $\deg P \geq 2$

Let $K = \mathbb{Q}$, $P = X^2 + 1$ (irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$), and set $Q
= P$, $R = P$.

- $v_P(Q) = v_P(P) = 1$.
- $v_P(R) = v_P(P) = 1$.
- $Q \circ R = P(P(X)) = (X^2+1)^2 + 1 = X^4 + 2X^2 + 2$.

Now compute $v_P(X^4 + 2X^2 + 2)$ by dividing by $P = X^2 + 1$:

$$X^4 + 2X^2 + 2 = (X^2 + 1)(X^2 + 1) + 1 = (X^2+1)^2 + 1.$$

The remainder upon division by $X^2 + 1$ is $1 \neq 0$, so $P \nmid P(P(X))$, gi
ving $v_P(Q \circ R) = 0$.

Thus $v_P(Q \circ R) = 0$ but $v_P(Q) \cdot v_P(R) = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$. The equalit
y **fails**.

**Key insight:** For $\deg P \geq 2$, $P$ is irreducible so $P(0) \neq 0$ (other
wise $X \mid P$, contradicting irreducibility). Writing $P(Y) = a_0 + a_1 Y + \c
dots + a_n Y^n$ with $a_0 = P(0) \neq 0$, we get:

$$P(P(X)) = a_0 + P(X)\cdot\bigl(a_1 + a_2 P(X) + \cdots + a_n P(X)^{n-1}\bigr),
$$

so $P(P(X)) \equiv a_0 \pmod{P(X)}$ with $a_0 \neq 0$, hence $v_P(P(P(X))) = 0$.
 Composition of $P$ with itself **destroys** the $P$-adic valuation rather than
multiplying it.

---

### When does the formula hold?

The formula **does hold** in the special case $P = X$ (up to association) **and*
* $v_X(R) \geq 1$ (i.e., $R(0) = 0$):

**Proof.** Write $R(X) = X^m \cdot S(X)$ with $S(0) \neq 0$, so $v_X(R) = m \geq
 1$. Write $Q(X) = X^k \cdot T(X)$ with $T(0) \neq 0$, so $v_X(Q) = k$. Then:

$$Q(R(X)) = R(X)^k \cdot T(R(X)) = X^{mk} \cdot S(X)^k \cdot T(R(X)).$$

Since $S(0) \neq 0$ and $T(R(0)) = T(0) \neq 0$, both $S(X)^k$ and $T(R(X))$ hav
e $v_X = 0$. Therefore:

$$v_X(Q \circ R) = mk = v_X(Q) \cdot v_X(R). \quad \checkmark$$

However, this is a restricted case. The formula fails whenever $v_P(R) = 0$ but
$P \mid (Q \circ R)$ (as in Counterexample 1), and it fails for $\deg P \geq 2$
even when both valuations are positive (as in Counterexample 2).

---

### Conclusion

The equality $v_P(Q \circ R) = v_P(Q) \cdot v_P(R)$ **does not hold in general**
. The fundamental obstruction is that polynomial composition does not respect th
e multiplicative structure of the $P$-adic valuation: composition can both *crea
te* $P$-factors (when $R$ maps a root of $P$ to a root of $Q$, as in Counterexam
ple 1) and *destroy* them (when $P(0) \neq 0$ and $P \mid R$, as in Counterexamp
le 2).
