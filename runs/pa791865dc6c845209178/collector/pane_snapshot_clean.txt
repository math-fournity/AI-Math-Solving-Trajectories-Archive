**Answer:** Yes. In fact, *every* continuous surjection $f:[0,1]\to[0,1]^2$ is n

and surjectivity itself forces the failure of bounded variation.

## Proof

We prove two facts:

**(A)** There exists a continuous surjection $f:[0,1]\to[0,1]^2$ (Peano, 1890).

**(B)** No continuous surjection $f:[0,1]\to[0,1]^2$ is of bounded variation.

Together these give the conclusion.

### (A) Existence

The classical Peano curve construction (or the Hilbert curve) gives a continuous
 map $f:[0,1]\to[0,1]^2$ whose image is all of $[0,1]^2$. We recall the construc
tion briefly. Subdivide $[0,1]^2$ into $4^n$ closed squares of side $2^{-n}$, or
dered so that consecutive squares share an edge, and map each dyadic subinterval
 $[(k-1)4^{-n},\,k4^{-n}]$ into the $k$-th square. These maps are consistent acr
oss $n$ and converge uniformly to a continuous $f$; since the image intersects e
very square at every scale, $f([0,1])=[0,1]^2$. Hence a continuous surjection ex
ists.

### (B) Surjectivity forces non‑BV

Recall the definition: $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^2$ is of **bounded variation** if

$$V(f):=\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|f(t_i)-f(t_{i-1})\|<\infty,$$

the supremum over all partitions $\mathcal{P}=\{0=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_n=1\}$.

**Claim.** If $f$ is continuous and of bounded variation with total variation $V
<\infty$, then the image $\Gamma:=f([0,1])$ has two‑dimensional Lebesgue measure
 zero.

*Proof of claim.* Define the variation function $v(t):=\mathrm{Var}(f;[0,t])$. I
t is increasing and bounded ($v(t)\le V$). For any $\delta>0$, partition $0=t_0<
t_1<\cdots<t_n=1$ so that

$$v(t_i)-v(t_{i-1})\le\delta\qquad\text{for all }i.$$

(This is possible: split the range $[0,V]$ into steps of size $\le\delta$ and pu
ll back through the increasing function $v$; then $n\le\lceil V/\delta\rceil+1$.
)

For any $s,u\in[t_{i-1},t_i]$,

$$\|f(s)-f(u)\|\le\mathrm{Var}(f;[s,u])\le v(t_i)-v(t_{i-1})\le\delta.$$

Hence $f([t_{i-1},t_i])\subseteq\overline{B}(f(t_{i-1}),\,\delta)$, a closed bal
l of radius $\delta$. Therefore

$$\Gamma=f([0,1])\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\overline{B}(f(t_{i-1}),\,\delta).$$

The total area of this cover is at most

$$n\cdot\pi\delta^{2}\le\Big(\lceil V/\delta\rceil+1\Big)\pi\delta^{2}\le\pi V\d
elta+2\pi\delta^{2}\xrightarrow[\delta\to 0]{}0.$$

Since $\Gamma$ is compact (hence measurable) and can be covered by sets of arbit
rarily small total area, $\lambda_2(\Gamma)=0$. $\square$

**Conclusion of (B).** Suppose, for contradiction, that $f:[0,1]\to[0,1]^2$ is c
ontinuous, surjective, and of bounded variation. By the claim, $\lambda_2(f([0,1
]))=0$. But surjectivity gives $f([0,1])=[0,1]^2$, which has $\lambda_2([0,1]^2)
=1\neq 0$ — a contradiction. Hence **no** continuous surjection $[0,1]\to[0,1]^2
$ is of bounded variation.

### Combining (A) and (B)

By (A) a continuous surjection $f:[0,1]\to[0,1]^2$ exists; by (B) every such sur
jection fails to be of bounded variation. Therefore:

> **A continuous function can be surjective from $[0,1]$ to $[0,1]^2$ without be
ing of bounded variation — and indeed surjectivity *forces* the function to be o
f unbounded variation.**

The intuitive content: a curve of bounded variation is rectifiable (finite lengt
h), and a rectifiable curve in the plane has Hausdorff dimension $\le 1$, hence
planar measure zero; it can never fill a square of positive area. Space‑filling
curves achieve surjectivity only by having infinite total variation — they "wigg
le" at every scale.
