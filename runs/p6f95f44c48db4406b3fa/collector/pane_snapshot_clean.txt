## Analysis

The problem asks to prove:

> Are there infinitely many primes $p$ such that $p - 1$ is a perfect square? Eq
uivalently, are there infinitely many primes of the form $n^2 + 1$?

This is **Landau's fourth problem**, one of the four famous problems about prime
s posed by Edmund Landau at the 1912 International Congress of Mathematicians. I
t is explicitly marked `@[category research open, AMS 11]` in the formalization,
 indicating it is a recognized **open research problem**.

### Current state of knowledge

1. **The conjecture**: It is widely believed that there are infinitely many prim
es of the form $n^2 + 1$. Computational evidence strongly supports this — primes
 of this form continue to appear at all searched ranges (e.g., $2, 5, 17, 37, 10
1, 197, 257, 401, 577, 677, \ldots$).

2. **What is known**:
   - **Bunyakovsky's conjecture** (1857) implies Landau's fourth problem as a sp
ecial case: a polynomial $f$ with positive leading coefficient, integer coeffici
ents, and $\gcd$ of its values equal to 1 should take prime values infinitely of
ten. For $f(n) = n^2+1$, the gcd condition holds (since $f(0)=1$). But Bunyakovs
ky's conjecture itself is **unproven**.
   - **Iwaniec (1978)** proved that there are infinitely many $n$ such that $n^2
 + 1$ is the product of **at most two** primes (i.e., $P_2$-numbers). This is th
e closest partial result, but it does not yield infinitely many primes.
   - **Friedlander–Iwaniec (1998)** proved that there are infinitely many primes
 of the form $x^2 + y^4$ — a related but different form. This is a deep sieve-th
eoretic achievement but does not resolve $n^2+1$.
   - **Heath-Brown** and others have shown that the number of primes $p \leq X$
of the form $n^2+1$ is $O(X^{1-\delta})$ for some $\delta > 0$ (upper bounds via
 sieve methods), but no matching lower bound of the right order is known.
   - Under the **Bateman–Horn conjecture** (a quantitative form of Bunyakovsky),
 the expected count is asymptotically $\sim C \sqrt{X}/\log X$ for an explicit c
onstant $C > 0$, but this remains unproven.

3. **Why it is hard**: The polynomial $n^2+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}$,
so there is no algebraic obstruction. However, current sieve methods can prove u

nd for primes in a single irreducible quadratic polynomial. The parity problem i
n sieve theory is the fundamental barrier: classical sieves cannot distinguish n
umbers with an odd number of prime factors (primes) from those with an even numb
er, when the sifting dimension is 1.
