Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether rational numbers $a_i, b_i,
 p_i, q_i$ exist such that $f(\pi) = \sum_{i=1}^M a_i \pi^{p_i}$ and $g(e) = \su
m_{i=1}^N b_i e^{q_i}$ are both nonzero and $f(\pi)/g(e) \in \mathbb{Q}$.

## Solution

**Yes, it is possible.**

### Construction

Take $M = N = 1$ with $a_1 = 1$, $b_1 = 1$, $p_1 = 0$, $q_1 = 0$ (all rational).
 Then:
- $f(\pi) = 1 \cdot \pi^0 = 1 \neq 0$,
- $g(e) = 1 \cdot e^0 = 1 \neq 0$,
- $\dfrac{f(\pi)}{g(e)} = \dfrac{1}{1} = 1 \in \mathbb{Q}$.

This satisfies all the requirements. $\square$

### Supplementary analysis: the nontrivial cases

To give a complete picture, I classify what happens when $f$ and $g$ are require
d to be nonconstant.

**Key Lemma.** *For distinct rational exponents $q_1, \dots, q_N$ with rational
coefficients $b_j$ (not all zero), $g(e) = \sum_{j=1}^N b_j e^{q_j}$ is rational
 if and only if all $q_j = 0$ (i.e., $g$ is constant).*

*Proof.* Write all $q_j$ with a common denominator $t$: $q_j = m_j/t$ for distin
ct integers $m_j$. Set $\beta = e^{1/t}$, so $g(e) = \sum b_j \beta^{m_j} =: Q(\
beta)$, a polynomial in $\beta$ with rational coefficients. Since $e$ is transce
ndental, $\beta = e^{1/t}$ is also transcendental (if $\beta$ were algebraic, th
en $e = \beta^t$ would be algebraic, contradiction). A nonconstant polynomial ev
aluated at a transcendental number is transcendental, hence irrational. Therefor
e $Q(\beta) \in \mathbb{Q}$ forces $Q$ to be constant, meaning all $m_j = 0$, i.
e., all $q_j = 0$. $\square$

By the identical argument (using that $\pi$ is transcendental), $f(\pi) = \sum a
_i \pi^{p_i}$ is rational if and only if all $p_i = 0$.

**Mixed case is impossible.** If $f$ is constant ($f(\pi) = A \in \mathbb{Q} \se
tminus \{0\}$) and $g$ is nonconstant, then $f(\pi)/g(e) = A/g(e) \in \mathbb{Q}
$ would force $g(e) \in \mathbb{Q}$, contradicting the Lemma. Symmetrically for
$g$ constant and $f$ nonconstant. So a rational ratio with exactly one of $f, g$
 nonconstant is **impossible**.

**Both nonconstant: equivalent to an open problem.** If both $f$ and $g$ are non
constant, write $p_i = r_i/s$ and $q_j = m_j/t$ with common denominators, and se
t $\alpha = \pi^{1/s}$, $\beta = e^{1/t}$ (both transcendental). Then $f(\pi) =
P(\alpha)$ and $g(e) = Q(\beta)$ for nonconstant polynomials $P, Q \in \mathbb{Q
}[x]$. The condition $f(\pi)/g(e) \in \mathbb{Q}$ becomes $P(\alpha) = r\, Q(\be
ta)$ for some $r \in \mathbb{Q}^*$, i.e., $P(\alpha) - r\,Q(\beta) = 0$ — an alg
ebraic relation between $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Since $\alpha$ is algebraic over $
\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$ and $\beta$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}(e)$, algebraic depend
ence of $\alpha, \beta$ is equivalent to algebraic dependence of $\pi, e$. Wheth
er $\pi$ and $e$ are algebraically independent is a famous **open problem** (not
 implied by Lindemann–Weierstrass, Gelfond–Schneider, Baker's theorem, or Nester
enko's results on $\pi$ and $e^\pi$).

### Conclusion

The answer to the problem as stated is **yes**: the trivial construction with co
nstant $f$ and $g$ (all exponents zero) yields a rational ratio. The only way to
 achieve a rational ratio is with both $f$ and $g$ constant; the mixed case is p
rovably impossible, and the fully nonconstant case is equivalent to the open pro
blem of algebraic independence of $\pi$ and $e$.
