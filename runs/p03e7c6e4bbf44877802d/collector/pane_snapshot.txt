## Proof

Let $E$ be a complex Hilbert space and $B \in \mathcal{L}(E)^+$ a positive (henc
e self-adjoint) operator. Assume $B$ is invertible. Let $B^{1/2}$ denote its uni
que positive square root, so $B^{1/2} \in \mathcal{L}(E)^+$ and $(B^{1/2})^2 = B
$. We show $B^{1/2}$ is invertible.

**Injectivity.** Suppose $B^{1/2}x = 0$ for some $x \in E$. Then
$$Bx = (B^{1/2})^2 x = B^{1/2}(B^{1/2}x) = B^{1/2}(0) = 0.$$
Since $B$ is invertible, $Bx = 0$ implies $x = 0$. Hence $\ker B^{1/2} = \{0\}$,
 so $B^{1/2}$ is injective.

**Surjectivity.** Let $y \in E$ be arbitrary. Since $B$ is invertible, it is sur
jective, so there exists $z \in E$ with $Bz = y$. Setting $w = B^{1/2}z \in E$,
we obtain
$$B^{1/2}w = B^{1/2}(B^{1/2}z) = (B^{1/2})^2 z = Bz = y.$$
Thus every $y \in E$ lies in the range of $B^{1/2}$, so $B^{1/2}$ is surjective.

**Bounded inverse.** $B^{1/2}$ is a bounded linear operator (being a positive op
erator obtained via the continuous functional calculus of the bounded self-adjoi
nt operator $B$). A bounded bijective linear map between Banach spaces has a bou
nded inverse by the Bounded Inverse Theorem (a consequence of the Open Mapping T
heorem). Therefore $B^{1/2}$ is invertible in $\mathcal{L}(E)$.

**Conclusion.** Yes, $B^{1/2}$ is invertible. In fact its inverse is $(B^{1/2})^
{-1} = B^{-1/2}$, the positive square root of $B^{-1}$, and one may verify direc
tly that $B^{-1/2}B^{1/2} = B^{1/2}B^{-1/2} = I$ via the functional calculus.
