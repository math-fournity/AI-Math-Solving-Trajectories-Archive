**Answer: Yes, $B$ must be the integral closure of $A$ in $L$.**

## Proof

Let $C$ denote the integral closure of $A$ in $L$. We show $B = C$.

**Step 1: $B$ is $G$-stable.**

The expression $B^G = \{b \in B \mid \sigma(b) = b \text{ for all } \sigma \in G
\}$ is well-defined only if every $\sigma \in G$ maps $B$ into itself. Thus $B$
is $G$-stable: $\sigma(B) \subseteq B$ for all $\sigma \in G$.

**Step 2: Every element of $B$ is integral over $A$.**

Let $b \in B$. Since $L/K$ is Galois (hence algebraic), $b$ is algebraic over $K
$. The orbit $\{\sigma(b) : \sigma \in G\}$ is finite: it is contained in the se
t of roots of the minimal polynomial of $b$ over $K$, which has degree $[K(b):K]
 < \infty$. Denote the distinct conjugates by $b_1, \dots, b_m$.

Set
$$f(x) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} (x - b_i) \in L[x].$$

We claim $f(x) \in A[x]$:

- **Coefficients lie in $B$:** Each $b_i = \sigma_i(b)$ for some $\sigma_i \in G
$, and since $B$ is $G$-stable, $b_i \in B$. The coefficients of $f$ are element
ary symmetric polynomials in $b_1, \dots, b_m$, hence belong to $B$ (as $B$ is a
 ring).

- **Coefficients are $G$-invariant:** For any $\tau \in G$, the map $b_i \mapsto
 \tau(b_i)$ permutes the set $\{b_1, \dots, b_m\}$ (since $\tau$ sends the orbit
 of $b$ to itself). Therefore $\tau$ permutes the factors of $f$, leaving $f$ un
changed. So every coefficient of $f$ is fixed by all $\tau \in G$.

Combining both points: the coefficients of $f$ lie in $B \cap L^G = B^G = A$. Th
us $f(x) \in A[x]$.

Since $b = \mathrm{id}(b) = b_i$ for some $i$, we have $f(b) = 0$. Therefore $b$
 is integral over $A$.

**Step 3: $B = C$.**

From Step 2, $B \subseteq C$ (every element of $B$ is integral over $A$ and lies
 in $L$). Since $B$ is a Dedekind domain, $B$ is integrally closed in its fracti
on field $L$. But $C$ is the set of *all* elements of $L$ integral over $A$, and
 $C$ is itself integral over $A$. Since $B$ is integrally closed in $L$ and $C \
subseteq L$ is integral over $A \subseteq B$, every element of $C$ is integral o
ver $B$; but $B$ is integrally closed in $L$, so $C \subseteq B$.

Therefore $B = C$: $B$ is the integral closure of $A$ in $L$. $\blacksquare$
