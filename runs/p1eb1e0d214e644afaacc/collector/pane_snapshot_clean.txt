## Proof that the statement is TRUE

uous curve with $f(0)=(0,0)$, $f(1)=(1,1)$, and $f(t)\in(0,1)^2$ for $t\in(0,1)$
. Since $[0,1]$ is compact and $[0,1]^2$ is Hausdorff, $f$ is a homeomorphism on
to its image, so $f([0,1])$ is a simple arc.

**Step 1: $f$ divides the square into two closed disks.**

Define two boundary paths from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$:
- $\Gamma_B$: along the bottom edge then right edge: $(0,0)\to(1,0)\to(1,1)$.
- $\Gamma_T$: along the left edge then top edge: $(0,0)\to(0,1)\to(1,1)$.

Since $f(t)\in(0,1)^2$ for $t\in(0,1)$, the arc $f$ meets the boundary of the sq
uare only at $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$. Therefore:
- $J_1 = f([0,1])\cup\Gamma_B$ is a simple closed curve (Jordan curve),
- $J_2 = f([0,1])\cup\Gamma_T$ is a simple closed curve.

By the Jordan–Schoenflies theorem, each $J_i$ bounds a closed disk. Let $R_1$ be
 the closed disk bounded by $J_1$ and $R_2$ that bounded by $J_2$. Then $R_1\cup
 R_2 = [0,1]^2$ and $R_1\cap R_2 = f([0,1])$.

**Step 2: Construct a homeomorphism $h$ mapping $f$ to the diagonal.**

The diagonal $D=\{(t,t):t\in[0,1]\}$ divides $[0,1]^2$ into two triangles:
- $T_1=\{(x,y)\in[0,1]^2:y\le x\}$ (bounded by $D$, bottom edge, right edge),
- $T_2=\{(x,y)\in[0,1]^2:y\ge x\}$ (bounded by $D$, left edge, top edge).

Both $R_1$ and $T_1$ are closed disks whose boundaries decompose into three arcs
: a "diagonal" arc ($f$ vs. $D$), the bottom edge, and the right edge.

Choose a homeomorphism $\alpha:f([0,1])\to D$ (e.g., $\alpha(f(t))=(t,t)$). Defi
ne $\phi_1:\partial R_1\to\partial T_1$ by using $\alpha$ on $f([0,1])$, the ide
ntity on the bottom edge, and the identity on the right edge. These agree at the
 shared endpoints $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$, so $\phi_1$ is a well-defined homeomorphi
sm of boundaries.

By the Schoenflies theorem (any homeomorphism between the boundaries of closed d
isks extends to a homeomorphism of the interiors—this follows from the Alexander
 trick: cone the boundary homeomorphism to the center), $\phi_1$ extends to a ho
meomorphism $\Phi_1:R_1\to T_1$.

Similarly, define $\phi_2:\partial R_2\to\partial T_2$ using $\alpha$ on $f([0,1
])$, the identity on the left edge, and the identity on the top edge, extending
to $\Phi_2:R_2\to T_2$.

Since $\Phi_1$ and $\Phi_2$ agree on $f([0,1])$ (both restrict to $\alpha$), the
y glue to a homeomorphism $h:[0,1]^2\to[0,1]^2$ satisfying:
- $h(f(t))=(t,t)$ for all $t\in[0,1]$ (maps $f$ to the diagonal $D$),
- $h$ fixes the boundary of the square pointwise (hence fixes all four corners),
- $h$ maps the interior $(0,1)^2$ to itself.

**Step 3: Construct $g$ for the diagonal case.**

For the diagonal $D$, take the anti-diagonal $g'(t)=(1-t,\,t)$. Then:
- $g'(0)=(1,0)$, $g'(1)=(0,1)$,
- $g'(t)\in(0,1)^2$ for $t\in(0,1)$,
- $g'$ is non-self-intersecting,
- $g'$ intersects $D$ exactly once: $g'(t)=(s,s)$ requires $1-t=s$ and $t=s$, gi
ving $t=s=\tfrac12$. The unique intersection is at $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$.

**Step 4: Map back to obtain $g$.**

Define $g = h^{-1}\circ g' : [0,1]\to[0,1]^2$. Then:
- $g(0)=h^{-1}(1,0)=(1,0)$ and $g(1)=h^{-1}(0,1)=(0,1)$ (since $h$ fixes corners

- For $t\in(0,1)$: $g'(t)\in(0,1)^2$ and $h^{-1}$ maps the interior to the inter
ior, so $g(t)\in(0,1)^2$.
- $g$ is non-self-intersecting (composition of a homeomorphism with an injective
 curve).
- **Intersection count:** $g(t_1)=f(t_0)$ iff $h^{-1}(g'(t_1))=f(t_0)$ iff $g'(t
_1)=h(f(t_0))=(t_0,t_0)$. This requires $1-t_1=t_0$ and $t_1=t_0$, yielding the
unique solution $t_0=t_1=\tfrac12$.

Therefore $g$ intersects $f$ at exactly one point, with unique parameters $t_0=t
_1=\tfrac12$.
