## Proof

**Reduction to the unit disk.** Set $g(w) = f(Rw)$ for $|w|<1$. Then $g$ is regu
lar in $|w|<1$, with $g'(0)=a_1 R$, and $D(g)=D(f)$ since $|w|<1 \Leftrightarrow
 |z|<R$. Thus it suffices to prove $|g'(0)|\le \tfrac12 D(g)$ for $g$ regular in
 $|w|<1$. Equivalently, we prove $|a_1|\,R\le \tfrac12 D(f)$ directly.

**Key observation (extracting the odd part).** For any $z$ with $|z|<R$, both $z
$ and $-z$ lie in the disk, so
$$\left|f(z)-f(-z)\right|\le D(f). \tag{1}$$
Write $f(z)=a_0+a_1 z+a_2 z^2+\cdots$. Then
$$f(z)-f(-z)=2a_1 z+2a_3 z^3+\cdots,\qquad \frac{f(z)-f(-z)}{2z}=a_1+a_3 z^2+a_5
 z^4+\cdots.$$
The right-hand side is a power series in $z$ (even in $z$) that is regular in $|
z|<R$ and equals $a_1$ at $z=0$.

**Averaging over a circle.** For $0<r<R$, by the mean-value property of holomorp
hic functions applied to the regular function $h(z)=\frac{f(z)-f(-z)}{2z}$:
$$a_1 = h(0) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} h\!\left(re^{i\theta}\right)d\theta =
 \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{f(re^{i\theta})-f(-re^{i\theta})}{2\,re^{i\the
ta}}\,d\theta.$$
Taking absolute values and using (1):
$$|a_1|\le \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\left|f(re^{i\theta})-f(-re^{i\theta
})\right|}{2r}\,d\theta \le \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{D(f)}{2r}\,d\theta
= \frac{D(f)}{2r}.$$
This holds for every $r<R$. Letting $r\to R$:
$$\boxed{|a_1|\le \frac{D(f)}{2R}},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad |a_1|\,R\le \tfrac12\
,D(f). \tag{$\star$}$$

**Sharpness of the factor $\tfrac12$.** Take $f(z)=z$ on $|z|<R$. Then $a_1=1$ a
nd
$$D(f)=\sup_{|z_1|<R,\,|z_2|<R}|z_1-z_2|=2R$$
(approached by $z_1\to R$, $z_2\to -R$). Hence $|a_1|\,R=R=\tfrac12\cdot 2R=\tfr
ac12\,D(f)$: equality is attained. Therefore the constant $\tfrac12$ cannot be r
eplaced by any smaller number.

**Geometric interpretation.** $D(f)$ is the **diameter** of the image domain $f(
\{|z|<R\})\subset\mathbb{C}$—the greatest distance between any two image points.
 The derivative $f'(0)=a_1$ is the local linear scaling of $f$ at the center; un
der the linear approximation $z\mapsto a_0+a_1 z$, the disk $|z|<R$ maps to a di
sk of radius $|a_1|R$, i.e. of diameter $2|a_1|R$.

Inequality $(\star)$ says:

> *The diameter of the image of the disk under $f$ is at least $2|f'(0)|\,R$—twi
ce the radius of the image under the linear part of $f$ at the center.*

In other words, no matter how the higher-order terms $a_2 z^2+\cdots$ bend and f
old the image, the image domain can never become *narrower* (in diameter) than t
he disk produced by the linear term alone. The extremal case is precisely the **
linear** (affine) map $f(z)=a_0+a_1 z$, whose image is a disk of diameter $2|a_1
|R$; here the image is as small as possible, and equality holds.

Equivalently, using the odd part $\varphi(z)=\frac{f(z)-f(-z)}{2}=a_1 z+a_3 z^3+
\cdots$: the values $\varphi(z)$ and $\varphi(-z)=-\varphi(z)$ are symmetric abo
ut the origin, so the image of $\varphi$ has diameter $\le D(f)$; the inequality
 then bounds the first coefficient of $\varphi$ (its derivative at $0$) by half
this diameter—exactly the statement that a domain symmetric about the origin and
 contained in a set of diameter $D$ has its "radial extent" $|\varphi'(0)|R$ at
most $D/2$.
