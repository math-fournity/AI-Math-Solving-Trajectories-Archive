## Proof

We prove the contrapositive: **if \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2\) is Lebesgue measu
rable, then its orthogonal projection onto every line is Lebesgue measurable in
\(\mathbb{R}\).** Since lines outside the unit square certainly exist (e.g. \(\e
ll = \{y=2\}\)), the hypothesis that *all* such projections are non-measurable f
orces at least one projection to be non-measurable, which by the contrapositive
forces \(A\) to be non-measurable.

---

### Step 1. Decomposition of a measurable set

Every Lebesgue measurable set \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2\) can be written as
\[
A = B \cup N,
\]
where \(B\) is a Borel set (in fact an \(F_\sigma\)) with \(B \subseteq A\), and
 \(N = A \setminus B\) has two-dimensional Lebesgue measure zero. This is the st
andard regularity theorem for Lebesgue measure.

### Step 2. Lipschitz maps send null sets to null sets

Let \(\pi_\ell : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \ell \cong \mathbb{R}\) be the orthogonal proj
ection onto a line \(\ell\). This map is Lipschitz with constant \(1\):
\[
|\pi_\ell(p) - \pi_\ell(q)| \le |p - q| \quad \forall\, p,q \in \mathbb{R}^2.
\]

**Claim.** \(\pi_\ell(N)\) has one-dimensional Lebesgue measure zero.

*Proof of claim.* Cover \(N\) by countably many squares \(Q_i\) with total area
\(< \varepsilon\). Each square \(Q_i\) of side \(s_i\) has image \(\pi_\ell(Q_i)
\) contained in an interval of length \(\le s_i\) (projection of a square onto a
 line has length at most its diameter, which is \(s_i\sqrt{2}\); more carefully,
 the projection of a set of diameter \(d\) onto a line has length \(\le d\)). He
nce
\[
\pi_\ell(N) \subseteq \bigcup_i \pi_\ell(Q_i), \qquad \sum_i |\pi_\ell(Q_i)| \le
 \sum_i s_i\sqrt{2} \le \sqrt{2}\sum_i \operatorname{area}(Q_i) < \sqrt{2}\,\var
epsilon.
\]
Since \(\varepsilon\) is arbitrary, \(\pi_\ell(N)\) is a null set. \(\square\)

In general, any Lipschitz map \(\mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^n\) with \(m \ge n\)
 maps Lebesgue-null sets to Lebesgue-null sets; the covering argument above is t
he special case \(m=2, n=1\).

### Step 3. Continuous images of Borel sets are analytic, hence measurable

The map \(\pi_\ell\) is continuous. The image \(\pi_\ell(B)\) of the Borel set \
(B\) under a continuous map is an **analytic** (Souslin) subset of \(\ell \cong
\mathbb{R}\).

A classical theorem of Lusin/Souslin states that **every analytic subset of \(\m
athbb{R}\) is universally measurable**, and in particular Lebesgue measurable. (
Analytic sets are projections of Borel sets in \(\mathbb{R}^2\); they are immedi
ately seen to be in the Lebesgue completion via the capacity/outer-regularity ar
gument, or equivalently they belong to every complete measure extending the Bore
l \(\sigma\)-algebra.)

### Step 4. The projection is measurable

Combining Steps 2 and 3:
\[
\pi_\ell(A) = \pi_\ell(B) \cup \pi_\ell(N),
\]
where \(\pi_\ell(B)\) is analytic (hence Lebesgue measurable) and \(\pi_\ell(N)\
) is a null set (hence Lebesgue measurable). Therefore \(\pi_\ell(A)\) is Lebesg
ue measurable.

This holds for **every** line \(\ell\).

### Step 5. Conclusion

We have shown:

> If \(A\) is Lebesgue measurable in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), then for every line \(\el
l\), the orthogonal projection \(\pi_\ell(A)\) is Lebesgue measurable in \(\math
bb{R}\).

The contrapositive is:

> If there exists even one line \(\ell\) such that \(\pi_\ell(A)\) is not Lebesg
ue measurable, then \(A\) is not Lebesgue measurable in \(\mathbb{R}^2\).

The hypothesis of the problem is stronger: it states that the projection onto **
every** line outside the unit square is non-measurable. Since lines outside the
unit square exist (the unit square is bounded, so e.g. \(\ell = \{y = 2\}\) lies
 outside it), at least one projection is non-measurable. By the contrapositive a
bove, \(A\) is not Lebesgue measurable in the plane.

\[
\boxed{\text{Yes. } A \text{ is not Lebesgue measurable in } \mathbb{R}^2.}
\]
