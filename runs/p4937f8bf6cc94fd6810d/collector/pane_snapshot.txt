# Proof: The operator $L = \partial_x^2 + c\partial_x + f'(v)$ generates a $C_0$
-semigroup

**Setting.** We work on the Hilbert space $X = L^2(\mathbb{R})$. The traveling w
ave $v = v(x)$ is a bounded solution of the traveling wave ODE $v'' + cv' + f(v)
 = 0$, connecting equilibria at $\pm\infty$. Since $v$ is bounded and $f'$ is co
ntinuous, the potential $q(x) := f'(v(x))$ is a bounded function: $\|q\|_\infty
< \infty$. We take the domain $D(L) = H^2(\mathbb{R})$.

**Strategy.** We split $L = L_0 + M$ where $L_0 = \partial_x^2 + c\,\partial_x$
(with domain $H^2(\mathbb{R})$) and $M$ is the bounded multiplication operator $
(Mg)(x) = q(x)\,g(x)$. We show:
1. $L_0$ generates a $C_0$-semigroup of contractions (via the Lumer–Phillips the
orem).
2. $M$ is a bounded perturbation, so by the **bounded perturbation theorem**, $L
 = L_0 + M$ generates a $C_0$-semigroup.

---

## Step 1: $L_0 = \partial_x^2 + c\,\partial_x$ generates a $C_0$-semigroup of c
ontractions on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$

We verify the hypotheses of the **Lumer–Phillips theorem**: a densely defined, d
issipative operator $A$ with $\operatorname{Range}(\lambda I - A) = X$ for some
$\lambda > 0$ generates a $C_0$-semigroup of contractions.

### (a) $L_0$ is densely defined

$D(L_0) = H^2(\mathbb{R})$ is dense in $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ (since $C_c^\infty(\mat
hbb{R}) \subset H^2(\mathbb{R})$ and $C_c^\infty$ is dense in $L^2$).

### (b) $L_0$ is dissipative

For $g \in H^2(\mathbb{R})$, compute the real part of the inner product:

$$\operatorname{Re}\langle L_0 g, g\rangle = \operatorname{Re}\langle g'', g\ran
gle + c\,\operatorname{Re}\langle g', g\rangle.$$

**First term.** Integration by parts (boundary terms vanish for $g \in H^2 \subs
et H^1$):

$$\langle g'', g\rangle = \int_{\mathbb{R}} g''(x)\,\overline{g(x)}\,dx = -\int_
{\mathbb{R}} |g'(x)|^2\,dx = -\|g'\|_{L^2}^2.$$

This is real and non-positive.

**Second term.** For $g \in H^1(\mathbb{R})$, integration by parts gives:

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}} g'(x)\,\overline{g(x)}\,dx = -\int_{\mathbb{R}} g(x)\,\overl
ine{g'(x)}\,dx = -\overline{\int_{\mathbb{R}} g'(x)\,\overline{g(x)}\,dx},$$

so $\langle g', g\rangle$ is purely imaginary, hence $\operatorname{Re}\langle g
', g\rangle = 0$.

Combining:

$$\operatorname{Re}\langle L_0 g, g\rangle = -\|g'\|_{L^2}^2 \leq 0 \quad \text{
for all } g \in H^2(\mathbb{R}).$$

Thus $L_0$ is **dissipative**.

### (c) Range condition: $\operatorname{Range}(\lambda I - L_0) = L^2(\mathbb{R}
)$ for every $\lambda > 0$

Via the Fourier transform $\mathcal{F}$, the operator $\lambda I - L_0$ becomes
multiplication in frequency space:

$$(\lambda I - L_0)g \;\longleftrightarrow\; \bigl(\lambda + \xi^2 - ic\xi\bigr)
\,\hat{g}(\xi), \qquad \xi \in \mathbb{R}.$$

The multiplier $m(\xi) = \lambda + \xi^2 - ic\xi$ satisfies:

$$\operatorname{Re} m(\xi) = \lambda + \xi^2 \geq \lambda > 0 \quad \text{for al
l } \xi \in \mathbb{R}.$$

Hence $|m(\xi)| \geq \lambda > 0$, so $m(\xi)^{-1}$ is bounded by $1/\lambda$. T
he inverse operator is multiplication by $m(\xi)^{-1}$ in Fourier space, which i
s a bounded operator on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$. Therefore $\lambda I - L_0$ is invert
ible with bounded inverse, and in particular $\operatorname{Range}(\lambda I - L
_0) = L^2(\mathbb{R})$.

### Conclusion of Step 1

By the **Lumer–Phillips theorem**, $L_0 = \partial_x^2 + c\,\partial_x$ with dom
ain $H^2(\mathbb{R})$ generates a $C_0$-semigroup of contractions $\{S_0(t)\}_{t
 \geq 0}$ on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$.

---

## Step 2: Bounded perturbation by $M = f'(v(\cdot))$

The multiplication operator $M$ defined by $(Mg)(x) = f'(v(x))\,g(x)$ is bounded
 on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$:

$$\|Mg\|_{L^2} \leq \|f'(v)\|_{L^\infty}\,\|g\|_{L^2},$$

where $\|f'(v)\|_{L^\infty} < \infty$ because $v$ is bounded and $f'$ is continu
ous (so $f'(v(\cdot))$ is a bounded continuous function). Thus $M \in \mathcal{L
}(L^2(\mathbb{R}))$.

**Bounded Perturbation Theorem** (see Pazy, *Semigroups of Linear Operators and
Applications to PDEs*, Ch. 3, or Engel–Nagel, *One-Parameter Semigroups*, Sec. I
II.1): *If $A$ generates a $C_0$-semigroup on a Banach space $X$ and $B \in \mat
hcal{L}(X)$ is bounded, then $A + B$ with domain $D(A)$ generates a $C_0$-semigr
oup on $X$.*

Applying this with $A = L_0$ and $B = M$:

$$\boxed{L = L_0 + M = \partial_x^2 + c\,\partial_x + f'(v), \qquad D(L) = H^2(\
mathbb{R}),}$$

generates a $C_0$-semigroup on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$.

---

## Remark (stronger result)

In fact, the semigroup is **analytic**. The operator $\partial_x^2$ generates an
 analytic (holomorphic) semigroup on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$. The first-order term $c\
,\partial_x$ is $\partial_x^2$-bounded with **relative bound $0$** (by the inter
polation inequality $\|g'\|_{L^2} \leq \varepsilon\|g''\|_{L^2} + C_\varepsilon\
|g\|_{L^2}$ for any $\varepsilon > 0$), and $M$ is bounded. By the perturbation
theorem for analytic semigroups (Henry, *Geometric Theory of Semilinear Paraboli
c Equations*, Ch. 3; or Pazy, Ch. 3, Thm 2.1), $L$ generates an **analytic $C_0$
-semigroup**, which is a strictly stronger conclusion.
