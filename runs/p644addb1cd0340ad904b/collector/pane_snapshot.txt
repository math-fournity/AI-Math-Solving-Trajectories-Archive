## Proof

**Claim:** The answer is **No**. In general, $A$ need not have the same Lebesgue
 measure as $E$.

**Counterexample.**

Let
$$E = \mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q} = \{\text{all irrational numbers}\}.$$

Then:

1. **$E$ is a subset of the irrational numbers.** Trivially, $E$ *is* the set of
 all irrational numbers.

2. **$E$ is dense in itself.** Every irrational number is a limit point of $E$:
for any $x \in E$ and any $\varepsilon > 0$, the interval $(x, x+\varepsilon)$ c
ontains infinitely many irrationals (since the irrationals are dense in $\mathbb
{R}$), so there exists $y \in E$ with $y \neq x$ and $|y - x| < \varepsilon$. He
nce every point of $E$ is an accumulation point of $E$, i.e., $E$ is dense in it
self.

3. **$E$ is Lebesgue measurable.** $E = \mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$, and $\
mathbb{Q}$ is countable hence measurable (with measure $0$). Therefore $E$ is me
asurable, with $\lambda(E) = \lambda(\mathbb{R}) - \lambda(\mathbb{Q}) = \infty
- 0 = \infty$.

   (If a finite-measure example is preferred, take $E = ([0,1] \cap (\mathbb{R}\
setminus\mathbb{Q}))$, the irrationals in $[0,1]$. This is dense in itself, meas
urable, and $\lambda(E) = 1$.)

Now set
$$A = \{\sqrt{2}\} \subseteq E.$$

Then:

1. **$A \subseteq E$.** $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational, so $\{\sqrt{2}\} \subseteq E$.

2. **$A$ is Lebesgue measurable.** $A$ is a singleton, hence a Borel set, hence
measurable, with $\lambda(A) = 0$.

3. **$\lambda(A) \neq \lambda(E)$.** We have $\lambda(A) = 0$ while $\lambda(E)
= \infty$ (or $\lambda(E) = 1$ in the bounded variant). In either case, $\lambda
(A) \neq \lambda(E)$.

**Conclusion.** All the hypotheses are satisfied — $A \subseteq E$, $E$ is a sub
set of the irrationals, $E$ is dense in itself, and both $A$ and $E$ are Lebesgu
e measurable — yet $A$ and $E$ have different Lebesgue measures. Therefore the s
tatement "$A$ has the same Lebesgue measure as $E$" is **false** in general.

The hypotheses impose no constraint forcing $\lambda(A) = \lambda(E)$: measurabi
lity of a subset says nothing about its measure equaling that of its superset, a
nd "dense in itself" is a topological condition with no measure-theoretic conseq
uence of this kind. A measurable subset of a measurable set can have any measure
 between $0$ and $\lambda(E)$ (inclusive), so equality is not forced.
