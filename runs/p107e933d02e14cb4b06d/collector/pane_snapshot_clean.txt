**Proof.**

We show that a polynomial $P(x) \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ satisfies $\frac{P(n+1)}{P(n)
} \in \mathbb{N}$ for every positive integer $n$ **if and only if** $P$ is a non
zero constant polynomial.

---

**Existence (constant case).** Take $P(x) = c$ for any nonzero $c \in \mathbb{Q}
$. Then $\frac{P(n+1)}{P(n)} = \frac{c}{c} = 1 \in \mathbb{N}$ for every positiv
e integer $n$. So such polynomials exist.

---

**Non-existence (non-constant case).** Suppose for contradiction that $P$ is non
-constant, with degree $d \geq 1$ and leading coefficient $a_d \neq 0$. Write

$$P(x) = a_d x^d + a_{d-1}x^{d-1} + \cdots + a_0.$$

Since the ratio $\frac{P(n+1)}{P(n)}$ must be defined and lie in $\mathbb{N}$ fo
r every positive integer $n$, we have $P(n) \neq 0$ for all $n \geq 1$.

We analyze the asymptotic behavior of the ratio. Expanding:

$$P(n) = a_d n^d\!\left(1 + \frac{a_{d-1}}{a_d\, n} + O(n^{-2})\right),$$

and using $(n+1)^d = n^d + d\,n^{d-1} + \binom{d}{2}n^{d-2} + \cdots$,

$$P(n+1) = a_d n^d + (a_d\, d + a_{d-1})\,n^{d-1} + O(n^{d-2}) = a_d n^d\!\left(
1 + \frac{d + a_{d-1}/a_d}{n} + O(n^{-2})\right).$$

Therefore

$$\frac{P(n+1)}{P(n)} = \frac{1 + \frac{d + a_{d-1}/a_d}{n} + O(n^{-2})}{1 + \fr
ac{a_{d-1}/a_d}{n} + O(n^{-2})} = \left(1 + \frac{d + a_{d-1}/a_d}{n} + O(n^{-2}
)\right)\!\left(1 - \frac{a_{d-1}/a_d}{n} + O(n^{-2})\right) = 1 + \frac{d}{n} +
 O(n^{-2}).$$

Since $d \geq 1$, the term $\frac{d}{n}$ is positive and dominates the $O(n^{-2}
)$ remainder for large $n$. Concretely, there exists $N$ such that for all $n >
N$:

$$0 < \frac{d}{n} + O(n^{-2}) < 1,$$

which gives

$$1 < \frac{P(n+1)}{P(n)} < 2 \qquad \text{for all } n > N.$$

But no natural number lies strictly between $1$ and $2$. (Under either conventio
n — $\mathbb{N} = \{1,2,3,\ldots\}$ or $\mathbb{N} = \{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ — the onl
y candidate near $1$ is $1$ itself, and the ratio is strictly greater than $1$.)
 This contradicts the requirement that $\frac{P(n+1)}{P(n)} \in \mathbb{N}$ for
**every** positive integer $n$.

Hence no non-constant polynomial can satisfy the condition. $\blacksquare$
