bseteq A$. We exhibit a concrete function $f \in B$ with $f \notin A$.

**The counterexample.** Take $n = 1$ and define
$$f(x) = |x|, \qquad x \in \mathbb{R}.$$

**Step 1: $f \in B$ ( $f$ has a weak partial derivative).**

Recall that a locally integrable function $g$ is the weak derivative of $f \in L
^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})$ if for every test function $\varphi \in C_c^\inft

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}} f(x)\,\varphi'(x)\,dx = -\int_{\mathbb{R}} g(x)\,\varphi(x)\
,dx.$$

The function $f(x)=|x|$ is continuous, hence locally integrable. Define
$$g(x) = \operatorname{sgn}(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & x > 0, \\ 0, & x = 0, \\ -1,
 & x < 0. \end{cases}$$
This $g$ is bounded and measurable, hence $g \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})$
.

For any $\varphi \in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R})$, split the integral at $0$ and inte
grate by parts on each half-line (where $f$ is smooth):
$$\int_{\mathbb{R}} |x|\,\varphi'(x)\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{0} (-x)\,\varphi'(x)\
,dx + \int_{0}^{\infty} x\,\varphi'(x)\,dx.$$

On $(-\infty,0)$: $\int_{-\infty}^{0}(-x)\varphi'(x)\,dx = \big[(-x)\varphi(x)\b
ig]_{-\infty}^{0} - \int_{-\infty}^{0}(-1)\varphi(x)\,dx = 0 + \int_{-\infty}^{0

ted and $-x\varphi(x)\to 0$ as $x\to 0^-$ and as $x\to -\infty$).

On $(0,\infty)$: $\int_{0}^{\infty} x\,\varphi'(x)\,dx = \big[x\varphi(x)\big]_{
0}^{\infty} - \int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(x)\,dx = 0 - \int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(x)\
,dx$.

This is exactly the weak-derivative identity with $g = \operatorname{sgn}$. Henc

**Step 2: $f \notin A$ ( $f$ is not continuously differentiable).**

The classical derivative of $f(x)=|x|$ does not exist at $x=0$: the left derivat
ive is $-1$ and the right derivative is $+1$. Since $f$ fails to be differentiab
le at a point, it is not differentiable everywhere, and therefore it is certainl

We have produced $f(x)=|x| \in \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ with $f \in B$ but $f \n

Intuitively, weak differentiability is a strictly weaker notion than classical c
ontinuous differentiability: it permits certain "kinks" and even discontinuities
 (in the derivative) that classical $C^1$ regularity forbids. The function $|x|$
 is the canonical witness of this gap.
