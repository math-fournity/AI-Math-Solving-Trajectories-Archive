## Proof

**Setup and key identity.** Let $M$ be interior to $\triangle ABC$. Define:
- $x = \angle MBC$, $z = \angle MCA$, $p = \angle MAB$ (one angle at each vertex
, measured from one side).
- The complementary angles: $\angle MCB = C - z$, $\angle MAC = A - p$, $\angle
MBA = B - x$.
- Let $\alpha = \angle BMC$, $\beta = \angle CMA$, $\gamma = \angle AMB$, with $
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=2\pi$.

**Step 1: Express $p(M)$ in terms of $x, z, p$.**

The distance from $M$ to $BC$ satisfies $\frac{1}{2}a\cdot d_a = \frac{1}{2}MB\c
dot MC\sin\alpha$, so $d_a = \frac{MB\cdot MC\sin\alpha}{a}$, and similarly for
$d_b, d_c$. Thus:

$$p(M) = \frac{d_a d_b d_c}{MA\cdot MB\cdot MC} = \frac{MA\cdot MB\cdot MC\cdot
\sin\alpha\sin\beta\sin\gamma}{abc}.$$

By the law of sines in $\triangle BCM$: $\frac{MC}{\sin x}=\frac{a}{\sin\alpha}$
, giving $MC\sin\alpha = a\sin x$. In $\triangle CAM$: $\frac{MA}{\sin z}=\frac{
b}{\sin\beta}$, giving $MA\sin\beta = b\sin z$. In $\triangle ABM$: $\frac{MB}{\
sin p}=\frac{c}{\sin\gamma}$, giving $MB\sin\gamma = c\sin p$. Therefore:

$$MA\cdot MB\cdot MC\cdot\sin\alpha\sin\beta\sin\gamma = (MA\sin\beta)(MB\sin\ga
mma)(MC\sin\alpha) = (b\sin z)(c\sin p)(a\sin x) = abc\sin x\sin z\sin p.$$

Substituting back:

$$\boxed{p(M) = \sin x\,\sin z\,\sin p.}$$

**Step 2: The constraint via trigonometric Ceva.**

The cevians from $B$ (at angle $x$ from $BC$), from $C$ (at angle $z$ from $CA$)
, and from $A$ (at angle $p$ from $AB$) concur at $M$. By the trigonometric form
 of Ceva's theorem:

$$\frac{\sin x}{\sin(B-x)}\cdot\frac{\sin z}{\sin(C-z)}\cdot\frac{\sin p}{\sin(A
-p)} = 1,$$

that is, $\sin x\sin z\sin p = \sin(B-x)\sin(C-z)\sin(A-p)$.

**Step 3: Maximizing $p(M) = \sin x\sin z\sin p$ subject to the Ceva constraint.
**

For any angle $\theta\in(0,\pi)$ and $t\in(0,\theta)$, the product-to-sum identi
ty gives:

$$\sin t\,\sin(\theta - t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[\cos(2t-\theta)-\cos\theta\bigr]
\le \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[1-\cos\theta\bigr] = \sin^2\!\tfrac{\theta}{2},$$

with equality if and only if $t = \theta/2$.

Applying this to each pair:

$$\sin x\,\sin(B-x)\le\sin^2\!\tfrac{B}{2},\qquad \sin z\,\sin(C-z)\le\sin^2\!\t
frac{C}{2},\qquad \sin p\,\sin(A-p)\le\sin^2\!\tfrac{A}{2}.$$

Multiplying:

$$\bigl[\sin x\sin z\sin p\bigr]\bigl[\sin(B-x)\sin(C-z)\sin(A-p)\bigr]\le\sin^2
\!\tfrac{A}{2}\,\sin^2\!\tfrac{B}{2}\,\sin^2\!\tfrac{C}{2}.$$

By the Ceva constraint, both bracketed factors equal $p(M)$, so:

$$p(M)^2 \le \sin^2\!\tfrac{A}{2}\,\sin^2\!\tfrac{B}{2}\,\sin^2\!\tfrac{C}{2},$$

hence $p(M)\le\sin\frac{A}{2}\sin\frac{B}{2}\sin\frac{C}{2}$, with equality if a
nd only if $x=B/2$, $z=C/2$, $p=A/2$ simultaneously, i.e., $M$ is the **incenter
** $I$.

Therefore:

$$\mu(ABC) = \sin\frac{A}{2}\,\sin\frac{B}{2}\,\sin\frac{C}{2},\qquad\text{achie
ved at }M=I.$$

**Step 4: Maximizing $\mu(ABC)$ over all triangles.**

Set $a=A/2$, $b=B/2$, $c=C/2$, so $a+b+c=\pi/2$ with $a,b,c>0$. We maximize $\si
n a\sin b\sin c$.

Since $\log\sin$ is strictly concave on $(0,\pi/2)$, Jensen's inequality gives:

$$\frac{\log\sin a+\log\sin b+\log\sin c}{3}\le\log\sin\frac{a+b+c}{3}=\log\sin\
frac{\pi}{6}=\log\frac{1}{2},$$

so $\sin a\sin b\sin c\le\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3=\frac{1}{8}$, with equality
if and only if $a=b=c=\pi/6$, i.e., $A=B=C=\pi/3$.

**Conclusion.** The point $M$ maximizing $p(M)$ is the **incenter** of $\triangl
e ABC$, and the maximum value is $\mu(ABC)=\sin\frac{A}{2}\sin\frac{B}{2}\sin\fr
ac{C}{2}$. Among all triangles, $\mu(ABC)$ is maximized by the **equilateral tri
angle**, where $\mu=\frac{1}{8}$.
