**Claim.** Yes — $A^k(U)=U$ implies $A(U)=U$.

**Proof.**

Let $N:=A-I$. Since $A$ is unipotent, $N$ is nilpotent; let $m\ge 1$ be its nilp
otency index, so $N^{m}=0$ and $N^{m-1}\neq 0$. The algebra $\mathbb{C}[A]=\math
bb{C}[I+N]=\mathbb{C}[N]$ is a commutative subalgebra of $\operatorname{End}(\ma
thbb{C}^n)$, isomorphic to $R:=\mathbb{C}[x]/(x^{m})$ via $x\mapsto N$. In parti
cular $\dim_{\mathbb{C}} R=m$, with basis $\{1,N,\dots,N^{m-1}\}$.

A subspace $U\subseteq\mathbb{C}^n$ is $A$-invariant iff it is $N$-invariant iff
 it is $R$-invariant. Likewise $U$ is $A^{k}$-invariant iff it is $\mathbb{C}[A^
{k}]$-invariant. We will show

$$\mathbb{C}[A^{k}]\;=\;\mathbb{C}[A]\;=\;R,$$

so that $A^{k}$-invariance and $A$-invariance coincide.

Set $B:=A^{k}-I=(I+N)^{k}-I$. Expanding in $R$ (where $N^{m}=0$):

$$B \;=\; kN+\binom{k}{2}N^{2}+\cdots+\binom{k}{m-1}N^{m-1} \;=\; kN\cdot W,$$

where $W:=I+\tfrac{k-1}{2}N+\cdots$ is a polynomial in $N$ with constant term $1
$. Since $W\equiv I\pmod{N}$, the element $W$ is a **unit** of $R$ (its inverse
is a finite polynomial in $N$, obtained by truncating the geometric series, whic
h terminates because $N$ is nilpotent). Also $k\neq 0$ in $\mathbb{C}$.

Now compute powers of $B$:

$$B^{j}=(kN)^{j}\,W^{j}=k^{j}\,N^{j}\,W^{j}.$$

Since $W^{j}$ is a unit with $W^{j}\equiv I\pmod{N}$, we have $W^{j}=I+(\text{te
rms of }N\text{-degree}\ge 1)$, hence

$$B^{j}=k^{j}N^{j}+\bigl(\text{terms of }N\text{-degree}>j\bigr),\qquad j=0,1,\d
ots,m-1.$$

Thus the transition matrix expressing $\{1,B,B^{2},\dots,B^{m-1}\}$ in the basis
 $\{1,N,\dots,N^{m-1}\}$ is **upper triangular** with diagonal entries $1,k,k^{2
},\dots,k^{m-1}$, all nonzero. Therefore $\{1,B,\dots,B^{m-1}\}$ is linearly ind
ependent. Since $\mathbb{C}[B]\subseteq R$ and $\dim R=m$, we conclude

$$\mathbb{C}[B]=R.$$

Because $A^{k}=I+B$ and $A=I+N$, we have $\mathbb{C}[A^{k}]=\mathbb{C}[B]=R=\mat
hbb{C}[N]=\mathbb{C}[A]$.

**Conclusion of the argument.** Since $A$ is unipotent, it is invertible; hence
$A^{k}$ is invertible. The condition $A^{k}(U)=U$ is therefore equivalent to $A^
{k}(U)\subseteq U$, i.e., $U$ is $A^{k}$-invariant, i.e., $U$ is $\mathbb{C}[A^{
k}]=R$-invariant. By the equality $\mathbb{C}[A^{k}]=\mathbb{C}[A]$, this is equ
ivalent to $U$ being $A$-invariant: $A(U)\subseteq U$. Applying invertibility of
 $A$ once more, $A(U)\subseteq U\iff A(U)=U$.

Therefore $A^{k}(U)=U\;\Longrightarrow\;A(U)=U$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark on the role of unipotence.** The argument hinges on $A^{k}-I=k(A-I)\cdo
t(\text{unit})$ inside $\mathbb{C}[A]$, which uses that the sole eigenvalue is $
1$ (so $k\neq 0$ makes $k$ a unit and $W\equiv I$). For a general invertible $A$
 the statement fails: e.g. $A=-I$, $k=2$ gives $A^{2}=I$, making every subspace
$A^{2}$-invariant, while $-I$-invariant subspaces need not be all of them when $
n>1$... in fact $-I$ preserves every subspace too, so take instead $A=\operatorn
ame{diag}(1,\zeta)$ with $\zeta$ a primitive $k$-th root of unity: then $A^{k}=I
$ preserves everything but $A$ does not. This confirms unipotence is essential.
