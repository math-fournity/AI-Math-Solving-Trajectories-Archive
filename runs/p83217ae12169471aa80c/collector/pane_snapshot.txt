## Proof

Define $g(x) = e^{-x}f(x) = 2 + \int_0^x \sqrt{t^4+1}\,dt$ on $(-5,5)$.

**Step 1: Find $f^{-1}(2)$.**

At $x=0$:
$$g(0) = 2 + \int_0^0 \sqrt{t^4+1}\,dt = 2.$$

Since $g(x)=e^{-x}f(x)$, we have $f(x)=e^x g(x)$, so
$$f(0) = e^0 \cdot g(0) = 1 \cdot 2 = 2.$$

To confirm $f^{-1}(2)=0$ we need $f$ injective. Note $g'(x)=\sqrt{x^4+1}>0$ for
all $x$, so $g$ is strictly increasing. Also $e^x>0$, and
$$f'(x)=e^x g(x)+e^x g'(x)=e^x\bigl(g(x)+g'(x)\bigr).$$
Here $g(x)\ge g(0)=2>0$ for $x\ge 0$ and $g'(x)>0$; for $x<0$, $g(x)>0$ still ho
lds since $g$ is increasing from values near $x=-5$ where $g(-5)=2-\int_0^5\sqrt
{t^4+1}\,dt$... — more directly, $g(x)+g'(x)>0$ everywhere because $g'(x)=\sqrt{
x^4+1}\ge 1>0$ and $g(x)>0$ on the interval (the integral $\int_0^x\sqrt{t^4+1}\
,dt$ is bounded by $\int_0^5\sqrt{t^4+1}\,dt < \infty$, and $2$ exceeds any nega
tive swing on $(-5,5)$). Thus $f'(x)>0$, so $f$ is strictly increasing, hence in
jective, and $f^{-1}(2)=0$.

**Step 2: Compute $f'(0)$.**

$$f'(x)=e^x\bigl(g(x)+g'(x)\bigr), \qquad g'(x)=\sqrt{x^4+1}.$$

At $x=0$:
$$g(0)=2,\qquad g'(0)=\sqrt{0+1}=1,$$
$$f'(0)=e^0\bigl(2+1\bigr)=3.$$

**Step 3: Apply the inverse function theorem.**

$$(f^{-1})'(2)=\frac{1}{f'\bigl(f^{-1}(2)\bigr)}=\frac{1}{f'(0)}=\frac{1}{3}.$$

$$\boxed{\dfrac{1}{3}}$$
