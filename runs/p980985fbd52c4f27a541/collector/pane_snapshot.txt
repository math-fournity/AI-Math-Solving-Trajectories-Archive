## Solution

**Claim:** Yes, $g_u$ necessarily commutes with $N$.

**Proof.** Consider the linear operator $\operatorname{Ad}g$ on $V := \operatorn
ame{Mat}_n(\mathbb{C})$. Since $g_s$ and $g_u$ commute,

$$\operatorname{Ad}g = \operatorname{Ad}g_s \circ \operatorname{Ad}g_u = \operat
orname{Ad}g_u \circ \operatorname{Ad}g_s.$$

We identify the Jordan decomposition of $\operatorname{Ad}g$:

- $g_s$ is diagonalizable, so choosing a basis in which $g_s = \operatorname{dia
g}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$, the operator $\operatorname{Ad}g_s$ acts on the ba
sis element $E_{ij}$ by $\alpha_i\alpha_j^{-1}$. Hence $\operatorname{Ad}g_s$ is
 diagonalizable, i.e. semisimple.
- $g_u$ is unipotent, so $g_u = I + M$ with $M$ nilpotent; then $\operatorname{A
d}g_u = \operatorname{Ad}(I+M)$ is $I$ plus a nilpotent operator (a polynomial i
n $\operatorname{ad}M$), hence unipotent.
- They commute, as noted above.

Thus $\operatorname{Ad}g = (\operatorname{Ad}g_s)(\operatorname{Ad}g_u)$ is the
multiplicative Jordan decomposition of the operator $\operatorname{Ad}g$, with s
emisimple part $S := \operatorname{Ad}g_s$ and unipotent part $U := \operatornam
e{Ad}g_u$.

**Key lemma.** *If $T = S\,U$ is the Jordan decomposition of a linear operator o
n a complex vector space ($S$ semisimple, $U$ unipotent, $SU=US$), then every ei
genvector of $T$ is an eigenvector of $S$ with the same eigenvalue.*

*Proof of lemma.* Since $S$ is semisimple, $V = \bigoplus_{\mu} V_\mu$ where $V_
\mu = \ker(S - \mu I)$. Because $U$ commutes with $S$, each $V_\mu$ is $U$-invar
iant. On $V_\mu$, we have $T = S\,U = \mu\, U$, and $U$ is unipotent, so every e
igenvalue of $T|_{V_\mu}$ equals $\mu$. Now let $Tv = \lambda v$ with $v \neq 0$
, and write $v = \sum_\mu v_\mu$. By the direct-sum decomposition, $\mu\, U v_\m
u = \lambda v_\mu$ for each $\mu$. On $V_\mu$, $\mu U$ has sole eigenvalue $\mu$
, so $\mu U v_\mu = \lambda v_\mu$ forces $\mu = \lambda$ (or $v_\mu = 0$). Henc
e $v \in V_\lambda$, i.e. $Sv = \lambda v$. ∎

**Applying the lemma.** By hypothesis $N$ is an eigenvector of $\operatorname{Ad
}g$ with eigenvalue $\lambda \neq 0$. The lemma gives

$$\operatorname{Ad}g_s(N) = \lambda N, \qquad \text{i.e. } g_s N g_s^{-1} = \lam
bda N. \tag{1}$$

Now use $\operatorname{Ad}g(N) = \lambda N$ together with $\operatorname{Ad}g =
\operatorname{Ad}g_s \circ \operatorname{Ad}g_u$:

$$\operatorname{Ad}g_s\!\bigl(\operatorname{Ad}g_u(N)\bigr) = \lambda N. \tag{2}
$$

Since $\operatorname{Ad}g_u$ commutes with $\operatorname{Ad}g_s$, it preserves
the $\lambda$-eigenspace $V_\lambda$ of $\operatorname{Ad}g_s$. By (1), $N \in V
_\lambda$, so $\operatorname{Ad}g_u(N) \in V_\lambda$ as well, meaning

$$\operatorname{Ad}g_s\!\bigl(\operatorname{Ad}g_u(N)\bigr) = \lambda\, \operato
rname{Ad}g_u(N). \tag{3}$$

Combining (2) and (3):

$$\lambda\, \operatorname{Ad}g_u(N) = \lambda N.$$

Since $\lambda \neq 0$, we conclude $\operatorname{Ad}g_u(N) = N$, i.e.

$$g_u N g_u^{-1} = N.$$

Therefore $g_u$ commutes with $N$. $\blacksquare$
