## Proof

**Answer: Yes, $f$ is necessarily the zero function on $[0,\pi]$.**

The key tool is the completeness of the sine system $\{\sin(nx) : n \geq 1\}$ in
 $L^2([0,\pi])$, which we establish via the odd extension and the completeness o
f the full trigonometric system.

---

**Step 1 (Odd extension).** Define $G$ on $[-\pi,\pi]$ by
$$
G(x) = \begin{cases} f(x), & 0 \leq x \leq \pi, \\ -f(-x), & -\pi \leq x < 0. \e
nd{cases}
$$
Then $G \in L^2([-\pi,\pi])$ and $G$ is odd: $G(-x) = -G(x)$.

**Step 2 (All Fourier coefficients of $G$ vanish).** The standard Fourier coeffi
cients of $G$ on $[-\pi,\pi]$ are
$$
a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} G(x)\cos(nx)\,dx, \qquad b_n = \frac{1}{\pi
}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} G(x)\sin(nx)\,dx.
$$
Since $G$ is odd and $\cos(nx)$ is even, the product $G(x)\cos(nx)$ is odd, so $
a_n = 0$ for all $n \geq 0$.
Since $G$ is odd and $\sin(nx)$ is odd, the product $G(x)\sin(nx)$ is even, so
$$
b_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} f(x)\sin(nx)\,dx = 0
$$
for all $n \geq 1$, by the hypothesis.

Thus every Fourier coefficient of $G$ is zero.

**Step 3 (Completeness of the trigonometric system).** We use the classical fact
 that the trigonometric system $\{1, \cos(nx), \sin(nx) : n \geq 1\}$ is complet
e in $L^2([-\pi,\pi])$:

*Proof of the fact.* Let $h \in L^2([-\pi,\pi])$ have all Fourier coefficients e
qual to zero. The Cesàro means (Fejér means) of the Fourier series of $h$ are
$$
\sigma_N(h)(x) = (F_N * h)(x),
$$
where $F_N$ is the Fejér kernel, a nonnegative approximate identity with $\frac{
1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} F_N = 1$. Since $\sigma_N$ is a linear combination of
the Fourier partial sums $S_0, \dots, S_N$, and every partial sum is built from
the (vanishing) Fourier coefficients, we have $\sigma_N(h) \equiv 0$ for all $N$
. On the other hand, the Fejér kernel being an approximate identity gives $\sigm
a_N(h) \to h$ in $L^2([-\pi,\pi])$. Hence $h = 0$ a.e. $\square$

**Step 4 (Conclusion).** Applying Step 3 to $G$, we obtain $G = 0$ a.e. on $[-\p
i,\pi]$. In particular $f = G|_{[0,\pi]} = 0$ a.e. on $[0,\pi]$.

Since $f$ is **continuous** on $[0,\pi]$, if $f(x_0) \neq 0$ for some $x_0 \in [
0,\pi]$, then by continuity $f$ is nonzero on an open neighborhood of $x_0$ (int
ersected with $[0,\pi]$), which is a set of positive Lebesgue measure. This cont
radicts $f = 0$ a.e. Therefore $f(x) = 0$ for every $x \in [0,\pi]$.

---

**Remark.** The hypothesis $n \geq 1$ (rather than $n \geq 0$) is essential and
sufficient: $\sin(0\cdot x) \equiv 0$ carries no information, and the family $\{
\sin(nx)\}_{n\geq 1}$ is already complete. Note also that no cosine moments are
assumed; the odd extension converts the sine-only data on $[0,\pi]$ into the ful
l set of Fourier coefficients on $[-\pi,\pi]$, which is why completeness applies
.
