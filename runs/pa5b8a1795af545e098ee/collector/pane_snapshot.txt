## Solution

**Claim.** The only $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ satisfying $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
f(t)\,e^{-t^2+2xt}\,dt = 0$ for a.e. $x\in\mathbb{R}$ is $f=0$ a.e.

**Proof.**

**Step 1: Rewrite as a convolution.** Complete the square:
$$-t^2+2xt = -(t-x)^2+x^2,$$
so, setting $\varphi(t)=e^{-t^2}$,
$$F(x):=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)\,e^{-t^2+2xt}\,dt = e^{x^2}\int_{-\infty}^{\
infty}f(t)\,e^{-(t-x)^2}\,dt = e^{x^2}\,(f*\varphi)(x).$$

Since $e^{-t^2+2xt}\le e^{x^2}$ and $f\in L^1$, the integral converges absolutel
y for every $x$, so $F$ is defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

**Step 2: Upgrade "a.e. zero" to "everywhere zero."** We have $\varphi\in L^1\ca
p L^\infty$ and $\varphi$ is uniformly continuous. Since $f\in L^1$ and $\varphi
$ is bounded and uniformly continuous, the convolution $f*\varphi$ is **continuo
us** (indeed uniformly continuous and bounded). The hypothesis $F(x)=0$ a.e. giv
es $(f*\varphi)(x)=0$ a.e. (since $e^{x^2}>0$). A continuous function vanishing
a.e. vanishes everywhere, hence
$$(f*\varphi)(x)=0\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.$$

**Step 3: Take the Fourier transform.** Both $f$ and $\varphi$ are in $L^1$, so
$f*\varphi\in L^1$ and the Fourier transform (with convention $\hat g(\xi)=\int
g(t)e^{-i\xi t}\,dt$) satisfies the convolution theorem:
$$\widehat{f*\varphi}(\xi)=\hat f(\xi)\,\hat\varphi(\xi).$$
Since $f*\varphi\equiv 0$, we get $\hat f(\xi)\,\hat\varphi(\xi)=0$ for all $\xi
$.

**Step 4: $\hat\varphi$ never vanishes.** The classical Gaussian integral gives
$$\hat\varphi(\xi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-t^2}e^{-i\xi t}\,dt=\sqrt{\pi}\,e^
{-\xi^2/4}>0\quad\text{for all }\xi\in\mathbb{R}.$$

**Step 5: Conclude.** Since $\hat\varphi(\xi)\neq 0$ everywhere, $\hat f(\xi)=0$
 for all $\xi$. By the injectivity (uniqueness theorem) of the Fourier transform
 on $L^1$, $f=0$ a.e.

Conversely, $f=0$ trivially satisfies the condition. $\blacksquare$
