**Answer: Yes, such distinct lambda terms exist.**

**Construction.** Let
- $M = \lambda x.\lambda y.\,y$ (the "false" combinator, $\mathsf{K}\mathsf{I}$)
,
- $B = \lambda z.\,z$ (the identity combinator $\mathsf{I}$).

These are distinct terms: $M = \lambda x.\lambda y.\,y$ is a two-level abstracti
on, while $B = \lambda z.\,z$ is a single-level abstraction, so $M \neq B$.

**Compute $\mathsf{nf}(MB)$.**
$$MB = (\lambda x.\lambda y.\,y)(\lambda z.\,z) \to_\beta \lambda y.\,y = \maths
f{I}.$$
This is already in normal form, so $\mathsf{nf}(MB) = \mathsf{I}$.

**Compute $\mathsf{nf}((MB)(MB))$.**
Since $MB \to_\beta^* \mathsf{I}$, we have
$$(MB)(MB) \to_\beta^* \mathsf{I}\,\mathsf{I} = (\lambda y.\,y)\,\mathsf{I} \to_
\beta \mathsf{I}.$$
Thus $\mathsf{nf}((MB)(MB)) = \mathsf{I}$.

**Conclusion.** $\mathsf{nf}(MB) = \mathsf{I} = \mathsf{nf}((MB)(MB))$, and $M \
neq B$. Therefore such distinct lambda terms $M$ and $B$ exist. $\blacksquare$
