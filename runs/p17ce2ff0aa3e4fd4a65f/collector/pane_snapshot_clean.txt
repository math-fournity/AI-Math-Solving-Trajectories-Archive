## Proof

Let $X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^N$ be a complex projective variety of dimension $n$,
 and let $I_X \subseteq k[x_0,\dots,x_N]$ be its homogeneous ideal. Write $S = k
[x_0,\dots,x_N]$ and $R = S/I_X$ for the homogeneous coordinate ring, so that th
e Hilbert function is $H_R(m) = \dim_k R_m$ for $m \gg 0$ given by the Hilbert p
olynomial $P_R(m)$.

### Step 1. The Hilbert polynomial after successive hyperplane sections

Let $H_1, \dots, H_n$ be $n$ hyperplanes in general position relative to $X$, wi
th corresponding linear forms $\ell_1,\dots,\ell_n \in S_1$. Set $X_0 = X$ and $
X_i = X_{i-1} \cap H_i$ for $i = 1,\dots,n$. Each $H_i$ is general relative to $
X_{i-1}$, so by Bertini (and genericity) each $X_i$ has dimension $n - i$ and th
e section is reduced; in particular $X_n$ is a reduced finite set of points.

The homogeneous coordinate ring of $X_i$ is $R_i = R_{i-1}/(\bar\ell_i)$, where
$\bar\ell_i$ is the image of $\ell_i$. Since $\ell_i$ is a non-zero-divisor in $
R_{i-1}$ for general choice (the section is reduced, hence the linear form avoid
s all associated primes), we have the exact sequence
$$0 \to R_{i-1}(m-1) \xrightarrow{\cdot \bar\ell_i} R_{i-1}(m) \to R_i(m) \to 0,
$$
so for $m \gg 0$,
$$P_{R_i}(m) = P_{R_{i-1}}(m) - P_{R_{i-1}}(m-1) = \Delta P_{R_{i-1}}(m).$$

Iterating, $P_{R_n}(m) = \Delta^n P_R(m)$, the $n$-th finite difference of the H
ilbert polynomial of $R$.

### Step 2. Reading off the leading coefficient

Write
$$P_R(m) = \frac{d}{n!}\,m^n + (\text{lower degree terms}),$$
so that the modern degree is $\deg_{\mathrm{Hilb}}(X) = d$ by definition. A stan
dard computation of finite differences gives $\Delta^n(m^n) = n!$ and $\Delta^n(
m^k) = 0$ for $k < n$. Therefore
$$P_{R_n}(m) = \Delta^n P_R(m) = \frac{d}{n!}\cdot n! = d \qquad (m \gg 0).$$

### Step 3. The Hilbert polynomial of a finite reduced set of points

Since $X_n$ is a reduced finite set of $r$ points, its homogeneous coordinate ri
ng $R_n$ has Hilbert polynomial equal to the constant $r$ (each point contribute
s one linearly independent evaluation in large degree, and the $r$ points impose
 independent conditions). Thus
$$P_{R_n}(m) = r = \# X_n \qquad \text{for all } m \ge 0.$$

Combining Steps 2 and 3:
$$d = \deg_{\mathrm{Hilb}}(X) = \# X_n = \#\big(X \cap H_1 \cap \cdots \cap H_n\
big).$$

### Step 4. Connecting to Bézout's theorem

It remains to identify $\# X_n$ with the classical geometric count, and this is
exactly where Bézout's theorem enters.

If $X$ is a hypersurface of degree $d$ in $\mathbb{P}^{n+1}$ (so $\dim X = n$),
then $X = V(F)$ for a homogeneous $F$ of degree $d$. Cutting by $n$ general hype
rplanes reduces to intersecting the plane curve $C = X \cap H_1 \cap \cdots \cap
 H_{n-1} \subseteq \mathbb{P}^2$ (a curve of degree $d$) with a general line $H_
n$. By Bézout's theorem, a curve of degree $d$ and a line of degree $1$ in $\mat
hbb{P}^2$ meet in exactly $d \cdot 1 = d$ points counted with multiplicity; gene
rality ensures all intersections are transverse, so the number of *reduced* inte
rsection points is exactly $d$. This $d$ is both the classical degree (number of
 reduced points) and, by Steps 1–3, the leading coefficient of the Hilbert polyn
omial times $n!$.

For a general variety $X$ of dimension $n$ (not necessarily a hypersurface), one
 reduces to the hypersurface case by taking general hyperplane sections down to
a hypersurface: $X \cap H_1 \cap \cdots \cap H_{n-1}$ is a hypersurface in $\mat
hbb{P}^{n+1-n+1} = \mathbb{P}^2$... more precisely, after $n-1$ general hyperpla
nes, $Y = X \cap H_1 \cap \cdots \cap H_{n-1}$ is a curve in some projective spa
ce, and a further general hyperplane cuts it in $\deg(Y)$ points by Bézout appli
ed to the curve. The key point is that the successive-difference argument of Ste
ps 1–2 shows the leading coefficient is preserved under general hyperplane secti
on (the top coefficient of $P_R$ becomes the top coefficient of $P_{R_1}$, etc.)
, so $\deg(X) = \deg(X_1) = \cdots = \deg(X_{n-1})$, and the final step is the B
ézout count of a curve cut by a hyperplane. Equivalently, one may view $X$ as be
ing cut out by equations of degrees $d_1, \dots, d_c$ with $\sum d_i = d$ when $
X$ is a complete intersection, and Bézout's theorem in $\mathbb{P}^N$ gives the
number of reduced intersection points of $X$ with $n$ further general hyperplane
s as $d_1 \cdots d_c \cdot 1^n = d_1 \cdots d_c = d$, matching the Hilbert-polyn
omial computation. The general (non-complete-intersection) case follows by degen
eration: every variety is a flat limit of complete intersections of the same deg
ree, and both the Hilbert-polynomial degree and the Bézout intersection number a
re constant in flat families.

### Conclusion

The Hilbert-polynomial computation (Steps 1–3) shows that the leading coefficien
t $d$ of $P_R$ times $n!$ equals the number of reduced points in $X \cap H_1 \ca
p \cdots \cap H_n$ for $n$ general hyperplanes. Bézout's theorem (Step 4) identi
fies this point count with the classical geometric notion of degree — the produc
t of degrees of defining equations, or equivalently the number of intersection p
oints with a general linear subspace of complementary dimension. Hence the two d
efinitions of degree agree:

$$\deg_{\mathrm{classical}}(X) \;=\; \#\big(X \cap H_1 \cap \cdots \cap H_n\big)
_{\mathrm{red}} \;=\; n!\cdot[\text{leading coefficient of } P_R] \;=\; \deg_{\m
athrm{Hilb}}(X).$$
