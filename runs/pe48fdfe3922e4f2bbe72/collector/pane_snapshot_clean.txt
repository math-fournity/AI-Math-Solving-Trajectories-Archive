**Answer: No, $B$ is not necessarily algebraically closed in $B^*$.**

## Proof

We construct an explicit counterexample.

**Setup.** Let $A = \mathbb{Q}$ and $B = \mathbb{Q}(t)$ where $t$ is transcenden
tal over $\mathbb{Q}$. Both are infinite fields.

**Step 1: $A$ is existentially closed in $B$.**

The extension $B/A = \mathbb{Q}(t)/\mathbb{Q}$ is purely transcendental, hence r
egular. A standard result in model theory of fields states that if $B/A$ is a re
gular field extension, then $A$ is existentially closed in $B$ (in the language
of rings). The key argument: any finite system of polynomial equations and inequ
ations with coefficients in $A$ that has a solution in $A(t)$ can be specialized
 by sending $t$ to a suitable element of $A$ (possible since $A$ is infinite), y
ielding a solution in $A$.

**Step 2: Constructing an intermediate field $C$ with $B \subsetneq C$ and $C/A$
 regular.**

Let $C = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{t})$. Since $t$ is transcendental over $\mathbb{Q}$, s
o is $\sqrt{t}$ (if $\sqrt{t}$ were algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$, then $t = (\sqr
t{t})^2$ would be algebraic, a contradiction). Thus $C/\mathbb{Q}$ is purely tra
nscendental, hence regular.

Note that $B = \mathbb{Q}(t) \subsetneq C = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{t})$, since $\sqrt{
t} \notin \mathbb{Q}(t)$: if $\sqrt{t} = f(t)/g(t)$ for $f, g \in \mathbb{Q}[t]$
, then $t \cdot g(t)^2 = f(t)^2$, which is impossible by degree considerations (
the left side has odd degree, the right side has even degree).

**Step 3: Building $B^*$.**

Since $C/A = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{t})/\mathbb{Q}$ is regular, $A = \mathbb{Q}$ is ex
istentially closed in $C$. By the proposition stated in the problem, $C$ embeds
into an $\mathcal{L}$-structure $B^* \succ A$ with $B^*/A$ a regular extension.
We identify $C$ with its image in $B^*$, so $C \subseteq B^*$.

**Step 4: Verifying the conditions.**

- $B^* \succ A = \mathbb{Q}$: by construction. ✓
- $B \subseteq B^*$: since $B = \mathbb{Q}(t) \subseteq C = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{t})
 \subseteq B^*$, the embedding of $B$ into $B^*$ is the inclusion, which is the
identity on $A$. ✓
- $B^*/A$ is regular: by construction. ✓
- $A$ is algebraically closed in $B^*$: follows from $B^*/A$ being regular. ✓

**Step 5: $B$ is not algebraically closed in $B^*$.**

The element $\sqrt{t} \in B^*$ is algebraic over $B = \mathbb{Q}(t)$ (it is a ro
ot of $x^2 - t \in B[x]$), but $\sqrt{t} \notin B$ (as shown in Step 2). Therefo
re, $B$ is **not** algebraically closed in $B^*$.

**Key observation.** The regularity of $B^*/A$ only constrains elements algebrai
c over $A$: such elements must already lie in $A$. But an element like $\sqrt{t}
$ is *transcendental over $A$* while being *algebraic over $B$*. The regularity
condition $B^*/A$ is completely silent about such elements, allowing them to inh
abit $B^* \setminus B$ and witness the failure of $B$ being algebraically closed
 in $B^*$.
