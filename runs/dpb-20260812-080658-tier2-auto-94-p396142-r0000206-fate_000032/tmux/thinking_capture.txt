Theorem. In a Boolean ring, every prime ideal is a maximal ideal.

Proof.

Recall that a Boolean ring is a ring $R$ (with identity $1$) in which every
element is idempotent: $$x^2 = x \quad \text{for all } x \in R.$$

Let $P$ be a prime ideal of $R$. We must show that $P$ is maximal, i.e., that
the quotient $R/P$ has no nontrivial proper ideals.

Step 1. The quotient $R/P$ is a Boolean ring.

For any $\bar{x} = x + P \in R/P$, $$\bar{x}^2 = \overline{x^2} = \bar{x},$$
since $x^2 = x$ in $R$. Hence $R/P$ is Boolean.

Step 2. The quotient $R/P$ is an integral domain (since $P$ is prime).

By definition of prime ideal, $P \neq R$ and $ab \in P \implies a \in P$ or $b
\in P$. This is equivalent to $R/P$ being an integral domain (commutative, with
$1 \neq 0$).

Step 3. A Boolean integral domain is a field.

Let $S = R/P$, a Boolean integral domain. Take any nonzero $a \in S$. Since $S$
is Boolean, $$a^2 = a \implies a^2 - a = 0 \implies a(a - 1) = 0.$$ Since $S$ is
an integral domain and $a \neq 0$, we conclude $$a - 1 = 0, \quad \text{i.e.,}
\quad a = 1.$$

Thus the only nonzero element of $S$ is $1$ itself, so $S = {0, 1} \cong
\mathbb{F}_2$, which is a field.

Step 4. Conclusion.

Since $R/P$ is a field, $P$ is a maximal ideal. $\blacksquare$

Remark on the key identity. The crux is the factorization $a(a-1)=0$ in a
Boolean ring: idempotence forces every element to satisfy $a(a-1)=0$, so in an
integral domain (no zero divisors) every nonzero element must equal $1$. This is
why a Boolean integral domain collapses to $\mathbb{F}_2$, leaving no room for
any ideal strictly between $P$ and $R$.
