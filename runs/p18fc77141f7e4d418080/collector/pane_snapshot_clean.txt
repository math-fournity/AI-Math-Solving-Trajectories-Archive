## Counterexample

Let $n = 2$, $C = (0,4) \times (0,1)$, and define
$$g(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 1)(x_1 - 3)\,e^{x_2}.$$

This is $C^\infty$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Verification that $\nabla g \neq 0$ in $C$:**

$$\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_1} = (2x_1 - 4)\,e^{x_2}, \qquad \frac{\partial g
}{\partial x_2} = (x_1-1)(x_1-3)\,e^{x_2}.$$

Since $e^{x_2} > 0$ always, $\nabla g = 0$ requires simultaneously:
- $2x_1 - 4 = 0 \implies x_1 = 2$, and
- $(x_1-1)(x_1-3) = 0 \implies x_1 = 1$ or $x_1 = 3$.

These conditions are contradictory, so $\nabla g \neq 0$ everywhere in $C$. ✓

**The zero set:**

$g = 0$ iff $x_1 = 1$ or $x_1 = 3$, so
$$M \cap C = \{1\}\times(0,1)\ \cup\ \{3\}\times(0,1),$$
which is nonempty. ✓

**The complement $C \setminus (M \cap C)$ has three connected components:**

| Region | Sign of $g$ | Connected component |
|---|---|---|
| $(0,1)\times(0,1)$ | $(x_1{-}1)(x_1{-}3) > 0$ (both factors negative) $\implie
s g > 0$ | Component 1 |
| $(1,3)\times(0,1)$ | $(x_1{-}1)(x_1{-}3) < 0$ (one positive, one negative) $\i
mplies g < 0$ | Component 2 |
| $(3,4)\times(0,1)$ | $(x_1{-}1)(x_1{-}3) > 0$ (both positive) $\implies g > 0$
 | Component 3 |

Each is a connected open rectangle, and together they form $C \setminus (M \cap
C)$. Thus $C \setminus (M \cap C)$ has **three** connected components, not two.
In particular, the set $\{g > 0\} \cap C$ is itself **disconnected** (it consist
s of components 1 and 3, which are separated by the strip $\{g < 0\}$).

## What IS true

The condition $\nabla g \neq 0$ in $C$ does imply:

1. **$M \cap C$ is a smooth embedded hypersurface** (codimension-1 submanifold)
in $C$, by the regular level set theorem.

2. **$C \setminus (M \cap C)$ is the disjoint union of two open sets:**
$$C \setminus (M \cap C) = \{x \in C : g(x) > 0\}\ \sqcup\ \{x \in C : g(x) < 0\
},$$
   both open by continuity of $g$. So the complement is always partitioned into
at most two *sign classes*.

3. **If $C$ is sufficiently small** (a small enough neighborhood of a single poi
nt $x \in M$), then by the implicit function theorem, $M \cap C$ is the graph of
 a smooth function over an $(n{-}1)$-dimensional domain, and both $\{g > 0\} \ca
p C$ and $\{g < 0\} \cap C$ are connected — yielding exactly two regions. This i
s the *local* statement.

**What fails** is the *global* statement for an arbitrary hypercube $C$ satisfyi
ng the gradient condition. The nonvanishing of $\nabla g$ on all of $C$ prevents
 the zero set from forming closed loops (which would require an interior critica
l point by the extreme value theorem), but it does **not** prevent the zero set
from having multiple connected components — each a properly embedded arc crossin
g $C$. Multiple such arcs can subdivide $C$ into more than two regions, as the c
ounterexample demonstrates.

---

## Conclusion

No, $M \cap C$ does not necessarily divide $C$ into exactly two connected open r
egions. The nonvanishing gradient condition ensures $M \cap C$ is a smooth hyper
surface and that $C \setminus (M \cap C)$ splits into the two sign-based open se
ts $\{g>0\}\cap C$ and $\{g<0\}\cap C$, but these sign-based sets need not each
be connected. The explicit counterexample $g(x_1,x_2)=(x_1-1)(x_1-3)e^{x_2}$ on
$C=(0,4)\times(0,1)$ satisfies all hypotheses yet produces three connected compo
nents in the complement.
