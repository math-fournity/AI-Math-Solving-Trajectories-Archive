## Proof

There are 7 seats, assigned to 7 passengers labeled $1,2,\dots,7$. Passenger $i$
 owns seat $i$. Passengers board in order $1,2,\dots,7$. Passenger 1 sits in a w
rong seat (uniformly at random among seats $\{2,3,\dots,7\}$). Each subsequent p
assenger $k$ sits in their own seat $k$ if it is free; if seat $k$ is occupied,
they pick uniformly at random among the remaining free seats. We (passenger 7) b
oard last. We want $\Pr[\text{seat 7 is free when we board}]$.

**Key observation.** At every moment, among the still-free seats, exactly one is
 "special" in the sense that whoever eventually takes it determines the fate of
passenger 7. We track a two-state invariant.

**Invariant.** After each passenger $k$ (for $1 \le k \le 6$) has sat down, cons
ider the set of free seats. We claim:

> Among the free seats, either seat 1 is free, or seat 7 is free — and at least
one of them is free. Moreover, **whenever a passenger is forced to choose random
ly among free seats, seats 1 and 7 (whichever of them remain free) are equally l
ikely to be chosen.**

Let us verify this holds inductively.

*Base case.* After passenger 1 sits: he did **not** sit in seat 1 (he sits in a
wrong seat). So seat 1 is free. He sat in some seat $j \in \{2,\dots,7\}$. If $j
 = 7$, then seat 7 is now occupied and seat 1 is free. If $j \ne 7$, then both s
eat 1 and seat 7 are free. In all cases at least one of $\{1,7\}$ is free. ✓

*Inductive step.* Suppose the invariant holds after passenger $k-1$ sits, and pa
ssenger $k$ now boards ($2 \le k \le 6$).

- **If seat $k$ is free:** passenger $k$ sits in seat $k$. This seat is neither
seat 1 nor seat 7 (since $2 \le k \le 6$). So the free-status of seats 1 and 7 i
s unchanged. The invariant is preserved. ✓

- **If seat $k$ is occupied:** passenger $k$ must choose randomly among all curr
ently free seats. By the inductive hypothesis, among the free seats, seat 1 and
seat 7 are both present (if both were free) or exactly one is present. Crucially
, **seat 1 and seat 7 play symmetric roles** in the random choice: passenger $k$
 picks uniformly among the free seats, so seat 1 and seat 7 each have the same p
robability of being selected. There are three outcomes:
  - Passenger $k$ picks seat 1 → seat 1 becomes occupied, seat 7's status unchan
ged. From now on seat 7 is the unique "special" free seat, and **every future fo
rced chooser who reaches seat 7's turn will leave seat 7 free** (because once se
at 1 is gone, the chain of displacement can never again target seat 1, and the p
rocess must terminate with the last passenger finding their own seat — see below
). Effectively, picking seat 1 "resolves" the chaos in passenger 7's favor.
  - Passenger $k$ picks seat 7 → seat 7 becomes occupied. Passenger 7 will not g
et their seat. Picking seat 7 "resolves" the chaos against passenger 7.
  - Passenger $k$ picks some other free seat $m \in \{2,\dots,6\}$ → neither sea
t 1 nor seat 7 is affected; the chaos continues with the same invariant, now wit
h passenger $m$'s seat occupied (a future displaced passenger).

The decisive point: **every time a random choice is made, seat 1 and seat 7 are
treated identically** — both are among the free seats (or the chaos has already
been resolved), and both have equal probability of being chosen. No random choic
e ever distinguishes seat 1 from seat 7. All the "other seat" choices merely del
ay the resolution without biasing it.

**Formalizing the symmetry.** Define the "resolution event": the first time some
 passenger, forced to choose randomly, picks either seat 1 or seat 7. (Such a ti
me must occur: the displaced-seat chain cannot cycle forever among $\{2,\dots,6\
}$ without eventually forcing a chooser who sees seat 1 or seat 7 among the free
 seats — and in fact the chain is a simple path through distinct seats, so it te
rminates.) At the moment of resolution, by the invariant, seat 1 and seat 7 are
both among the free seats and are chosen with equal probability. Therefore:

$$\Pr[\text{resolution picks seat 1}] = \Pr[\text{resolution picks seat 7}].$$

If resolution picks seat 1: the displacement chain ends (seat 1's owner, passeng
er 1, is already seated), so all subsequent passengers $k+1, \dots, 6$ find thei
r own seats free, and **passenger 7 finds seat 7 free**.

If resolution picks seat 7: seat 7 is occupied, so **passenger 7 does not get se
at 7**.

Since these two resolution outcomes are the only ways the process terminates, an
d they are equally likely:

$$\Pr[\text{passenger 7 gets seat 7}] = \Pr[\text{resolution picks seat 1}] = \f
rac{1}{2}.$$

**Why the chain must terminate at seat 1 or seat 7.** The displacement process c
reates a chain of occupied "wrong" seats. Starting from passenger 1 occupying se
at $j_1$, then whoever owns $j_1$ is displaced and either sits correctly (chain
ends at seat 1 — impossible here since passenger 1 already sat) or displaces ano
ther, etc. The chain visits distinct seats (a seat, once occupied, stays occupie
d). The only seats whose "owners" are already seated and thus terminate the chai
n are seat 1 (owner passenger 1, already seated) and seat 7 (owner passenger 7,
not yet boarded — so selecting seat 7 also terminates the chain by occupying the
 last passenger's seat). Hence the chain terminates exactly when seat 1 or seat
7 is selected, confirming the resolution event is well-defined and exhaustive.

**Conclusion.** The probability that the last passenger (passenger 7) sits in th
eir own seat is

$$\boxed{\dfrac{1}{2}}.$$
