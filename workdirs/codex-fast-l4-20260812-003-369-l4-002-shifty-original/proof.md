# Proof that $S = \mathbb{Z}$

## Setup

Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ satisfy:

1. There exist $a, b \in S$ with $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(a-2, b-2) = 1$.
2. (Closure) If $x, y \in S$ (possibly $x = y$), then $x^2 - y \in S$.

We prove $S = \mathbb{Z}$.

---

## Step 1: Reflections and translations

**Definition.** For each $x \in S$, define the *reflection* $R_x : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ by
$$R_x(y) = x^2 - y.$$

By condition 2, if $y \in S$ then $R_x(y) \in S$. So each $R_x$ maps $S$ into $S$.

**Composition of two reflections is a translation.** For $x, z \in S$:
$$R_x(R_z(y)) = x^2 - (z^2 - y) = y + (x^2 - z^2).$$

So the composition $R_x \circ R_z$ is translation by $x^2 - z^2$. Since both $R_x$ and $R_z$ map $S$ to $S$, their composition does too:

> **(T)** If $y \in S$ and $x, z \in S$, then $y + (x^2 - z^2) \in S$.

---

## Step 2: The translation subgroup $G$

Let
$$G = \langle\, x^2 - z^2 : x, z \in S \,\rangle \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$$
be the subgroup generated by all translation amounts arising from (T).

**$G$ is indeed a subgroup.** It is non-empty (contains $0 = x^2 - x^2$). It is closed under negation: if $t = x^2 - z^2$ with $x, z \in S$, then $-t = z^2 - x^2$ is also a generator. It is closed under addition by construction (it is the subgroup generated by the set of all $x^2 - z^2$).

Since every subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ is of the form $d\mathbb{Z}$ for some $d \geq 0$, write $G = d\mathbb{Z}$.

**$S$ is a union of full cosets of $G$.** By property (T), if $s \in S$ and $t \in G$, then $s + t \in S$ (since $t$ is a sum of generators, each of which translates $S$ to $S$). So $s + G \subseteq S$ for every $s \in S$.

**Consequence:** If $d = 1$, then $G = \mathbb{Z}$, so every $s \in S$ satisfies $s + \mathbb{Z} \subseteq S$, giving $S = \mathbb{Z}$.

Our goal is therefore to prove $d = 1$.

---

## Step 3: All elements of $S$ have the same square modulo $d$

For any $x, z \in S$, the integer $x^2 - z^2$ is a generator of $G = d\mathbb{Z}$, so $d \mid (x^2 - z^2)$. Equivalently:

> **(Q)** For all $x, z \in S$: $\;x^2 \equiv z^2 \pmod{d}$.

---

## Step 4: Key divisibility relations

**Relation A: $d \mid a^3(a - 2)$.**

Since $a \in S$, by closure (taking $x = y = a$) we have $a^2 - a \in S$. Now both $a$ and $a^2 - a$ are in $S$, so by (Q):
$$a^2 \equiv (a^2 - a)^2 \pmod{d}.$$

Computing:
$$(a^2 - a)^2 - a^2 = a^4 - 2a^3 + a^2 - a^2 = a^4 - 2a^3 = a^3(a - 2).$$

So $d \mid a^3(a - 2)$.

**Relation B: $d \mid b^3(b - 2)$.**

By the identical argument with $b$ in place of $a$: $d \mid b^3(b - 2)$.

**Relation C: $d \mid (a^2 - b^2)$.**

Since $a, b \in S$, this is immediate from (Q).

---

## Step 5: Ruling out $d = 0$

If $d = 0$, then $G = \{0\}$, and Relations A and B become:
$$a^3(a - 2) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad b^3(b - 2) = 0.$$

So $a \in \{0, 2\}$ and $b \in \{0, 2\}$. But then:
$$\gcd(a, b) \in \{\gcd(0,0),\, \gcd(0,2),\, \gcd(2,0),\, \gcd(2,2)\} = \{0, 2, 2, 2\},$$
which never equals $1$. This contradicts condition 1. So $d \neq 0$, hence $d \geq 1$.

---

## Step 6: Ruling out $d \geq 2$ (prime case analysis)

Suppose $d \geq 2$. Let $p$ be any prime divisor of $d$. From Relations A, B, C:

- From $d \mid a^3(a-2)$: $\;p \mid a^3(a-2)$, so $p \mid a$ or $p \mid (a - 2)$.
- From $d \mid b^3(b-2)$: $\;p \mid b^3(b-2)$, so $p \mid b$ or $p \mid (b - 2)$.
- From $d \mid (a^2 - b^2)$: $\;p \mid (a - b)(a + b)$, so $p \mid (a - b)$ or $p \mid (a + b)$.

This gives four cases based on the first two divisibilities:

**Case 1: $p \mid a$ and $p \mid b$.**
Then $p \mid \gcd(a, b) = 1$. Contradiction.

**Case 2: $p \mid a$ and $p \mid (b - 2)$.**
Then $a \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$ and $b \equiv 2 \pmod{p}$, so:
$$(a - b)(a + b) \equiv (0 - 2)(0 + 2) = -4 \pmod{p}.$$
Since $p \mid (a-b)(a+b)$, we get $p \mid 4$, so $p = 2$. But $p = 2$ and $p \mid a$ means $a$ is even; $p \mid (b-2)$ means $b$ is even. So $2 \mid \gcd(a, b) = 1$. Contradiction.

**Case 3: $p \mid (a - 2)$ and $p \mid b$.**
Then $a \equiv 2 \pmod{p}$ and $b \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$, so:
$$(a - b)(a + b) \equiv (2 - 0)(2 + 0) = 4 \pmod{p}.$$
So $p \mid 4$, giving $p = 2$. But then $a \equiv 2 \equiv 0 \pmod{2}$ (so $a$ is even) and $b$ is even, hence $2 \mid \gcd(a, b) = 1$. Contradiction.

**Case 4: $p \mid (a - 2)$ and $p \mid (b - 2)$.**
Then $p \mid \gcd(a - 2, b - 2) = 1$. Contradiction.

All four cases yield contradictions, so no prime divides $d$. Since $d \geq 1$, we conclude $d = 1$.

---

## Step 7: Conclusion

Since $d = 1$, we have $G = \mathbb{Z}$. By the coset property from Step 2, every $s \in S$ satisfies $s + \mathbb{Z} \subseteq S$, i.e., $S = \mathbb{Z}$.

$\blacksquare$
