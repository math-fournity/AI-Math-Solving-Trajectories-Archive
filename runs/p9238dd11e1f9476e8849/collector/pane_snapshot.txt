## Proof

We evaluate

$$I(a)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(ax)}{x(x^{2}+1)}\,dx,\qquad a\ge 0.$$

**Step 1. Differentiate under the integral sign.**

Formally,

$$I'(a)=\frac{d}{da}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(ax)}{x(x^{2}+1)}\,dx=\int_
{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(ax)\cdot x}{x(x^{2}+1)}\,dx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{\cos(ax)}{x^{2}+1}\,dx.$$

This interchange is justified by dominated convergence: the differentiated integ
rand satisfies

$$\left|\frac{\cos(ax)}{x^{2}+1}\right|\le\frac{1}{x^{2}+1}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
,$$

uniformly in $a$ on every bounded interval, so Leibniz's rule applies.

**Step 2. Evaluate $I'(a)=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(ax)}{x^
{2}+1}\,dx$.**

This is the classical Fourier transform of $(x^{2}+1)^{-1}$. For $a>0$, integrat
e $f(z)=\dfrac{e^{iaz}}{z^{2}+1}$ over the closed contour consisting of $[-R,R]$
 plus the upper semicircle $C_{R}$ of radius $R$. The only pole inside is at $z=
i$, a simple pole with residue

$$\operatorname{Res}_{z=i}\frac{e^{iaz}}{z^{2}+1}=\frac{e^{ia\cdot i}}{2i}=\frac
{e^{-a}}{2i}.$$

By the residue theorem,

$$\int_{-R}^{R}\frac{e^{iax}}{x^{2}+1}\,dx+\int_{C_{R}}\frac{e^{iaz}}{z^{2}+1}\,
dz=2\pi i\cdot\frac{e^{-a}}{2i}=\pi e^{-a}.$$

On $C_{R}$, $|e^{iaz}|=e^{-a\,\mathrm{Im}\,z}\le 1$ (since $a>0$, $\mathrm{Im}\,
z\ge 0$), so by Jordan's lemma (or a direct $1/R$ estimate) the arc integral van
ishes as $R\to\infty$. Taking real parts (the imaginary part is the integral of
an odd function and equals $0$),

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(ax)}{x^{2}+1}\,dx=\pi e^{-a},\qquad a>0.$$

For $a=0$ this gives $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^{2}+1}=\pi=\pi e^{0}$,
so the formula $I'(a)=\pi e^{-a}$ holds for all $a\ge 0$.

**Step 3. Integrate back.**

Since $I(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(0)}{x(x^{2}+1)}\,dx=0$, the fundam
ental theorem of calculus gives

$$I(a)=I(0)+\int_{0}^{a}I'(t)\,dt=\int_{0}^{a}\pi e^{-t}\,dt=\pi\Big[-e^{-t}\Big
]_{0}^{a}=\pi\bigl(1-e^{-a}\bigr).$$

**Conclusion.**

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(ax)}{x(x^{2}+1)}\,dx=\pi
\bigl(1-e^{-a}\bigr),\qquad a\ge 0.}$$

### PROOF COMPLETE
