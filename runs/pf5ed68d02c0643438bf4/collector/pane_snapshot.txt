## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, there exists a sequence in ℝ with an uncountable number of accum
ulation points.

**Construction.** Since the rationals ℚ are countable, there exists a bijection
(an enumeration) $q : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{Q}$. We take the sequence $(q_n)_{n
 \in \mathbb{N}}$ to be any such enumeration of all rational numbers.

**Definition (accumulation point of a sequence).** A point $x \in \mathbb{R}$ is
 an accumulation point of the sequence $(q_n)$ if, for every $\varepsilon > 0$,
the set $\{n \in \mathbb{N} : |q_n - x| < \varepsilon\}$ is infinite.

**Key fact: ℚ is dense in ℝ.** For every $x \in \mathbb{R}$ and every $\varepsil
on > 0$, the open interval $(x - \varepsilon,\, x + \varepsilon)$ contains infin
itely many rational numbers.

**Verification.** Let $x \in \mathbb{R}$ be arbitrary and let $\varepsilon > 0$.
 By density of ℚ in ℝ, the interval $(x - \varepsilon,\, x + \varepsilon)$ conta
ins infinitely many distinct rationals. Since $(q_n)$ is a bijection onto ℚ, eve
ry rational number appears exactly once in the sequence. Therefore the set
$$\{n \in \mathbb{N} : |q_n - x| < \varepsilon\}$$
is in bijection with the (infinite) set of rationals in $(x-\varepsilon, x+\vare
psilon)$, and hence is infinite.

Since this holds for every $\varepsilon > 0$, the point $x$ is an accumulation p
oint of $(q_n)$. Since $x \in \mathbb{R}$ was arbitrary, **every** real number i
s an accumulation point of the sequence.

**Conclusion.** The set of accumulation points of $(q_n)$ is all of ℝ, which is
uncountable. Hence there exists a sequence in ℝ with an uncountable number of ac
cumulation points. $\blacksquare$
