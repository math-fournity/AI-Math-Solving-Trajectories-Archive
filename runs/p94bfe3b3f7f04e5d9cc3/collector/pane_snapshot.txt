## Proof

**Claim.** Let $k$ be an infinite field and $f(X,Y) \in k[X,Y]$. If $f(x,y)=0$ f
or every $(x,y)\in k^2$, then $f(X,Y)=0$ as a polynomial. Consequently the natur
al evaluation map $k[X,Y]\to k[X,Y]^*$ is an isomorphism.

**Key lemma (univariate case).** *If $k$ is an infinite field and $p(T)\in k[T]$
 satisfies $p(t)=0$ for all $t\in k$, then $p=0$.*

*Proof of lemma.* Suppose $p\neq 0$ and let $d=\deg p$. A nonzero polynomial of
degree $d$ has at most $d$ roots in any field. Since $k$ is infinite, $|k|>d$, s
o $p$ cannot vanish on all of $k$ — contradiction. Hence $p=0$. $\square$

**Main proof.** Write $f$ as a polynomial in $Y$ with coefficients in $k[X]$:
$$f(X,Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{n} g_i(X)\,Y^{i}, \qquad g_i(X)\in k[X].$$

**Step 1 (fix $X$, use the lemma in $Y$).** For any fixed $x\in k$, define
$$h_x(Y):=f(x,Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{n} g_i(x)\,Y^{i}\in k[Y].$$
By hypothesis $f(x,y)=0$ for every $y\in k$, so $h_x(y)=0$ for every $y\in k$. S
ince $k$ is infinite, the lemma gives $h_x\equiv 0$, i.e.
$$g_i(x)=0 \quad\text{for every } i\in\{0,\dots,n\}\text{ and every }x\in k.$$

**Step 2 (use the lemma in $X$).** For each $i$, the univariate polynomial $g_i(
X)\in k[X]$ vanishes on every $x\in k$. Since $k$ is infinite, the lemma gives $
g_i\equiv 0$ as a polynomial.

**Step 3 (conclude).** Since every coefficient $g_i$ is the zero polynomial,
$$f(X,Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}0\cdot Y^{i}=0.$$

**Isomorphism.** The evaluation map
$$\varepsilon:k[X,Y]\longrightarrow k[X,Y]^*,\qquad f\mapsto\big((x,y)\mapsto f(
x,y)\big)$$
is well defined and surjective by the very definition of $k[X,Y]^*$. The argumen
t above shows $\ker\varepsilon=\{0\}$, so $\varepsilon$ is injective. Being a bi
jective ring homomorphism, $\varepsilon$ is an isomorphism:
$$k[X,Y]\cong k[X,Y]^*.$$

**Remark on the role of infiniteness.** The hypothesis that $k$ is infinite is e
ssential. For example, over the finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$, the nonzero polynom
ial $X^{q}-X$ vanishes on every element of $\mathbb{F}_q$, so the evaluation map
 $k[X]\to k[X]^*$ (and likewise in two variables, e.g. $X^{q}-X$) is **not** inj
ective. $\square$
