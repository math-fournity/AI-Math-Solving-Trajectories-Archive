## Solution

**Setup.** We have a complete graph $K_{12}$ on 12 vertices (stations), each edg
e colored red or blue. Let the red degree of a vertex $v$ be $d_v$. Station $A$
has odd $d_A$; the other 11 vertices have 11 **distinct** red degrees.

**Step 1: Determine the 11 distinct degrees.**

Each of the 11 non-$A$ vertices has red degree in $\{0,1,\dots,11\}$. Since they
 are 11 distinct values chosen from 12, exactly one value $m \in \{0,\dots,11\}$
 is missing. Their sum is $66 - m$.

The sum of all red degrees equals $2E_{\text{red}}$ (even), so $d_A + 66 - m$ is
 even. Since $d_A$ is odd and 66 is even, $m$ must be **odd**: $m \in \{1,3,5,7,
9,11\}$.

**Graph-theoretic constraint:** a vertex of degree $0$ and a vertex of degree $1
1$ cannot coexist (a degree-11 vertex is adjacent to everyone, so no vertex can
have degree 0). Thus the 11 distinct values cannot include both $0$ and $11$, fo
rcing $m \in \{0, 11\}$.

Combining: $m = 11$ (the only odd option). The 11 distinct red degrees are $\{0,
1,2,\dots,10\}$, with sum $55$.

**Step 2: Determine the unique graph structure.**

Label the non-$A$ vertices $v_0, v_1, \dots, v_{10}$ where $d_{v_k} = k$.

- $v_0$ (degree 0) has no red edges to anyone.
- $v_{10}$ (degree 10) is missing a red edge to exactly one vertex — necessarily
 $v_0$ — so $v_{10}$ is red-adjacent to $A, v_1, \dots, v_9$.
- $v_1$ (degree 1) has its single red edge to $v_{10}$, so it is not red-adjacen
t to $A$ or any other $v_j$.
- Inductively, $v_k$ is red-adjacent to $v_j$ **iff** $k + j \geq 11$ (among non
-$A$ vertices), and $v_k$ is red-adjacent to $A$ **iff** $k \geq 6$.

Verification: for $k \leq 5$, $v_k$ has red neighbors $v_{11-k},\dots,v_{10}$ (t
hat's $k$ vertices), no edge to $A$ → degree $k$. ✓
For $k \geq 6$, $v_k$ has $k-1$ red neighbors among non-$A$ vertices plus $A$ →
degree $k$. ✓

So $A$'s red neighbors are $v_6, v_7, v_8, v_9, v_10$, giving $d_A = 5$ (odd ✓).

**Step 3: Count red-cherries.**

A red-cherry is a pair of red edges sharing a vertex. The number centered at ver
tex $v$ is $\binom{d_v}{2}$. Each all-red triangle contributes 3 red-cherries; e
ach triangle with exactly 2 red edges contributes 1. So:

$$3R_3 + R_2B_1 = \sum_v \binom{d_v}{2} = \binom{5}{2} + \sum_{k=0}^{10}\binom{k
}{2} = 10 + \binom{11}{3} = 10 + 165 = 175.$$

**Step 4: Count $R_3$ directly.**

*Triangles containing $A$:* $A$'s red neighbors are $\{v_6,\dots,v_{10}\}$. Tria
ngle $\{A, v_i, v_j\}$ is all-red iff $i+j \geq 11$. The minimum sum is $6+7=13
\geq 11$, so all $\binom{5}{2}=10$ such triangles are all-red.

*Triangles not containing $A$:* $\{v_i, v_j, v_k\}$ with $i<j<k$ is all-red iff
$i+j \geq 11$ (the binding constraint). For each valid pair $(i,j)$, $k$ ranges
over $\{j+1,\dots,10\}$, giving $10-j$ choices:

| $j$ | valid $i$ (with $i<j$, $i+j\geq 11$) | count of $i$ | choices for $k$ |
triangles |
|-----|---------------------------------------|--------------|-----------------|
-----------|
| 9 | $i \in \{2,\dots,8\}$ | 7 | 1 | 7 |
| 8 | $i \in \{3,\dots,7\}$ | 5 | 2 | 10 |
| 7 | $i \in \{4,5,6\}$ | 3 | 3 | 9 |
| 6 | $i = 5$ | 1 | 4 | 4 |

Total: $7 + 10 + 9 + 4 = 30$.

$$R_3 = 10 + 30 = 40.$$

**Step 5: Compute $R_2B_1$ and the answer.**

From $3R_3 + R_2B_1 = 175$:

$$3(40) + R_2B_1 = 175 \implies R_2B_1 = 55.$$

$$\boxed{R_3 + R_2B_1 = 40 + 55 = 95}.$$

**Verification.** Total triangles $= \binom{12}{3} = 220$. By the blue-degree sy
mmetry ($d^B_A = 6$, $d^B_{v_k} = 11-k$), blue-cherries $= \binom{6}{2}+\sum_{k=
0}^{10}\binom{11-k}{2} = 15 + 220 = 235$, giving $3B_3 + R_1B_2 = 235$. Solving
with $R_3+R_2B_1+R_1B_2+B_3=220$ yields $B_3=55$, $R_1B_2=70$. Check: $40+55+70+
55=220$ ✓, $3(40)+55=175$ ✓, $3(55)+70=235$ ✓.
