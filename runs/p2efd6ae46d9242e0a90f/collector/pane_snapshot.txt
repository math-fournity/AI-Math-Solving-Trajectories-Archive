## Proof

We compute the divergence of

$$v(x) = \int_{r\in M} \rho(r)\,\frac{x-r}{\|x-r\|^{3}}\,dr,$$

where the integral is taken component-wise over $r\in M\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}$, a
nd $\rho:M\to\mathbb{R}$ is continuous.

---

### Step 1. Formal differentiation under the integral

Formally differentiating under the integral sign at a point $x\in M$ with $x\not
in\operatorname{supp}\rho$ (or more carefully, at a point where $x$ is not a cha
rge point), the integrand is smooth in $x$, so

$$\nabla\!\cdot v(x) = \int_{r\in M} \rho(r)\,\nabla_{x}\!\cdot\!\left(\frac{x-r
}{\|x-r\|^{3}}\right)dr.$$

For $x\neq r$ the vector field $K(x-r):=\dfrac{x-r}{\|x-r\|^{3}}$ is the gradien
t of $\dfrac{-1}{\|x-r\|}$ (up to sign: $\nabla_{x}\dfrac{1}{\|x-r\|}=-\dfrac{x-
r}{\|x-r\|^{3}}$, so $K=-\nabla_{x}\varphi$ with $\varphi=1/\|x-r\|$). Being a g
radient of a harmonic function away from its singularity, $K$ is divergence-free
 away from $r$:

$$\nabla_{x}\!\cdot\!\left(\frac{x-r}{\|x-r\|^{3}}\right)=0,\qquad x\neq r.$$

So the formal computation gives $0$ — but this misses the singularity at $x=r$,
which is exactly where the charge sits. The correct object is the **distribution
al divergence** of the kernel.

---

### Step 2. Distributional divergence of the Coulomb kernel in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$

**Claim.** In $\mathbb{R}^{3}$,

$$\nabla\!\cdot\!\left(\frac{x}{\|x\|^{3}}\right)=4\pi\,\delta_{0}$$

as distributions.

*Proof of claim.* Let $K(x)=x/\|x\|^{3}$. For any test function $\varphi\in C^{\
infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{3})$ we must show

$$\langle \nabla\!\cdot K,\varphi\rangle = -\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}} K(x)\cdot\nabl
a\varphi(x)\,dx = 4\pi\,\varphi(0).$$

Let $B_{\varepsilon}$ be the ball of radius $\varepsilon$ and $S_{R}$ the ball o
f radius $R$ containing $\operatorname{supp}\varphi$. On the annulus $A_{\vareps
ilon,R}=S_{R}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}$ the field $K$ is smooth and divergence-f
ree, so by the divergence theorem applied to the vector field $K\,\varphi$ (prod
uct rule: $\nabla\!\cdot(\varphi K)=\nabla\varphi\cdot K+\varphi\,\nabla\!\cdot
K=\nabla\varphi\cdot K$ on the annulus since $\nabla\!\cdot K=0$ there):

$$\int_{A_{\varepsilon,R}} K\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx = \int_{\partial A_{\varepsil
on,R}} \varphi\,K\cdot n\,dS.$$

The outer boundary $\partial S_{R}$ contributes $0$ because $\varphi$ vanishes t
here. On the inner boundary $\partial B_{\varepsilon}$ the outward normal of the
 annulus points **inward**, $n=-x/\|x\|$, and

$$K(x)\cdot n = \frac{x}{\|x\|^{3}}\cdot\!\left(-\frac{x}{\|x\|}\right)=-\frac{1
}{\|x\|^{2}}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}.$$

Hence

$$\int_{\partial B_{\varepsilon}} \varphi\,K\cdot n\,dS = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon^
{2}}\int_{\partial B_{\varepsilon}}\varphi\,dS = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot
 \varepsilon^{2}\!\int_{S^{2}}\varphi(\varepsilon\omega)\,d\omega \xrightarrow[\
varepsilon\to 0]{} -4\pi\,\varphi(0).$$

Therefore

$$-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}} K\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx = \lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(
-\int_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}K\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx\right) = -(-4\pi\varphi(0))=4\p
i\varphi(0),$$

proving the claim. $\square$

---

### Step 3. Convolution with the kernel

The field can be written as a convolution (extending $\rho$ by $0$ outside $M$):

$$v(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}} \rho(r)\,K(x-r)\,dr = (\rho * K)(x),\qquad K(y)=\
frac{y}{\|y\|^{3}}.$$

Taking the distributional divergence and using that differentiation commutes wit
h convolution,

$$\nabla\!\cdot v = \rho * (\nabla\!\cdot K) = \rho * (4\pi\,\delta_{0}) = 4\pi\
,\rho.$$

Concretely, for any test function $\varphi$,

$$\langle \nabla\!\cdot v,\varphi\rangle = -\int v\cdot\nabla\varphi = -\iint \r
ho(r)K(x-r)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)\,dr\,dx = \int \rho(r)\,\langle\nabla\!\cdot K(
\cdot-r),\varphi\rangle\,dr = \int \rho(r)\,4\pi\varphi(r)\,dr = 4\pi\langle\rho
,\varphi\rangle,$$

where the interchange of $r$-integration with the distributional pairing is just
ified by $\rho$ being continuous (hence locally integrable) and $\varphi$ compac
tly supported. This identifies $\nabla\!\cdot v = 4\pi\rho$ as distributions, an
d since $\rho$ is continuous, both sides are continuous functions, so the equali
ty holds pointwise.

---

### Step 4. Conclusion

$$\boxed{\;\nabla\!\cdot v(x) = 4\pi\,\rho(x)\;}$$

This is precisely Gauss's law in three dimensions: the divergence of the Coulomb
 field generated by a charge density $\rho$ equals $4\pi$ times the charge densi
ty (in Gaussian units, where the Coulomb kernel is $(x-r)/\|x-r\|^{3}$ rather th
an $(x-r)/(4\pi\|x-r\|^{3})$).
