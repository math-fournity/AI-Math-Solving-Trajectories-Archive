**Theorem.** Let $B$ be a Banach space and $p \ge 1$. The space $L^p(\mathbb{R}^
n, B)$ of (equivalence classes of) Bochner-measurable functions $f:\mathbb{R}^n
\to B$ with $\|f\|_p := \bigl(\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \|f(x)\|_B^p\,dx\bigr)^{1/p} <
 \infty$ is complete, hence a Banach space.

**Proof.**

We must show that every absolutely summable series in $L^p(\mathbb{R}^n,B)$ conv
erges; equivalently (and more directly), that every Cauchy sequence converges to
 an element of the space. We use the standard characterization: a normed space i
s complete iff every series $\sum_k f_k$ with $\sum_k \|f_k\|_p < \infty$ conver
ges in norm.

*Step 1: Setup.* Let $(f_k)_{k\ge 1} \subset L^p(\mathbb{R}^n, B)$ with
$$\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \|f_k\|_p =: M < \infty. \tag{1}$$

Define the scalar-valued function
$$g(x) := \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \|f_k(x)\|_B, \qquad G_N(x) := \sum_{k=1}^{N} \|f_
k(x)\|_B.$$

Each $G_N$ is measurable (finite sum of measurable scalar functions, since each
$f_k$ is Bochner-measurable, hence $x\mapsto \|f_k(x)\|_B$ is measurable). By th
e triangle/Minkowski inequality in $L^p$,
$$\|G_N\|_p \le \sum_{k=1}^{N} \|f_k\|_p \le M.$$

By Fatou's lemma (applied to the nonnegative measurable $G_N$),
$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} g(x)^p\,dx = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \liminf_{N\to\infty} G_N(
x)^p\,dx \le \liminf_{N\to\infty} \int G_N^p \le M^p.$$

So $g \in L^p(\mathbb{R}^n)$, and in particular $g(x) < \infty$ for a.e. $x$.

*Step 2: Pointwise convergence.* For a.e. $x$, the series $\sum_k f_k(x)$ is abs
olutely convergent in $B$:
$$\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \|f_k(x)\|_B = g(x) < \infty.$$

Since $B$ is **Banach** (complete), absolute convergence implies convergence, so
 the limit
$$f(x) := \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} f_k(x) \in B$$
exists for a.e. $x$. Define $f(x)=0$ on the null set where the series fails to c
onverge. The partial sums $S_N(x) := \sum_{k=1}^N f_k(x)$ are Bochner-measurable
 (finite sums of Bochner-measurable functions), and $S_N(x) \to f(x)$ a.e., so $
f$ is Bochner-measurable (being the a.e. limit of Bochner-measurable $B$-valued
functions).

*Step 3: $f \in L^p$ and norm convergence.* We have
$$\|f(x)\|_B = \Bigl\|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} f_k(x)\Bigr\|_B \le \sum_{k=1}^{\infty
} \|f_k(x)\|_B = g(x),$$
so $\|f(\cdot)\|_B \le g \in L^p$, giving $f \in L^p(\mathbb{R}^n, B)$.

Moreover,
$$\|f - S_N\|_p = \Bigl(\int \Bigl\|\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty} f_k(x)\Bigr\|_B^p\,dx\
Bigr)^{1/p} \le \Bigl(\int \Bigl(\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\|f_k(x)\|_B\Bigr)^p dx\Bi
gr)^{1/p} = \|g - G_N\|_p.$$

Since $G_N \nearrow g$ in $L^p$ (monotone convergence, or dominated convergence
with dominating function $g$), $\|g - G_N\|_p \to 0$. Hence
$$\|f - S_N\|_p \to 0,$$
i.e. $\sum_{k=1}^N f_k \to f$ in $L^p(\mathbb{R}^n, B)$.

*Step 4: Conclusion.* We have shown that every absolutely summable series in $L^
p(\mathbb{R}^n, B)$ converges. This is equivalent to completeness of the normed
space. Therefore $L^p(\mathbb{R}^n, B)$ is a Banach space. $\blacksquare$

**Remark on the role of completeness of $B$.** The completeness of $B$ is essent
ial: in Step 2, pointwise convergence of $\sum_k f_k(x)$ requires that absolutel
y convergent series in $B$ converge, which is precisely the completeness of $B$.
 If $B$ were not complete, one could construct a Cauchy sequence in $L^p(\mathbb
{R}^n,B)$ whose pointwise limit lands in the completion $\overline{B}\setminus B
$, so the limit would not be $B$-valued and $L^p(\mathbb{R}^n,B)$ would fail to
be complete.
