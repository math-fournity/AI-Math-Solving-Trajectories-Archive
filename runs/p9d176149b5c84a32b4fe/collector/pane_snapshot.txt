## Proof

**Setup.** Let $\|a\| = r$, $\|b\| = s$, and $\theta = \arccos(\rho) \in [0, \pi
]$ be the angle between $a$ and $b$. Then:
- $\langle a, b\rangle = rs\cos\theta$,
- $\|a - b\|^2 = r^2 + s^2 - 2rs\cos\theta$,
- $\sqrt{\|a\|^2\|b\|^2 - \langle a,b\rangle^2} = rs\sin\theta$ (since $\sin\the
ta \geq 0$ on $[0,\pi]$).

The inequality becomes (after dividing by $s^2$, setting $u = r/s > 0$):

$$\frac{\tfrac{1}{2}(u^2+1) - u\cos\theta + \tfrac{u}{\pi}(\theta\cos\theta - \s
in\theta)}{u^2 + 1 - 2u\cos\theta} \geq c.$$

Define the ratio as $f(u, \theta)$. We seek $\inf f > 0$.

**Key simplification.** Set $\alpha = \cos\theta$ and $\beta = \frac{\theta\cos\
theta - \sin\theta}{\pi}$, so $\gamma = \alpha - \beta = \cos\theta\!\left(1 - \
frac{\theta}{\pi}\right) + \frac{\sin\theta}{\pi}$. Then:

$$f(u,\theta) = \frac{\tfrac{1}{2}(u^2+1) - u\,\gamma}{u^2 + 1 - 2u\,\alpha}.$$

**Optimizing over $u$.** Setting $\frac{\partial f}{\partial u} = 0$ yields (aft
er straightforward computation):

$$(\gamma - \alpha)(u^2 - 1) = 0.$$

For $\theta \neq 0$, we have $\gamma \neq \alpha$ (since $\beta \neq 0$), so the
 unique critical point is $u = 1$. As $u \to 0^+$ or $u \to \infty$, $f \to \fra
c{1}{2}$. Since $f(1,\theta) \leq \frac{1}{2}$ (shown below), $u = 1$ is the **g
lobal minimum** over $u$ for each fixed $\theta \in (0, \pi]$.

**Evaluating at $u = 1$.**

$$f(1, \theta) = \frac{1 - \cos\theta + \frac{\theta\cos\theta - \sin\theta}{\pi
}}{2(1-\cos\theta)} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{h(\theta)}{2\pi}, \quad h(\theta) := \
frac{\theta\cos\theta - \sin\theta}{1 - \cos\theta}.$$

**Monotonicity of $h$.** Compute:

$$h'(\theta) = \frac{\sin\theta\,(\sin\theta - \theta)}{(1-\cos\theta)^2}.$$

For $\theta \in (0, \pi)$: $\sin\theta > 0$ and $\sin\theta < \theta$, so $h'(\t
heta) < 0$. Thus $h$ is **strictly decreasing** on $[0, \pi]$.

**Boundary values.**
- As $\theta \to 0^+$: $\theta\cos\theta - \sin\theta \approx -\theta^3/3$, $1 -
 \cos\theta \approx \theta^2/2$, so $h \to 0$.
- At $\theta = \pi$: $h(\pi) = \frac{-\pi}{2} = -\frac{\pi}{2}$.

Therefore $f(1, \theta)$ decreases from $\frac{1}{2}$ to $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{-\
pi/2}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{4}$.

**Parallel case ($\theta = 0$).** Directly: $f(u, 0) = \frac{\frac{1}{2}(u-1)^2}
{(u-1)^2} = \frac{1}{2}$ (for $u \neq 1$), and at $u = 1$ both numerator and den
ominator vanish, giving $f = 0/0$; by continuity $f \to \frac{1}{2}$.

**Conclusion.** Combining all cases:

$$\inf_{\substack{u > 0,\; \theta \in [0,\pi] \\ (u,\theta)\neq(1,0)}} f(u, \the
ta) = \min_{\theta \in [0,\pi]} f(1, \theta) = f(1, \pi) = \frac{1}{4}.$$

**Verification of the extremal case.** At $\theta = \pi$, $u = 1$ (i.e., $a = -b
$, $\|a\| = \|b\| = r$):
- LHS $= \frac{1}{2}\|2a\|^2 - 0 + (-r^2)\cdot\frac{\pi}{\pi} = 2r^2 - r^2 = r^2
$,
- RHS $= c \cdot 4r^2$,
- requiring $c \leq \frac{1}{4}$.

Since the infimum $\frac{1}{4}$ is **attained** (not merely approached) and is s
trictly positive, the inequality holds for **all** nonzero $a, b$ with $c = \fra
c{1}{4}$, and no larger $c$ works.

**Yes**, such a positive constant exists. The optimal (largest) value is $\boxed
{c = \dfrac{1}{4}}$.
