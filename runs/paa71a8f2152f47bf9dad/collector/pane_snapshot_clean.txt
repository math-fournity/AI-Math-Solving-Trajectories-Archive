## Solution

**Claim:** $g_1 \geq g_2$ does **not** have to be true. We provide a counterexam
ple.

### The Counterexample

Let $G = \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$ with componentwise addition and **lexicog
raphic order** (i.e., $(a,b) \leq (c,d)$ iff $a < c$, or $a = c$ and $b \leq d$)
.

**$G$ is a divisible totally ordered additive abelian group:**
- *Additive abelian group:* Componentwise addition on $\mathbb{R}^2$ is clearly
an abelian group.
- *Totally ordered:* Lexicographic order on $\mathbb{R}^2$ is a total order, and
 it is compatible with addition (if $(a,b) \leq (c,d)$, then $(a,b)+(e,f) \leq (
c,d)+(e,f)$ for any $(e,f)$).
- *Divisible:* For any $(a,b) \in G$ and any positive integer $n$, the element $
(a/n,\, b/n)$ satisfies $n \cdot (a/n, b/n) = (a,b)$. So every element is $n$-di
visible.

Set:
$$g_2 = (1, 0), \qquad g_1 = (1, -1).$$

### Verification

**Step 1: $g_1 < g_2$.**

$$g_1 - g_2 = (1,-1) - (1,0) = (0, -1).$$

Since the first coordinates are equal ($0 = 0$) and $-1 < 0$ in the second coord
inate, we have $(0,-1) < (0,0)$, i.e., $g_1 < g_2$.

**Step 2: $g_1 \geq \left(1 - \tfrac{1}{n}\right) g_2$ for every integer $n > 1$
.**

Compute:
$$\left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right) g_2 = g_2 - \frac{g_2}{n} = (1,0) - \left(\frac{1
}{n}, 0\right) = \left(1 - \frac{1}{n},\, 0\right).$$

Then:
$$g_1 - \left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right) g_2 = (1, -1) - \left(1 - \frac{1}{n},\, 0\
right) = \left(\frac{1}{n},\, -1\right).$$

For any integer $n > 1$, we have $\frac{1}{n} > 0$. In lexicographic order, a po
sitive first coordinate means the element is strictly greater than $(0,0)$ regar
dless of the second coordinate:
$$\left(\frac{1}{n},\, -1\right) > (0, 0).$$

Therefore $g_1 \geq \left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right) g_2$ (in fact, strictly) for ev
ery integer $n > 1$.

### Why the Argument Fails (and Why the Counterexample Works)

In an **Archimedean** ordered group (or in $\mathbb{R}$), the condition $g_1 - g
_2 \geq -g_2/n$ for all $n$ would force $g_1 - g_2 \geq 0$ by taking $n \to \inf
ty$, since $-g_2/n \to 0$. However, a general divisible ordered abelian group ne
ed not be Archimedean. In the lexicographic order on $\mathbb{R}^2$, the element
 $(0, -1)$ is a **negative infinitesimal** relative to $(1, 0)$: it is less than
 every positive element with a positive first coordinate, yet greater than every
 element with a negative first coordinate. The sequence $-g_2/n = (-1/n, 0)$ alw
ays has a negative first coordinate, so it is always below $(0, -1)$, no matter
how large $n$ gets. The "limit" $-g_2/n \to 0$ never reaches or exceeds the infi
nitesimal $(0, -1)$, so the inequality $g_1 \geq (1-1/n)g_2$ holds for all $n$ w
hile $g_1 < g_2$.

### Conclusion

The statement "$g_1 \geq g_2$" does **not** have to be true. The divisible total
ly ordered additive abelian group $G = \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$ (lexicograp
hic order) with $g_1 = (1, -1)$ and $g_2 = (1, 0)$ provides a counterexample whe
re $g_1 \geq (1 - 1/n)\,g_2$ for all integers $n > 1$, yet $g_1 < g_2$.
