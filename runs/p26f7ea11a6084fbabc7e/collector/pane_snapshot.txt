## Proof

We work in the classification of finite-type Dynkin diagrams. A connected Dynkin
 diagram is of **finite type** if and only if its symmetrized bilinear form is *
*positive definite**.

### Key Principle

If a diagram $D$ is of finite type (positive definite form), then every subdiagr
am $D' \subseteq D$ is also of finite type. This is because the symmetrized form
 of $D$, restricted to the subspace spanned by the simple roots of $D'$, equals
the symmetrized form of $D'$. A positive definite form restricted to any subspac
e remains positive definite.

The **affine** Dynkin diagrams $\tilde{X}$ are precisely the connected diagrams
whose symmetrized forms are positive semidefinite but **not** positive definite
(determinant 0, one-dimensional kernel). These are the minimal non-finite diagra
ms. So: **if $D$ contains an affine subdiagram, $D$ is not of finite type.**

### Step 1: Finite-type diagrams are trees

If $D$ contains a cycle, the cycle is a subdiagram of type $\tilde{A}_n$ (affine
, determinant 0), so $D$ is not finite type. Hence any finite-type diagram is a
**tree**.

### Step 2: Two double edges yield a path with double edges at both ends

Suppose the tree $D$ has two double edges. Let $P$ be the minimal subtree contai
ning both. Since $D$ is a tree, $P$ is a **path**:

$$v_0 \xrightarrow{\text{double}} v_1 - v_2 - \cdots - v_{m-1} \xrightarrow{\tex
t{double}} v_m$$

with single edges in the interior (for $m \geq 2$; if $m = 1$, the two double ed
ges share vertex $v_1$). This path has $m+1$ vertices with double edges at **bot
h ends**.

### Step 3: Arrow directions are forced by length consistency

In a Dynkin diagram, a **single edge** connects roots of **equal length**, while
 a **double edge** connects a long root and a short root (arrow pointing from lo
ng to short). Along the path, the single edges force $v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_{m-1}$
 to all have the **same length**.

- **If $v_1$ is long** (arrow at the first double edge points from $v_1$ to $v_0
$, i.e., **outward**): then $v_1, \ldots, v_{m-1}$ are all long. Since $v_{m-1}$
 is long, the arrow at the second double edge must point from $v_{m-1}$ to $v_m$
 (**outward**), and $v_m$ is short. **Both arrows point outward.**

- **If $v_1$ is short** (arrow points from $v_0$ to $v_1$, i.e., **inward**): th
en $v_1, \ldots, v_{m-1}$ are all short. Since $v_{m-1}$ is short, the arrow at
the second double edge must point from $v_m$ to $v_{m-1}$ (**inward**), and $v_m
$ is long. **Both arrows point inward.**

The arrows **cannot** point in opposite directions, as this would require an int
erior vertex to be both long and short — a contradiction.

### Step 4: The "both outward" case gives $\tilde{C}_k$

When both arrows point outward, the subdiagram is:

$$v_0 \Leftarrow v_1 - v_2 - \cdots - v_{m-1} \Rightarrow v_m$$

with $v_0, v_m$ **short** and $v_1, \ldots, v_{m-1}$ **long**. This is precisely
 the affine Dynkin diagram $\tilde{C}_k$ (for appropriate $k \geq 2$): a path wi
th double edges at both ends, arrows pointing outward, short roots at the ends a
nd long roots in the interior.

**Verification that $\tilde{C}_k$ is affine (determinant 0):**

Normalize so short roots have $(\alpha, \alpha) = 1$ and long roots have $(\alph
a, \alpha) = 2$. The symmetrized form on the path is the $(m{+}1) \times (m{+}1)
$ matrix:

$$S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 &
 -1 & 2 & \ddots & 0 \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \ddots & -1 \\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & -1 &
 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

The vector $(1, 1, \ldots, 1)$ lies in the kernel:
- **Row 0:** $1 \cdot 1 + (-1) \cdot 1 = 0$ ✓
- **Each interior row $i$:** $(-1) \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 1 + (-1) \cdot 1 = 0$ ✓
- **Last row:** $(-1) \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 = 0$ ✓

The kernel is one-dimensional, so $\det S = 0$. The form is positive semidefinit
e but **not** positive definite, confirming $\tilde{C}_k$ is **affine**.

### Step 5: The "both inward" case is also affine

When both arrows point inward, the path is $v_0\,(\text{long}) \Rightarrow v_1\,
(\text{short}) - \cdots - v_{m-1}\,(\text{short}) \Leftarrow v_m\,(\text{long})$
. Normalize with $(\text{short},\text{short}) = 2$, $(\text{long},\text{long}) =
 4$. The symmetrized form has diagonal $(4, 2, \ldots, 2, 4)$, with $-2$ at the
double-edge positions and $-1$ at the single-edge positions. The vector $(1, 2,
2, \ldots, 2, 1)$ lies in the kernel (verified by direct substitution at each ro
w), so $\det = 0$ and this subdiagram is also affine.

### Step 6: Conclusion

In both cases, the minimal path containing two double edges is an **affine subdi
agram** — type $\tilde{C}_k$ when arrows point outward (the case highlighted in
the problem), or another affine type when arrows point inward. Since a finite-ty
pe diagram **cannot** contain an affine subdiagram (by the Key Principle: a posi
tive definite form restricted to a subspace is still positive definite, but an a
ffine form is only semidefinite), we conclude:

**A finite-type Dynkin diagram has at most one double edge.**

Specifically, when the two double edges are oriented "away from each other" (bot
h arrows pointing outward, which is forced when the interior vertices are long),
 the offending subdiagram is exactly $\tilde{C}_k$ — the affine extension of the
 type-$C_k$ chain, obtained by attaching a short root via a double edge at each
end of a chain of long roots. The all-ones vector in the kernel witnesses the de
generacy: each root contributes equally, and the "extra" root at each end (compa
red to the finite $C_k$) is precisely what makes the form degenerate. $\blacksqu
are$
