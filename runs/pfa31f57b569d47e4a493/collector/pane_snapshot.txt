## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. For every non-differentiable continuous function $f \in C([0,1])
$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a differentiable function $g \in C([
0,1])$ such that $g \in B(f,\varepsilon)$, i.e., $d(f,g) < \varepsilon$.

**Proof.**

Let $f \in C([0,1])$ be continuous (possibly non-differentiable), and let $\vare
psilon > 0$ be arbitrary.

By the **Weierstrass Approximation Theorem**, for every continuous function $f \
colon [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a polynomi
al $p \colon [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ such that

$$\sup_{x \in [0,1]} |f(x) - p(x)| < \varepsilon.$$

Set $g = p$. Since every polynomial is differentiable everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$
 (and in particular on $[0,1]$), $g$ is differentiable. Moreover, $g$ is continu
ous, so $g \in C([0,1])$. Finally,

$$d(f, g) = \sup_{x \in [0,1]} |f(x) - g(x)| < \varepsilon,$$

which means $g \in B(f, \varepsilon)$.

**Remark on the quantifier structure.** The problem asks whether there *exists*
a differentiable $g$ inside $B(f,\varepsilon)$ "for some $\varepsilon > 0$." In
fact the result is stronger: the conclusion holds for **every** $\varepsilon > 0
$. This is because the set of polynomials (a subset of the differentiable functi
ons) is **dense** in $(C([0,1]), d)$. Density means precisely that every open ba
ll around any element of $C([0,1])$ contains a polynomial, hence a differentiabl
e function. Therefore the non-differentiability of $f$ is irrelevant—the differe
ntiable functions are dense, so they penetrate every open ball regardless of the
 center.

**Conclusion.** Such a differentiable function $g$ always exists; indeed, for an
y $\varepsilon > 0$ one may take $g$ to be a polynomial furnished by the Weierst
rass Approximation Theorem.
