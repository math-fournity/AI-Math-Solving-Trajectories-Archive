## Proof

**Step 1: Determine the splitting field of $x^4+4$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.**

Using the Sophie Germain identity,
$$x^4+4=(x^2-2x+2)(x^2+2x+2).$$

The roots of $x^2-2x+2$ are $1\pm i$, and the roots of $x^2+2x+2$ are $-1\pm i$.
 Hence the four roots are
$$1+i,\;1-i,\;-1+i,\;-1-i.$$
All of them lie in $\mathbb{Q}(i)$. Conversely, $i=(1+i)-1$ belongs to the field
 generated over $\mathbb{Q}$ by the roots, so the splitting field is exactly $\m
athbb{Q}(i)$.

**Step 2: Express $-1$ as a sum of two squares in $\mathbb{Q}(i)$.**

Since $i\in\mathbb{Q}(i)$ and $i^2=-1$, we immediately have the (trivial) repres
entation
$$-1=i^2+0^2.$$

For a nontrivial representation with both summands nonzero, observe
$$\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^2+\left(\frac{5i}{4}\right)^2=\frac{9}{16}+\frac{25i^
2}{16}=\frac{9}{16}-\frac{25}{16}=\frac{-16}{16}=-1.$$
Both $\frac{3}{4}$ and $\frac{5i}{4}$ are nonzero elements of $\mathbb{Q}(i)$.

**Conclusion.** The splitting field of $x^4+4$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $\mathbb{Q}(
i)$, and within it
$$-1=\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^2+\left(\frac{5i}{4}\right)^2,$$
so $-1$ can indeed be expressed as the sum of two squares in the splitting field
. $\blacksquare$
