## Proof

**Claim:** The number of primes congruent to $1 \pmod{6}$ is asymptotically equa
l to the number of primes congruent to $5 \pmod{6}$.

Let $\pi(x; a, q)$ denote the number of primes $p \le x$ with $p \equiv a \pmod{
q}$.

We will show that
$$\pi(x; 1, 6) \sim \pi(x; 5, 6) \sim \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{x}{\ln x} \quad \text{a
s } x \to \infty,$$
which in particular implies $\pi(x;1,6) \sim \pi(x;5,6)$.

### Step 1: Reduction to Dirichlet $L$-functions

The key tool is the **Prime Number Theorem for arithmetic progressions**. For a
modulus $q$ and a residue $a$ with $\gcd(a,q)=1$, the asymptotic
$$\pi(x; a, q) \sim \frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\,\frac{x}{\ln x}$$
holds if and only if the Dirichlet $L$-function $L(s, \chi)$ has **no zeros on t
he line $\Re(s) = 1$** for every non-principal character $\chi \pmod{q}$.

Here $q = 6$, $\varphi(6) = 2$, and the reduced residues are $a \in \{1, 5\}$. S
o if the zero-free condition holds, we immediately get
$$\pi(x; 1, 6) \sim \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{x}{\ln x}, \qquad \pi(x; 5, 6) \sim \frac
{1}{2}\,\frac{x}{\ln x},$$
and hence $\pi(x;1,6) \sim \pi(x;5,6)$.

### Step 2: The character group mod 6

The group $(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z})^{\times} = \{1, 5\}$ has order $2$, so there
 are exactly two Dirichlet characters mod $6$:

- $\chi_0$: the principal character, $\chi_0(1)=\chi_0(5)=1$.
- $\chi_1$: the non-principal (real, quadratic) character, with $\chi_1(1)=1$, $
\chi_1(5)=-1$.

This $\chi_1$ is the real primitive character of conductor $3$ (the Legendre sym
bol mod $3$, extended to modulus $6$), since $5 \equiv 2 \pmod{3}$ and $2$ is a
non-residue mod $3$.

### Step 3: $L(s, \chi_1)$ is zero-free on $\Re(s) = 1$

The $L$-function associated to $\chi_1$ is
$$L(s, \chi_1) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\chi_1(n)}{n^s} = 1 - \frac{1}{2^s} +
 \frac{1}{4^s} - \frac{1}{5^s} + \frac{1}{7^s} - \cdots$$

We claim $L(1, \chi_1) \neq 0$. Since $\chi_1$ is real, $L(s,\chi_1)$ is a real-
valued function on the real line. A classical argument (going back to Dirichlet)
 shows:

Consider the product $\zeta(s)\, L(s, \chi_1)$. Its Dirichlet series has non-neg
ative coefficients, because for a prime $p$:
- If $p \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$ (i.e., $\chi_1(p) = 1$): the local factor is $(1-p^{-
s})^{-1}(1-p^{-s})^{-1}$, contributing positive coefficients.
- If $p \equiv 2 \pmod{3}$ (i.e., $\chi_1(p) = -1$): the local factor is $(1-p^{
-s})^{-1}(1+p^{-s})^{-1} = (1-p^{-2s})^{-1}$, contributing non-negative coeffici
ents.
- If $p = 3$: the local factor of $L(s,\chi_1)$ is $1$ (since $\chi_1(3)=0$), so
 the product's local factor is $(1-3^{-s})^{-1}$, non-negative coefficients.

So $\zeta(s) L(s, \chi_1) = \sum a_n n^{-s}$ with $a_n \ge 0$ for all $n$.

Now suppose for contradiction that $L(1, \chi_1) = 0$. Then $\zeta(s) L(s, \chi_
1)$ would be analytic at $s=1$ (the pole of $\zeta$ at $s=1$ cancelled by the ze
ro of $L$). Moreover, $\zeta(s) L(s, \chi_1)$ has a Dirichlet series with non-ne
gative coefficients converging for $\Re(s) > 1$. By a classical Tauberian/positi
vity argument (Landau's theorem: a Dirichlet series with non-negative coefficien
ts cannot have a singularity to the right of its abscissa of convergence), the s
eries $\sum a_n n^{-s}$ would converge for all $\Re(s) > 0$ (since the product i
s analytic there after cancellation at $s=1$, and the only other possible singul
arity is at $s=0$ which is to the left).

But then consider $s = 1/2 + it$ near $s = 1/2$. The function $\zeta(s) L(s, \ch
i_1)$ at $s = 1/2$ equals $\zeta(1/2) L(1/2, \chi_1)$, which is a finite value.
However, the key contradiction comes from examining the behavior: the series $\s
um a_n n^{-s}$ converging at $\Re(s) > 0$ would imply $\sum a_n / \sqrt{n}$ conv
erges. But $a_n \ge 1$ whenever $n$ is a perfect square composed of primes $\equ
iv 1 \pmod 3$ (in fact $a_{n^2} \ge 1$ for all $n$ coprime to $6$, since the coe
fficient of $n^{-2s}$ in the product picks up at least $1$ from each prime facto
r's square term). This forces $\sum_{n} a_n / \sqrt{n} \ge \sum_{n, \gcd(n,6)=1}
 1/n = \infty$, a contradiction.

Therefore $L(1, \chi_1) \neq 0$.

More generally, since $\chi_1$ is a **real** character, the only possible zero o
n $\Re(s)=1$ would be at $s=1$ itself (complex zeros $1+it$ with $t \neq 0$ woul
d come in conjugate pairs and would force $L(s,\chi_1^2) = L(s, \chi_0)$ to have
 a zero, contradicting the pole of $\zeta$). We just showed $s=1$ is not a zero.
 Hence **$L(s, \chi_1)$ has no zeros on $\Re(s) = 1$**.

### Step 4: Conclusion via the PNT for arithmetic progressions

Since the only non-principal character mod $6$ is $\chi_1$, and $L(s, \chi_1)$ i
s zero-free on $\Re(s) = 1$, the hypotheses of the Prime Number Theorem for arit
hmetic progressions are satisfied for modulus $q = 6$.

Therefore:
$$\pi(x; 1, 6) \sim \frac{1}{\varphi(6)}\,\frac{x}{\ln x} = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{x
}{\ln x},$$
$$\pi(x; 5, 6) \sim \frac{1}{\varphi(6)}\,\frac{x}{\ln x} = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{x
}{\ln x}.$$

Consequently:
$$\boxed{\pi(x; 1, 6) \sim \pi(x; 5, 6) \quad \text{as } x \to \infty.}$$

The number of primes congruent to $1 \pmod{6}$ **is** asymptotically equal to th
e number of primes congruent to $5 \pmod{6}$. Both classes receive exactly half
of all primes (excluding $2$ and $3$), in the asymptotic sense. $\blacksquare$
