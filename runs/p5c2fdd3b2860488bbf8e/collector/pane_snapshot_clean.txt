**Problem.** Determine whether $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{3}) \cap \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]
{7}) = \mathbb{Q}$.

**Answer.** Yes, the intersection is $\mathbb{Q}$.

**Proof.**

Set $\alpha = \sqrt[4]{3}$ and $\beta = \sqrt[4]{7}$, and let $K = \mathbb{Q}(\a
lpha) \cap \mathbb{Q}(\beta)$.

**Step 1: Degrees and divisibility.**

The polynomials $x^4 - 3$ and $x^4 - 7$ are irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ (Eisen
stein at $p=3$ and $p=7$ respectively), so
$$[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}] = [\mathbb{Q}(\beta):\mathbb{Q}] = 4.$$
Since $K \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$, the tower law gives $:\mathbb{Q}] \mid
 4$, hence $:\mathbb{Q}] \in \{1,2,4\}$.

**Step 2: Key Lemma — the unique quadratic subfield.**

*Lemma.* If $n > 0$ and $x^4 - n$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, then $\mathb
b{Q}(\sqrt[4]{n})$ has exactly one quadratic subfield, namely $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{
n})$.

*Proof of Lemma.* Let $\gamma = \sqrt[4]{n}$. The splitting field of $x^4 - n$ i
s $L = \mathbb{Q}(\gamma, i)$, with $[L:\mathbb{Q}] = 8$ (since $i \notin \mathb
b{R} \supseteq \mathbb{Q}(\gamma)$). Thus $G := \operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q})
 \cong D_4$, generated by
$$\rho: \gamma \mapsto i\gamma,\; i \mapsto i, \qquad \sigma: \gamma \mapsto \ga
mma,\; i \mapsto -i,$$
where $\sigma$ is complex conjugation. The subgroup fixing $\mathbb{Q}(\gamma)$
is $H = \langle \sigma \rangle = \{1, \sigma\}$.

Subfields of $\mathbb{Q}(\gamma)$ correspond (Galois correspondence) to subgroup
s $J$ with $H \leq J \leq G$. The subgroups of $D_4$ containing $\sigma$ are:
- $\{1, \sigma\}$ (order 2) $\leftrightarrow$ $\mathbb{Q}(\gamma)$,
- $\langle \rho^2, \sigma \rangle = \{1, \rho^2, \sigma, \rho^2\sigma\}$ (order
4) $\leftrightarrow$ the unique quadratic subfield,
- $G$ (order 8) $\leftrightarrow$ $\mathbb{Q}$.

There is exactly **one** subgroup of order 4 containing $H$, namely $\langle \rh

he fixed field is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{n})$. $\quad\blacksquare$

**Step 3: Apply to both fields.**

By the Lemma:
- The unique quadratic subfield of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{3})$ is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqr
t{3})$.
- The unique quadratic subfield of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{7})$ is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqr
t{7})$.

These are distinct: if $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3}) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{7})$, then $\sqr
t{3} = a + b\sqrt{7}$ for some $a,b \in \mathbb{Q}$, squaring gives $3 = a^2 + 7

mpossible), if $a=0$ then $3 = 7b^2$ so $b^2 = 3/7$, not a rational square. Cont
radiction. Hence $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3}) \neq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{7})$.

**Step 4: Eliminate the cases.**

- **$:\mathbb{Q}] = 4$:** Then $K = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha) = \mathbb{Q}(\beta)$. T

{3})$ and $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{7})$, so again $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3}) = \mathbb{Q

- **$:\mathbb{Q}] = 1$:** $K = \mathbb{Q}$. This is consistent.

**Conclusion.** The only possibility is $:\mathbb{Q}] = 1$, i.e.,
$$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{3}) \cap \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{7}) = \mathbb{Q}.$$
