## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, there exists $c \in (a,b)$ satisfying the equation.

**Setup.** Let $g(x) = f^{(n+1)}(x)$ and $w(x) = (b-x)^n$. Since $f$ is $(n+1)$-
times continuously differentiable on $(\tilde a, \tilde b)$ and $[a,b]\subset(\t
ilde a,\tilde b)$, $g$ is continuous on $[a,b]$. The weight satisfies $w(x)\ge 0
$ on $[a,b]$ (as $b-x\ge 0$), and

$$\int_a^b w(x)\,dx = \int_a^b (b-x)^n\,dx = \frac{(b-a)^{n+1}}{n+1} > 0.$$

**Weighted mean value theorem (with open-interval refinement).** Since $g$ is co
ntinuous on the compact interval $[a,b]$, it attains its minimum $m$ and maximum
 $M$. Define the weighted average

$$\bar g \;=\; \frac{\int_a^b w(x)\,g(x)\,dx}{\int_a^b w(x)\,dx}.$$

Then $m \le \bar g \le M$.

*Case 1: $g$ is constant on $[a,b]$.* Then $g\equiv \bar g$, and every $c\in(a,b
)$ works.

*Case 2: $g$ is not constant, so $m < M$.* We show the inequalities are strict.
Suppose for contradiction $\bar g = M$. Then

$$\int_a^b w(x)\bigl(M - g(x)\bigr)\,dx = 0.$$

Both $w(x)\ge 0$ and $M-g(x)\ge 0$ are continuous, so $w(x)(M-g(x))=0$ for all $
x\in[a,b]$. Since $g$ is not constant, there exists $x_0\in[a,b]$ with $g(x_0)<M
$, i.e. $M-g(x_0)>0$. By continuity, $M-g(x)>0$ on an open interval $I\ni x_0$.

- If $x_0\ne b$: then $w(x_0)=(b-x_0)^n>0$, so $w(x_0)(M-g(x_0))>0$, contradicti
on.
- If $x_0 = b$: by continuity $M-g(x)>0$ for $x$ near $b$, and for $x<b$ suffici
ently close to $b$, $w(x)=(b-x)^n>0$; the product is positive on a set of positi
ve measure, contradicting the integral being $0$.

Hence $\bar g < M$. A symmetric argument gives $\bar g > m$. So $m < \bar g < M$
.

Let $x_m, x_M\in[a,b]$ be points where $g$ attains $m$ and $M$. Since $g$ is not
 constant, $x_m\ne x_M$. By the intermediate value theorem applied to $g$ on the
 interval between $x_m$ and $x_M$, there exists $c$ strictly between $x_m$ and $
x_M$ with $g(c)=\bar g$. Since $c$ lies strictly between two points of $[a,b]$,
we have $c\in(a,b)$.

**Conclusion.** In both cases, there exists $c\in(a,b)$ with $g(c)=\bar g$, i.e.

$$f^{(n+1)}(c) = \frac{\int_a^b (b-x)^n\, f^{(n+1)}(x)\,dx}{\frac{(b-a)^{n+1}}{n
+1}},$$

which rearranges to

$$\int_a^b (b-x)^n\, f^{(n+1)}(x)\,dx = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{n+1}\,(b-a)^{n+1}.$$

Therefore such a point $c\in(a,b)$ always exists. $\blacksquare$
