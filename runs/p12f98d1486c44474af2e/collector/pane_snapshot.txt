Answer: Yes, $K$ is necessarily compact.

Proof

Setup. Let $K \subset E$ be closed and bounded, and suppose that for every $
\epsilon > 0$, there exists a finite-dimensional subspace $V \subset E$ with
$d(x, V) < \epsilon$ for all $x \in K$. We show $K$ is compact.

Since $E$ is a Banach space (complete metric space), it suffices to show that
$K$ is totally bounded (a closed, totally bounded subset of a complete metric
space is compact).

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Step 1: $K$ is bounded. By hypothesis, there exists $M > 0$ such that $|x| \le
M$ for all $x \in K$.

Step 2: Approximate $K$ by a bounded set in a finite-dimensional space. Fix $
\epsilon > 0$. By the approximation property, there exists a finite-dimensional
subspace $V \subset E$ such that $$d(x, V) < \epsilon \quad \text{for all } x
\in K.$$ For each $x \in K$, choose $v_x \in V$ with $|x - v_x| < \epsilon$
(possible since $d(x,V) < \epsilon$). Then: $$|v_x| \le |v_x - x| + |x| <
\epsilon + M.$$ So the set $S := {v_x : x \in K} \subset V$ is contained in the
closed ball $\overline{B_V(0, M+\epsilon)}$.

Step 3: $S$ is totally bounded. Since $V$ is finite-dimensional, the closed ball
$\overline{B_V(0, M+\epsilon)}$ is compact (Heine–Borel in finite dimensions).
Hence $S$ is totally bounded: there exist $w_1, \dots, w_n \in V$ such that $$S
\subset \bigcup_{i=1}^n B_V(w_i, \epsilon).$$

Step 4: $K$ is covered by finitely many $2\epsilon$-balls. For any $x \in K$,
the associated $v_x \in S$ satisfies $|v_x - w_i| < \epsilon$ for some $i$. By
the triangle inequality: $$|x - w_i| \le |x - v_x| + |v_x - w_i| < \epsilon +
\epsilon = 2\epsilon.$$ Therefore: $$K \subset \bigcup_{i=1}^n B_E(w_i,
2\epsilon).$$

Step 5: $K$ is totally bounded. Since $\epsilon > 0$ was arbitrary, for every $
\delta > 0$ (set $\epsilon = \delta/2$) we can cover $K$ by finitely many balls
of radius $\delta$. Thus $K$ is totally bounded.

Step 6: $K$ is compact. $K$ is a closed, totally bounded subset of the complete
metric space $E$. Therefore $K$ is compact. $\blacksquare$

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Remark. The hypothesis that $K$ is bounded is essential: the set $K =
\mathbb{R},e_1 \subset \ell^2$ (an entire line) is closed, satisfies $d(x,
\operatorname{span}(e_1)) = 0$ for all $x \in K$, but is not compact (it is
unbounded). The boundedness hypothesis is what allows the projected set $S$ to
lie in a compact ball of $V$, enabling total boundedness.
