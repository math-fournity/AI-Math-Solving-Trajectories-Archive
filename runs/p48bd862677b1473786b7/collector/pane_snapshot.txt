## Proof

We interpret "completely defined by its derivatives at a single point" in the st
andard sense: a function $f$ is completely defined by its derivatives at a point
 $a$ if the Taylor series of $f$ at $a$,
$$T_a(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n,$$
converges to $f(x)$ for every $x$ in the domain (i.e., $f$ is real analytic at $
a$, with positive radius of convergence recovering $f$ globally). We show that t
here exists an infinitely differentiable function for which this fails.

**Construction.** Define $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ by
$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} e^{-1/x^2}, & x \neq 0, \\ 0, & x = 0. \end{cases}
$$

**Step 1: $f$ is infinitely differentiable on $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$.**
For $x\neq 0$, $f$ is a composition of smooth functions ($x\mapsto -1/x^2$ and $
t\mapsto e^t$), hence $f\in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})$.

**Step 2: $f$ is infinitely differentiable at $0$, with $f^{(n)}(0)=0$ for all $
n\geq 0$.**
We prove by induction that for each $n\geq 0$:
- $f^{(n)}(0) = 0$, and
- for $x\neq 0$, $f^{(n)}(x) = P_n(1/x)\, e^{-1/x^2}$ for some polynomial $P_n$.

*Base case ($n=0$):* $f(0)=0$ by definition, and for $x\neq 0$, $f(x)=e^{-1/x^2}
=P_0(1/x)\,e^{-1/x^2}$ with $P_0(t)=1$.

*Inductive step:* Assume the claim holds for $n$. For $x\neq 0$,
$$f^{(n+1)}(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[P_n(1/x)\,e^{-1/x^2}\bigr].$$
Using the chain rule, $\frac{d}{dx}(1/x) = -1/x^2$ and $\frac{d}{dx}e^{-1/x^2} =
 \frac{2}{x^3}e^{-1/x^2}$, so
$$f^{(n+1)}(x) = \bigl[-P_n'(1/x)/x^2 + 2P_n(1/x)/x^3\bigr]e^{-1/x^2} = P_{n+1}(
1/x)\,e^{-1/x^2},$$
where $P_{n+1}(t) = -t^2 P_n'(t) + 2t^3 P_n(t)$ is again a polynomial. This esta
blishes the form of $f^{(n+1)}$ away from $0$.

Now we verify differentiability at $0$:
$$f^{(n+1)}(0) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f^{(n)}(h)-f^{(n)}(0)}{h} = \lim_{h\to 0}\fr
ac{P_n(1/h)\,e^{-1/h^2}}{h} = \lim_{h\to 0} P_n(1/h)\cdot\frac{1}{h}\cdot e^{-1/
h^2}.$$
Substituting $t=1/h$ (so $t\to\pm\infty$), this becomes
$$\lim_{t\to\pm\infty} P_n(t)\cdot t \cdot e^{-t^2} = 0,$$
since $e^{-t^2}$ decays faster than any polynomial grows. Hence $f^{(n+1)}(0)=0$
, completing the induction.

Therefore $f\in C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ and $f^{(n)}(0)=0$ for every $n\geq 0$.

**Step 3: The Taylor series of $f$ at $0$ does not recover $f$.**
Since every derivative at $0$ vanishes, the Taylor series at the origin is
$$T_0(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}x^n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} 0 =
 0 \quad \text{for all } x.$$
However, $f(x) = e^{-1/x^2} > 0$ for every $x\neq 0$. Thus $T_0(x)\neq f(x)$ for
 all $x\neq 0$.

**Conclusion.** The function $f$ is infinitely differentiable, yet its Taylor se
ries at the point $0$—which encodes all of its derivatives at that single point—
is identically zero and fails to equal $f$ at any point other than $0$. Therefor
e $f$ cannot be completely defined by its derivatives at a single point. This pr
oves that such a function exists. $\blacksquare$
