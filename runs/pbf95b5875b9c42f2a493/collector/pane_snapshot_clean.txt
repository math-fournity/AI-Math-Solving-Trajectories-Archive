## Proof

**Claim.** Let $J_k(\lambda)$ denote the $k \times k$ Jordan block with eigenval
ue $\lambda$. For $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$,
 the matrices $J_k(\lambda)^n$ and $J_k(\lambda^n)$ are similar **if and only if
 $n \geq 1$** (equivalently, $n\lambda^{n-1}\neq 0$). In particular, when $\math
bb{N}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}$, the similarity holds for **every** $n\in\mathbb{N}$ and
every $\lambda\neq 0$ — no further condition is required.

---

### Setup

Write $J_k(\lambda) = \lambda I + N$, where $N$ is the nilpotent block with $1$'
s on the superdiagonal ($N^k=0$, $N^{k-1}\neq 0$). Since $I$ and $N$ commute, th
e binomial theorem gives

$$
J_k(\lambda)^n = (\lambda I + N)^n = \lambda^n I + \binom{n}{1}\lambda^{n-1}N +
\binom{n}{2}\lambda^{n-2}N^2 + \cdots + \binom{n}{n}N^n.
$$

Set $A := J_k(\lambda)^n - \lambda^n I$. Then

$$
A = N\cdot g(N), \qquad g(t) := \binom{n}{1}\lambda^{n-1} + \binom{n}{2}\lambda^
{n-2}t + \cdots + \binom{n}{n}t^{n-1}.
$$

Equivalently $A = g(N)\,N$ since $g(N)$ is a polynomial in $N$ and hence commute
s with $N$.

### Key observation

The constant term of $g(t)$ is $g(0) = n\lambda^{n-1}$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ (chara
cteristic $0$):

$$
g(0) = n\lambda^{n-1} = 0 \iff n = 0 \text{ or } \lambda = 0.
$$

Since $\lambda \neq 0$ is given, $g(0)\neq 0$ **iff $n \geq 1$**. When $g(0)\neq
 0$, the polynomial $g$ has nonzero constant term, so $g(N)$ is invertible (its
inverse is a polynomial in $N$).

### Sufficiency ($n\geq 1$, $\lambda\neq 0$ $\Rightarrow$ similar)

Assume $n\geq 1$ and $\lambda\neq 0$. Then $g(N)$ is invertible and commutes wit
h $N$. For every $m\geq 1$,

$$
A^m = \bigl(g(N)\,N\bigr)^m = g(N)^m\, N^m,
$$

using commutativity. Since $g(N)^m$ is invertible:

$$
A^m = 0 \iff N^m = 0 \iff m \geq k,
$$

and $A^{k-1} = g(N)^{k-1}N^{k-1}\neq 0$ (product of an invertible matrix with $N
^{k-1}\neq 0$). Hence $A$ is nilpotent of **index exactly $k$**, which forces it
s Jordan form to be the single block $J_k(0)$. Therefore

$$
J_k(\lambda)^n = \lambda^n I + A \sim \lambda^n I + J_k(0) = J_k(\lambda^n).
$$

### Necessity ($n=0$ fails for $k\geq 2$)

If $n=0$: $J_k(\lambda)^0 = I_k$, which is diagonal, hence similar to $J_k(\lamb
da^0)=J_k(1)$ only when $k=1$. For $k\geq 2$ the similarity fails ($I_k$ has $k$
 Jordan blocks of size $1$, while $J_k(1)$ is a single block of size $k$). So $n
\geq 1$ is necessary (for $k\geq 2$).

### Conclusion

Over $\mathbb{C}$, the similarity $J_k(\lambda)^n \sim J_k(\lambda^n)$ holds pre
cisely when

$$
\boxed{n\lambda^{n-1}\neq 0, \quad\text{i.e.,}\quad n\geq 1 \text{ and } \lambda
\neq 0.}
$$

Since $\lambda\neq 0$ is already assumed, the **only additional condition** is $
n\geq 1$. Under the convention $\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}$, no additional condi
tion is needed: the similarity is automatic for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$ and all $\l
ambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$. The essential mechanism is that $n\lambda^{n-
1}\neq 0$ keeps the nilpotent part of $J_k(\lambda)^n$ a single Jordan block of
size $k$, matching $J_k(\lambda^n)$.
