## Proof that the equality does NOT hold in general

**Setup.** Let $n = 2$. Take
$$Y = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad X = \begin{pmatrix} 0
 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

**Verifying hypotheses.** $X$ is Hermitian (real symmetric). $Y$ is Hermitian wi
th eigenvalues $1, 0$, hence positive semi-definite. ✓

**Computing $YXY$.**
$$YX = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 &
0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
$$YXY = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 &
 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Since $YXY = 0$, its positive part is:
$$(YXY)^+ = 0.$$

**Computing $X^+$.** The eigenvalues of $X$ are $+1$ and $-1$, with normalized e
igenvectors $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,1)^T$ and $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,-1)^T$ respect
ively. The positive part retains only the $+1$ eigenvalue:
$$X^+ = 1 \cdot \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1
 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

**Computing $YX^+Y$.**
$$YX^+ = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 \
\ 1/2 & 1/2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
$$YX^+Y = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0
\\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

**Comparison.**
$$(YXY)^+ = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \neq \begin{pmatrix} 1/
2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = YX^+Y.$$

## Conceptual explanation

The positive part $A^+ = f(A)$ with $f(t) = \max(t,0)$ is a **functional calculu
s** operation: it acts on each eigenvalue independently in the eigenbasis of $A$
. The map $X \mapsto YXY$ is a **congruence** that changes the eigenbasis (the e
igenvectors of $YXY$ are not $Y$-images of the eigenvectors of $X$ in general).
Since functional calculus and congruence do not commute, there is no reason for
$(YXY)^+$ to equal $YX^+Y$.

In the counterexample above, $Y$ projects onto the first coordinate. The matrix
$X$ has a positive eigenvalue $+1$ whose eigenvector $(1,1)^T$ has a component i
n the range of $Y$, so $YX^+Y \neq 0$. But $YXY = 0$ because the range of $Y$ (t
he first coordinate) is mapped by $X$ into the kernel of $Y$ (the second coordin
ate), and then $Y$ kills it. The positive part of the zero matrix is zero, givin
g the contradiction.

**Remark.** The equality fails even when $Y$ is positive *definite*. For instanc
e, with $Y = \mathrm{diag}(2,1)$ and the same $X$, one computes $(YXY)^+ = \begi
n{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ while $YX^+Y = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\
 1 & 1/2 \end{pmatrix}$, which are again unequal. The root cause is the same: co
ngruence and functional calculus do not commute.

## Conclusion

The equality $(YXY)^+ = YX^+Y$ **does not hold** in general. A concrete countere
xample is $Y = \mathrm{diag}(1,0)$, $X = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pma
trix}$, for which $(YXY)^+ = 0 \neq YX^+Y = \mathrm{diag}(1/2, 0)$.
