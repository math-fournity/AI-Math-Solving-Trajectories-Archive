## Proof

**Answer: No. The Jacobian of \(f\) at \(p\) need not be singular.** The argumen
t proposed in the problem rests on a misstatement of the inverse function theore
m.

### The flaw in the proposed implication

The inverse function theorem does **not** state:

> "If \(Df(p)\) is non-singular, then \(f\) is differentiable on some open set c
ontaining \(p\)."

What it actually states is:

> If \(f\) is **continuously differentiable** (\(C^{1}\)) on an open set \(U\) c
ontaining \(p\), **and** \(Df(p)\) is non-singular, then \(f\) is a local \(C^{1
}\)-diffeomorphism near \(p\) (in particular, \(f\) is differentiable on some ne
ighborhood of \(p\), and has a local \(C^{1}\) inverse).

The hypothesis "\(f\) is \(C^{1}\) on a neighborhood of \(p\)" is an essential *
*precondition** of the theorem, not a conclusion. The non-singularity of \(Df(p)
\) alone, without the surrounding \(C^{1}\) hypothesis, yields nothing.

In our situation, \(f\) is differentiable only at the single point \(p\). Hence
\(f\) is **not** \(C^{1}\) on any open neighborhood of \(p\) (it is not even dif
ferentiable at any other point of such a neighborhood). The precondition of the
inverse function theorem fails, so the theorem is simply **inapplicable**. There
 is no contradiction to derive, and therefore no logical pressure forcing \(Df(p
)\) to be singular.

### A concrete counterexample with non-singular Jacobian

To make this definitive, we exhibit a function differentiable at exactly one poi
nt, with **non-singular** Jacobian there.

Let \(D:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\) be the Dirichlet-type function
\[
D(x)=\begin{cases}1,&x\in\mathbb{Q}^{n},\\0,&x\notin\mathbb{Q}^{n}.\end{cases}
\]
Define
\[
f(x)=x+\lVert x\rVert^{2}D(x),\qquad p=0.
\]

**Differentiability at \(0\) with non-singular Jacobian.** We have \(f(0)=0\), a
nd
\[
\frac{\lVert f(h)-f(0)-Ih\rVert}{\lVert h\rVert}
=\frac{\lVert h+\lVert h\rVert^{2}D(h)-h\rVert}{\lVert h\rVert}
=\lVert h\rVert\,D(h)\le \lVert h\rVert\to 0.
\]
So \(f\) is differentiable at \(0\) with \(Df(0)=I\), which is **non-singular**.

**Non-differentiability everywhere else.** For any \(x_{0}\ne 0\), the term \(\l
Vert x\rVert^{2}D(x)\) is discontinuous at \(x_{0}\): along rational points \(x\
to x_{0}\) it tends to \(\lVert x_{0}\rVert^{2}\ne 0\), while along irrational p
oints it tends to \(0\). Since \(x\mapsto x\) is continuous, \(f\) is discontinu
ous at every \(x_{0}\ne 0\), hence certainly not differentiable there.

Thus \(f\) is differentiable **only** at \(p=0\), yet \(Df(p)=I\) is non-singula
r.

### Conclusion

The inverse function theorem cannot be invoked, because its \(C^{1}\)-on-a-neigh
borhood hypothesis is violated. The non-singularity of \(Df(p)\) at an isolated
point of differentiability is perfectly consistent, as the explicit construction
 above demonstrates. Therefore the Jacobian of \(f\) at \(p\) **need not be sing
ular**.
