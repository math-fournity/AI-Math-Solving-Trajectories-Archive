## Solution

We determine the **maximum** number of regions into which $n$ unit spheres (thei
r surfaces) partition $\mathbb{R}^3$.

### Setup and general position

Place the $n$ unit sphere centers in a small cluster so that every pair of cente
rs is at distance strictly between $0$ and $2$. Then every pair of spheres inter
sects in a circle. By a generic perturbation of the centers we may assume **gene
ral position**:
- every pair of spheres meets in a (non-degenerate) circle,
- every triple of spheres meets in at most $2$ points,
- no four spheres share a common point,
- no tangencies occur.

This is achievable with unit spheres (cluster the centers in a tiny ball and per
turb), so the maximum region count is attainable.

### Recurrence

Let $R(n)$ be the maximum number of regions of $\mathbb{R}^3$ cut by $n$ spheres
 in general position, with $R(0)=1$.

Add the $n$-th sphere. Its surface is cut by the previous $n-1$ spheres in $n-1$
 circles (in general position on the sphere). Each patch of the new sphere's sur
face, lying inside one existing region, splits that region into two — adding exa
ctly one new region per patch. Hence

$$R(n) = R(n-1) + S(n-1),$$

where $S(m)$ is the number of regions into which $m$ circles in general position
 divide a sphere's surface.

### Regions on a sphere cut by $m$ circles

On a sphere, $m$ circles in general position (each pair meeting in $2$ points, n
o three concurrent) satisfy the recurrence $S(m)=S(m-1)+2(m-1)$: the $m$-th circ
le is cut by the previous $m-1$ circles in $2(m-1)$ points, hence into $2(m-1)$
arcs, each arc splitting one existing region. With $S(0)=1$:

$$S(m) = 1 + \sum_{j=1}^{m} 2(j-1) = 1 + m(m-1) = m^2-m+1 \quad (m\ge 0).$$

Check: $S(0)=1$, $S(1)=1$ (one circle → $2$ regions? No).

Wait — one circle on a sphere divides it into **$2$** regions, so $S(1)=2$. Let
me correct the base: a single circle gives $2$ regions, so $S(1)=2=S(0)+2\cdot 0
$ would give $1$. The issue is the first circle: it is a closed curve with no pr
ior intersection points, yet it still splits the sphere into $2$ regions (one cl
osed curve → $+1$ region). The correct recurrence is $S(m)=S(m-1)+\max(1,2(m-1))
$, i.e. $S(1)=S(0)+1=2$, and $S(m)=S(m-1)+2(m-1)$ for $m\ge 2$.

Thus $S(0)=1$, $S(1)=2$, and for $m\ge 2$: $S(m)=m^2-m+2$. One verifies $m^2-m+2
$ also gives $S(1)=2$ but $S(0)=2\neq 1$. So

$$S(m)=\begin{cases}1,&m=0,\\ m^2-m+2,&m\ge 1.\end{cases}$$

### Summing the recurrence

For $n\ge 1$:

$$R(n)=R(0)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(k)=1+S(0)+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(k^2-k+2).$$

Since $S(0)=1$:

$$R(n)=2+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k^2-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k+2(n-1).$$

Using $\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k^2=\tfrac{(n-1)n(2n-1)}{6}$ and $\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k=\tfra
c{(n-1)n}{2}$:

$$R(n)=2+\frac{(n-1)n(2n-1)}{6}-\frac{(n-1)n}{2}+2(n-1).$$

Combine the two fractions:

$$\frac{(n-1)n(2n-1)-3(n-1)n}{6}=\frac{(n-1)n(2n-4)}{6}=\frac{(n-1)n(n-2)}{3}.$$

So

$$R(n)=2+\frac{(n-1)n(n-2)}{3}+2(n-1)=2+\frac{(n-1)\bigl(n(n-2)+6\bigr)}{3}.$$

$$R(n)=2+\frac{(n-1)(n^2-2n+6)}{3}=\frac{6+(n-1)(n^2-2n+6)}{3}.$$

Expanding $(n-1)(n^2-2n+6)=n^3-3n^2+8n-6$:

$$R(n)=\frac{6+n^3-3n^2+8n-6}{3}=\frac{n^3-3n^2+8n}{3}=\boxed{\dfrac{n(n^2-3n+8)
}{3}}.$$

### Verification

| $n$ | Formula | Direct count |
|-----|---------|--------------|
| $0$ | $0$ (formula valid for $n\ge1$; $R(0)=1$) | $1$ |
| $1$ | $\frac{1\cdot 6}{3}=2$ | inside/outside ✓ |
| $2$ | $\frac{2\cdot 6}{3}=4$ | ✓ |
| $3$ | $\frac{3\cdot 8}{3}=8$ | ✓ |
| $4$ | $\frac{4\cdot 12}{3}=16$ | ✓ |
| $5$ | $\frac{5\cdot 18}{3}=30$ | ✓ |

All agree with the recurrence $R(n)=R(n-1)+S(n-1)$.

### Answer

The number of regions formed by $n$ unit spheres in general position in $\mathbb
{R}^3$ is

$$\boxed{\dfrac{n(n^2-3n+8)}{3}}.$$
