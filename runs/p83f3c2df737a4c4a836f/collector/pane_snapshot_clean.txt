## Solution

The statement is **FALSE** in general. It holds over an algebraically closed fie
ld (by Hilbert's Nullstellensatz) but fails over non-algebraically closed fields
. The problem does not specify that the base field is algebraically closed, so t
he general statement is false.

### Counterexample

Work over the field $k = \mathbb{R}$, in the polynomial ring $\mathbb{R}[x]$, an
d take $f = x^2 + 1$.

**Step 1: Compute $X = V(f)$.**

$V(f) = \{a \in \mathbb{R} : a^2 + 1 = 0\}$. Since $a^2 + 1 \geq 1 > 0$ for all
$a \in \mathbb{R}$, there are no real solutions. Thus
$$X = V(f) = \varnothing \subset \mathbb{R}^1.$$

**Step 2: Compute $I(X)$.**

By definition, $I(X) = \{g \in \mathbb{R}[x] : g(a) = 0 \text{ for all } a \in X
\}$. Since $X = \varnothing$, the condition "$g(a) = 0$ for all $a \in \varnothi
ng$" is vacuously true for every polynomial $g$. Therefore
$$I(X) = I(\varnothing) = \mathbb{R}[x].$$

**Step 3: Compute $\sqrt{(f)}$.**

The polynomial $x^2 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$ (it has no real roots
and is degree 2). Since $\mathbb{R}[x]$ is a PID, an irreducible element generat
es a prime ideal. A prime ideal is radical (if $g^n \in P$ and $P$ is prime, the
n $g \in P$). Hence
$$\sqrt{(f)} = \sqrt{(x^2+1)} = (x^2+1).$$

**Step 4: Compare.**

$$I(X) = \mathbb{R}[x] \neq (x^2+1) = \sqrt{(f)}.$$

For instance, $1 \in I(X) = \mathbb{R}[x]$, but $1 \notin (x^2+1) = \sqrt{(f)}$
(since $1^n = 1 \notin (x^2+1)$ for any $n$).

### Conclusion

The equality $I(X) = \sqrt{(f)}$ fails for $f = x^2 + 1$ over $\mathbb{R}$. The
statement is therefore **false** in general.

**Remark.** Over an algebraically closed field $k$, the statement *is* true: Hil
bert's Nullstellensatz gives $I(V(J)) = \sqrt{J}$ for every ideal $J$, and takin
g $J = (f)$ yields exactly $I(V(f)) = \sqrt{(f)}$. The counterexample exploits t
he failure of the Nullstellensatz over non-algebraically-closed fields: $x^2+1$
has no real zeros, so $V(f)$ is empty while $\sqrt{(f)}$ is a proper ideal.
