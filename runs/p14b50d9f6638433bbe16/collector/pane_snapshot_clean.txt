## Proof

We seek the analytic continuation of $x^x$ (defined for real $x>0$) to the domai
n $\Omega = \mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]$.

**Step 1: Represent $x^x$ on $(0,\infty)$.**

For real $x>0$, we have $x^x = \exp(x\ln x)$, where $\ln$ is the natural real lo
garithm.

**Step 2: Define the analytic continuation.**

The principal logarithm $\operatorname{Log} z$ is analytic on $\Omega = \mathbb{
C}\setminus(-\infty,0]$, and agrees with $\ln x$ on $(0,\infty)$. Define
$$F(z) = \exp\!\big(z\,\operatorname{Log} z\big),\qquad z\in\Omega.$$
Since $z\mapsto z\,\operatorname{Log} z$ is analytic on $\Omega$ (product of ana
lytic functions) and $\exp$ is entire, $F$ is analytic on $\Omega$. For real $x>
0$,
$$F(x)=\exp(x\ln x)=x^x,$$
so $F$ is an analytic continuation of $x^x$ to $\Omega$.

**Step 3: Uniqueness.**

The interval $(0,\infty)\subset\Omega$ has accumulation points in $\Omega$, and
$\Omega$ is connected (in fact simply connected). By the identity theorem, an an
alytic function on $\Omega$ is uniquely determined by its values on $(0,\infty)$
. Hence $f=F$, i.e. the continuation is unique.

**Step 4: Evaluate at $z=i$.**

We have $i\in\Omega$. The principal logarithm of $i$ is
$$\operatorname{Log} i = \ln|i| + i\arg(i) = \ln 1 + i\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{2} = \tfr
ac{i\pi}{2}.$$
Therefore
$$f(i)=F(i)=\exp\!\big(i\cdot\tfrac{i\pi}{2}\big)=\exp\!\big(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big
)=\frac{1}{e^{\pi/2}}.$$

$$\boxed{\,f(i)=e^{-\pi/2}\,}$$
