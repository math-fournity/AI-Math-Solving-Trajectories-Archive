The statement is **false**. Not every irrational number has the property that ea
ch digit appears with limiting frequency 1/10 in its decimal expansion. Below is
 a complete proof by explicit counterexample.

---

## Counterexample

Define the real number

$$x = 0.1010010001000010000010000001\ldots$$

constructed as follows: the digit $1$ appears exactly at positions

$$1,\; 2,\; 4,\; 7,\; 11,\; 16,\; 22,\; \ldots,\; \tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}+1,\; \ldots$
$

(i.e., at position $\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}+1$ for $n=1,2,3,\ldots$), and every other
digit is $0$.

### Claim 1: $x$ is irrational.

A real number is rational if and only if its decimal expansion is eventually per
iodic. In the expansion of $x$, the gaps between consecutive occurrences of the
digit $1$ are

$$1,\; 2,\; 3,\; 4,\; 5,\; 6,\; \ldots$$

which grow without bound. An eventually periodic sequence has bounded gaps betwe
en any fixed digit that appears infinitely often. Since the gaps between $1$'s a
re unbounded, the expansion cannot be eventually periodic. Therefore $x$ is **ir
rational**. $\checkmark$

### Claim 2: The digit frequencies do not approach $1/10$.

Let $N$ be a large integer, and let $f_N(d)$ denote the frequency of digit $d$ a
mong the first $N$ decimal digits of $x$.

The $n$-th occurrence of $1$ is at position $p_n = \tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}+1 \sim \tfr
ac{n^2}{2}$. Inverting, the number of $1$'s among the first $N$ digits is

$$\#\{n : p_n \le N\} \sim \sqrt{2N}.$$

Therefore

$$f_N(1) = \frac{\#\{\text{1's in first } N \text{ digits}\}}{N} \sim \frac{\sqr
t{2N}}{N} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{N}} \longrightarrow 0.$$

Since the only digits appearing are $0$ and $1$,

$$f_N(0) = 1 - f_N(1) \longrightarrow 1.$$

The remaining digits $2,3,\ldots,9$ have frequency exactly $0$ for all $N$.

### Conclusion

For the irrational number $x$:

$$\lim_{N\to\infty} f_N(0) = 1 \neq \tfrac{1}{10}, \qquad \lim_{N\to\infty} f_N(
1) = 0 \neq \tfrac{1}{10}, \qquad f_N(d) = 0 \neq \tfrac{1}{10} \text{ for } d\i
n\{2,\ldots,9\}.$$

No digit achieves limiting frequency $1/10$. Hence the statement

> "for any irrational number, the frequency of each digit in its decimal expansi
on approaches $\frac{1}{10}$"

is **false**.

---

**Remark.** The property described is *normality in base 10*. It is known that a
lmost every real number (in the Lebesgue-measure sense) is normal, and in partic
ular almost every irrational number is normal. However, as the counterexample ab
ove shows, normality is **not** a consequence of irrationality alone. Indeed, co
nstructing explicit examples of normal numbers is notoriously difficult (e.g., C
hampernowne's constant $0.123456789101112\ldots$ is known to be normal in base 1
0), whereas constructing irrational *non-normal* numbers, as done here, is strai
ghtforward.
