## Proof that the statement is FALSE

We exhibit a concrete counterexample.

**Choice of functions.** Let
$$f(t) = e^{-t^2}, \qquad g(t) = 1 \;\text{for all } t \in \mathbb{R}.$$

**Verification of hypotheses.**
- $f(t)=e^{-t^2}$ is $C^\infty$ on $\mathbb{R}$ and takes real (hence complex) v
alues. ✓
- $g(t)=1$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. ✓
- $g$ is of bounded variation: for any partition, $\sum |g(x_i)-g(x_{i-1})| = 0$
, so the total variation is $0 < \infty$. ✓

**Computing the left-hand side (Riemann integral).**
$$\text{LHS} = \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(t)\,g(t)\,dt = \int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-t^2}\cdo
t 1\,dt = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-t^2}\,dt = \sqrt{\pi}.$$

This is the classical Gaussian integral, which converges and equals $\sqrt{\pi}$
.

**Computing the right-hand side (Riemann–Stieltjes integral).**

The Riemann–Stieltjes integral $\int_{\mathbb{R}} f'(t)\,dg(t)$ is defined as th
e limit
$$\lim_{\substack{a\to-\infty\\ b\to+\infty}} \int_a^b f'(t)\,dg(t).$$

On any compact interval $[a,b]$, the Riemann–Stieltjes sum is
$$S(P) = \sum_{i} f'(\xi_i)\,\bigl[g(x_i)-g(x_{i-1})\bigr]$$
for a partition $P: a=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_n=b$ and tags $\xi_i\in[x_{i-1},x_i]$. Si
nce $g$ is constant, $g(x_i)-g(x_{i-1})=0$ for every $i$, so $S(P)=0$ for every
partition and every choice of tags. Therefore
$$\int_a^b f'(t)\,dg(t) = 0 \quad\text{for all } a,b,$$
and consequently
$$\text{RHS} = \lim_{\substack{a\to-\infty\\ b\to+\infty}} 0 = 0.$$

**Comparison.**
$$\text{LHS} = \sqrt{\pi} \neq 0 = \text{RHS}.$$

The two sides are unequal, so the stated identity does not hold in general.

---

**Remark (why the identity cannot hold in general).** The integration-by-parts f
ormula for Riemann–Stieltjes integrals gives, on $[a,b]$,
$$\int_a^b f'\,dg = \bigl[f'g\bigr]_a^b - \int_a^b g\,df' = f'(b)g(b)-f'(a)g(a)
- \int_a^b g(t)\,f''(t)\,dt,$$
which involves $f''$ and $g$, not $f$ and $g$. There is no general identity rela
ting $\int fg\,dt$ to $\int f'\,dg$; the counterexample above (with $g$ constant
, so $dg=0$) makes the discrepancy maximal. $\blacksquare$
