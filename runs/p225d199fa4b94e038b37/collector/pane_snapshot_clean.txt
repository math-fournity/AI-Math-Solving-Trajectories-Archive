## Proof

**Setup.** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. random variables with common density
$f$. Since $f \geq 0$ is continuous and $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1$,
the $X_i$ are genuine continuous random variables. Then

$$I_n(r) = \mathbb{P}\!\left(X_1^2 + X_2^2 + \cdots + X_n^2 \le r^2\right).$$

Set $Y_i = X_i^2 \ge 0$ and $S_n = Y_1 + \cdots + Y_n$, so $I_n(r) = \mathbb{P}(
S_n \le r^2)$.

**Key observation.** Since $f$ is continuous and integrates to $1$, $f$ is stric
tly positive on some nontrivial interval, so $X$ is not almost surely $0$. Hence

$$\mathbb{E}[Y_1] = \mathbb{E}[X_1^2] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^2 f(x)\,dx > 0
.$$

**Applying the Strong Law of Large Numbers.** The $Y_i$ are i.i.d. nonnegative w
ith finite positive mean $\mu := \mathbb{E}[X_1^2] > 0$. By the SLLN,

$$\frac{S_n}{n} = \frac{Y_1 + \cdots + Y_n}{n} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \mu > 0
.$$

Consequently $S_n \to +\infty$ almost surely.

**Conclusion via dominated convergence.** For any fixed $r \ge 0$, the indicator
 $\mathbf{1}_{\{S_n \le r^2\}} \to 0$ almost surely (since $S_n \to \infty$ a.s.
 eventually exceeds the fixed value $r^2$). Being bounded by $1$, dominated conv
ergence gives

$$I_n(r) = \mathbb{P}(S_n \le r^2) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{S_n \le r^2
\}}\right] \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 0.$$

Therefore, for every fixed $r \ge 0$,

$$\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty} I_n(r) = 0.}$$
