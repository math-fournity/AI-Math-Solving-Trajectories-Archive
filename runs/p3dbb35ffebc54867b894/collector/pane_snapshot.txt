**Problem.** Determine whether it is possible to ascertain if a given univariate
 polynomial with integer coefficients has algebraic roots.

**Answer: Yes — it is always possible, and the determination is trivial.**

---

### Proof

**Definition (algebraic number).** A complex number $\alpha$ is *algebraic* if t
here exists a non-zero polynomial $g(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ (equivalently $\mathb
b{Q}[x]$) such that $g(\alpha) = 0$.

**Key observation.** Let $f(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ be any non-zero univariate pol
ynomial with integer coefficients, and let $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$ be any root o
f $f$, i.e., $f(\alpha) = 0$. Then by the very definition above, $\alpha$ is alg
ebraic — the witnessing polynomial is $f$ itself (which is non-zero, has integer
 coefficients, and vanishes at $\alpha$).

**Conclusion per case.** Let $f(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ be given. We distinguish c
ases, all of which are effectively decidable from the coefficients of $f$:

1. **$f$ is non-constant ($\deg f \geq 1$).** By the Fundamental Theorem of Alge
bra, $f$ has at least one root $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$. By the key observation,
every such root is algebraic. Hence $f$ has algebraic roots. ✓

2. **$f$ is a non-zero constant ($\deg f = 0$, $f \not\equiv 0$).** Then $f$ has
 no roots at all, so it has no algebraic roots (vacuously, it does not "have alg
ebraic roots" in the existential sense). ✓

3. **$f \equiv 0$ (the zero polynomial).** Every complex number is a root; in pa
rticular every algebraic number is a root, so $f$ has algebraic roots. ✓

**Decidability.** Determining which case applies requires only inspecting the co
efficients of $f$:
- Check whether all coefficients are zero → case 3.
- Otherwise check whether $\deg f \geq 1$ → case 1.
- Otherwise → case 2.

This is a finite, effective procedure on the integer coefficients. No search, no
 approximation, and no undecidability arises.

**Remark.** The question might superficially appear to concern a difficult decis
ion problem (akin to Hilbert's Tenth Problem on Diophantine equations, which *is
* undecidable in the multivariate setting). However, the univariate case is fund
amentally different: every root of a univariate integer polynomial is automatica
lly algebraic *by definition*, so the only thing to decide is whether the polyno
mial has any root at all — and that is settled trivially by degree inspection to
gether with the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra. The hardness of Hilbert's Tenth
Problem arises from the existential quantification over *integer* solutions in *
many* variables, which is an entirely different question from whether the comple
x roots of a single univariate polynomial are algebraic.

---

Therefore, **yes, it is possible to ascertain whether a given univariate polynom
ial with integer coefficients has algebraic roots**: the answer is "yes" for eve
ry non-constant such polynomial (and for the zero polynomial), and "no" only for
 non-zero constant polynomials — and which case holds is effectively decidable b
y a trivial inspection of the coefficients.
