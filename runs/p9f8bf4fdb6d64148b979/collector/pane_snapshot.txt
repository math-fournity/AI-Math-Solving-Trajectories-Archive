**Proof.**

Since $g$ is locally integrable, it is integrable on every compact interval. Def
ine the indefinite integral
$$
G(x) = \int_{0}^{x} g(t)\,dt,
$$
where we understand $\int_0^x = -\int_x^0$ when $x < 0$. The hypothesis $\int_a^
b g(t)\,dt = 0$ for every $a, b$ implies, in particular, that
$$
G(x) = \int_0^x g(t)\,dt = 0
$$
for every $x$. Hence $G \equiv 0$.

By the **Lebesgue Differentiation Theorem**, every locally integrable function $
g$ satisfies
$$
\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{h}\int_{x}^{x+h} g(t)\,dt = g(x)
$$
for almost every $x$. Equivalently, the indefinite integral $G(x) = \int_0^x g(t
)\,dt$ is differentiable at almost every $x$, and
$$
G'(x) = g(x) \quad \text{for a.e. } x.
$$

Since $G \equiv 0$ is identically zero, $G'(x) = 0$ for every $x$. Therefore
$$
g(x) = G'(x) = 0 \quad \text{for a.e. } x.
$$

Thus $g = 0$ almost everywhere. $\blacksquare$
