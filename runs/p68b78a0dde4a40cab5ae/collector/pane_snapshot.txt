Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether a compact metric space with
out isolated points, equipped with a homeomorphism, must admit a strictly positi
ve invariant measure.

**Answer: No, such a measure need not exist.**

**Counterexample.** Let $X = [0,1]$ with the standard metric, and let $f: X \to
X$ be defined by $f(x) = x^2$.

**Verification of hypotheses.**
- $X = [0,1]$ is a compact metric space.
- $X$ has no isolated points: every point is a limit point.
- $f(x) = x^2$ is a homeomorphism of $[0,1]$: it is continuous, strictly increas
ing, $f(0)=0$, $f(1)=1$ (so bijective), with continuous inverse $f^{-1}(y) = \sq
rt{y}$.

**Claim.** The only $f$-invariant Borel probability measures on $[0,1]$ are of t
he form $\mu = (1-p)\,\delta_0 + p\,\delta_1$ for some $p \in [0,1]$. In particu
lar, no $f$-invariant measure is strictly positive.

**Proof of claim.** Let $\mu$ be an $f$-invariant probability measure. Then for
every continuous function $g: [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ and every $n \geq 0$:
$$\int g\,d\mu = \int g \circ f^n\,d\mu = \int g\!\left(x^{2^n}\right) d\mu(x).$
$

Now analyze the pointwise behavior of $g(x^{2^n})$ as $n \to \infty$:
- For $x \in [0,1)$: $x^{2^n} \to 0$, so $g(x^{2^n}) \to g(0)$.
- For $x = 1$: $1^{2^n} = 1$, so $g(1^{2^n}) = g(1)$.

Since $g$ is continuous on the compact set $[0,1]$, it is bounded: $|g(x^{2^n})|
 \leq \|g\|_\infty$. By the Dominated Convergence Theorem:
$$\int g\!\left(x^{2^n}\right) d\mu(x) \;\longrightarrow\; g(0)\,\mu([0,1)) + g(
1)\,\mu(\{1\}) = g(0)(1-p) + g(1)\,p,$$
where $p = \mu(\{1\})$.

Since $\int g\,d\mu = \int g(x^{2^n})\,d\mu$ for all $n$, the left side is const
ant in $n$, so:
$$\int g\,d\mu = g(0)(1-p) + g(1)\,p \quad \text{for all continuous } g.$$

This means $\mu = (1-p)\,\delta_0 + p\,\delta_1$. $\square$

**No strictly positive invariant measure exists.** Every invariant measure $\mu
= (1-p)\,\delta_0 + p\,\delta_1$ satisfies $\mu((0, \tfrac{1}{2})) = 0$, yet $(0
, \tfrac{1}{2})$ is a nonempty open set. Therefore no $f$-invariant measure is s
trictly positive.

**Conclusion.** Under the stated hypotheses (compact metric space without isolat
ed points, $f$ a homeomorphism), a strictly positive $f$-invariant measure does
**not** necessarily exist. The pair $X = [0,1]$, $f(x) = x^2$ provides an explic
it counterexample: the dynamics are gradient-like (all orbits in $(0,1)$ converg
e to $0$ forward and to $1$ backward), forcing every invariant measure to be sup
ported on the fixed-point set $\{0, 1\}$, which is a proper—and in particular no
t all of $X$—subset, making strict positivity impossible.
