## Proof

**Claim.** Yes. On any compact connected Lie group $G$ there exists a metric (in
 the metric-space sense) that induces the underlying topology of $G$.

**Step 1 — $G$ is a smooth manifold whose topology is the underlying topology.**
 By definition a Lie group is a smooth manifold equipped with smooth group opera
tions. The topology of $G$ as a Lie group *is* its manifold topology. Hence it s
uffices to produce a metric inducing the manifold topology.

**Step 2 — Construct a left-invariant Riemannian metric on $G$.** Let $\mathfrak
{g}=T_eG$ be the Lie algebra. Choose any positive-definite inner product $\langl
e\cdot,\cdot\rangle_e$ on $\mathfrak{g}$. For $g\in G$ and tangent vectors $X_g,
Y_g\in T_gG$, set
$$
\langle X_g,Y_g\rangle_g \;=\; \big\langle (dL_{g^{-1}})_g X_g,\;(dL_{g^{-1}})_g
 Y_g\big\rangle_e,
$$
where $L_{g^{-1}}\colon G\to G$ is left translation by $g^{-1}$. Left-invariance
 guarantees this is well-defined and smooth in $g$, so it defines a Riemannian m
etric on $G$. (Compactness is not even needed here; it would additionally allow
averaging over the Haar measure to obtain a *bi-invariant* metric, but left-inva
riance already suffices for what follows.)

**Step 3 — Define the Riemannian distance.** For $p,q\in G$ let
$$
d(p,q)=\inf\big\{\,\mathrm{Length}(\gamma)\;:\;\gamma\colon[0,1]\to G\text{ piec
ewise smooth, }\gamma(0)=p,\;\gamma(1)=q\,\big\}.
$$

**Step 4 — $d$ is a genuine (finite) metric.**

*Finiteness.* Since $G$ is a connected manifold, any two points $p,q$ can be joi
ned by a piecewise smooth curve (connect them by a finite chain of coordinate ch
arts, each chart being path-connected). Hence $d(p,q)<\infty$. **This is exactly
 where connectedness is used**: without it, points in distinct components would
have $d(p,q)=\infty$, giving only an *extended* metric.

*Symmetry.* The Riemannian metric is symmetric, so reversing a curve preserves i
ts length; thus $d(p,q)=d(q,p)$.

*Triangle inequality.* If $\gamma_1$ joins $p$ to $r$ and $\gamma_2$ joins $r$ t
o $q$, the concatenation joins $p$ to $q$ with length $\mathrm{Length}(\gamma_1)
+\mathrm{Length}(\gamma_2)$. Taking infima gives $d(p,q)\le d(p,r)+d(r,q)$.

*Non-degeneracy ($d(p,q)=0\Rightarrow p=q$).* Around any point $g\in G$ the Riem
annian exponential map $\exp_g$ is a diffeomorphism from a small ball $B(0,\vare
psilon)\subset T_gG$ onto a neighborhood $U$ of $g$. On $U$ the Riemannian dista
nce satisfies $d(g,h)=\|\exp_g^{-1}(h)\|$ for $h$ near $g$, which is $0$ only wh
en $h=g$. Hence $d(p,q)=0$ forces $p=q$.

**Step 5 — $d$ induces the manifold topology.** In any coordinate neighborhood t
he Riemannian metric tensor is represented by a positive-definite matrix varying
 smoothly, so on a compactly contained sub-neighborhood it is uniformly bi-Lipsc
hitz equivalent to the Euclidean metric of the chart. Therefore the $d$-balls an
d the coordinate (manifold) neighborhoods generate the same topology. Since $G$
is covered by such neighborhoods, the metric topology of $d$ coincides with the
manifold topology.

**Step 6 — Conclusion.** The manifold topology is the underlying topology of $G$
, and $d$ induces exactly this topology. Therefore a metric on $G$ inducing the
underlying topology exists. $\blacksquare$
