## Derivation of the Euler–Lagrange Equation

We seek a function $u(x)$ that minimizes the functional

$$J[u] = \int_{a}^{b} f(u, u', x)\, dx,$$

where $u' = du/dx$, subject to fixed boundary conditions $u(a) = A$, $u(b) = B$.

### Step 1: Variational setup

Let $u(x)$ be a minimizer, and let $\eta(x)$ be an arbitrary smooth test functio
n satisfying $\eta(a) = \eta(b) = 0$ (so that boundary conditions are preserved)
. Define the one-parameter family of admissible functions

$$u_\varepsilon(x) = u(x) + \varepsilon\,\eta(x).$$

Since $u$ is a minimizer, the function

$$\Phi(\varepsilon) = J[u_\varepsilon] = \int_a^b f\!\bigl(u + \varepsilon\eta,\
; u' + \varepsilon\eta',\; x\bigr)\, dx$$

has a minimum at $\varepsilon = 0$, hence

$$\Phi'(0) = 0.$$

### Step 2: Differentiate under the integral

Differentiating under the integral sign (justified by the smoothness of $f$ and
compactness of $[a,b]$):

$$\Phi'(\varepsilon) = \int_a^b \left[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\,\eta \;+\;
 \frac{\partial f}{\partial u'}\,\eta' \right] dx,$$

where the partial derivatives are evaluated at $(u_\varepsilon, u_\varepsilon',
x)$. Setting $\varepsilon = 0$:

$$\Phi'(0) = \int_a^b \left[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\,\eta \;+\; \frac{\pa
rtial f}{\partial u'}\,\eta' \right] dx = 0 \quad \text{for all admissible } \et
a.$$

### Step 3: Integrate by parts

The second term involves $\eta'$. Integrate it by parts:

$$\int_a^b \frac{\partial f}{\partial u'}\,\eta'\, dx = \left[ \frac{\partial f}
{\partial u'}\,\eta \right]_a^b - \int_a^b \frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}
{\partial u'}\right) \eta\, dx.$$

Because $\eta(a) = \eta(b) = 0$, the boundary term vanishes:

$$\left[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial u'}\,\eta \right]_a^b = 0.$$

Substituting back:

$$\Phi'(0) = \int_a^b \left[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial u} - \frac{d}{dx}\!\left
(\frac{\partial f}{\partial u'}\right) \right] \eta\, dx = 0 \quad \text{for all
 admissible } \eta.$$

### Step 4: Apply the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations

**Lemma (du Bois-Reymond).** If a continuous function $g(x)$ on $[a,b]$ satisfie
s

$$\int_a^b g(x)\,\eta(x)\, dx = 0$$

for every smooth $\eta$ with $\eta(a)=\eta(b)=0$, then $g(x) \equiv 0$ on $[a,b]
$.

*Proof of lemma.* Suppose for contradiction that $g(x_0) \neq 0$ at some interio
r point $x_0$; by continuity $g$ keeps a fixed sign on some subinterval $[c,d] \
subset (a,b)$. Choose a nonnegative bump function $\eta$ supported in $[c,d]$ wi
th $\eta(x_0) > 0$. Then the integral has the sign of $g(x_0)$, contradicting th
at it equals zero. Hence $g \equiv 0$. $\square$

Applying the lemma with

$$g(x) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial u} - \frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\
partial u'}\right),$$

we conclude:

### Result: the Euler–Lagrange equation

$$\boxed{\;\frac{\partial f}{\partial u} - \frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}
{\partial u'}\right) = 0\;}$$

or equivalently,

$$\frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial u'}\right) = \frac{\partial f}{
\partial u}.$$

### Remarks on the total derivative

The total derivative $d/dx$ acts on $\partial f/\partial u'$ as a function of $(
u(x), u'(x), x)$, so by the chain rule it expands to

$$\frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial u'}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 f
}{\partial x\,\partial u'} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial u\,\partial u'}\,u' +
\frac{\partial^2 f}{(\partial u')^2}\,u'',$$

giving the expanded (second-order) form

$$\frac{\partial^2 f}{(\partial u')^2}\,u'' + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial u\,\p
artial u'}\,u' + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial u'} - \frac{\partial f
}{\partial u} = 0.$$

This is a second-order ODE for $u(x)$, supplemented by the boundary conditions $
u(a)=A$, $u(b)=B$.

### Summary

The necessary condition for $u$ to be a stationary point (in particular, a minim
izer) of $J[u]$ is obtained by: (1) perturbing $u$ by an admissible variation $\
varepsilon\eta$, (2) requiring the first variation $\delta J = \Phi'(0)$ to vani
sh, (3) transferring the derivative off $\eta$ via integration by parts (the bou
ndary term vanishing due to fixed endpoints), and (4) invoking the fundamental l
emma to conclude that the integrand coefficient of the arbitrary $\eta$ must van
ish identically. This yields the Euler–Lagrange equation.
