## Proof

**Notation.** Let $s(n)$ denote the sum of decimal digits of $n$. Let $A(n) = \#
\{m \leq n : s(m) \text{ is prime}\}$. We prove $\lim_{n\to\infty} A(n)/n = 0$.

---

### Step 1: Bounding the maximum number of $j$-digit numbers with a fixed digit
sum

For $j \geq 1$, let $N_j(t)$ denote the number of $j$-digit positive integers (i
.e., integers in $[10^{j-1}, 10^j)$) with digit sum equal to $t$. We claim:

$$\max_t\, N_j(t) = O\!\left(\frac{10^{j-1}}{\sqrt{j}}\right). \tag{1}$$

A $j$-digit number has first digit $d_1 \in \{1,\dots,9\}$ and remaining digits
$d_2,\dots,d_j \in \{0,\dots,9\}$. Writing $c_m(u)$ for the number of tuples in
$\{0,\dots,9\}^m$ with sum $u$, we have:

$$N_j(t) = \sum_{d_1=1}^{9} c_{j-1}(t - d_1).$$

Now $c_m(u) = 10^m \cdot \Pr(S_m = u)$, where $S_m$ is the sum of $m$ i.i.d. ran
dom variables uniformly distributed on $\{0,1,\dots,9\}$. This distribution has
mean $\mu = 9/2$ and variance $\sigma^2 = 99/12 = 33/4$, and is aperiodic (the s
upport contains both 0 and 1). By the **Local Central Limit Theorem** for lattic
e distributions,

$$\Pr(S_m = u) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi m}}\, e^{-(u - m\mu)^2/(2m\sigma^2)}
+ o(1/\sqrt{m}),$$

uniformly in $u$. In particular, $\max_u \Pr(S_m = u) \leq C/\sqrt{m}$ for an ab
solute constant $C$ and all $m \geq 1$. Therefore:

$$\max_u\, c_m(u) = O\!\left(\frac{10^m}{\sqrt{m}}\right),$$

and consequently $N_j(t) \leq 9 \cdot O(10^{j-1}/\sqrt{j}) = O(10^{j-1}/\sqrt{j}
)$, establishing (1).

---

### Step 2: Bounding the count of $j$-digit numbers with prime digit sum

The digit sum of a $j$-digit number lies in $\{1, 2, \dots, 9j\}$. By **Chebyshe
v's theorem**, the prime counting function satisfies $\pi(x) = O(x / \ln x)$. Th
us the number of primes in $\{1, \dots, 9j\}$ is $O(j / \ln j)$.

Using (1), the number of $j$-digit numbers with prime digit sum is:

$$\sum_{\substack{p \leq 9j \\ p \text{ prime}}} N_j(p) \;\leq\; \pi(9j) \cdot \
max_t N_j(t) \;=\; O\!\left(\frac{j}{\ln j}\right) \cdot O\!\left(\frac{10^{j-1}
}{\sqrt{j}}\right) = O\!\left(\frac{10^{j-1}\,\sqrt{j}}{\ln j}\right). \tag{2}$$

---

### Step 3: Bounding $A(10^k)$

Since every positive integer $\leq 10^k$ has at most $k$ digits:

$$A(10^k) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \sum_{\substack{p \text{ prime}} N_j(p) \;\leq\; \sum
_{j=2}^{k} O\!\left(\frac{10^{j-1}\sqrt{j}}{\ln j}\right) + O(1),$$

where the $O(1)$ accounts for $j=1$ (there are 4 one-digit primes: 2, 3, 5, 7).

The ratio of consecutive terms in the sum satisfies:

$$\frac{10^{j}\sqrt{j+1}/\ln(j+1)}{10^{j-1}\sqrt{j}/\ln j} = 10 \cdot \sqrt{\fra
c{j+1}{j}} \cdot \frac{\ln j}{\ln(j+1)} \;\geq\; 5$$

for all sufficiently large $j$. Hence the sum is dominated by its last term:

$$A(10^k) = O\!\left(\frac{10^{k-1}\sqrt{k}}{\ln k}\right). \tag{3}$$

---

### Step 4: The density tends to zero

**Along powers of 10:** From (3),

$$\frac{A(10^k)}{10^k} = O\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{k}}{\ln k}\right) \longrightarrow
0 \quad \text{as } k \to \infty. \tag{4}$$

**For general $n$:** Given any $n \geq 10$, let $k$ be such that $10^{k-1} \leq
n < 10^k$. Then $A(n) \leq A(10^k)$ and $n \geq 10^{k-1}$, so:

$$\frac{A(n)}{n} \;\leq\; \frac{A(10^k)}{10^{k-1}} \;=\; O\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{k}
}{\ln k}\right) \longrightarrow 0$$

as $k \to \infty$ (equivalently, as $n \to \infty$).

---

### Conclusion

We have shown that for every $n \geq 10$,

$$\frac{A(n)}{n} = O\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{k}}{\ln k}\right) \quad \text{where } k
= \lfloor \log_{10} n \rfloor + 1,$$

and $\sqrt{k}/\ln k \to 0$ as $k \to \infty$. Therefore:

$$\boxed{\lim_{n \to +\infty} \frac{A(n)}{n} = 0.}$$

The set of natural numbers whose digit sum is prime has **zero natural density**
. $\blacksquare$
