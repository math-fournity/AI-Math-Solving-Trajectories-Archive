## Proof

**Notation and setup.** Since at least one of $\mu, \nu$ is finite, $\lambda = \
mu - \nu$ is a well-defined signed measure (no $\infty - \infty$ ambiguity on an
y measurable set). Let $X = P \cup N$ be a Hahn decomposition of $\lambda$: $\la
mbda \geq 0$ on $P$, $\lambda \leq 0$ on $N$.

**Key consequence of $\lambda$ being a signed measure.** For every measurable $E
$, $\lambda(E) = \mu(E) - \nu(E)$ is defined, so we never have $\mu(E) = \nu(E)
= +\infty$. On $P$ we have $\mu \geq \nu$; if $\nu(P) = +\infty$ then $\mu(P) =
+\infty$ (since $\mu \geq \nu$), contradicting definability of $\lambda(P)$. Hen
ce
$$\nu(P) < +\infty \quad\text{and similarly}\quad \mu(N) < +\infty.$$

**Definitions of the two sides.** Since $f \geq 0$:
- $\int_X f\,d\mu, \int_X f\,d\nu \in [0,+\infty]$. The **RHS** $\int_X f\,d\mu
- \int_X f\,d\nu$ exists $\iff$ not both are $+\infty$.
- The **LHS** is $\int_X f\,d\lambda = \underbrace{\int_P f\,d\lambda}_{=:A} - \
underbrace{\int_N f\,d(-\lambda)}_{=:B}$, where $A, B \in [0,+\infty]$ are integ
rals against *positive* measures ($\lambda|_P$ and $(-\lambda)|_N$), hence alway
s well-defined. The LHS exists $\iff$ not both $A = +\infty$ and $B = +\infty$.

### The RHS existing implies the LHS existing (reverse impossible)

**Claim.** $A = +\infty \implies \int_X f\,d\mu = +\infty$, and $B = +\infty \im
plies \int_X f\,d\nu = +\infty$.

*Proof of claim.* Suppose $\int_X f\,d\mu < +\infty$. Then $\int_P f\,d\mu < +\i
nfty$ and $\int_N f\,d\mu < +\infty$. Since $\nu \leq \mu$ on $P$, $\int_P f\,d\
nu \leq \int_P f\,d\mu < +\infty$. With $\int_P f\,d\mu$ and $\int_P f\,d\nu$ bo
th finite, the standard identity $\int_P f\,d\lambda = \int_P f\,d\mu - \int_P f
\,d\nu$ holds, giving $A \leq \int_P f\,d\mu < +\infty$. Contrapositive: $A = +\
infty \implies \int_X f\,d\mu = +\infty$. The argument for $B$ is symmetric (usi
ng $\mu(N) < +\infty$ and $\mu \leq \nu$ on $N$). $\square$

Consequently, $A = B = +\infty \implies \int_X f\,d\mu = \int_X f\,d\nu = +\inft
y$, i.e., **if the LHS fails to exist, the RHS also fails to exist.** Contraposi
tively:

> **If the RHS exists, then the LHS exists.**

So the two sides cannot fail "in the RHS-only" direction.

### The LHS can exist while the RHS does not (the example)

Take:
- $X = \{a\}$, $\mathfrak{A} = \{\varnothing, X\}$,
- $\mu(X) = \nu(X) = 1$ (both finite positive measures),
- $\lambda = \mu - \nu$: the **zero** signed measure, $\lambda(X) = 0$,
- $f(a) = +\infty$ (non-negative, extended real-valued, unbounded).

**LHS:** $\displaystyle\int_X f\,d\lambda = f(a)\cdot\lambda(X) = (+\infty)\cdot
 0 = 0.$ This exists (using the measure-theoretic convention $+\infty \cdot 0 =
0$; equivalently, $f = +\infty$ only on a set of $\lambda$-measure $0$).

**RHS:** $\displaystyle\int_X f\,d\mu - \int_X f\,d\nu = \bigl(f(a)\cdot 1\bigr)
 - \bigl(f(a)\cdot 1\bigr) = (+\infty) - (+\infty)$, which is **undefined**.

Thus the LHS exists (equals $0$) while the RHS does not.

### Conclusion

**Yes, it is possible** for exactly one side to exist. The situation is *asymmet
ric*:

- If $\int_X f\,d\mu - \int_X f\,d\nu$ exists, then $\int_X f\,d\lambda$ necessa
rily exists (proven above).
- But $\int_X f\,d\lambda$ can exist while $\int_X f\,d\mu - \int_X f\,d\nu$ doe
s not, as the example shows: the "cancellation" $\mu = \nu$ makes $\lambda = 0$
so $\int f\,d\lambda = 0$, yet both $\int f\,d\mu$ and $\int f\,d\nu$ are $+\inf
ty$ (because $f = +\infty$ on a set of positive $\mu$- and $\nu$-measure), yield
ing the indeterminate $\infty - \infty$ on the RHS.

The root cause: the signed-measure integral $\int f\,d\lambda$ "sees" the *net*
measure $\lambda = \mu - \nu$ (which can vanish by cancellation), whereas the RH
S computes the two integrals separately and only then subtracts—so cancellation
at the measure level is lost, and $\infty - \infty$ can appear.
