Yes, such a ring exists. Here is the proof.

---

**Theorem.** There exists a finite non-commutative local ring $R$ with identity
such that $J(R)$ is simple as a left $R$-module.

**Construction.** Let $q = p^{n}$ with $n \geq 2$, and let $\sigma \in \operator
name{Aut}(\mathbb{F}_{q})$ be the nontrivial Frobenius automorphism $\sigma(x) =
 x^{p}$. As an additive group, set
\[
R = \mathbb{F}_{q} \oplus \mathbb{F}_{q},
\]
and define multiplication by
\[
(\alpha,\, j)\cdot(\beta,\, k) \;=\; \bigl(\alpha\beta,\;\; \alpha k + \sigma(\b
eta)\,j\bigr),
\]
where the products $\alpha k$ and $\sigma(\beta)\,j$ use the field multiplicatio
n of $\mathbb{F}_{q}$ (identifying the second summand with $\mathbb{F}_{q}$).

Let $J := \{0\}\oplus \mathbb{F}_{q}$.

**Verification.**

*Associativity.* Compute both sides of $((\alpha,j)(\beta,k))(\gamma,l)=(\alpha,
j)((\beta,k)(\gamma,l))$:
\[
\text{LHS}=(\alpha\beta\gamma,\;\alpha\beta\,l+\sigma(\gamma)\alpha\,k+\sigma(\g
amma)\sigma(\beta)\,j),
\]
\[
\text{RHS}=(\alpha\beta\gamma,\;\alpha\beta\,l+\alpha\,\sigma(\gamma)\,k+\sigma(
\beta\gamma)\,j).
\]
Since $\mathbb{F}_{q}$ is commutative, $\sigma(\gamma)\alpha=\alpha\,\sigma(\gam
ma)$, and since $\sigma$ is a field automorphism, $\sigma(\gamma)\sigma(\beta)=\
sigma(\beta\gamma)$. Hence LHS $=$ RHS.

*Identity.* $(1,0)$ is a two-sided identity since $\sigma(1)=1$.

*$J^{2}=0$.* $(0,j)(0,k)=(0,\,0)$, so $J^{2}=0$.

*Units and locality.* For $\alpha\neq 0$, the element $(\alpha,j)$ is a unit: it
s inverse is
\[
(\alpha,j)^{-1}=\bigl(\alpha^{-1},\;-\alpha^{-1}\sigma(\alpha^{-1})\,j\bigr),
\]
as verified by direct computation using $\sigma(\alpha)\,\alpha^{-1}\sigma(\alph
a^{-1})=\alpha^{-1}\sigma(\alpha\alpha^{-1})=\alpha^{-1}$. Thus the non-units ar
e exactly $J=\{0\}\oplus\mathbb{F}_{q}$, so $J(R)=J$ and $R/J\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}
$ is a field. Hence $R$ is local with unique maximal ideal $J$.

*$J$ is simple as a left $R$-module.* Since $J^{2}=0$, the left action of $R$ on
 $J$ factors through $R/J\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}$. Concretely, $(\alpha,j)\cdot(0,k)
=(0,\alpha k)$, so $J$ is a one-dimensional $\mathbb{F}_{q}$-vector space under
the $R$-action. Its $R$-submodules are precisely its $\mathbb{F}_{q}$-subspaces,
 so $J$ is simple as a left $R$-module.

*Non-commutativity.* Compare
\[
(\alpha,0)(0,k)=(0,\alpha k),\qquad (0,k)(\alpha,0)=(0,\sigma(\alpha)\,k).
\]
Choosing $\alpha$ with $\sigma(\alpha)\neq\alpha$ (which exists since $\sigma$ i
s nontrivial, as $n\geq 2$) and $k\neq 0$ gives $(\alpha,0)(0,k)\neq(0,k)(\alpha
,0)$. Thus $R$ is non-commutative.

*Finiteness.* $|R|=q^{2}$, finite.

**Concrete instance.** Taking $q=4$ (so $p=2$, $n=2$, $\sigma(x)=x^{2}$) yields
a ring of order $16$ that is finite, non-commutative, local, unital, with $J(R)$
 simple as a left module. $\square$
