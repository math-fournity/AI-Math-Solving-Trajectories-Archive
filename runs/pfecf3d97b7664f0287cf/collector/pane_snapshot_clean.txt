Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether a bijection $f: \mathbb{Z}
\to \mathbb{Z}^2$ can be extended to a continuous $F: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^
2$.

**Answer: Yes, such a bijection exists.**

In fact, I'll show that *any* bijection $f: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}^2$ can be
extended to a continuous map $F: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^2$.

---

**Proof.**

**Step 1 (Existence of a bijection).** Both $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}^2$ are
countably infinite, so there exists a bijection $f: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}^2$
.

**Step 2 (Construction of the extension).** Given any bijection $f: \mathbb{Z} \
to \mathbb{Z}^2$, define $F: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^2$ by linear interpolatio
n on each unit interval. Specifically, for each $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $t \in [n
, n+1]$, set:

$$F(t) = (n + 1 - t)\, f(n) + (t - n)\, f(n+1).$$

This is the convex combination of $f(n)$ and $f(n+1)$ with weight $(t-n) \in [0,
1]$, so $F(t)$ lies in $\mathbb{R}^2$ (as a linear interpolation of two points i
n $\mathbb{Z}^2 \subset \mathbb{R}^2$).

**Step 3 (Well-definedness at integers).** At $t = n$, the formula on $[n-1, n]$
 gives:
$$F(n) = (n - n)\, f(n-1) + (n - (n-1))\, f(n) = f(n).$$
The formula on $[n, n+1]$ gives:
$$F(n) = (n+1-n)\, f(n) + (n-n)\, f(n+1) = f(n).$$
Both agree, so $F$ is well-defined and $F(n) = f(n)$ for every $n \in \mathbb{Z}
$.

**Step 4 (Continuity).**
- On each open interval $(n, n+1)$, $F$ is an affine function of $t$ (hence cont
inuous).
- At each integer $n$: as $t \to n^-$ (within $[n-1,n]$), $F(t) \to f(n)$ by con
tinuity of the affine map on $[n-1,n]$. As $t \to n^+$ (within $[n,n+1]$), $F(t)
 \to f(n)$ by continuity of the affine map on $[n,n+1]$. Both one-sided limits e
qual $F(n) = f(n)$, so $F$ is continuous at $n$.

Since $F$ is continuous at every point of $\mathbb{R}$, it is continuous on $\ma
thbb{R}$.

**Step 5 (Conclusion).** $F: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^2$ is continuous and $F|_
{\mathbb{Z}} = f$, which is a bijection from $\mathbb{Z}$ to $\mathbb{Z}^2$. $\b
lacksquare$

---

**Remark.** The key observation is that $\mathbb{Z}$ is a closed discrete subset
 of $\mathbb{R}$, and the complement $\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Z}$ is a disj
oint union of open intervals $(n, n+1)$. Any function defined on $\mathbb{Z}$ wi
th values in a path-connected space (here $\mathbb{R}^2$) can be extended contin
uously to $\mathbb{R}$ by filling each gap $[n, n+1]$ with a continuous path fro
m $f(n)$ to $f(n+1)$. Linear interpolation provides one such path. No constraint
 on the bijection $f$ is needed — the extension works for *every* bijection.
