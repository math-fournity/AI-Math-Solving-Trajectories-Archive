## Proof

**Claim:** $f \in H^{1/2}[-\pi,\pi]$.

**Key observations:**

**Step 1: $f$ is smooth on $\mathbb{R}$.**

The function $f(x) = \sin(x)/x$ for $x \neq 0$ with $f(0)=1$ extends to an entir
e function on $\mathbb{C}$, since
$$\frac{\sin x}{x} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n x^{2n}}{(2n+1)!},$$
and this power series has infinite radius of convergence. In particular, $f \in
C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$, hence $f \in C^1[-\pi,\pi]$.

**Step 2: $f \in H^1[-\pi,\pi]$.**

Since $f$ is $C^1$ on the compact interval $[-\pi,\pi]$, its classical derivativ
e $f'$ is continuous on $[-\pi,\pi]$, hence bounded, hence $f' \in L^2[-\pi,\pi]
$. Also $f$ itself is continuous on $[-\pi,\pi]$, hence $f \in L^2[-\pi,\pi]$. T
he classical derivative coincides with the weak derivative (since $f$ is $C^1$),
 so:
$$\|f\|_{H^1}^2 = \|f\|_{L^2}^2 + \|f'\|_{L^2}^2 < \infty.$$
Therefore $f \in H^1[-\pi,\pi]$.

**Step 3: Sobolev nesting gives $f \in H^{1/2}[-\pi,\pi]$.**

The Sobolev spaces on a bounded domain satisfy the nesting property: for $0 \leq
 s \leq t$,
$$H^t[-\pi,\pi] \hookrightarrow H^s[-\pi,\pi].$$
This follows directly from the Fourier characterization: $f \in H^t$ means $\sum
_n (1+n^2)^t |c_n|^2 < \infty$, which implies $\sum_n (1+n^2)^s |c_n|^2 \leq \su
m_n (1+n^2)^t |c_n|^2 < \infty$ when $s \leq t$.

Taking $s = 1/2$ and $t = 1$:
$$H^1[-\pi,\pi] \hookrightarrow H^{1/2}[-\pi,\pi].$$

**Conclusion.**

Since $f \in H^1[-\pi,\pi]$ and $H^1[-\pi,\pi] \subset H^{1/2}[-\pi,\pi]$, we co
nclude:
$$\boxed{f \in H^{1/2}[-\pi,\pi].}$$

**Remark.** In fact, $f$ being $C^\infty$ on $[-\pi,\pi]$ implies $f \in H^s[-\p
i,\pi]$ for every $s \geq 0$. The only obstruction to higher regularity in the p
eriodic Fourier characterization comes from the mismatch $f'(\pi) = -1/\pi \neq
1/\pi = f'(-\pi)$, which limits the periodic extension's smoothness, but this st
ill yields $f \in H^s$ for all $s < 3/2$ via Fourier coefficients decaying as $|
c_n| \sim 1/n^2$—well beyond the $s = 1/2$ threshold required here.
