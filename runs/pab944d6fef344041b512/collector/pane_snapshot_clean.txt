## Analysis of the singularity structure

I examine each factor at $z=-2$.

**Denominator.** $(2+z^3)^2$. Evaluating at $z=-2$:
$$2+(-2)^3 = 2-8 = -6 \neq 0, \qquad (2+z^3)^2\big|_{z=-2} = (-6)^2 = 36 \neq 0.
$$
The roots of $2+z^3=0$ are the three cube roots of $-2$, namely $z = -\sqrt[3]{2
},\; \sqrt[3]{2}\,e^{i\pi/3},\; \sqrt[3]{2}\,e^{-i\pi/3}$ (real root $\approx -1

minator.

**Factor $(z-1)^{1/3}$.** This is a multi-valued function with a single branch p
oint at $z=1$ (a branch point of order $1/3$). The point $z=-2$ is at distance $
3$ from the branch point. Choosing any branch cut from $z=1$ to $\infty$ that av
oids a neighborhood of $z=-2$ (e.g. a ray from $1$ toward $+\infty$ along the re
al axis), the chosen branch of $(z-1)^{1/3}$ is **single-valued and holomorphic*
* in a disk around $z=-2$.

**Factor $\sin z$.** Entire; holomorphic everywhere, in particular at $z=-2$.

## Conclusion: $z=-2$ is a regular point

With a suitable branch choice, all three factors are holomorphic in a neighborho
od of $z=-2$, and the denominator is nonzero there. Therefore

$$f(z) = \frac{(z-1)^{1/3}\sin z}{(2+z^3)^2}$$

is **holomorphic at $z=-2$**; equivalently, $z=-2$ is a regular (non-singular) p
oint of $f$.

The Laurent expansion of $f$ about $z=-2$ therefore contains **no principal part
**; its Laurent series reduces to a Taylor series. The residue — the coefficient
 of $(z+2)^{-1}$ — is consequently

$$\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_{z=-2} f(z) = 0.}$$
