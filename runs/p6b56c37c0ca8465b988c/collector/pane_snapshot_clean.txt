Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether a continuous (bounded) line
ar operator $T$ on a Hilbert space $H$ satisfying $(Tx, x) \leq k(x,x)$ with $k
< 0$ must be injective.

**Answer: Yes, $T$ is necessarily injective.**

**Proof.**

The condition $(Tx, x) \leq k(x, x)$ holds for all $x \in H$, with $k < 0$.

Suppose, for contradiction, that $T$ is not injective. Then there exists $x_0 \i
n H$ with $x_0 \neq 0$ such that $Tx_0 = 0$.

Evaluating the given inequality at $x = x_0$:

$$(Tx_0, x_0) \leq k(x_0, x_0).$$

Since $Tx_0 = 0$, the left-hand side is $(0, x_0) = 0$. The right-hand side is $
k\|x_0\|^2$.

Since $x_0 \neq 0$, we have $\|x_0\|^2 > 0$, and since $k < 0$, we get:

$$k\|x_0\|^2 < 0.$$

So the inequality becomes:

$$0 \leq k\|x_0\|^2 < 0,$$

which is a contradiction. Therefore, no such nonzero $x_0$ exists, meaning $\ker
(T) = \{0\}$.

Hence $T$ is injective. $\blacksquare$

**Remark on the setting.** In a complex Hilbert space, the inequality $(Tx,x) \l
eq k(x,x)$ implicitly requires $(Tx,x)$ to be real for all $x$ (since it is boun
ded above by a real number), which forces $T$ to be self-adjoint. In a real Hilb
ert space, no such issue arises. In either case, the argument above applies unch
anged: the key point is that $(Tx,x) = 0$ whenever $Tx = 0$, and this is incompa
tible with the strictly negative upper bound $k\|x\|^2 < 0$ for $x \neq 0$.
