Yes, such a function exists. The standard example is **Thomae's function**.

## Proof

Define $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ by

$$
f(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{q}, & x=\dfrac{p}{q}\text{ with }p\in\mathbb{Z},\ q\
in\mathbb{Z}_{>0},\ \gcd(p,q)=1,\\[6pt] 0, & x\notin\mathbb{Q}.\end{cases}
$$

We prove $f$ is continuous at every irrational and discontinuous at every ration
al.

---

### Discontinuity at every rational

Let $x_0=p/q\in\mathbb{Q}$ (in lowest terms). Then $f(x_0)=1/q>0$. Since the irr
ationals are dense in $\mathbb{R}$, there is a sequence $(x_n)$ of irrationals w
ith $x_n\to x_0$. For every $n$, $f(x_n)=0$, so

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(x_n)=0\neq \frac{1}{q}=f(x_0).
$$

Hence $f$ is discontinuous at $x_0$. $\checkmark$

---

### Continuity at every irrational

Let $x_0$ be irrational, so $f(x_0)=0$. Let $\varepsilon>0$. We seek $\delta>0$
such that $|x-x_0|<\delta\Rightarrow |f(x)|<\varepsilon$.

Choose $N\in\mathbb{N}$ with $1/N<\varepsilon$. Consider

$$
S=\Bigl\{\frac{p}{q}\in(x_0-1,\,x_0+1):p\in\mathbb{Z},\ 1\le q\le N,\ \gcd(p,q)=
1\Bigr\}.
$$

For each fixed $q$, only finitely many integers $p$ satisfy $p/q\in(x_0-1,x_0+1)
$; summing over the finitely many $q\le N$, the set $S$ is **finite**. Since $x_
0$ is irrational, $x_0\notin S$, so

$$
\delta:=\min\Bigl(1,\ \min_{s\in S}|x_0-s|\Bigr)>0.
$$

Now suppose $|x-x_0|<\delta$.

- **If $x$ is irrational:** $f(x)=0<\varepsilon$.
- **If $x=p/q$ is rational (lowest terms):** Since $\delta\le 1$, we have $x\in(
x_0-1,x_0+1)$. Since $|x-x_0|<\delta\le\min_{s\in S}|x_0-s|$, we have $x\notin S
$, which forces $q>N$. Therefore

$$
f(x)=\frac{1}{q}<\frac{1}{N}<\varepsilon.
$$

In both cases $|f(x)-f(x_0)|=|f(x)|<\varepsilon$. Hence $f$ is continuous at $x_
0$. $\checkmark$

---

### Conclusion

The set of continuity points of $f$ is exactly $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$,
the irrationals. Therefore there **does exist** a function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\math
bb{R}$ continuous only at the irrational numbers.
