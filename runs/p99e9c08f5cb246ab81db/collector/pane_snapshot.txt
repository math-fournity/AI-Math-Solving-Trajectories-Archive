Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether polynomials with polynomial
ly-bounded degree can vanish at exponentially many prescribed points.

**Answer: No**

**Proof:**

The condition $|2m| < 2^n$ is equivalent to $|m| < 2^{n-1}$. The integers $m$ sa
tisfying this are
$$m \in \{-(2^{n-1}-1),\, \ldots,\, -1,\, 0,\, 1,\, \ldots,\, 2^{n-1}-1\},$$
giving exactly $2(2^{n-1}-1)+1 = 2^n - 1$ values.

**Condition 1 requires $p(2m)=0$ for all such $m$.** The points $\{2m : |m|<2^{n
-1}\}$ are $2^n-1$ distinct even integers, so $p$ must have at least $2^n-1$ dis
tinct roots. By the fundamental theorem of algebra, a nonzero polynomial of degr
ee $D$ has at most $D$ roots. Therefore we must have
$$\deg(p) \geq 2^n - 1.$$

But $\deg(p) = cn^{c'}$ for fixed positive constants $c, c'$. Since $2^n - 1$ gr
ows **exponentially** in $n$ while $cn^{c'}$ grows only **polynomially**, for al
l sufficiently large $n$:
$$cn^{c'} < 2^n - 1.$$
This is a contradiction: $p$ cannot have $2^n-1$ roots with degree only $cn^{c'}
$.

**Condition 2 requires $q(2m+1)=0$ for all such $m$.** By the identical argument
, the points $\{2m+1 : |m|<2^{n-1}\}$ are $2^n-1$ distinct odd integers, so $q$
must have at least $2^n-1$ roots, requiring
$$\deg(q) \geq 2^n - 1.$$
But $\deg(q) = dn^{d'}$ is polynomial in $n$, so for sufficiently large $n$, $dn
^{d'} < 2^n - 1$, again a contradiction.

**Conclusion:** For any fixed positive constants $c, c', d, d'$, the required de
gree $cn^{c'}$ (resp. $dn^{d'}$) is polynomial in $n$, while the number of manda
ted roots is $2^n - 1$, which is exponential. Since exponential growth dominates
 polynomial growth, no choice of fixed constants can satisfy the root-count requ
irement for all $n$. Such polynomials do not exist.
