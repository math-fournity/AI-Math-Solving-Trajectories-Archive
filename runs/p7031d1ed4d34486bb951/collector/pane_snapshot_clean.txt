## Answer

**TRUE.** In a Hausdorff topological space, σ-compactness is equivalent to the p
roperty that every closed set is a countable union of compact sets.

## Proof

Let $X$ be a Hausdorff topological space. Define:

- **(A)** $X$ is $\sigma$-compact: $X = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} K_n$ where each $
K_n$ is compact.
- **(B)** Every closed subset of $X$ is a countable union of compact sets.

We prove (A) $\iff$ (B).

### (B) $\Rightarrow$ (A)

The space $X$ is closed in itself. By (B), $X$ is a countable union of compact s
ets, which is exactly (A). $\square$

### (A) $\Rightarrow$ (B)

Assume $X = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} K_n$ with each $K_n$ compact. Let $F \subsete
q X$ be an arbitrary closed set. Then:

$$F = F \cap X = F \cap \left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} K_n\right) = \bigcup_{n=1}^
{\infty} (F \cap K_n).$$

We claim each $F \cap K_n$ is compact, using two standard facts:

1. **In a Hausdorff space, every compact subset is closed.** Since $X$ is Hausdo
rff and $K_n$ is compact, $K_n$ is closed in $X$.
2. **A closed subset of a compact set is compact.** Since $F$ is closed and $K_n
$ is closed (by fact 1), $F \cap K_n$ is closed. As a closed subset of the compa
ct set $K_n$, the set $F \cap K_n$ is compact.

Therefore:

$$F = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} (F \cap K_n)$$

is a countable union of compact sets. Since $F$ was an arbitrary closed set, (B)
 holds. $\square$

### Remark on the role of Hausdorff

The Hausdorff hypothesis is essential for the direction (A) $\Rightarrow$ (B). T
he argument relies on compact sets being closed (fact 1), which holds in Hausdor
ff spaces but fails in general. For instance, in a non-Hausdorff space where a c
ompact set is not closed, intersecting a closed set with it need not yield a clo
sed subset of that compact set, breaking the chain of reasoning.

Both directions are established, so the equivalence holds.
