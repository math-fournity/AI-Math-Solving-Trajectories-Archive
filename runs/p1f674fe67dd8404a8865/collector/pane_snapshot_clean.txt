## Evaluation of $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos^2 x}{x^2}\,dx$

### Setup

Since $\cos^2 x = \operatorname{Re}\!\left(\frac{1+e^{2ix}}{2}\right)$, define

$$f(z)=\frac{1+e^{2iz}}{2z^2},$$

so that $\operatorname{Re} f(x)=\frac{\cos^2 x}{x^2}$ for $x\in\mathbb{R}\setmin
us\{0\}$.

### Singularity analysis at $z=0$

Expanding $e^{2iz}=1+2iz-2z^2+\cdots$ gives

$$1+e^{2iz}=2+2iz-2z^2+\cdots,\qquad f(z)=\frac{1}{z^2}+\frac{i}{z}-1+\cdots$$

This is a **double pole** with $\operatorname{Res}(f,0)=i$. The $\frac{1}{z^2}$
term is the key: it produces a non-integrable singularity, since $\frac{\cos^2 x
}{x^2}\sim\frac{1}{x^2}$ as $x\to 0$ (because $\cos^2 0=1\neq 0$).

### Contour

Use the indented semicircular contour $\Gamma=[-R,-\epsilon]\cup C_\epsilon\cup[
\epsilon,R]\cup C_R$, where:
- $C_\epsilon$: semicircle of radius $\epsilon$ in the upper half-plane, travers
ed **clockwise** ($\theta:\pi\to 0$),
- $C_R$: semicircle of radius $R$ in the upper half-plane, traversed **countercl
ockwise**.

The pole at $0$ is **excluded**, so by Cauchy's theorem:

$$\int_{-R}^{-\epsilon}f(x)\,dx+\int_{C_\epsilon}f(z)\,dz+\int_{\epsilon}^{R}f(x
)\,dx+\int_{C_R}f(z)\,dz=0.$$

### Large arc $C_R$ vanishes

For $z=Re^{i\theta}$, $\theta\in(0,\pi)$: $|e^{2iz}|=e^{-2R\sin\theta}\leq 1$, s
o $|f(z)|\leq\frac{1}{R^2}$. Hence

$$\left|\int_{C_R}f\,dz\right|\leq\frac{\pi R}{R^2}=\frac{\pi}{R}\longrightarrow
 0\quad\text{as }R\to\infty.$$

### Small arc $C_\epsilon$ — extracting the divergence

Parametrize $z=\epsilon e^{i\theta}$, $\theta:\pi\to 0$, $dz=i\epsilon e^{i\thet
a}\,d\theta$:

$$\int_{C_\epsilon}f(z)\,dz=\frac{i}{2\epsilon}\int_{\pi}^{0}\!\bigl(1+e^{2i\eps
ilon e^{i\theta}}\bigr)\,e^{-i\theta}\,d\theta.$$

Using $e^{2i\epsilon e^{i\theta}}=1+2i\epsilon e^{i\theta}+O(\epsilon^2)$:

$$\bigl(1+e^{2i\epsilon e^{i\theta}}\bigr)e^{-i\theta}=2e^{-i\theta}+2i\epsilon+
O(\epsilon^2).$$

Therefore:

$$\int_{C_\epsilon}f\,dz=\frac{i}{2\epsilon}\int_{\pi}^{0}\!\bigl(2e^{-i\theta}+
2i\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2)\bigr)d\theta$$

**First term:** $\displaystyle\frac{i}{\epsilon}\int_{\pi}^{0}e^{-i\theta}\,d\th
eta=\frac{i}{\epsilon}\cdot\frac{e^{-i\theta}}{-i}\bigg|_{\pi}^{0}=\frac{i}{\eps
ilon}\cdot\frac{1-(-1)}{-i}=\frac{i}{\epsilon}\cdot\frac{2}{-i}=-\frac{2}{\epsil
on}.$

**Second term:** $\displaystyle\frac{i}{2\epsilon}\cdot 2i\epsilon\cdot(\theta)\
big|_{\pi}^{0}=i^2(0-\pi)=(-1)(-\pi)=\pi.$

Combining:

$$\int_{C_\epsilon}f(z)\,dz=-\frac{2}{\epsilon}+\pi+O(\epsilon).$$

### Assembling the result

From Cauchy's theorem:

$$\int_{|x|\in[\epsilon,R]}f(x)\,dx=-\int_{C_\epsilon}f\,dz-\int_{C_R}f\,dz\;\lo
ngrightarrow\;\frac{2}{\epsilon}-\pi\quad(R\to\infty,\;\epsilon\to 0^+).$$

Taking real parts:

$$\int_{|x|>\epsilon}\frac{\cos^2 x}{x^2}\,dx=\frac{2}{\epsilon}-\pi+O(\epsilon)
.$$

### Verification via decomposition

Cross-check: $\frac{\cos^2 x}{x^2}=\frac{1}{x^2}-\frac{\sin^2 x}{x^2}$, so

$$\int_{|x|>\epsilon}\frac{\cos^2 x}{x^2}\,dx=\underbrace{\int_{|x|>\epsilon}\fr
ac{dx}{x^2}}_{=\,2/\epsilon}-\underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^2 x}{
x^2}\,dx}_{=\,\pi}+\underbrace{\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\frac{\sin^2 x}{x^2}\,
dx}_{=\,2\epsilon+O(\epsilon^3)}=\frac{2}{\epsilon}-\pi+O(\epsilon).\quad\checkm
ark$$

(The identity $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^2 x}{x^2}\,dx=\pi$ follows from
 the same contour method applied to $\frac{1-e^{2iz}}{2z^2}$, which has only a s
imple pole with residue $-i$ and a removable singularity in its real part.)

### Conclusion

As $\epsilon\to 0^+$, the integral grows without bound:

$$\int_{|x|>\epsilon}\frac{\cos^2 x}{x^2}\,dx\;\sim\;\frac{2}{\epsilon}\;\longri
ghtarrow\;+\infty.$$

$$\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos^2 x}{x^2}\,dx \text{ diverges (to }+\
infty\text{), due to the non-integrable } \frac{1}{x^2}\text{ singularity at }x=
0.}$$

The Hadamard finite part (regularized value) is $-\pi$, obtained by subtracting
the divergent $\frac{2}{\epsilon}$ contribution. The divergence is intrinsic: si
nce $\cos^2 0 = 1 \neq 0$, the integrand behaves like $1/x^2$ near the origin, w
hich is not integrable. (In contrast, $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^2 x}{x^
2}\,dx=\pi$ converges because $\sin^2 x/x^2\to 1$, giving a removable singularit
y.)
