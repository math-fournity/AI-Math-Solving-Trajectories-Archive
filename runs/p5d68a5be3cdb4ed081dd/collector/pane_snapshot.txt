## Proof

**Answer: Yes.** We construct $G_1$ explicitly.

Let $n = |G|$, and let $k$ be the common order of $x$ and $y$.

**Embedding.** Consider the *left regular representation*
$$\phi: G \to \operatorname{Sym}(G) \cong S_n, \qquad \phi(g): h \mapsto gh.$$
This is an injective homomorphism: if $\phi(g) = \phi(g')$, then $ge = g'e$ (tak
ing $h = e$), so $g = g'$. Thus $\phi$ embeds $G$ into $S_n$. Set $G_1 := S_n$.

**Cycle structure of $\phi(x)$.** The permutation $\phi(x)$ acts on $G$ by $h \m
apsto xh$. Its orbits under iteration are the left cosets $\langle x \rangle h =
 \{x^i h : i = 0, \dots, k-1\}$. Each coset has size $k = \operatorname{ord}(x)$
, and there are $n/k$ cosets. Since $x$ has order $k$, each orbit is a cycle of
length exactly $k$. Therefore $\phi(x)$ is a product of $n/k$ disjoint $k$-cycle
s.

**Cycle structure of $\phi(y)$.** By the identical argument with $y$ in place of
 $x$: $\phi(y)$ is a product of $n/k$ disjoint $k$-cycles (the left cosets of $\
langle y \rangle$).

**Conjugacy.** Two permutations in $S_n$ are conjugate if and only if they have
the same cycle type. Since $\phi(x)$ and $\phi(y)$ are both products of $n/k$ di
sjoint $k$-cycles, they have the same cycle type, hence are conjugate in $S_n =
G_1$.

**Conclusion.** The left regular representation $\phi: G \hookrightarrow S_{|G|}
$ is an embedding of the finite group $G$ into the finite group $S_{|G|}$, and $
\phi(x), \phi(y)$ are conjugate in $S_{|G|}$. $\blacksquare$
