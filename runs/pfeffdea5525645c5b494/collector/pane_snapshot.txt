No, it is not possible. Here is the proof.

## Step 1: Identify the surface

We classify closed (compact, without boundary) surfaces and their fundamental gr
oups.

**Orientable** surfaces $\Sigma_g$ (genus $g$):
- $g=0$: $S^2$, $\pi_1 = 0$.
- $g=1$: $T^2$, $\pi_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$.
- $g\ge 2$: $\pi_1 = \langle a_1,b_1,\dots,a_g,b_g \mid [a_1,b_1]\cdots[a_g,b_g]
=1\rangle$, non-abelian.

**Non-orientable** surfaces $N_k$ (connected sum of $k$ copies of $\mathbb{RP}^2
$):
$$\pi_1(N_k) = \langle a_1,\dots,a_k \mid a_1^2 a_2^2 \cdots a_k^2 = 1\rangle.$$
- $k=1$: $\mathbb{RP}^2$, $\pi_1 = \langle a \mid a^2=1\rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}_
2$. ✓
- $k=2$: Klein bottle, $\pi_1 = \langle a,b\mid a^2b^2=1\rangle$, non-abelian.
- $k\ge 2$: non-abelian.

So among **closed** surfaces, the unique one with $\pi_1\cong\mathbb{Z}_2$ is $\
mathbb{RP}^2$.

**Surfaces with boundary.** A compact surface with nonempty boundary deformation
-retracts onto a 1-dimensional CW-complex (a graph), so its $\pi_1$ is a free gr
oup $F_r$. Since $\mathbb{Z}_2$ is not free (it has torsion), no compact surface
 with boundary has $\pi_1\cong\mathbb{Z}_2$.

Hence the surface in question must be $\mathbb{RP}^2$.

## Step 2: A closed embedded surface in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is orientable

**Theorem.** Every compact connected surface $S$ (without boundary) embedded in
$\mathbb{R}^3$ is orientable.

*Proof.* Let $i:S\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^3$ be an embedding. Since $S$ is comp
act, it is closed as a subset of $\mathbb{R}^3$. By the **Jordan–Brouwer separat
ion theorem**, $\mathbb{R}^3\setminus S$ has exactly two connected components: a
 bounded "interior" $U$ and an unbounded "exterior" $V$, and $S = \partial U = \
partial V$.

Define a continuous unit normal field $n:S\to S^2$ by taking, at each $p\in S$,
the unit normal pointing into $V$ (the unbounded component). Concretely, for sma
ll $\varepsilon>0$ and $p\in S$, exactly one of $p+\varepsilon n(p)$, $p-\vareps
ilon n(p)$ lies in $V$; set $n(p)$ to be the direction landing in $V$. This choi
ce varies continuously with $p$ (the two sides are locally separated by $S$, and
 the "exterior" side is selected globally by connectedness of $V$).

A global continuous normal field on a surface is equivalent to an orientation. T
herefore $S$ is orientable. $\square$

## Step 3: Conclusion

$\mathbb{RP}^2$ is **non-orientable** (its orientation double cover is $S^2$, an
d the antipodal deck transformation reverses orientation; equivalently, $\mathbb
{RP}^2$ contains a Möbius band, an embedded one-sided subsurface).

By Step 2, any compact surface embedded in $\mathbb{R}^3$ must be orientable. Si
nce $\mathbb{RP}^2$ is non-orientable, it **cannot** be embedded in $\mathbb{R}^
3$.

(Equivalently, this is consistent with the classical fact that $\mathbb{RP}^2$ a
dmits only *immersions* into $\mathbb{R}^3$ — such as Boy's surface or the cross
-cap — every one of which has self-intersections.)

Therefore, **no**: a surface with fundamental group $\mathbb{Z}_2$ cannot be emb
edded in $\mathbb{R}^3$ without self-intersection.
