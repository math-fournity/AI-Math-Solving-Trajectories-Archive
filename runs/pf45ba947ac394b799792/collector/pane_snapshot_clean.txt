We evaluate

$$x = \frac{n!\,\binom{m}{n}}{m^n}, \qquad m = 52!,\quad n \approx 10^{14}.$$

### Step 1. Simplify the expression

Using $\binom{m}{n} = \frac{m!}{n!\,(m-n)!}$,

$$x = \frac{n! \cdot \frac{m!}{n!\,(m-n)!}}{m^n} = \frac{m!}{(m-n)!\,m^n} = \fra
c{m(m-1)(m-2)\cdots(m-n+1)}{m^n}.$$

This is the familiar "birthday-problem" product — the probability that $n$ draws
 (with replacement) from a set of size $m$ are all distinct:

$$x = \prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(1 - \frac{k}{m}\right).$$

### Step 2. Take logarithms and expand

$$\ln x = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\ln\!\left(1 - \frac{k}{m}\right).$$

Since $k \le n-1 \approx 10^{14}$ and $m = 52! \approx 8.07\times 10^{67}$, the
ratio $k/m \le 10^{14}/(8\times 10^{67}) \approx 1.2\times 10^{-54} \ll 1$. So t
he Taylor expansion $\ln(1-u) = -u - u^2/2 - \cdots$ converges rapidly:

$$\ln x \approx -\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k \;-\; \frac{1}{2m^2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-
1}k^2 \;-\;\cdots$$

### Step 3. Estimate the leading term

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}\approx \frac{n^2}{2},\qquad \sum_{k=0}^{n
-1}k^2 \approx \frac{n^3}{3}.$$

**Leading term:**

$$\frac{n^2}{2m} \approx \frac{(10^{14})^2}{2\cdot 8.07\times 10^{67}} = \frac{1
0^{28}}{1.614\times 10^{68}} \approx 6.2\times 10^{-41}.$$

**Second-order term:**

$$\frac{n^3}{6m^2} \approx \frac{10^{42}}{6\cdot(8.07\times 10^{67})^2} = \frac{
10^{42}}{3.9\times 10^{136}} \approx 2.6\times 10^{-95},$$

which is utterly negligible (as are all higher-order terms).

### Step 4. Conclusion

$$\ln x \approx -6.2\times 10^{-41} \approx 0,$$

hence

$$\boxed{x \approx e^{-6.2\times 10^{-41}} \approx 1 - 6.2\times 10^{-41} \appro
x 1.}$$

The deviation from 1 is of order $10^{-41}$, far below any practical precision.
**The expression is extremely close to 1** (essentially equal to 1), not 0.

**Intuition:** $n \approx 10^{14}$ is astronomically small compared to $m = 52!
\approx 8\times 10^{67}$. With a "universe" of $\sim 10^{67}$ possibilities, dra
wing only $\sim 10^{14}$ samples makes a collision essentially impossible — the
no-collision probability is, for all practical purposes, exactly 1.
