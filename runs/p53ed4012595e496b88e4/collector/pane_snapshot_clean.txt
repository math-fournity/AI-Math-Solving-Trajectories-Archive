**Answer: Yes.** A continuous function on an open set $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ t
hat is uniformly continuous on every bounded subset of $U$ admits a unique conti
nuous extension to $\overline{U}$.

---

**Proof.**

**Step 1 (Cauchy preservation).** We first show:

> *If $(x_k)$ is a Cauchy sequence in $U$, then $(f(x_k))$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb
{R}$.*

Since $(x_k)$ is Cauchy, the set $A=\{x_k:k\ge 1\}$ is a bounded subset of $U$.
By hypothesis $f|_A$ is uniformly continuous. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $\de
lta>0$ so that $|f(a)-f(b)|<\varepsilon$ whenever $a,b\in A$ with $\|a-b\|<\delt
a$. Since $(x_k)$ is Cauchy, there is $N$ with $\|x_m-x_n\|<\delta$ for $m,n\ge
N$; hence $|f(x_m)-f(x_n)|<\varepsilon$. Thus $(f(x_k))$ is Cauchy, and being in
 $\mathbb{R}$ it converges.

**Step 2 (existence of boundary limits).** Let $x\in\partial U$ and let $(x_k)$
be any sequence in $U$ with $x_k\to x$. Then $(x_k)$ is Cauchy in $U$, so by Ste
p 1 the limit $\lim_k f(x_k)$ exists.

We show the limit is independent of the sequence. Let $(x_k),(y_k)$ be two seque
nces in $U$ both converging to $x$. Interleave them: $z_1=x_1,z_2=y_1,z_3=x_2,z_
4=y_2,\dots$ Then $(z_k)$ is Cauchy in $U$ (it converges to $x$), so by Step 1 $
(f(z_k))$ converges. Its two subsequences $(f(x_k))$ and $(f(y_k))$ therefore sh
are the same limit.

Define
$$
g(x)=\lim_{k\to\infty}f(x_k)\quad\text{for any }x_k\to x,\ x_k\in U,
$$
and set $g(x)=f(x)$ for $x\in U$. This is well defined and extends $f$.

**Step 3 ($g$ maps Cauchy sequences in $\overline{U}$ to Cauchy sequences).** Le
t $(w_k)$ be a Cauchy sequence in $\overline{U}$. It is bounded, so $w_k\in B(0,
R)$ for some $R$. Set $U'=U\cap B(0,R+1)$, a bounded subset of $U$; $f$ is unifo
rmly continuous on $U'$.

For each $k$ pick $u_k\in U$ with $\|u_k-w_k\|<1/k$ (if $w_k\in U$ take $u_k=w_k
$; otherwise approximate using $w_k\in\overline{U}$). For large $k$, $u_k\in U'$
. Also $(u_k)$ is Cauchy in $U$ (since $\|u_m-u_n\|\le\|u_m-w_m\|+\|w_m-w_n\|+\|
w_n-u_n\|\to 0$), so by Step 1, $(f(u_k))$ is Cauchy.

We claim $g(w_k)-f(u_k)\to 0$. Fix $\varepsilon>0$ and let $\delta>0$ be the uni
form-continuity modulus of $f$ on $U'$. For each $k$, by definition of $g(w_k)$
as a limit, choose $v_k\in U$ with $\|v_k-w_k\|<\min(1/k,\delta)$ and $|f(v_k)-g
(w_k)|<\varepsilon$. For large $k$, $v_k\in U'$, and
$$
\|v_k-u_k\|\le\|v_k-w_k\|+\|w_k-u_k\|<\tfrac1k+\tfrac1k\to 0,
$$
so eventually $\|v_k-u_k\|<\delta$, giving $|f(v_k)-f(u_k)|<\varepsilon$ by unif
orm continuity on $U'$. Hence
$$
|g(w_k)-f(u_k)|\le|g(w_k)-f(v_k)|+|f(v_k)-f(u_k)|<2\varepsilon
$$
for all large $k$. Since $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, $g(w_k)-f(u_k)\to 0$.

Therefore $(g(w_k))$, being the sum of a Cauchy sequence $(f(u_k))$ and a null s
equence, is itself Cauchy.

**Step 4 (continuity of $g$).** Let $x\in\overline{U}$ and let $w_k\to x$ in $\o
verline{U}$. Form the interleaved sequence $z_1=w_1,z_2=x,z_3=w_2,z_4=x,\dots$;
it is Cauchy in $\overline{U}$ (it converges to $x$). By Step 3, $(g(z_k))$ is C
auchy, hence convergent. Its subsequence $(g(x))$ is constant, so the whole sequ
ence converges to $g(x)$; in particular the subsequence $(g(w_k))$ converges to
$g(x)$. Thus $g$ is continuous at $x$.

Since $x\in\overline{U}$ was arbitrary, $g:\overline{U}\to\mathbb{R}$ is continu
ous, and by construction $g|_U=f$. Uniqueness follows because $U$ is dense in $\
overline{U}$ and a continuous function is determined by its values on a dense su
bset.

Hence $f$ can be continuously extended to $\overline{U}$. $\blacksquare$
