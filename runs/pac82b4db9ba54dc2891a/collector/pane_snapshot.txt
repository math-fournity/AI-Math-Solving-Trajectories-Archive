## Answer

The statement is **true in ZFC** (Zermelo–Fraenkel with the Axiom of Choice) but
 **false in ZF alone**. Below I give both the positive result (ZFC) and the coun
terexample (ZF).

---

### Part 1 — The statement is true in ZFC

**Claim.** In ZFC, every uncountable set $U$ can be written as $U = V \sqcup W$
with $V, W$ both uncountable.

**Proof.** By the Well-Ordering Theorem (equivalent to the Axiom of Choice), $U$
 admits a well-ordering, so $|U| = \kappa$ for some cardinal $\kappa$. Since $U$
 is uncountable, $\kappa \ge \aleph_1$; in particular $\kappa$ is an infinite ca
rdinal.

A standard theorem of ZFC cardinal arithmetic states that for every infinite car
dinal $\kappa$,
$$\kappa + \kappa = \kappa \qquad (\text{equivalently } 2\cdot\kappa = \kappa).$
$
*Brief justification:* $\kappa \le \kappa + \kappa \le \kappa\cdot\kappa = \kapp
a$ (the last equality uses that $\kappa\times\kappa$ is well-orderable of type $
\kappa$ for infinite $\kappa$, a standard consequence of AC), so equality holds
throughout by Cantor–Bernstein.

Since $2\cdot\kappa = \kappa$, there exists a bijection $f : \kappa \to \kappa \
times \{0,1\}$. Fix a well-ordering $U = \{u_\alpha : \alpha < \kappa\}$ and def
ine
$$V = \{u_\alpha : f(\alpha) \in \kappa\times\{0\}\}, \qquad W = \{u_\alpha : f(
\alpha) \in \kappa\times\{1\}\}.$$
Then $V$ and $W$ are disjoint and $V \cup W = U$. Moreover $|V| = |W| = \kappa$
(because $f$ restricts to bijections $\kappa \to \kappa\times\{i\}$ for $i\in\{0
,1\}$). Since $\kappa \ge \aleph_1$, both $V$ and $W$ are uncountable. $\blacksq
uare$

*Remark.* Only a weak fragment of Choice is needed — it suffices that every unco
untable set is Dedekind-infinite (has a countably infinite subset) together with
 the cardinal-arithmetic fact used above; full AC is more than enough.

---

### Part 2 — The statement is false in ZF alone

**Claim.** In ZF (without Choice) the statement fails: there exist models of ZF
containing an uncountable set that cannot be partitioned into two uncountable su
bsets.

**Proof.** Recall that a set $A$ is **amorphous** if $A$ is infinite but cannot
be written as the disjoint union of two infinite subsets. Amorphous sets are con
sistent with ZF: they exist, for instance, in Fraenkel–Mostowski permutation mod
els of ZFA (ZF with atoms), and the existence of amorphous sets in pure ZF is ob
tained by the Jech–Sochor transfer theorem (embedding a bounded fragment of a ZF
A permutation model into a symmetric submodel of a forcing extension of ZF). Hen
ce there is a model $\mathcal M \models \mathrm{ZF}$ containing an amorphous set
 $A$.

We verify that such an $A$ is a counterexample.

1. **$A$ is uncountable.** An amorphous set is infinite by definition. It is *De
dekind-finite*: if it contained a countably infinite subset $\{a_0, a_1, a_2, \d
ots\}$, then $\{a_{2n}:n\in\omega\}$ and $\{a_{2n+1}:n\in\omega\}$ would be two
disjoint infinite subsets, contradicting amorphousness. Being infinite and Dedek
ind-finite, $A$ is neither finite nor in bijection with $\omega$; i.e. $A$ is un
countable.

2. **$A$ cannot be split into two uncountable parts.** Suppose $A = V \sqcup W$
with $V, W$ uncountable. Then $V$ and $W$ are in particular infinite (every unco
untable set is infinite). This partitions the amorphous set $A$ into two infinit
e subsets — a contradiction.

Therefore $A$ is an uncountable set that is not the union of two disjoint uncoun
table subsets. So the statement is not provable in ZF. $\blacksquare$

---

### Conclusion

The proposition "every uncountable set is the disjoint union of two uncountable
subsets" is

- **true** in ZFC (and already follows from modest choice principles ensuring un
countable sets are Dedekind-infinite), and
- **false** in ZF, with amorphous sets providing explicit counterexamples.

Thus the statement is *independent of ZF* and is settled positively exactly by t
he choice principles that rule out amorphous / pathological Dedekind-finite unco
untable sets.
