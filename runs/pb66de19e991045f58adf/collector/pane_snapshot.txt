**Answer: Yes, $A$ must be a scalar multiple of the identity.**

---

**Proof.**

Let $v_1,\dots,v_{n+1}$ be the eigenvectors, with $Av_i=\lambda_i v_i$, and such
 that any $n$ of them are linearly independent.

**Lemma (direct sum of eigenspaces).** *If $\mu_1,\dots,\mu_k$ are distinct eige
nvalues and $w_s\in E_{\mu_s}$, then $w_1+\cdots+w_k=0$ implies every $w_s=0$. C
onsequently, a collection of eigenvectors grouped by distinct eigenvalue is line
arly independent if and only if each group is internally linearly independent.*

*Proof of lemma.* Standard: apply $(A-\mu_2 I)\cdots(A-\mu_k I)$ to the sum to i
solate $w_1$, etc. $\square$

---

**Step 1. Each $v_j$ lies in the span of those $v_i$ ($i\neq j$) sharing its eig
envalue.**

Since any $n$ of the vectors are independent, $\{v_i:i\neq j\}$ is a basis. Writ
e
$$v_j=\sum_{i\neq j}c_i v_i.$$
Applying $A$ and subtracting $\lambda_j$ times the above:
$$0=\sum_{i\neq j}c_i(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)v_i.$$
By linear independence, $c_i(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)=0$ for all $i\neq j$, so $c_i=
0$ whenever $\lambda_i\neq\lambda_j$. Hence
$$v_j\in\operatorname{span}\{v_i:i\neq j,\ \lambda_i=\lambda_j\}.\tag{$\ast$}$$

---

**Step 2. Group by eigenvalue; every group has size $\ge 2$.**

Let $G_s=\{i:\lambda_i=\mu_s\}$ with $|G_s|=m_s$, where $\mu_1,\dots,\mu_k$ are
the distinct eigenvalues, $\sum_s m_s=n+1$.

If some group $G_s$ is a singleton, say $G_s=\{j\}$, then $(\ast)$ gives $v_j\in
\operatorname{span}(\emptyset)=\{0\}$, so $v_j=0$—impossible for an eigenvector.
 Therefore
$$m_s\ge 2\quad\text{for every }s.$$

Moreover, $(\ast)$ shows that within each group, every vector is in the span of
the others, so
$$\operatorname{rank}\{v_i:i\in G_s\}\le m_s-1.\tag{$\dagger$}$$

---

**Step 3. There cannot be two or more groups.**

Suppose $k\ge 2$. Remove a vector $v_j$ from group $G_s$. The remaining $n$ vect
ors are linearly independent. Group them by eigenvalue and apply the Lemma: inde
pendence holds iff each group's remaining vectors are internally independent.

- From $G_s$: $m_s-1$ vectors remain; they must be independent, so $\operatornam
e{rank}\{v_i:i\in G_s\}\ge m_s-1$. Combined with $(\dagger)$: rank $=m_s-1$. (Co
nsistent.)
- From any other group $G_t$ ($t\neq s$): all $m_t$ vectors remain; they must be
 independent, requiring $\operatorname{rank}\{v_i:i\in G_t\}=m_t$.

But $(\dagger)$ gives $\operatorname{rank}\{v_i:i\in G_t\}\le m_t-1<m_t$ (since
$m_t\ge 2$). **Contradiction.**

Therefore $k\ge 2$ is impossible.

---

**Step 4. Conclusion.**

Thus $k=1$: all $n+1$ eigenvectors share a single eigenvalue $\lambda$. Any $n$
of $v_1,\dots,v_{n+1}$ form a basis of the $n$-dimensional space, and $A$ acts a
s $\lambda I$ on each basis vector. Hence $A=\lambda I$, a scalar multiple of th
e identity. $\blacksquare$
