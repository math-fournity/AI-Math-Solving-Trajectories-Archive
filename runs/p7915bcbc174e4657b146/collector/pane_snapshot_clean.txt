I'll solve this directly. The problem asks for the value of $b$ such that a non-
constant integrable $g$ satisfies the integral mean-value identity for all $u<v$
.

**Answer: $b = \tfrac{1}{2}$.**

## Proof

**Step 0 — Answer-leak check.** The problem statement contains no solution, hint
, or formalization revealing the answer. Proceeding.

**Step 1 — Ruling out $b\in\{0,1\}$.**

If $b=1$, then $bu+(1-b)v=u$, and the identity gives $g(u)=\frac1{v-u}\int_u^v g
$ for all $v>u$, i.e. $\int_u^v g=(v-u)g(u)$. Differentiating in $v$ (justified
once $g$ is continuous, established below) yields $g(v)=g(u)$ for all $v>u$, so
$g$ is constant — contradiction.

If $b=0$, then $bu+(1-b)v=v$, giving $\int_u^v g=(v-u)g(v)$; differentiating in
$u$ gives $g(u)=g(v)$ for all $u<v$ — again constant, contradiction.

Hence $b\neq 0,1$, so $1-b\neq 0$ and $b\neq 0$.

**Step 2 — Continuity of $g$.**

Parametrize the identity by $x:=bu+(1-b)v$ and $t:=v-u>0$. Solving,
$$u=x-(1-b)t,\qquad v=x+bt,$$
and the identity becomes, for every $x\in\mathbb R$ and $t>0$,
$$t\,g(x)=\int_{x-(1-b)t}^{x+bt} g(w)\,dw. \tag{$\ast$}$$
The right-hand side is continuous in $(x,t)$ (an $L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}$ function i
ntegrated between moving limits). Fixing any $t_0>0$,
$$g(x)=\frac1{t_0}\int_{x-(1-b)t_0}^{x+bt_0}g(w)\,dw,$$
which is continuous in $x$. So $g$ is continuous.

**Step 3 — A weighted Jensen equation.**

Since $g$ is continuous, apply the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to $(\ast)$ a
nd differentiate in $t$: the left side gives $g(x)$, the right side gives $b\,g(
x+bt)+(1-b)\,g(x-(1-b)t)$. Thus for all $x\in\mathbb R$, $t>0$,
$$g(x)=b\,g(x+bt)+(1-b)\,g(x-(1-b)t). \tag{$\dagger$}$$

Set $U:=x+bt$ and $W:=x-(1-b)t$. Then $x=(1-b)U+bW$ (direct verification), and $
U-W=t>0$. So for all $U>W$,
$$g\bigl((1-b)U+bW\bigr)=b\,g(U)+(1-b)\,g(W). \tag{$\ddagger$}$$
By continuity of $g$, ($\ddagger$) extends to all $U,W\in\mathbb R$.

**Step 4 — Continuous solutions of ($\ddagger$) are affine.**

Let $\lambda:=1-b$ (so $b=1-\lambda$, with $\lambda,1-\lambda\neq 0$). Equation
($\ddagger$) reads
$$g(\lambda U+(1-\lambda)W)=(1-\lambda)\,g(U)+\lambda\,g(W).$$
Define $F(x):=g(x)-g(0)$, so $F(0)=0$. Setting $W=0$ and $U=0$ gives
$$F(\lambda U)=(1-\lambda)F(U),\qquad F((1-\lambda)W)=\lambda F(W).$$
Now substitute $a:=\lambda U$, $c:=(1-\lambda)W$ (so $U=a/\lambda$, $W=c/(1-\lam
bda)$, and these range over all of $\mathbb R$). The main equation becomes
$$F(a+c)=(1-\lambda)F(a/\lambda)+\lambda F(c/(1-\lambda)).$$
Using $F(\lambda z)=(1-\lambda)F(z)\Rightarrow F(a/\lambda)=F(a)/(1-\lambda)$ an
d $F((1-\lambda)z)=\lambda F(z)\Rightarrow F(c/(1-\lambda))=F(c)/\lambda$,
$$F(a+c)=F(a)+F(c)\qquad\forall a,c\in\mathbb R.$$
So $F$ is additive and continuous, hence $F(x)=\alpha x$ for some $\alpha\in\mat
hbb R$. Therefore
$$g(x)=\alpha x+\beta\qquad(\beta:=g(0)),$$
i.e. $g$ is affine. Since $g$ is non-constant, $\alpha\neq 0$.

**Step 5 — Determining $b$.**

Substitute the affine form $g(w)=\alpha w+\beta$ into the original identity. The
 right side is
$$\frac1{v-u}\int_u^v(\alpha w+\beta)\,dw=\alpha\cdot\frac{u+v}{2}+\beta=g\!\lef
t(\frac{u+v}{2}\right).$$
The left side is $g(bu+(1-b)v)=\alpha\bigl(bu+(1-b)v\bigr)+\beta$. Equality for
all $u<v$ requires
$$bu+(1-b)v=\frac{u+v}{2}\quad\forall u<v.$$
Rearranging, $(b-\tfrac12)u+(1-b-\tfrac12)v=0$, i.e. $(b-\tfrac12)(u-v)=0$ for a
ll $u<v$. Since $u\neq v$, we conclude
$$b=\frac12.$$

Conversely, $b=\tfrac12$ works for every non-constant affine $g$ (and indeed the
 argument shows affine functions are the only continuous solutions). $\blacksqua
re$

$$\boxed{b=\tfrac12}$$
