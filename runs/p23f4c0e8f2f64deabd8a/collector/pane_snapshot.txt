## Proof

Let

$$
K_L \;=\; \{\,k\in 2\pi\mathbb Z^{3} : |k|\le \sqrt{\pi}\,L\,\},
$$

and let $N=|K_L|<\infty$ (the cutoff $|k|\le\sqrt\pi L$ makes the set finite). F
ix an ordering $k_1,\dots,k_N$ of $K_L$ and define the $N\times N$ matrix

$$
M_{ij} \;=\; \langle e_{k_i}\,|\,(H_{\mathrm{per}})_{q}\,|\,e_{k_j}\rangle .
$$

**Step 1 — $M$ is Hermitian.** Since $(H_{\mathrm{per}})_{q}$ is a Hamiltonian o
perator it is self-adjoint, $(H_{\mathrm{per}})_{q}^{\dagger}=(H_{\mathrm{per}})
_{q}$. Hence

$$
M_{ij}=\langle e_{k_i}|(H_{\mathrm{per}})_{q}|e_{k_j}\rangle
      =\langle e_{k_j}|(H_{\mathrm{per}})_{q}^{\dagger}|e_{k_i}\rangle^{*}
      =\langle e_{k_j}|(H_{\mathrm{per}})_{q}|e_{k_i}\rangle^{*}
      =M_{ji}^{*},
$$

so $M=M^{\dagger}$: $M$ is a finite-dimensional Hermitian matrix.

**Step 2 — Spectral theorem applies.** $M$ is an $N\times N$ Hermitian matrix ov
er $\mathbb C$. By the finite-dimensional spectral theorem there exist

* a unitary matrix $U\in U(N)$ (so $U^{\dagger}U=I$), and
* a real diagonal matrix $D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_N)$ with
 $\lambda_i\in\mathbb R$,

such that

$$
M \;=\; U\,D\,U^{\dagger}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad M\,U \;=\; U\,D .
$$

The columns $u^{(n)}=(U_{1n},\dots,U_{Nn})^{\top}$ are orthonormal eigenvectors
of $M$ and the $\lambda_n$ are the (real) eigenvalues. This is the diagonalizati
on.

**Step 3 — Physical meaning.** Because $(H_{\mathrm{per}})_{q}$ is periodic, it
commutes with lattice translations and so preserves the quasi-momentum $q$; the
matrix $M$ is the representation of $(H_{\mathrm{per}})_{q}$ in the sector of fi
xed $q$ on the truncated plane-wave basis $\{|e_k\rangle\}_{k\in K_L}$. The eige
nvalues $\lambda_n$ are therefore the band energies $E_n(q)$ (converging to the
true bands as $L\to\infty$), and the eigenstate associated with $\lambda_n$ is r
econstructed from the eigenvector as

$$
|\psi_n(q)\rangle \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} U_{in}\,|e_{k_i}\rangle .
$$

**Step 4 — Concrete diagonalization procedure.** Since $M$ is Hermitian, one use
s a Hermitian eigensolver (not a general one), which is both faster and guarante
ed to return real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors:

1. Assemble $M_{ij}=\langle e_{k_i}|(H_{\mathrm{per}})_{q}|e_{k_j}\rangle$ for $
i,j=1,\dots,N$, exploiting the Hermitian symmetry to store only one triangle.
2. Feed $M$ to a Hermitian eigensolver — e.g. LAPACK routines `zheevd`/`zheevr`/
`zheevx` (complex Hermitian), or in high-level interfaces `numpy.linalg.eigh` /
`scipy.linalg.eigh` / `scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh` for the sparse case, / `Eigen:
:SelfAdjointEigenSolver` — which compute $D$ and $U$ with $M=UDU^{\dagger}$.
3. The output gives the real spectrum $\{\lambda_n\}= \{E_n(q)\}$ and the unitar
y $U$ whose columns are the plane-wave coefficients of the eigenstates.

Because $M$ is Hermitian, the algorithm is numerically stable: the eigenvalues c
ome out real to machine precision and the eigenvectors are orthonormal up to rou
nding, with condition number controlled by the eigenvalue gaps. No complex balan
cing or non-unitary similarity transform is required, which is the precise sense
 in which Hermiticity makes the diagonalization well-posed and efficient.

---

**Conclusion.** The matrix $M_{ij}=\langle e_{k_i}|(H_{\mathrm{per}})_{q}|e_{k_j
}\rangle$ indexed by the finite set $K_L=\{k\in2\pi\mathbb Z^{3}:|k|\le\sqrt\pi
L\}$ is a finite-dimensional Hermitian matrix. By the spectral theorem it is dia
gonalized by a unitary $U$: $M=U\,\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_N)
\,U^{\dagger}$ with real $\lambda_n$. The diagonalization is performed numerical
ly with a Hermitian eigensolver (LAPACK `zheev*` / `numpy.linalg.eigh` / `scipy.
linalg.eigh` / sparse `eigsh`); the eigenvalues are the band energies $E_n(q)$ a
nd the columns of $U$ are the plane-wave coefficients of the corresponding eigen
states $|\psi_n(q)\rangle=\sum_i U_{in}|e_{k_i}\rangle$.
