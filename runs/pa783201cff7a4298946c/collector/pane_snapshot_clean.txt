## Proof

We determine all $a\in\mathbb{R}$ such that
$$f_n(x,y,z)=\frac{1}{n^{2}+x^{a}+2y^{a}+3z^{a}}$$
is (Lebesgue) integrable over $[0,+\infty)^{3}$ for **every** $n\in\mathbb{N}\cu
p\{0\}$.

Since $f_n\ge 0$ on the domain (for $a\neq 0$ the values $0^{a}$ occur only on a
 measure‑zero set and are irrelevant), integrability is equivalent to finiteness
 of
$$I_n(a)=\int_{[0,\infty)^3}\frac{dx\,dy\,dz}{n^{2}+x^{a}+2y^{a}+3z^{a}}.$$

---

### The case $n=0$ rules out every $a$

For $n=0$ the function becomes
$$f_0(x,y,z)=\frac{1}{x^{a}+2y^{a}+3z^{a}}.$$

Set $g(x,y,z)=x^{a}+2y^{a}+3z^{a}$. This is positively homogeneous of degree $a$
:
$$g(\lambda x,\lambda y,\lambda z)=\lambda^{a}g(x,y,z),\qquad \lambda>0.$$

Pass to spherical coordinates in the first octant: $(x,y,z)=r\omega$ with $r\in[
0,\infty)$ and $\omega\in S^{2}_{+}:=\{\omega\in S^{2}:\omega_i\ge 0\}$. Then $d
x\,dy\,dz=r^{2}\,dr\,d\sigma(\omega)$ and $g(r\omega)=r^{a}g(\omega)$, so
$$I_0(a)=\Bigl(\int_{S^{2}_{+}}\frac{d\sigma(\omega)}{g(\omega)}\Bigr)\int_{0}^{
\infty}r^{\,2-a}\,dr.$$

**The angular factor is finite for every $a\neq 0$.** Indeed:
- For $a>0$, $g(\omega)=\omega_1^{a}+2\omega_2^{a}+3\omega_3^{a}>0$ on the compa
ct set $S^{2}_{+}$ (at least one coordinate is positive on the sphere), so $1/g$
 is continuous and bounded.
- For $a<0$, $g(\omega)\to+\infty$ as $\omega$ approaches the boundary of the oc
tant (where some coordinate vanishes), while $g$ stays positive and finite in th
e interior; hence $1/g$ is bounded and tends to $0$ at the boundary, so it is in
tegrable.

Thus for every $a\neq 0$ the finiteness of $I_0(a)$ is governed entirely by the
radial integral
$$\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\,2-a}\,dr=\int_{0}^{1}r^{\,2-a}\,dr+\int_{1}^{\infty}r^{\
,2-a}\,dr.$$

The first term converges iff $2-a>-1$, i.e. $a<3$.
The second term converges iff $2-a<-1$, i.e. $a>3$.

These two conditions are **mutually exclusive**, so
$$\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\,2-a}\,dr=+\infty\quad\text{for every }a\in\mathbb{R}\set
minus\{0\}.$$

For $a=0$ we have $g\equiv 6$, so $f_0\equiv \tfrac16$ and $I_0(0)=\tfrac16\cdot
\operatorname{vol}([0,\infty)^3)=+\infty$.

Consequently
$$I_0(a)=+\infty\qquad\text{for every }a\in\mathbb{R}.$$

So $f_0$ is **never** integrable over $[0,+\infty)^3$, regardless of $a$.

---

### Conclusion

The requirement "integrable for all $n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}$" includes $n=0$, a
nd the case $n=0$ is impossible for every real $a$. Therefore the set of admissi
ble values is empty:

$$\boxed{\,\text{There is no }a\in\mathbb{R}\text{ for which }f_n\text{ is integ
rable over }[0,+\infty)^3\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.\,}$$

(Even if one restricted attention to $n\ge 1$, the condition would be $a>3$ — th
e term $n^{2}>0$ regularizes the origin, leaving only the decay condition at inf
inity, $\int_{1}^{\infty}r^{2-a}dr<\infty\iff a>3$. The inclusion of $n=0$ remov
es this entire range by reintroducing the incompatible origin condition $a<3$.)
