## Proof

**Answer: Yes. If every real-valued differentiable function on $X \subset \mathb
b{R}^n$ is bounded, then $X$ is compact.**

**Interpretation.** A function $f: X \to \mathbb{R}$ is *differentiable* if it e
xtends to a differentiable function $F: U \to \mathbb{R}$ on some open neighborh
ood $U \supseteq X$. (This is the natural notion for an arbitrary subset $X \sub
set \mathbb{R}^n$; when $X$ is itself open it reduces to the usual definition.)

We show $X$ is closed and bounded, hence compact by the Heine–Borel theorem.

---

**Step 1: $X$ is bounded.**

Suppose $X$ is unbounded. Consider $f(x) = x_1$, the projection onto the first c
oordinate. This is differentiable on all of $\mathbb{R}^n$ (hence on any open se
t containing $X$), and its restriction to $X$ is unbounded since $X$ is unbounde
d. This contradicts the hypothesis. Therefore $X$ is bounded.

---

**Step 2: $X$ is closed.**

Suppose $X$ is not closed. Then there exists a limit point $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$
of $X$ with $p \notin X$. Consider

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\|x - p\|}.$$

This function is differentiable (in fact smooth) on the open set $U = \mathbb{R}
^n \setminus \{p\}$, which contains $X$ since $p \notin X$. Its restriction to $
X$ is unbounded: because $p$ is a limit point of $X$, there exists a sequence $(
x_k) \subset X$ with $x_k \to p$, so $\|x_k - p\| \to 0$ and thus $f(x_k) = 1/\|
x_k - p\| \to +\infty$. This contradicts the hypothesis. Therefore $X$ is closed
.

---

**Conclusion.** $X$ is closed and bounded in $\mathbb{R}^n$, so by the Heine–Bor
el theorem, $X$ is compact. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The classical result that "$X$ is compact iff every *continuous* fun
ction on $X$ is bounded" uses the witnesses $f(x)=\|x\|$ (for unboundedness) and
 $f(x)=1/\|x-p\|$ (for non-closedness). Both of these are in fact differentiable
 on the relevant domains, so the same argument succeeds even for the much smalle
r class of differentiable functions.
