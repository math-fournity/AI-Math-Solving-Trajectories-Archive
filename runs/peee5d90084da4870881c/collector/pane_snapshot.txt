## Solution

**Problem.** Find all natural numbers $n$ such that $\varphi(n^4+1) = 8n$.

### Step 1. A useful lower bound

We use the classical bound:

> **Lemma.** For every positive integer $m \notin \{2, 6\}$, one has $\varphi(m)
 \ge \sqrt{m}$.

*Proof sketch.* The inequality is multiplicative in the following sense: if $\gc
d(a,b)=1$ and both $\varphi(a)\ge\sqrt a$, $\varphi(b)\ge\sqrt b$ hold, then $\v
arphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)\ge\sqrt{ab}$. For a prime power $p^a$ ($a\ge 1$),
 the inequality $p^{a-1}(p-1)\ge p^{a/2}$ reduces to $p^{a/2-1}(p-1)\ge 1$, whic
h holds for all $(p,a)$ **except** $(p,a)=(2,1)$, where $\varphi(2)=1<\sqrt 2$.
Thus the only prime-power factor that can break the bound is a single factor of
$2$. Tracking this deficit through products shows the bound fails **only** when
$n=2$ (just the factor $2$) or $n=2\cdot 3=6$ (where $\varphi(6)=2<\sqrt 6$); fo
r $n=2\cdot m$ with odd $m\ge 5$ one checks $\varphi(m)/\sqrt m \ge \sqrt 2$ dir
ectly, restoring the bound. ∎

### Step 2. Bounding $n$

Set $m = n^4+1$. We need $\varphi(m)=8n$.

- **$n=1$:** $m=2$, which is an exception to the lemma; directly $\varphi(2)=1\n
e 8$. ✗
- **$n\ge 2$:** Then $m=n^4+1\ge 17$, so $m\notin\{2,6\}$ and the lemma gives
$$8n = \varphi(n^4+1) \ge \sqrt{n^4+1} > n^2.$$
Hence $n^2 < 8n$, i.e. $n < 8$. So $n\in\{2,3,4,5,6,7\}$.

### Step 3. Checking $n=2,\dots,7$

|-----|---------|---------------|-------------------|------|--------|

| $3$ | $82$ | $2\cdot 41$ | $1\cdot 40=40$ | $24$ | ✗ |

| $5$ | $626$ | $2\cdot 313$ | $1\cdot 312=312$ | $40$ | ✗ |

| $7$ | $2402$ | $2\cdot 1201$ | $1\cdot 1200=1200$ | $56$ | ✗ |

(Primality checks: $257$ is a Fermat prime; $1297$ and $1201$ have no prime divi
sors $\le\sqrt{1297}\approx 36.0$ and $\le\sqrt{1201}\approx 34.6$ respectively,
 verified by trial division up to those limits.)

### Conclusion

The only natural number satisfying $\varphi(n^4+1)=8n$ is

$$\boxed{n=2}.$$
