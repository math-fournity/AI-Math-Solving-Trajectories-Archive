## Proof

**Answer: Yes, $G$ acts transitively on the prime ideals of $B$ lying over $p$.*
*

The key observation is that primes of $B$ lying over $p$ are in *$G$-equivariant
 bijection* with primes of the localization $B_p$ lying over $m_p$, so transitiv
ity on one side is literally equivalent to transitivity on the other.

---

### The bijection

Let $S = A \setminus p$, so that $A_p = S^{-1}A$ with maximal ideal $m_p = S^{-1
}p$, and $B_p = S^{-1}B$.

The standard localization correspondence gives an order-preserving bijection
$$
\{\,\mathfrak q \in \operatorname{Spec} B_p\,\} \;\longleftrightarrow\; \{\,\mat
hfrak Q \in \operatorname{Spec} B : \mathfrak Q \cap S = \varnothing\,\},
\$
via $\mathfrak q \mapsto \mathfrak q \cap B$ and $\mathfrak Q \mapsto S^{-1}\mat
hfrak Q = \mathfrak Q B_p$.

The condition $\mathfrak Q \cap S = \varnothing$ is equivalent to $\mathfrak Q \
cap A \subseteq p$ (since $\mathfrak Q \cap A$ is prime and $S = A\setminus p$).

Now track the contraction to $A_p$. For $\mathfrak q = S^{-1}\mathfrak Q \in \op
eratorname{Spec} B_p$,
$$
\mathfrak q \cap A_p = S^{-1}(\mathfrak Q \cap A).
$$
We have $\mathfrak q \cap A_p = m_p = S^{-1}p$ if and only if $S^{-1}(\mathfrak
Q \cap A) = S^{-1}p$. Since $\mathfrak Q \cap A \subseteq p$, the inclusion $S^{
-1}(\mathfrak Q \cap A) \subseteq S^{-1}p$ is automatic, and equality holds iff
$p \subseteq \mathfrak Q \cap A$, i.e. iff $\mathfrak Q \cap A = p$.

Therefore the localization correspondence restricts to a bijection
$$
\Phi : \{\mathfrak Q \in \operatorname{Spec} B : \mathfrak Q \cap A = p\} \;\xri
ghtarrow{\;\sim\;}\; \{\mathfrak q \in \operatorname{Spec} B_p : \mathfrak q \ca
p A_p = m_p\},
\qquad \Phi(\mathfrak Q) = \mathfrak Q B_p.
$$

### $G$-equivariance

The Galois group $G = \operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ fixes $A$ pointwise, hence fixes
$S = A \setminus p$ setwise, and therefore acts on $B_p = S^{-1}B$ by $\sigma(b/
s) = \sigma(b)/s$. For any $\mathfrak Q$ over $p$ and any $\sigma \in G$,
$$
\Phi(\sigma \mathfrak Q) = (\sigma \mathfrak Q) B_p = \sigma(\mathfrak Q B_p) =
\sigma(\Phi(\mathfrak Q)),
$$
since $\sigma$ fixes the elements of $S$. Thus $\Phi$ is $G$-equivariant.

### Transitivity transfers

By hypothesis, $G$ acts transitively on the set $\{\mathfrak q \in \operatorname
{Spec} B_p : \mathfrak q \cap A_p = m_p\}$. Let $\mathfrak Q_1, \mathfrak Q_2$ b
e two primes of $B$ lying over $p$. Then $\Phi(\mathfrak Q_1), \Phi(\mathfrak Q_
2)$ are primes of $B_p$ over $m_p$, so by hypothesis there exists $\sigma \in G$
 with
$$
\sigma(\Phi(\mathfrak Q_1)) = \Phi(\mathfrak Q_2).
$$
By $G$-equivariance,
$$
\Phi(\sigma \mathfrak Q_1) = \Phi(\mathfrak Q_2),
$$
and since $\Phi$ is injective, $\sigma \mathfrak Q_1 = \mathfrak Q_2$.

Hence $G$ acts transitively on the primes of $B$ lying over $p$.

### Remark

This is exactly the mechanism by which the classical transitivity theorem is pro
ved: one first localizes at $p$ (reducing to a semilocal situation where $A_p$ i
s local and $B_p$ has only finitely many primes over $m_p$), establishes transit
ivity there, and then pulls back through the $G$-equivariant bijection $\Phi$. T
he hypothesis that $A$ is integrally closed (with $B$ the integral closure in th
e Galois extension $L/K$) is precisely what makes the localized transitivity sta
tement valid; the passage from $B_p$ back to $B$ is then the formal, hypothesis-
free step recorded above.
