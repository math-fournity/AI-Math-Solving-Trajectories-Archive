## Proof

**Claim.** The smallest field extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ containing a transcenden
tal number (such as $e$) is $\mathbb{Q}(t)$, where $t$ is the transcendental num
ber. As a $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space, its dimension is **countably infinite** ($\
aleph_0$).

---

**Step 1. The smallest extension is $\mathbb{Q}(t)$.**

Let $t$ be transcendental over $\mathbb{Q}$, and let $K \supseteq \mathbb{Q}$ be
 any field with $t \in K$. Since $K$ is a field containing $\mathbb{Q}$ and $t$,
 it must contain every polynomial expression $p(t)$ with $p \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$,
and every quotient $p(t)/q(t)$ with $p, q \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$, $q \neq 0$. The se
t of all such quotients is precisely $\mathbb{Q}(t)$, the field of rational func
tions in $t$. Hence $\mathbb{Q}(t) \subseteq K$, so $\mathbb{Q}(t)$ is the small
est field extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ containing $t$.

**Step 2. The dimension is infinite.**

Consider the set $S = \{1, t, t^2, t^3, \dots\} \subset \mathbb{Q}(t)$. We claim
 $S$ is linearly independent over $\mathbb{Q}$. Suppose, for contradiction, that
 some finite subset is linearly dependent: there exist $a_0, a_1, \dots, a_n \in
 \mathbb{Q}$, not all zero, with
$$a_0 + a_1 t + a_2 t^2 + \cdots + a_n t^n = 0.$$
Then $t$ is a root of the nonzero polynomial $f(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_n
x^n \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$, contradicting the transcendence of $t$ over $\mathbb{Q}$
. Therefore $S$ is linearly independent, and since $S$ is infinite, $\dim_{\math
bb{Q}} \mathbb{Q}(t) = \infty$.

**Step 3. The dimension is countably infinite.**

$\mathbb{Q}(t)$ consists of quotients $p(t)/q(t)$ with $p, q \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$,
 $q \neq 0$. The set $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ is countable (finite polynomials over a cou
ntable coefficient field), so the set of pairs $(p, q)$ is countable, and thus $
\mathbb{Q}(t)$ is countable. A countably infinite vector space over the countabl
e field $\mathbb{Q}$ has dimension at most $\aleph_0$. Combined with Step 2 (the
 dimension is infinite), we conclude:
$$\dim_{\mathbb{Q}} \mathbb{Q}(t) = \aleph_0.$$

---

**Conclusion.** The smallest field extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ containing a transc
endental number $t$ is $\mathbb{Q}(t)$, and its dimension as a $\mathbb{Q}$-vect
or space is **countably infinite** ($\aleph_0$).
