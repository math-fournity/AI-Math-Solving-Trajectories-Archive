## Proof

**Claim.** Yes. Under the hypothesis
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|f_{k+1}(t)-f_k(t)|<\infty,
\]
the sequence $(f_n)$ converges uniformly on $[0,T]$ to a continuous function $f:
[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}$.

Set
\[
M_k:=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|f_{k+1}(t)-f_k(t)|,\qquad S:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}M_k<\infty
.
\]

**Step 1 — $(f_n)$ is uniformly Cauchy.**

For indices $n>m\ge 0$ and any $t\in[0,T]$, telescoping gives
\[
|f_n(t)-f_m(t)|\le\sum_{k=m}^{n-1}|f_{k+1}(t)-f_k(t)|\le\sum_{k=m}^{n-1}M_k\le\s
um_{k=m}^{\infty}M_k.
\]
The right-hand side no longer depends on $t$, so taking the supremum over $t\in[
0,T]$,
\[
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|f_n(t)-f_m(t)|\le\sum_{k=m}^{\infty}M_k.
\]
Since $S<\infty$, its tail $\sum_{k=m}^{\infty}M_k\to 0$ as $m\to\infty$. Hence
for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $N$ such that for all $n>m\ge N$,
\[
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|f_n(t)-f_m(t)|<\varepsilon.
\]
Thus $(f_n)$ is uniformly Cauchy on $[0,T]$.

**Step 2 — Pointwise limit exists.**

Fix $t\in[0,T]$. The estimate above gives $|f_n(t)-f_m(t)|\le\sum_{k=m}^{\infty}
M_k\to0$, so $(f_n(t))$ is a Cauchy sequence in $\mathbb{R}$. By completeness of
 $\mathbb{R}$ it converges; define
\[
f(t):=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_n(t).
\]
This defines a function $f:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}$.

**Step 3 — Convergence is uniform.**

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ so that $\sum_{k=N}^{\infty}M_k<\varepsilon$. Fo
r $n\ge N$ and any $t\in[0,T]$,
\[
|f_n(t)-f(t)|=\lim_{m\to\infty}|f_n(t)-f_m(t)|\le\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}M_k\le\sum_{
k=N}^{\infty}M_k<\varepsilon.
\]
(The inequality passes to the limit because $|f_n(t)-f_m(t)|\le\sum_{k=n}^{m-1}M
_k\le\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}M_k$ for all $m>n$, and the bound is independent of $m$.
) Taking the supremum over $t$,
\[
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|f_n(t)-f(t)|\le\varepsilon\qquad\text{for all }n\ge N.
\]
Hence $f_n\to f$ uniformly on $[0,T]$.

**Step 4 — $f$ is continuous.**

Each $f_n$ is continuous on $[0,T]$, and the uniform limit of continuous functio
ns on a topological space is continuous. Explicitly: given $t_0\in[0,T]$ and $\v
arepsilon>0$, pick $N$ with $\sup_t|f_N(t)-f(t)|<\varepsilon/3$; by continuity o
f $f_N$ pick $\delta>0$ with $|t-t_0|<\delta\Rightarrow|f_N(t)-f_N(t_0)|<\vareps
ilon/3$; then for $|t-t_0|<\delta$,
\[
|f(t)-f(t_0)|\le|f(t)-f_N(t)|+|f_N(t)-f_N(t_0)|+|f_N(t_0)-f(t_0)|<\varepsilon.
\]
So $f$ is continuous at $t_0$, and since $t_0$ was arbitrary, $f$ is continuous
on $[0,T]$.

**Conclusion.** The hypothesis $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|f_{k+1}(t)-f
_k(t)|<\infty$ implies that $(f_n)$ is uniformly Cauchy, hence converges uniform
ly on $[0,T]$ to a function $f$, and because each $f_n$ is continuous, the unifo
rm limit $f$ is continuous. Therefore such a continuous $f$ exists. $\blacksquar
e$
