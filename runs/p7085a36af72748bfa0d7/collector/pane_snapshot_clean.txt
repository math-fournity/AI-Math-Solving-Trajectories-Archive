## Solution

The question asks whether every compact subset of a topological vector space (TV
S) is both closed and bounded. The answer splits into two parts, and the overall
 conclusion depends on whether the TVS is assumed Hausdorff.

**Claim 1.** *In every topological vector space (Hausdorff or not), every compac
t set is bounded.*

**Proof of Claim 1.** Let $K$ be a compact subset of a TVS $X$, and let $U$ be a
ny neighborhood of $0$. We must show $K \subseteq \lambda U$ for some scalar $\l
ambda > 0$.

Since $U$ is a neighborhood of $0$, by the TVS axioms there exists a **balanced*
* neighborhood $V$ of $0$ with $V \subseteq U$. Every neighborhood of $0$ in a T
VS is **absorbing**: for any $x \in X$, the map $t \mapsto tx$ is continuous at
$t = 0$ with $0 \cdot x = 0 \in V$, so for sufficiently large $n \in \mathbb{N}$
, $\frac{1}{n}x \in V$, i.e., $x \in nV$. Thus

$$X = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} nV.$$

The family $\{nV : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$ is an open cover of $K$. By compactness,
there exist $n_1, \dots, n_k$ with

$$K \subseteq n_1 V \cup \cdots \cup n_k V.$$

Let $N = \max(n_1, \dots, n_k)$. Since $V$ is balanced, $n_i V \subseteq N V$ fo
r each $i$ (because $\frac{n_i}{N} \leq 1$ and $V$ balanced gives $n_i V = N \cd
ot \frac{n_i}{N} V \subseteq N V$). Hence

$$K \subseteq NV \subseteq NU.$$

Since $U$ was an arbitrary neighborhood of $0$, $K$ is bounded. $\square$

**Claim 2.** *In a Hausdorff TVS, every compact set is closed.*

**Proof of Claim 2.** Let $K$ be compact in a Hausdorff TVS $X$. We show $X \set
minus K$ is open. Let $y \notin K$. For each $x \in K$, since $X$ is Hausdorff (
hence $T_1$, and in a TVS $T_1$ implies Hausdorff), there exist disjoint open ne
ighborhoods $U_x$ of $x$ and $V_x$ of $y$. By continuity of addition, we may shr
ink $U_x$ so that $U_x - y$ is contained in a balanced neighborhood; more direct
ly, using the TVS structure: for each $x \in K$, choose a balanced neighborhood
$W_x$ of $0$ such that $(y + W_x) \cap (x + W_x) = \emptyset$ (possible by Hausd
orff + continuity of addition). Then $\{x + W_x : x \in K\}$ is an open cover of
 $K$; extract a finite subcover $\{x_i + W_{x_i}\}_{i=1}^m$. Let $W = W_{x_1} \c
ap \cdots \cap W_{x_m}$, a neighborhood of $0$. Then $y + W$ is a neighborhood o
f $y$ disjoint from each $x_i + W_{x_i}$ (since $W \subseteq W_{x_i}$ and $(y +
W_{x_i}) \cap (x_i + W_{x_i}) = \emptyset$), hence disjoint from $K$. So $y$ is
an interior point of $X \setminus K$, proving $K$ is closed. $\square$

**Claim 3.** *In a non-Hausdorff TVS, a compact set need not be closed.*

**Proof of Claim 3 (counterexample).** Let $X$ be any nonzero real vector space
equipped with the **indiscrete topology** $\tau = \{\emptyset, X\}$. Addition an
d scalar multiplication are continuous (the preimage of the only nontrivial open
 set $X$ under any map is $X$, which is open), so $X$ is a TVS. It is not Hausdo
rff.

Every subset of $X$ is compact: any open cover of a set $S$ must contain $X$ (th
e only nonempty open set), so $\{X\}$ is a finite subcover. In particular, any s
ingleton $\{x\}$ with $x \neq 0$ is compact. However, the only closed sets are $
\emptyset$ and $X$, so $\{x\}$ is **not closed**. Thus $\{x\}$ is compact but no
t closed. $\square$

---

### Conclusion

**No**, not every compact subset of a topological vector space is both closed an
d bounded in full generality.

- **Boundedness**: Every compact subset of *any* TVS is bounded (Claim 1). This
always holds.
- **Closedness**: Every compact subset of a **Hausdorff** TVS is closed (Claim 2
). However, in a non-Hausdorff TVS compact sets need not be closed (Claim 3).

Therefore:
- If "topological vector space" is taken to include the Hausdorff axiom (as in R
udin, Schaefer, and much of functional analysis), then **yes**: every compact su
bset is both closed and bounded.
- If "topological vector space" is taken in the general (not necessarily Hausdor
ff) sense (as in Bourbaki), then **no**: compact sets are always bounded but nee
d not be closed, as the indiscrete-topology counterexample shows.

The Heine-Borel property (compact $\Longrightarrow$ closed and bounded) for comp
act sets thus holds precisely in the Hausdorff setting; it is the Hausdorff sepa
ration that supplies closedness, while boundedness is a purely algebraic-topolog
ical consequence of compactness valid in every TVS.
