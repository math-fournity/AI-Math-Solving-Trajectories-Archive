## Proof

We identify $S^3 = \{(z_1, z_2) \in \mathbb{C}^2 : |z_1|^2 + |z_2|^2 = 1\}$. The
 map $I$ is the identity $I(z_1,z_2)=(z_1,z_2)$, and $A$ is the antipodal map $A
(z_1,z_2)=(-z_1,-z_2)$.

### Step 1: Degree argument

The homotopy classes of maps $S^3 \to S^3$ are classified by degree, since $\pi_
3(S^3)\cong\mathbb{Z}$ and the degree map gives the isomorphism. Two maps $S^3\t
o S^3$ are homotopic if and only if they have the same degree.

- $\deg(I) = +1$.
- $\deg(A) = (-1)^{n+1}$ for the antipodal map on $S^n$. For $n=3$: $\deg(A)=(-1
)^4=+1$.

Since $\deg(I)=\deg(A)=+1$, the maps $I$ and $A$ **are homotopic**.

### Step 2: Explicit homotopy via $SU(2)$

We can do better and construct an explicit homotopy of the required form. The ke
y observation is that $S^3 \cong SU(2)$, where a point $(z_1,z_2)\in S^3$ corres
ponds to the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} z_1 & -\overline{z_2}\\ z_2 & \overline{z_1
}\end{pmatrix}\in SU(2)$, and the antipodal map $A$ is left-multiplication by $-
I\in SU(2)$.

Since $SU(2)$ is path-connected, there exists a continuous path $\gamma:[0,1]\to
 SU(2)$ with $\gamma(0)=I$ and $\gamma(1)=-I$. A concrete choice is:

$$\gamma(t) = \begin{pmatrix} e^{i\pi t} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{-i\pi t} \end{pmatrix}.$$

This lies in $SU(2)$ for all $t$ (it is unitary with determinant $e^{i\pi t}\cdo
t e^{-i\pi t}=1$), with $\gamma(0)=I$ and $\gamma(1)=-I$.

### Step 3: The homotopy

Define $F:S^3\times[0,1]\to S^3$ by the action of $\gamma(t)$ on $(z_1,z_2)$ vie
wed as a column vector:

$$\boxed{F(z_1, z_2, t) = \bigl(e^{i\pi t}\, z_1,\; e^{-i\pi t}\, z_2\bigr).}$$

This has the required form $F=(F_1, F_2)$ with $F_1(z_1,z_2,t)=e^{i\pi t}z_1$ an
d $F_2(z_1,z_2,t)=e^{-i\pi t}z_2$, both complex-valued functions.

**Verification:**

1. **Endpoints:**
   - $F(z_1,z_2,0) = (z_1, z_2) = I(z_1,z_2)$. ✓
   - $F(z_1,z_2,1) = (e^{i\pi}z_1,\, e^{-i\pi}z_2) = (-z_1, -z_2) = A(z_1,z_2)$.
 ✓

2. **Lands on $S^3$:**
$$|F_1|^2 + |F_2|^2 = |e^{i\pi t}z_1|^2 + |e^{-i\pi t}z_2|^2 = |z_1|^2 + |z_2|^2

So $F(z_1,z_2,t)\in S^3$ for all $t$. ✓

3. **Continuity:** $F_1$ and $F_2$ are continuous (in fact smooth) in all variab

### Conclusion

**Yes, it is possible** to construct such a homotopy. The identity map $I$ and t
he antipodal map $A$ on $S^3$ are homotopic because both have degree $+1$ (the a

$$F(z_1, z_2, t) = \bigl(e^{i\pi t}\, z_1,\; e^{-i\pi t}\, z_2\bigr),$$

which continuously rotates the phases of $z_1$ and $z_2$ in opposite directions,
