## Solution

### 1. Problem Structure and the Role of the Decision Variables

The model seeks to minimize

$$TC(\lambda, r) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\sqrt{a\,\theta_i}\int_{0}^{\infty}F\!\l
eft(\frac{\theta_i+r-t}{\sqrt{a\,\theta_i}}\right)d\Pr(D\le t)\;+\;\gamma\,(r+\t
heta_i)\right\}$$

over the pair $\zeta = (\lambda, r)$, where

$$F(x) = (\alpha+\beta)\,\Phi(x) + x\big[(\alpha+\beta)\,\Phi(x)-\alpha\big].$$

The two decision variables play distinct roles:

| Variable | Role | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| $\lambda$ | **Sequencing parameter** | Determines the order in which jobs (or
job-groups $N_i$) are processed, and hence determines the values $\theta_1, \the
ta_2, \dots, \theta_n$ (the cumulative expected processing times of preceding jo
bs). |
| $r$ | **Due-date allowance (slack)** | Sets every due date as $d_i = \theta_i
+ r$. Increasing $r$ pushes due dates later, trading tardiness reduction against
 increased earliness and due-date penalty $\gamma(r+\theta_i)$. |

The variance condition $\sigma_i^2 = a\,\mu_i$ (variance proportional to mean, w
ith common constant $a$) is the structural keystone: it makes the standard devia
tion of the cumulative processing time up to group $i$ equal to $\sqrt{a\,\theta
_i}$, so the cost contribution of each group depends on $\theta_i$ alone, not on
 the individual variances of the constituent jobs. This decoupling is what allow
s the sequence (governed by $\lambda$) and the allowance $r$ to be optimized in
a nested fashion.

### 2. Derivation of $F(x)$ — Why the Cost Has This Form

For a job in group $N_i$ with expected start time $\theta_i$ and processing time
 $P\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\,a\mu)$, the completion time is $C = \theta_i + P$ (to f
irst order, the start-time variance is absorbed into the $\sqrt{a\theta_i}$ scal
ing under the proportional-variance assumption). Given a realized due date $D =
t$, the earliness and tardiness are

$$E = \max(0,\, t - C),\qquad T = \max(0,\, C - t).$$

Setting $x = (\theta_i + r - t)/\sqrt{a\,\theta_i}$ and standardizing via $Z = (
C - \theta_i)/\sqrt{a\,\theta_i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, one obtains

$$\alpha\,\mathbb{E}[E] + \beta\,\mathbb{E}[T] = \sqrt{a\,\theta_i}\;\Big\{(\alp
ha+\beta)\,\Phi(x) + x\big[(\alpha+\beta)\,\Phi(x) - \alpha\big]\Big\} = \sqrt{a
\,\theta_i}\;F(x).$$

The term $\gamma(r+\theta_i)$ is the due-date assignment cost (penalizing due da
tes that are set too far in the future). The outer integral $\int_0^\infty (\cdo
t)\,d\Pr(D\le t)$ averages $F$ over the common due-date distribution.

### 3. Convexity of $TC$ in $r$ (for Fixed Sequence)

For a fixed sequence (fixed $\lambda$, hence fixed $\theta_i$), define

$$g(r) := TC(\lambda,\,r).$$

**Claim:** $g(r)$ is strictly convex in $r$.

*Sketch.* Differentiating under the integral (permissible by dominated convergen
ce since $F$ is smooth and the due-date distribution has finite moments):

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Big[\sqrt{a\theta_i}\,F(x)\Big] = \sqrt{a\theta_i}
\,F'(x)\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{a\theta_i}} = F'(x),$$

where $x = (\theta_i + r - t)/\sqrt{a\theta_i}$. A direct computation gives

$$F'(x) = (\alpha+\beta)\,\Phi(x) + x\,(\alpha+\beta)\,\varphi(x) + \big[(\alpha
+\beta)\Phi(x)-\alpha\big] - x\,(\alpha+\beta)\varphi(x) = 2(\alpha+\beta)\Phi(x
) - \alpha.$$

Hence

$$\frac{\partial g}{\partial r} = \sum_i\int_0^\infty\big[2(\alpha+\beta)\Phi(x_
i(r,t))-\alpha\big]\,d\Pr(D\le t) + n\,\gamma.$$

Since $\Phi$ is monotonically increasing in $r$ (because $x_i$ increases with $r
$), each summand $2(\alpha+\beta)\Phi(x_i)-\alpha$ is increasing in $r$, so $\pa
rtial g/\partial r$ is increasing in $r$, proving $g$ is convex. Moreover $\part
ial g/\partial r \to -\alpha\cdot n + n\gamma$ as $r\to -\infty$ (where $\Phi\to
 0$) and $\partial g/\partial r \to (\alpha+\beta)\cdot n + n\gamma > 0$ as $r\t
o+\infty$ (where $\Phi\to 1$), so $g$ has a unique finite minimizer $r^*(\lambda
)$. ∎

This convexity (unimodality) is the essential condition that licenses the use of
 **Fibonacci search**.

### 4. Algorithm 39.3 — The Nested Optimization Procedure

Algorithm 39.3 exploits the structure above by decomposing the joint minimizatio
n over $(\lambda, r)$ into:

- an **outer search** over the sequencing parameter $\lambda$, and
- an **inner search** over the allowance $r$ (via Fibonacci search) for each fix
ed $\lambda$.

#### Step 0 — Initialization

1. Choose a search interval $[\lambda_{\min},\,\lambda_{\max}]$ for the outer va
riable. (Typically $\lambda\in[0,1]$ or a range determined by the cost ratios.)
2. Choose a search interval $[r_L,\,r_U]$ for the inner variable, wide enough to
 bracket $r^*$ (e.g., $r_L = 0$, $r_U$ large enough that $\partial g/\partial r
> 0$ at $r_U$).
3. Set convergence tolerances $\varepsilon_\lambda$, $\varepsilon_r$ and the Fib
onacci iteration count $N$ (determined by $\varepsilon_r$ and the initial interv
al width, via $F_N^{-1}(r_U - r_L) \le \varepsilon_r$).

#### Step 1 — Outer Search over $\lambda$ (Fibonacci or Golden-Section)

For the current value of $\lambda$:

**(a) Determine the sequence.** The parameter $\lambda$ induces a sequencing rul
e. Under the proportional-variance assumption $\sigma_i^2 = a\mu_i$, the optimal
 sequence for a given $\lambda$ is obtained by ordering the jobs (or job-groups)
 by a $\lambda$-dependent priority index. Specifically, jobs are sorted in **non
-decreasing order of $\mu_i$** when $\lambda$ favors earliness reduction (SEPT —
 Shortest Expected Processing Time), and the ordering shifts as $\lambda$ change
s to reflect the earliness–tardiness–due-date trade-off. The cumulative means $\
theta_1, \dots, \theta_n$ are then computed from this sequence.

**(b) Inner Fibonacci search for $r^*(\lambda)$.** With the $\theta_i$ fixed, ev
aluate $g(r) = TC(\lambda, r)$ via the Fibonacci search procedure (detailed in §
5 below). This yields $r^*(\lambda)$ and the corresponding cost $g^*(\lambda) =
TC(\lambda, r^*(\lambda))$.

**(c) Record and reduce the outer interval.** Compare $g^*(\lambda)$ at the two
current Fibonacci probe points of the outer search; discard the sub-interval tha
t cannot contain $\lambda^*$ (exploiting the unimodality of $g^*(\lambda)$ in $\
lambda$).

#### Step 2 — Iterate

Repeat Step 1 with the reduced $[\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max}]$ interval until
 the interval width falls below $\varepsilon_\lambda$.

#### Step 3 — Output

$$\boxed{\zeta^* = (\lambda^*,\, r^*)},\qquad r^* = r^*(\lambda^*).$$

### 5. Fibonacci Search for $r^*$ (Inner Loop)

Given the fixed $\theta_i$ (from the current $\lambda$) and the interval $[r_L,
r_U]$:

1. **Set the Fibonacci numbers.** Choose $N$ such that $F_N > (r_U - r_L)/\varep
silon_r$, where $F_N$ is the $N$-th Fibonacci number ($F_0 = F_1 = 1$).

2. **Place the first two probes:**
$$r_1 = r_L + \frac{F_{N-2}}{F_N}(r_U - r_L),\qquad r_2 = r_L + \frac{F_{N-1}}{F
_N}(r_U - r_L).$$

3. **Evaluate** $g(r_1)$ and $g(r_2)$ (each evaluation requires computing the in
tegral $\int_0^\infty F(\cdot)\,d\Pr(D\le t)$ numerically for every group $i$).

4. **Interval reduction:**
   - If $g(r_1) < g(r_2)$: the minimum lies in $[r_L, r_2]$. Set $r_U \leftarrow
 r_2$, $r_2 \leftarrow r_1$, and place a new $r_1 = r_L + \frac{F_{N-3}}{F_{N-1}
}(r_U - r_L)$.
   - If $g(r_1) \ge g(r_2)$: the minimum lies in $[r_1, r_U]$. Set $r_L \leftarr
ow r_1$, $r_1 \leftarrow r_2$, and place a new $r_2 = r_L + \frac{F_{N-2}}{F_{N-
1}}(r_U - r_L)$.

5. **Decrement** $N \leftarrow N-1$ and repeat from step 3 until $N = 1$.

6. **Return** $r^*(\lambda) \approx (r_L + r_U)/2$ with accuracy $\le \varepsilo
n_r$.

**Why Fibonacci search (and not, say, Newton's method)?**

- $g(r)$ involves an expectation over the due-date distribution $D$; its derivat
ive $\partial g/\partial r = \sum_i \int [2(\alpha+\beta)\Phi(x_i) - \alpha]\,d\
Pr(D\le t) + n\gamma$ exists in principle but requires numerical integration at
each step, making Newton-type methods expensive and potentially unstable.
- Fibonacci search uses **only function evaluations** (no derivatives), requires
 the **minimum number of evaluations** among all derivative-free interval-reduct
ion methods for a given final interval width, and is **guaranteed to converge**
for any unimodal function.
- The convexity established in §3 guarantees unimodality, so the Fibonacci searc
h provably finds the global minimizer $r^*(\lambda)$.

### 6. Determination of $\lambda^*$ (Outer Loop)

The outer function

$$h(\lambda) := \min_r\, TC(\lambda, r) = TC(\lambda,\, r^*(\lambda))$$

is unimodal in $\lambda$ under the proportional-variance assumption, because:

- The sequencing rule induced by $\lambda$ changes the $\theta_i$ monotonically
as $\lambda$ varies (jobs are re-ordered by their $\lambda$-dependent priority).
- The cost surface $TC(\lambda, r)$ is jointly quasi-convex: for each $\lambda$,
 the inner minimization already selects the best $r$, and the residual function
$h(\lambda)$ inherits unimodality from the convexity of $TC$ in each variable se
parately and the monotonicity of the sequencing map.

Therefore the **same Fibonacci search technique** (or equivalently golden-sectio
n search) is applied to $h(\lambda)$ on $[\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max}]$, with
 each function evaluation of $h$ requiring one complete inner Fibonacci search f
or $r^*(\lambda)$.

### 8. Summary of the Optimal Solution

The optimal solution $\zeta^* = (\lambda^*, r^*)$ is obtained by **Algorithm 39.
3** as a two-level nested search:

1. **Outer level (sequencing):** Search over $\lambda$ using Fibonacci (or golde
n-section) search on the unimodal function $h(\lambda) = \min_r TC(\lambda, r)$.
 Each value of $\lambda$ determines a job sequence (via the $\lambda$-dependent
priority rule enabled by the proportional-variance condition $\sigma_i^2 = a\mu_
i$) and hence the cumulative means $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_n$.

2. **Inner level (allowance):** For each fixed $\lambda$ (fixed sequence), find
$r^*(\lambda)$ by **Fibonacci search** on the convex function $g(r) = TC(\lambda
, r)$. The Fibonacci search progressively reduces the bracketing interval $[r_L,
 r_U]$ using Fibonacci-number ratios, requiring only function evaluations (no de
rivatives), and converges to the unique global minimizer.

3. **Output:** $\lambda^*$ is the $\lambda$ that minimizes $h(\lambda)$; $r^* =
r^*(\lambda^*)$ is the corresponding optimal allowance. The optimal due dates ar
e $d_i^* = \theta_i^* + r^*$, where $\theta_i^*$ are the cumulative means under
the optimal sequence.

The methods are applied precisely when conditions C1–C7 hold: normal processing
times, proportional variance, common due-date distribution, non-negative costs,
finite due-date moments, and the resulting unimodality of $TC$ in $r$ and of $h$
 in $\lambda$.
