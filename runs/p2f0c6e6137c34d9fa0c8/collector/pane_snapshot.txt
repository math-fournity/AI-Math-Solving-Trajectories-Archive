## Proof

**Claim:** The statement $\pi_n(D^{n+1}) = 0$ holds for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

**Key fact.** The $(n+1)$-dimensional disk $D^{n+1} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1} :
 \|x\| \le 1\}$ is **contractible**. A contraction is given by the straight-line
 homotopy
$$H : D^{n+1} \times [0,1] \to D^{n+1}, \qquad H(x, t) = (1-t)\,x.$$
This satisfies $H(x, 0) = x$ (the identity) and $H(x, 1) = 0$ (the constant map
to the origin), and $H(x,t) \in D^{n+1}$ for all $t$ since $\|(1-t)x\| = (1-t)\|
x\| \le 1$. Hence $D^{n+1}$ deformation retracts to a point.

**Consequence for homotopy groups.** A standard theorem of homotopy theory state
s:

> *If $X$ is contractible, then $\pi_k(X, x_0) = 0$ for every $k \geq 1$ and eve
ry basepoint $x_0 \in X$.*

*Proof of the theorem.* Let $H: X \times [0,1] \to X$ be a contraction to a poin
t $p \in X$, so $H(x,0) = x$ and $H(x,1) = p$. Let $f : (S^k, s_0) \to (X, x_0)$
 be any based map representing an element of $\pi_k(X, x_0)$. Define
$$F : S^k \times [0,1] \to X, \qquad F(s, t) = H(f(s), t).$$
Then $F(s, 0) = f(s)$ and $F(s, 1) = p$, so $F$ is a homotopy from $f$ to the co
nstant map $c_p$. It remains to check this is a *based* homotopy. Along the base
point:
$$F(s_0, t) = H(f(s_0), t) = H(x_0, t),$$
which traces a path $\gamma(t) = H(x_0, t)$ from $x_0$ to $p$. To convert this i
nto a based homotopy (fixing the basepoint throughout), compose $F$ with a homot
opy that slides the basepoint back along $\gamma^{-1}$. Concretely, since $D^{n+
1}$ is in particular **simply connected** (being contractible), the path $\gamma
$ is unique up to homotopy, and the standard basepoint-adjustment argument shows
 $f$ is based-homotopic to $c_{x_0}$. Thus every element of $\pi_k(X, x_0)$ is n
ull-homotopic, i.e., $\pi_k(X, x_0) = 0$. $\square$

**Applying to the problem.** Since $D^{n+1}$ is contractible, by the theorem abo
ve $\pi_k(D^{n+1}) = 0$ for every $k \geq 1$. In particular, taking $k = n$ (whi
ch is $\geq 1$ for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$, under the convention $\mathbb{N} =
\{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$; and also for $n = 0$ under the convention $\mathbb{N} = \{0
, 1, 2, \dots\}$, since $\pi_0$ of a contractible—and hence path-connected—space
 is a singleton, i.e. trivial):

$$\pi_n(D^{n+1}) = 0 \qquad \text{for all } n \in \mathbb{N}.$$

**Conclusion.** The statement is **true**. The essential reason is that every di
sk $D^{m}$ (of any dimension $m \geq 1$) is contractible via the straight-line h
omotopy to its center, and contractible spaces have trivial homotopy groups in a
ll degrees. No special relationship between the index $n$ of the homotopy group
and the dimension $n+1$ of the disk is needed—the result holds simply because $D
^{n+1}$ is contractible, so *all* of its homotopy groups vanish, not just $\pi_n
$.
