## Answer

**No, $P_T$ is not necessarily coercive.** Under the stated hypotheses (coercivi
ty alone, with no boundedness/continuity/growth assumption on $P$), a translatio
n can destroy coercivity. Below is an explicit counterexample.

---

### Counterexample

Let $X = \ell^{2}$, which is a Banach space, and identify $X^{*}\cong \ell^{2}$
via the Riesz map so that the duality pairing $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is the
 $\ell^{2}$ inner product. Let $\{e_{1},e_{2},\dots\}$ be the standard basis.

**Define the map.** For $v\in \ell^{2}$ set
$$
h(v)=\begin{cases}\|v\|^{4}\Big(e_{1}-\dfrac{\langle e_{1},v\rangle}{\|v\|^{2}}\
,v\Big),& v\neq 0,\\[4pt] 0,& v=0,\end{cases}
\qquad P(v)=v+h(v).
$$
Note that $h(v)\perp v$, since
$$
\langle h(v),v\rangle=\|v\|^{4}\big(\langle e_{1},v\rangle-\langle e_{1},v\rangl
e\big)=0.
$$

**$P$ is coercive.** Indeed
$$
\langle P(v),v\rangle=\langle v,v\rangle+\langle h(v),v\rangle=\|v\|^{2}.
$$
Taking $f(s)=s$ (so $f:\mathbb R^{+}\to\mathbb R^{+}$ and $\lim_{s\to+\infty}f(s
)=+\infty$) gives
$$
\langle P(v),v\rangle=\|v\|^{2}=f(\|v\|)\,\|v\|,
$$
so the coercivity inequality $\langle P(v),v\rangle\ge f(\|v\|)\|v\|$ holds (wit
h equality). ✓

**The translation fails to be coercive.** Take $w=e_{1}$ and consider $P_{T}(u)=
P(u+e_{1})$. Test it along the sequence
$$
u_{n}=n\,e_{2},\qquad n\to+\infty,\qquad \|u_{n}\|=n\to\infty.
$$
Put $v_{n}=u_{n}+e_{1}=e_{1}+n\,e_{2}$. Then $\|v_{n}\|=\sqrt{n^{2}+1}$ and $\la
ngle e_{1},v_{n}\rangle=1$. We compute
$$
\langle P_{T}(u_{n}),u_{n}\rangle=\langle P(v_{n}),\,v_{n}-e_{1}\rangle=\langle
P(v_{n}),v_{n}\rangle-\langle P(v_{n}),e_{1}\rangle.
$$

- First term (coercivity of $P$):
$$
\langle P(v_{n}),v_{n}\rangle=\|v_{n}\|^{2}=n^{2}+1.
$$

- Second term:
$$
\langle P(v_{n}),e_{1}\rangle=\langle v_{n},e_{1}\rangle+\langle h(v_{n}),e_{1}\
rangle.
$$
Here $\langle v_{n},e_{1}\rangle=1$, and
$$
\langle h(v_{n}),e_{1}\rangle=\|v_{n}\|^{4}\Big(1-\frac{\langle e_{1},v_{n}\rang
le^{2}}{\|v_{n}\|^{2}}\Big)=(n^{2}+1)^{2}\Big(1-\frac{1}{n^{2}+1}\Big)=(n^{2}+1)
^{2}\cdot\frac{n^{2}}{n^{2}+1}=(n^{2}+1)n^{2}=n^{4}+n^{2}.
$$
Hence $\langle P(v_{n}),e_{1}\rangle=1+n^{4}+n^{2}$.

Combining,
$$
\langle P_{T}(u_{n}),u_{n}\rangle=(n^{2}+1)-(1+n^{4}+n^{2})=-n^{4}\xrightarrow[n
\to\infty]{}-\infty.
$$

In particular
$$
\frac{\langle P_{T}(u_{n}),u_{n}\rangle}{\|u_{n}\|}=\frac{-n^{4}}{n}=-n^{3}\xrig
htarrow[n\to\infty]{}-\infty.
$$

**Conclusion.** If $P_{T}$ were coercive, there would exist $g:\mathbb R^{+}\to\
mathbb R^{+}$ with $\lim_{s\to+\infty}g(s)=+\infty$ and $\langle P_{T}(u),u\rang
le\ge g(\|u\|)\|u\|$ for all $u$; dividing by $\|u\|$ would force $\langle P_{T}
(u),u\rangle/\|u\|\to+\infty$ along every sequence with $\|u\|\to\infty$. The se
quence $(u_{n})$ above violates this. Therefore **$P_{T}$ is not coercive**.

---

### Remark

The failure comes from the fact that coercivity controls only the component of $
P(v)$ along $v$ (via $\langle P(v),v\rangle$); it places **no bound** on the ort
hogonal component of $P(v)$, which can grow arbitrarily fast. Upon translation $
u\mapsto u+w$, that uncontrolled component pairs with the fixed vector $w$ and c
an dominate the coercive term $\langle P(v),v\rangle$. If one additionally assum
es that $P$ maps bounded sets to bounded sets (or any polynomial/growth bound on
 $\|P(v)\|$), then a standard estimate shows $P_{T}$ is again coercive — but und
er the hypotheses stated in the problem, the answer is **no**.
