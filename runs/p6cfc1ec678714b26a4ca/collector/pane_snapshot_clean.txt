## Solution

**Key observation.** The ring $R = \mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^n-1)$ is a quotient of the P
ID $\mathbb{Q}[x]$. By the ideal correspondence theorem, the prime ideals of $R$
 are in bijection with the prime ideals of $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ that contain $(x^n-1)
$.

Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is a field, $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ is a Euclidean domain (hence a PI
D). Its prime ideals are $(0)$ and the principal ideals $(f)$ generated by irred
ucible polynomials $f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$. The ideal $(x^n-1)$ is nonzero, so $(0
) \not\supseteq (x^n-1)$. For an irreducible $f$, we have $(x^n-1) \subseteq (f)
$ if and only if $f \mid (x^n-1)$.

Therefore:

> **The number of distinct prime ideals of $\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^n-1)$ equals the nu
mber of distinct irreducible factors of $x^n-1$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.**

**Factorization via cyclotomic polynomials.** The classical factorization over $
\mathbb{Q}$ is
$$x^n - 1 = \prod_{d \mid n} \Phi_d(x),$$
where $\Phi_d(x)$ is the $d$-th cyclotomic polynomial. Each $\Phi_d(x)$ is irred
ucible over $\mathbb{Q}$ (a standard theorem: $\Phi_d$ is the minimal polynomial
 of a primitive $d$-th root of unity over $\mathbb{Q}$, hence irreducible). The
factors $\Phi_d$ for distinct $d$ are distinct (they have different roots — prim
itive $d$-th roots of unity). Thus the number of distinct irreducible factors of
 $x^n-1$ equals the number of positive divisors of $n$, namely $\tau(n)$.

**Solving $\tau(n) = 3$.** We need the number of positive divisors of $n$ to be
exactly $3$. Writing $n = p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_k^{a_k}$, we have $\tau(n) = (a_1+1
)\cdots(a_k+1) = 3$. Since $3$ is prime, the only possibility is $k=1$ and $a_1+
1 = 3$, i.e., $a_1 = 2$. Hence $n = p^2$ for some prime $p$.

**Verification with small cases.**
- $n = 4 = 2^2$: $x^4-1 = (x-1)(x+1)(x^2+1)$ — three irreducible factors, hence
three prime ideals. ✓
- $n = 9 = 3^2$: $x^9-1 = \Phi_1\,\Phi_3\,\Phi_9 = (x-1)(x^2+x+1)(x^6+x^3+1)$ —
three factors. ✓
- $n = 6$ (not of the form $p^2$): $\tau(6)=4$, giving $x^6-1 = \Phi_1\Phi_2\Phi
_3\Phi_6$ — four factors, four prime ideals. ✓
- $n = 2$ (prime, not $p^2$): $\tau(2)=2$, giving $x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1)$ — two facto
rs. ✓

**Conclusion.** The positive integers $n$ for which $\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^n-1)$ has
exactly $3$ distinct prime ideals are precisely the squares of primes:

$$\boxed{n = p^2 \text{ where } p \text{ is a prime.}}$$
