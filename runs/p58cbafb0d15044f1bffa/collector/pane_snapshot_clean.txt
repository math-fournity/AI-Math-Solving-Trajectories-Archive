## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, a bounded linear operator on a Hilbert space can have a spectrum
 consisting of exactly one point.

**Example 1 (Scalar operator).** Let $H$ be any nonzero Hilbert space and let $T
 = \lambda I$ for some $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$, where $I$ is the identity opera
tor. For any $\mu \in \mathbb{C}$,

$$T - \mu I = (\lambda - \mu)I.$$

This operator is invertible (with bounded inverse $\frac{1}{\lambda-\mu}I$) if a
nd only if $\lambda - \mu \neq 0$, i.e., $\mu \neq \lambda$. Hence

$$\sigma(T) = \{\lambda\},$$

a single point.

**Example 2 (Nilpotent operator, showing the phenomenon is not restricted to sca
lar operators).** Let $H = \mathbb{C}^2$ and let $T = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0
 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Then $T^2 = 0$. For any $\mu \neq 0$, the operator $T - \mu
 I$ is invertible with inverse

$$(T - \mu I)^{-1} = -\frac{1}{\mu}\sum_{k=0}^{1}\left(\frac{T}{\mu}\right)^k =
-\frac{1}{\mu}\left(I + \frac{T}{\mu}\right),$$

which is bounded. Since the spectrum of any bounded operator on a complex Banach
 space (hence on a Hilbert space) is **nonempty**, and we have shown $\sigma(T)
\subseteq \{0\}$, it follows that

$$\sigma(T) = \{0\}.$$

**General remark.** In fact, an operator $T$ satisfies $\sigma(T) = \{\lambda\}$
 if and only if $T = \lambda I + N$ where $N$ is quasinilpotent (i.e., $\|N^n\|^
{1/n} \to 0$). The two examples above correspond to $N = 0$ and $N$ nilpotent, r
espectively.

**Conclusion.** A bounded linear operator on a Hilbert space can indeed have a s
pectrum consisting of a single point; the scalar operator $\lambda I$ and nilpot
ent operators provide explicit instances.
