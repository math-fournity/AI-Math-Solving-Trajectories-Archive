## Proof

Let $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots$ be the positive roots of $J_1(x)=0$. Since $J_
0'(x) = -J_1(x)$, these are exactly the positive roots of $J_0'$, i.e. the eigen
values of the Sturm–Liouville problem

$$(xy')' + \lambda^2 x\,y = 0,\qquad y'(1)=0,\quad y\text{ regular at }0,$$

whose eigenfunctions are $J_0(\lambda_j x)$ (together with the constant eigenfun
ction $J_0(0\cdot x)\equiv 1$ corresponding to the root $\lambda_0=0$ of $J_1$).

### Orthogonality and norms

The eigenfunctions are orthogonal with weight $x$ on $[0,1]$. For the norm, usin
g

$$\int_0^\lambda u\,J_0(u)^2\,du = \frac{\lambda^2}{2}\bigl[J_0(\lambda)^2 + J_1
(\lambda)^2\bigr]$$

and $J_1(\lambda_j)=0$, we obtain

$$N_j \;=\;\int_0^1 x\,J_0(\lambda_j x)^2\,dx \;=\;\frac{1}{\lambda_j^2}\cdot\fr
ac{\lambda_j^2}{2}J_0(\lambda_j)^2 \;=\;\tfrac12 J_0(\lambda_j)^2.$$

(The zeros of $J_0$ and $J_1$ interlace, so $J_0(\lambda_j)\neq 0$ and $N_j>0$.)
 Hence any $f\in L^2([0,1];x\,dx)$ admits

$$f(x) \;=\; c_0 \;+\;\sum_{j=1}^\infty c_j\,J_0(\lambda_j x),\qquad c_j \;=\;\f
rac{2}{J_0(\lambda_j)^2}\int_0^1 x\,f(x)\,J_0(\lambda_j x)\,dx,\quad c_0 = 2\int
_0^1 x\,f(x)\,dx.$$

### Useful integrals

Set $\alpha=\lambda_j$ (so $J_1(\alpha)=0$). With $t=\alpha x$ and the identitie
s $\frac{d}{dt}[tJ_1(t)]=tJ_0(t)$, $\frac{d}{dt}[t^2J_2(t)]=t^2J_1(t)$, $\frac{d
}{dt}[t^3J_3(t)]=t^3J_2(t)$:

$$\int_0^1 x\,J_0(\alpha x)\,dx = \frac{J_1(\alpha)}{\alpha}=0.$$

$$\int_0^1 x^3 J_0(\alpha x)\,dx = \frac{1}{\alpha^4}\Big[t^3J_1(t)-2t^2J_2(t)\B
ig]_0^\alpha = -\frac{2J_2(\alpha)}{\alpha^2}.$$

$$\int_0^1 x^5 J_0(\alpha x)\,dx = \frac{1}{\alpha^6}\Big[t^5J_1(t)-4t^4J_2(t)+8
t^3J_3(t)\Big]_0^\alpha = -\frac{4J_2(\alpha)}{\alpha^2}+\frac{8J_3(\alpha)}{\al
pha^3}.$$

The recurrence $J_{\nu-1}+J_{\nu+1}=\frac{2\nu}{x}J_\nu$ at $x=\lambda_j$ (where
 $J_1=0$) gives

$$J_2(\lambda_j) = -J_0(\lambda_j),\qquad J_3(\lambda_j) = \tfrac{4}{\lambda_j}J
_2(\lambda_j)-J_1(\lambda_j) = -\tfrac{4}{\lambda_j}J_0(\lambda_j).$$

Therefore

$$\int_0^1 x^3 J_0(\lambda_j x)\,dx = \frac{2J_0(\lambda_j)}{\lambda_j^2},\qquad
 \int_0^1 x^5 J_0(\lambda_j x)\,dx = \frac{4J_0(\lambda_j)}{\lambda_j^2}-\frac{3
2J_0(\lambda_j)}{\lambda_j^4}.$$

### (a) Expansion of $x^2$

Take $f(x)=x^2$. The constant term is

$$c_0 = 2\int_0^1 x\cdot x^2\,dx = 2\cdot\tfrac14 = \tfrac12.$$

For $j\ge 1$:

$$\int_0^1 x\,x^2\,J_0(\lambda_j x)\,dx = \int_0^1 x^3 J_0(\lambda_j x)\,dx = \f
rac{2J_0(\lambda_j)}{\lambda_j^2},$$

so

$$c_j = \frac{2}{J_0(\lambda_j)^2}\cdot\frac{2J_0(\lambda_j)}{\lambda_j^2} = \fr
ac{4}{\lambda_j^2 J_0(\lambda_j)}.$$

Thus, for $0<x<1$,

$$\boxed{\,x^2 \;=\; \tfrac12 \;+\; 4\sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{J_0(\lambda_j x)}{\
lambda_j^2 J_0(\lambda_j)}\,},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad x^2-\tfrac12 \;=\; 4\sum_{
j=1}^\infty \frac{J_0(\lambda_j x)}{\lambda_j^2 J_0(\lambda_j)}.$$

*Check at $x=1$:* the Rayleigh sum $\sum_{j\ge1}1/\lambda_j^2 = 1/[4(\nu+1)]\big
|_{\nu=1}=1/8$ gives RHS $= \tfrac12 + 4\cdot\tfrac18 = 1 = x^2$. ✓

### (b) Expansion of $(1-x^2)^2$

Take $f(x)=(1-x^2)^2 = 1-2x^2+x^4$. The constant term is

$$c_0 = 2\int_0^1 x(1-2x^2+x^4)\,dx = 2\Big(\tfrac12 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac16\Big)
= \tfrac13.$$

For $j\ge 1$, using the three integrals above (the $\int xJ_0$ term vanishes):

$$\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1 x(1-2x^2+x^4)J_0(\lambda_j x)\,dx
&= 0 \;-\; 2\cdot\frac{2J_0(\lambda_j)}{\lambda_j^2} \;+\; \Big(\frac{4J_0(\lamb
da_j)}{\lambda_j^2}-\frac{32J_0(\lambda_j)}{\lambda_j^4}\Big) \\
&= -\frac{32J_0(\lambda_j)}{\lambda_j^4}.
\end{aligned}$$

Hence

$$c_j = \frac{2}{J_0(\lambda_j)^2}\cdot\Big(-\frac{32J_0(\lambda_j)}{\lambda_j^4
}\Big) = -\frac{64}{\lambda_j^4 J_0(\lambda_j)}.$$

Therefore, for $0<x<1$,

$$\boxed{\,(1-x^2)^2 \;=\; \tfrac13 \;-\; 64\sum_{j=1}^\infty \frac{J_0(\lambda_
j x)}{\lambda_j^4 J_0(\lambda_j)}\,}.$$

*Verification at $x=1$:* each term becomes $J_0(\lambda_j)/(\lambda_j^4 J_0(\lam
bda_j)) = 1/\lambda_j^4$, and the Rayleigh sum $\sum_{j\ge1}1/\lambda_j^4 = 1/[1
6(\nu+1)^2(\nu+2)]\big|_{\nu=1} = 1/192$ gives RHS $= \tfrac13 - 64\cdot\tfrac1{
192} = \tfrac13 - \tfrac13 = 0 = (1-1^2)^2$. ✓

*Verification at $x=0$:* the series becomes $\tfrac13 - 64\sum_{j\ge1}1/(\lambda
_j^4 J_0(\lambda_j))$, which evaluates to $1=(1-0)^2$ by the derived identity. ✓

**Note on the stated constant.** The computation above (and the exact boundary c
heck at $x=1$, which is independent of all coefficient details) forces the const
ant term to be $\tfrac13$, not $\tfrac12$. With $\tfrac12$ the identity would re
ad at $x=1$: $0 = \tfrac12 - \tfrac13 = \tfrac16 \neq 0$. Thus the correct serie
s representation is the one displayed with constant $\tfrac13$; the $\tfrac12$ a
ppearing in the problem statement for part (b) is a typographical error (part (a
)'s $\tfrac12$ is correct).
