Looking at this problem, I need to prove that an entire, 1-periodic function $f$
 that is real on two vertical lines and decays to 0 in a strip must be identical
ly zero.

**Proof.**

Since $f$ is entire with period 1, we have $f(z+1)=f(z)$ for all $z\in\mathbb{C}
$.

**Step 1: Reflection Principle.** Since $f$ is entire and real on the line $\ope
ratorname{Re}(z)=1$, define $h(z)=\overline{f(2-\bar z)}$. This is entire (compo
sition of anti-holomorphic operations yielding a holomorphic function). On $\ope
ratorname{Re}(z)=1$, we have $2-\bar z = z$, so $h(z)=\overline{f(z)}=f(z)$ (sin
ce $f$ is real there). By the identity theorem, $h(z)=f(z)$ for all $z$, giving:
$$f(2-\bar z)=\overline{f(z)} \quad \text{for all } z\in\mathbb{C}. \tag{1}$$

(Note: the condition that $f$ is real on $\operatorname{Re}(z)=2$ is redundant,
since $f(2+iy)=f(1+iy)$ by periodicity, and the latter is real.)

**Step 2: Decay extends to all of $\mathbb{R}$ and both directions.** The hypoth
esis gives: for every $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $Y>0$ such that $|f(x+iy)|<\
varepsilon$ for all $x\in[1,2]$ and $y>Y$.

By periodicity, for any $x_0\in\mathbb{R}$, there exists $x_1\in[1,2]$ with $x_0
-x_1\in\mathbb{Z}$, so $f(x_0+iy)=f(x_1+iy)$, hence $|f(x_0+iy)|<\varepsilon$ fo
r $y>Y$. Thus:
$$f(x+iy)\to 0 \text{ as } y\to+\infty, \text{ uniformly in } x\in\mathbb{R}. \t
ag{2}$$

For $y\to-\infty$: write $y=-t$ with $t\to+\infty$. By (1), $f(x+iy)=\overline{f
(2-x-iy)}=\overline{f(2-x+it)}$. As $t\to+\infty$, by (2) we have $f(2-x+it)\to
0$, so:
$$f(x+iy)\to 0 \text{ as } y\to-\infty, \text{ uniformly in } x\in\mathbb{R}. \t
ag{3}$$

**Step 3: Transfer to the punctured plane.** Define $g(w)=f\!\left(\frac{\log w}
{2\pi i}\right)$ for $w\in\mathbb{C}^*$. Since $f$ has period 1, $g$ is well-def
ined (independent of branch of $\log$) and holomorphic on $\mathbb{C}^*$.

Under $w=e^{2\pi i z}$: when $\operatorname{Im}(z)=y\to+\infty$, $|w|=e^{-2\pi y
}\to 0$; when $y\to-\infty$, $|w|\to\infty$. The uniform decay (2),(3) over one
period in $x$ translates to:
$$g(w)\to 0 \text{ as } w\to 0 \quad \text{and} \quad g(w)\to 0 \text{ as } w\to
\infty.$$

**Step 4: Conclusion.** Since $g(w)\to 0$ as $w\to 0$, the singularity at $0$ is
 removable with $g(0)=0$. Since $g(w)\to 0$ as $w\to\infty$, the singularity at
$\infty$ is removable with $g(\infty)=0$. Thus $g$ extends to a holomorphic func
tion on $\hat{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}$.

A function holomorphic on all of $\hat{\mathbb{C}}$ is constant (by Liouville's
theorem). Since $g(0)=0$, we have $g\equiv 0$, and therefore $f\equiv 0$. $\blac
ksquare$
