Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether $\|A\| = \|A^t\|$ where $A^
t = [a_{j,i}]$ is the transpose (not the adjoint).

**Answer: Yes, the operator norms are equal.**

**Proof.**

The space $\mathcal{H}^{\oplus n}$ carries the natural direct-sum inner product
$$\langle (h_1,\dots,h_n),(g_1,\dots,g_n)\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n \langle h_i, g_i
\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.$$

Under the identification $\mathcal{H}^{\oplus n} \cong \mathcal{H}\otimes \mathb
b{C}^n$, the operator $A = [a_{ij}]$ (with scalar entries) acts as
$$A = I_{\mathcal{H}} \otimes M, \qquad M = [a_{ij}] \in M_n(\mathbb{C}),$$
acting as the identity on $\mathcal{H}$ and as $M$ on $\mathbb{C}^n$. Likewise $
A^t = I_{\mathcal{H}} \otimes M^t$. Since the operator norm of a tensor product
of bounded operators is the product of the norms,
$$\|A\| = \|M\|, \qquad \|A^t\| = \|M^t\|,$$
where $\|M\|, \|M^t\|$ are the operator norms on $\mathbb{C}^n$ with the standar
d inner product. It suffices to show $\|M\| = \|M^t\|$.

Recall that for any matrix $B$, $\|B\|^2 = \lambda_{\max}(B^*B)$, where $B^* = \
overline{B^t}$ is the conjugate transpose. Thus
$$\|M\|^2 = \lambda_{\max}(M^* M) = \lambda_{\max}(\overline{M^t}\, M),$$
$$\|M^t\|^2 = \lambda_{\max}\big((M^t)^*\, M^t\big) = \lambda_{\max}(\overline{M
}\, M^t).$$

I claim $\overline{M^t}\, M$ and $\overline{M}\, M^t$ have the same eigenvalues.
 The argument proceeds in two steps.

**Step 1 ($AB$ and $BA$ share eigenvalues).** For any square matrices $B, C$ of
the same size, $BC$ and $CB$ have the same characteristic polynomial. Indeed, fo
r $\lambda \neq 0$, by Sylvester's determinant identity $\det(I - \lambda^{-1}BC
) = \det(I - \lambda^{-1}CB)$, so $\det(BC - \lambda I) = \det(CB - \lambda I)$;
 the case $\lambda = 0$ follows by continuity (or directly from $\det(BC)=\det B
\det C = \det(CB)$).

Applying this with $B = \overline{M}$, $C = M^t$:
$$\overline{M}\, M^t \sim_{\text{eig}} M^t\, \overline{M}.$$

**Step 2 (transpose preserves eigenvalues).** A matrix and its transpose have th
e same characteristic polynomial (since $\det(B^t - \lambda I) = \det(B - \lambd
a I)$). Now observe:
$$(\overline{M^t}\, M)^t = M^t\, (\overline{M^t})^t = M^t\, \overline{M},$$
using $(\overline{M^t})^t = \overline{(M^t)^t} = \overline{M}$. Therefore
$$M^t\, \overline{M} \sim_{\text{eig}} \overline{M^t}\, M.$$

**Combining:** $\overline{M}\, M^t \sim_{\text{eig}} M^t\, \overline{M} \sim_{\t
ext{eig}} \overline{M^t}\, M = M^*M.$

Hence
$$\|M^t\|^2 = \lambda_{\max}(\overline{M}\, M^t) = \lambda_{\max}(\overline{M^t}
\, M) = \lambda_{\max}(M^* M) = \|M\|^2.$$

Since norms are non-negative, $\|M^t\| = \|M\|$, and therefore
$$\boxed{\|A\| = \|A^t\|.}$$

**Remark.** The same conclusion holds for real Hilbert spaces (where $M^* = M^t$
 and the argument reduces directly to $M^t M \sim_{\text{eig}} M M^t$). The esse
ntial ingredients are: (i) $AB$ and $BA$ have identical spectra for square matri
ces, and (ii) a matrix and its transpose have identical spectra.
