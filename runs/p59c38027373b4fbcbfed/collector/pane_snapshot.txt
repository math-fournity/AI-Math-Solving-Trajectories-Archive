## Proof

Let $f$ be an entire function. Since $f$ is entire, it admits a power series exp
ansion centered at the origin that converges for all $z \in \mathbb{C}$:

$$f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n z^n, \qquad a_n = \frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}.$$

Consider $g(z) = f(1/z)$, which is holomorphic on $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$.
Substituting $w = 1/z$ into the power series gives the Laurent expansion of $g$
about $0$:

$$g(z) = f(1/z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n z^{-n} = a_0 + \frac{a_1}{z} + \frac{a
_2}{z^2} + \cdots.$$

Recall that $0$ is a **removable singularity** of $g$ if and only if the Laurent
 expansion of $g$ about $0$ contains no terms with negative powers of $z$; equiv
alently, $\lim_{z \to 0} g(z)$ exists (and is finite).

Therefore, $0$ is a removable singularity of $f(1/z)$ if and only if

$$a_n = 0 \quad \text{for all } n \geq 1.$$

This forces

$$f(z) = a_0,$$

i.e., $f$ is a constant function.

**Verification.** If $f(z) = c$ is constant, then $f(1/z) = c$ for all $z \neq 0
$, and defining $g(0) = c$ extends $g$ to an entire function. Hence $0$ is (triv
ially) a removable singularity. Conversely, if $f$ is non-constant entire, then
some $a_n \neq 0$ for $n \geq 1$:

- If only finitely many $a_n$ ($n \geq 1$) are nonzero, then $f(1/z)$ has a pole
 at $0$.
- If infinitely many $a_n$ ($n \geq 1$) are nonzero, then $f(1/z)$ has an essent
ial singularity at $0$ (by the Casorati–Weierstrass characterization, or directl
y: the Laurent series has infinitely many negative-power terms).

In neither case is $0$ removable.

**Conclusion.** The entire functions $f$ for which $0$ is a removable singularit
y of $f(1/z)$ are precisely the constant functions:

$$\boxed{f(z) \equiv c, \quad c \in \mathbb{C}.}$$
