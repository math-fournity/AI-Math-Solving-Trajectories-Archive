## Proof

Let $R = \mathbb{Q}[x,y,z]$ and let $I = \langle x, y, z \rangle$. The Koszul co
mplex $K = K_\bullet(x,y,z;R)$ associated to the ordered sequence $(x, y, z)$ is

$$K: \quad 0 \longrightarrow R \xrightarrow{\;\phi_3\;} R^3 \xrightarrow{\;\phi_
2\;} R^3 \xrightarrow{\;\phi_1\;} R \longrightarrow 0.$$

We label the modules by homological degree:
- $K_3 = R$, $K_2 = R^3$, $K_1 = R^3$, $K_0 = R$.

**The differentials.** With respect to the standard bases, the Koszul differenti
als are the exterior-multiplication maps:

$$\phi_1 : R^3 \to R, \qquad (a,b,c) \mapsto ax + by + cz,$$

$$\phi_2 : R^3 \to R^3, \qquad (p,q,r) \mapsto \bigl(-py-qz,\; -qy+rz,\; px+ry\b
igr),$$

equivalently $\phi_2$ is left-multiplication by the matrix

$$\phi_2 = \begin{pmatrix} -y & -z & 0 \\ 0 & y & z \\ x & 0 & y \end{pmatrix} \
quad \text{(up to the standard sign convention)},$$

and

$$\phi_3 : R \to R^3, \qquad 1 \mapsto (z, -y, x).$$

One verifies directly that $\phi_1 \circ \phi_2 = 0$ and $\phi_2 \circ \phi_3 =
0$, so $K$ is indeed a chain complex.

**The first homology.** By definition,

$$H_1(K) = \ker(\phi_1) \,/\, \operatorname{im}(\phi_2).$$

Here $\ker(\phi_1) = \{(a,b,c) \in R^3 : ax + by + cz = 0\}$ is the (first) syzy
gy module of $I$, and $\operatorname{im}(\phi_2)$ is the module of Koszul syzygi
es generated by the three rows/columns of $\phi_2$.

**Exactness from regularity.** The sequence $(x, y, z)$ is a **regular sequence*
* in $R = \mathbb{Q}[x,y,z]$:

1. $x$ is a non-zerodivisor in the domain $R$;
2. $y$ is a non-zerodivisor in $R/\langle x \rangle \cong \mathbb{Q}[y,z]$;
3. $z$ is a non-zerodivisor in $R/\langle x,y\rangle \cong \mathbb{Q}[z]$.

A fundamental theorem of the Koszul complex states:

> **Theorem.** If $f_1, \dots, f_n$ is an $R$-regular sequence, then the Koszul
complex $K_\bullet(f_1,\dots,f_n;R)$ is acyclic, i.e. $H_i(K_\bullet) = 0$ for a
ll $i \geq 1$.

*Proof of the theorem (sketch, by induction on $n$).* For $n=1$, $K_\bullet(f_1)
$ is $0 \to R \xrightarrow{\cdot f_1} R \to 0$, and $H_1 = \ker(\cdot f_1) = 0$
precisely because $f_1$ is a non-zerodivisor. For the inductive step, $K_\bullet
(f_1,\dots,f_n) \cong K_\bullet(f_1,\dots,f_{n-1}) \otimes_R K_\bullet(f_n)$, an
d the Künneth spectral sequence together with the fact that $f_n$ is regular on
$R/\langle f_1,\dots,f_{n-1}\rangle$ collapses, giving acyclicity. $\square$

Applying the theorem to the regular sequence $(x, y, z)$ in $R$, the Koszul comp
lex $K$ is acyclic. In particular,

$$H_1(K) = \ker(\phi_1)\,/\,\operatorname{im}(\phi_2) = 0.$$

Equivalently, $\ker(\phi_1) = \operatorname{im}(\phi_2)$: every syzygy of $(x,y,
z)$ is a Koszul syzygy, which is precisely Hilbert's syzygy theorem statement fo
r the maximal ideal of $\mathbb{Q}[x,y,z]$.

**Conclusion.** Since $(x,y,z)$ is a regular sequence in $R = \mathbb{Q}[x,y,z]$
, the Koszul complex is exact, and therefore

$$\boxed{H_1(K) = \ker(\phi_1)/\operatorname{im}(\phi_2) = 0.}$$
