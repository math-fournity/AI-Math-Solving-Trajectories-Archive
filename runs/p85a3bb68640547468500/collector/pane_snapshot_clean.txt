## Proof

We compute the Lebesgue integral
$$\int_{[0,\pi]} \sin x \cdot \epsilon[\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}]\, d\mu,$$
where $\epsilon[\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}]$ is the characteristic function o
f the irrationals.

**Step 1: Identify the integrand.** Let
$$f(x) = \sin x \cdot \epsilon[\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}](x).$$
For $x \in [0,\pi]$:
- If $x \in \mathbb{Q}$, then $\epsilon[\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}](x) = 0$,
so $f(x) = 0$.
- If $x \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$, then $\epsilon[\mathbb{R}\setminus\m
athbb{Q}](x) = 1$, so $f(x) = \sin x$.

**Step 2: Compare with $\sin x$ almost everywhere.** Let $g(x) = \sin x$ on $[0,
\pi]$. The set where $f$ and $g$ differ is
$$\{x \in [0,\pi] : f(x) \neq g(x)\} = [0,\pi] \cap \mathbb{Q}.$$
Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable, $\mu([0,\pi]\cap\mathbb{Q}) = 0$. Hence $f = g$
 almost everywhere on $[0,\pi]$.

**Step 3: Equality of the integrals.** Functions that agree almost everywhere ha
ve equal Lebesgue integrals (provided one is integrable, which implies the other
 is). Therefore
$$\int_{[0,\pi]} f\, d\mu = \int_{[0,\pi]} \sin x\, d\mu.$$

**Step 4: Compute the integral.** The function $\sin x$ is continuous, hence Rie
mann integrable, and its Lebesgue integral coincides with its Riemann integral:
$$\int_{[0,\pi]} \sin x\, d\mu = \int_0^\pi \sin x\, dx = \Big[-\cos x\Big]_0^\p
i = -\cos\pi + \cos 0 = -(-1) + 1 = 2.$$

**Conclusion.** The value of the Lebesgue integral is

$$\boxed{2}$$
