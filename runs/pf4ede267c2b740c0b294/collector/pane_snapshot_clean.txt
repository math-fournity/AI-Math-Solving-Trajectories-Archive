Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether an order-preserving group a
ction on an ordered set forces the group to be left-orderable.

## Analysis

The key observation is that the problem does **not** require the action to be **
faithful** (i.e., different group elements may act identically). Nor does it spe
cify that the order is **linear** (total). This leaves room for counterexamples.

**Key fact**: Every left-orderable group is torsion-free.

*Proof of fact*: Suppose $G$ admits a left-invariant total order $<$, and let $g
 \neq e$ with $g^n = e$ for some $n \geq 2$. Since $<$ is total, either $e < g$
or $g < e$.

- If $e < g$: multiplying on the left by $g$ gives $g < g^2$, and iterating, $e
< g < g^2 < \cdots < g^n = e$, a contradiction.
- If $g < e$: multiplying on the left by $g^{-1}$ gives $e < g^{-1}$. Since $g^{
-1}$ also has order $n$, this reduces to the first case, again a contradiction.

So no left-orderable group can contain torsion. $\square$

## Counterexample

Let $G = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} = \{e, \sigma\}$ with $\sigma^2 = e$, and let $G
$ act on $\mathbb{Z}$ (with its usual linear order) by the **trivial action**: $
g \cdot n = n$ for all $g \in G$, $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

**The action preserves order**: If $a < b$, then $g \cdot a = a < b = g \cdot b$
 for every $g \in G$. ✓

**But $G$ is not left-orderable**: $G$ contains $\sigma$ of order 2, so $G$ has
torsion. By the fact above, $G$ cannot admit any left-invariant total order.

## Conclusion

The implication does **not** hold in general. An order-preserving action on an o
rdered set does not imply the existence of a left order on $G$.

The obstruction is that the action may fail to be **faithful** — here the entire
 nontrivial element $\sigma$ lies in the kernel, so the action carries no inform
ation that could distinguish $\sigma$ from $e$ in any induced order. (Indeed, th
e standard positive result is: $G$ is left-orderable **if and only if** $G$ acts
 **faithfully** on a **linearly** ordered set by order-preserving permutations.
The problem as stated omits both hypotheses.)
