**Answer: Yes.** For every sequence $(a_n)_{n=0}^\infty$ in a real Banach space
$X$, there exists $f \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}, X)$ with $f^{(n)}(0) = a_n$ for al
l $n$.

---

## Proof

This is the Borel theorem (Borel's lemma) extended to Banach-space-valued functi
ons. The completeness of $X$ is essential: it lets us apply the Weierstrass $M$-
test and differentiate term-by-term.

### Setup

Choose a bump function $\phi \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ with
$$\phi \equiv 1 \text{ on } [-1,1], \qquad \operatorname{supp}(\phi) \subset [-2
,2].$$
Set $M_j := \|\phi^{(j)}\|_\infty < \infty$ for each $j \geq 0$.

For each $n \geq 0$ define
$$g_n(x) := \frac{a_n}{n!}\, x^n\, \phi(b_n x),$$
where the parameters $b_n \geq 1$ will be chosen below. The candidate is
$$f(x) := \sum_{n=0}^\infty g_n(x).$$

### Key properties of $g_n$

**Support.** Since $\operatorname{supp}(\phi) \subset [-2,2]$, each $g_n$ is sup
ported in $[-2/b_n,\, 2/b_n]$.

**Value at the origin.** For $|x| < 1/b_n$ we have $\phi(b_n x) = 1$, so $g_n(x)
 = \frac{a_n}{n!} x^n$ near $0$. Consequently
$$g_n^{(k)}(0) = \begin{cases} a_n & \text{if } k = n, \\ 0 & \text{if } k \neq
n. \end{cases} \tag{$\star$}$$

**Derivative bound.** By the Leibniz rule, for any $k \geq 0$:
$$g_n^{(k)}(x) = \frac{a_n}{n!} \sum_{j=0}^{\min(k,n)} \binom{k}{j} \frac{n!}{(n
-j)!}\, x^{n-j}\, b_n^{k-j}\, \phi^{(k-j)}(b_n x).$$

On the support $|x| \leq 2/b_n$, so $|x^{n-j}| \leq (2/b_n)^{n-j}$. Substituting
:
$$\|g_n^{(k)}\|_\infty \leq \|a_n\|\, b_n^{k-n} \underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{\min(k,n
)} \binom{k}{j} \frac{2^{n-j}}{(n-j)!}\, M_{k-j}}_{=:\, S_{k,n}}. \tag{1}$$

**Estimating $S_{k,n}$ for $n > k$.** When $n > k$, the sum runs over $j = 0, \l
dots, k$. The $j$-th term satisfies
$$\frac{2^{n-j}}{(n-j)!} = \frac{2^n}{n!}\cdot\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-j+1)}{2^j} \l
eq \frac{2^n}{n!}\cdot\frac{n^j}{2^j}.$$

Hence
$$S_{k,n} \leq \frac{2^n}{n!}\underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{k} \binom{k}{j}\frac{n^j}{2
^j}\, M_{k-j}}_{=:\, R_k(n)},$$
where $R_k(n)$ is a polynomial in $n$ of degree $k$ (with coefficients depending
 on $k$ and the $M_j$). Combining with (1), for $n > k$:
$$\|g_n^{(k)}\|_\infty \leq \|a_n\|\, \frac{2^n\, R_k(n)}{n!\, b_n^{\,n-k}}. \ta
g{2}$$

### Choosing $b_n$

For each $n \geq 1$, select $b_n \geq 1$ large enough that for **every** $k \in
\{0, 1, \ldots, n-1\}$:
$$\|a_n\|\, \frac{2^n\, R_k(n)}{n!\, b_n^{\,n-k}} \leq 2^{-n}. \tag{3}$$

This is a finite system of lower bounds on $b_n$ (one per value of $k < n$), eac
h of the form
$$b_n \geq \left(\|a_n\|\, \frac{4^n\, R_k(n)}{n!}\right)^{1/(n-k)},$$
so a suitable $b_n$ always exists. (If $a_n = 0$, set $b_n = 1$; all constraints
 are trivially met.)

### Convergence of the series

Fix $k \geq 0$. We show $\sum_{n=0}^\infty \|g_n^{(k)}\|_\infty < \infty$:

- **Finitely many terms $n \leq k$:** each $\|g_n^{(k)}\|_\infty$ is a finite co
nstant (depending on the already-chosen $b_n$). Their sum is finite.

- **Tail $n > k$:** by the choice (3),
$$\sum_{n > k} \|g_n^{(k)}\|_\infty \leq \sum_{n > k} 2^{-n} < \infty.$$

Therefore $\sum_n g_n^{(k)}$ converges **uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$** (in the nor
m of $X$) for every $k$, by the Weierstrass $M$-test — valid here because $X$ is
 **complete**.

### $f$ is smooth

We prove by induction on $k$ that $f$ is $k$-times differentiable with $f^{(k)}
= \sum_n g_n^{(k)}$ (uniformly convergent).

*Base case ($k=0$):* Uniform convergence of $\sum g_n$ gives a continuous $X$-va
lued limit.

*Inductive step:* Assume $f^{(k)} = \sum_n g_n^{(k)}$ (uniformly). Each $g_n^{(k
)}$ is $C^1$ with $(g_n^{(k)})' = g_n^{(k+1)}$, and $\sum_n g_n^{(k+1)}$ converg
es uniformly. By the Banach-valued differentiation theorem (justified below), $f
^{(k)}$ is differentiable and
$$(f^{(k)})' = \sum_n g_n^{(k+1)} =: f^{(k+1)}.$$

**Justification of term-by-term differentiation in Banach spaces.** For $C^1$ fu
nctions $u_n : \mathbb{R} \to X$ with $\sum u_n$ converging at one point and $\s
um u_n'$ converging uniformly, the identity
$$u_n(x) - u_n(a) = \int_a^x u_n'(t)\,dt$$
(the fundamental theorem of calculus for Banach-valued functions, valid for the
Riemann/Bochner integral) gives, upon summing and interchanging sum and integral
 (permitted by uniform convergence),
$$\sum_n u_n(x) = \sum_n u_n(a) + \int_a^x \sum_n u_n'(t)\,dt.$$
The right-hand side is $C^1$ with derivative $\sum_n u_n'(x)$. $\square$

Thus $f \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}, X)$.

### The Taylor coefficients match

Using $(\star)$ and the term-by-term differentiation just established:
$$f^{(k)}(0) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty g_n^{(k)}(0) = g_k^{(k)}(0) = a_k \qquad \text{
for every } k \geq 0.$$

### Conclusion

For **every** sequence $(a_n)$ in a real Banach space $X$, the function
$$f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{a_n}{n!}\, x^n\, \phi(b_n x),$$
with $b_n$ chosen as above, is smooth $\mathbb{R} \to X$ and satisfies $f^{(n)}(
0) = a_n$ for all $n$. The completeness of $X$ is used crucially (Weierstrass $M
$-test and the Banach-valued FTC). $\blacksquare$
