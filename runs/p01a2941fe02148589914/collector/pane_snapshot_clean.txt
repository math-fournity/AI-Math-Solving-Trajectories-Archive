## Proof

We evaluate the integral

$$I = \int_{0}^{2\pi} e^{e^{it}} \, dt.$$

**Step 1: Expand the integrand as a power series.**

The exponential function has the everywhere-convergent power series $e^{z} = \su
m_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{z^{n}}{n!}$. Substituting $z = e^{it}$ (which has $|z|=1$
) gives

$$e^{e^{it}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(e^{it})^{n}}{n!} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
 \frac{e^{int}}{n!}.$$

**Step 2: Justify termwise integration.**

The series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{int}}{n!}$ converges uniformly on $[0,2
\pi]$ since $\left|\frac{e^{int}}{n!}\right| \le \frac{1}{n!}$ and $\sum \frac{1
}{n!} = e < \infty$ (Weierstrass $M$-test). Uniform convergence permits exchangi
ng the sum and integral:

$$I = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n!} \int_{0}^{2\pi} e^{int} \, dt.$$

**Step 3: Evaluate each integral.**

For $n = 0$: $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi} e^{0} \, dt = 2\pi$.

For $n \ge 1$: $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi} e^{int} \, dt = \left[\frac{e^{int}
}{in}\right]_{0}^{2\pi} = \frac{e^{2\pi i n} - 1}{in} = \frac{1-1}{in} = 0$,

since $e^{2\pi i n} = 1$ for every integer $n$.

**Step 4: Conclude.**

Only the $n=0$ term survives:

$$I = \frac{1}{0!}\cdot 2\pi + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\cdot 0 = 2\pi.$$

Therefore,

$$\boxed{\int_{0}^{2\pi} e^{e^{it}} \, dt = 2\pi.}$$
