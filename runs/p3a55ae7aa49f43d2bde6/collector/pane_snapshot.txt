## Solution

**Yes, such a function exists.** A simple example is:

$$f : \mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}, \qqua
d f(x) = x + 1.$$

**Verification of the three required properties.**

1. **Well-defined (codomain is the irrationals).** Let $x \in \mathbb{R} \setmin
us \mathbb{Q}$. Suppose for contradiction that $f(x) = x+1 \in \mathbb{Q}$. Then
 $x = (x+1) - 1$ would be the difference of two rationals, hence rational — cont
radicting $x \notin \mathbb{Q}$. Therefore $f(x) \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \mathb
b{Q}$.

2. **Continuity.** The map $g : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $g(x) = x+1$, is con
tinuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$. The function $f$ is the restriction of $g$ to th
e subspace $\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{R}$. A restriction
 of a continuous function to a subspace is continuous in the subspace topology.
Hence $f$ is continuous.

3. **No fixed point.** For every $x$, $f(x) = x + 1 \neq x$, since $1 \neq 0$.

All three conditions are satisfied, so a continuous function $f : \mathbb{R} \se
tminus \mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$ with $f(x) \neq x$ for al
l $x \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$ **does exist**, with $f(x) = x+1$ bein
g an explicit example. $\blacksquare$
