## Proof

**Claim:** No. There does **not** exist an $L^1$ function whose Fourier series c
onverges almost everywhere but fails to converge to the function itself. In othe
r words, if $f \in L^1(\mathbb{T})$ and its Fourier series converges a.e. to som
e finite-valued function $g$, then $g = f$ a.e.

---

### Setup and notation

Let $f \in L^1(\mathbb{T})$, and let
$$S_N(f)(x) = \sum_{k=-N}^{N} \hat{f}(k)\, e^{ikx}$$
denote the $N$-th symmetric partial sum of its Fourier series, where $\hat{f}(k)
 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(t)\,e^{-ikt}\,dt$.

Let
$$\sigma_N(f)(x) = \frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N} S_n(f)(x)$$
denote the $(N)$-th Cesàro (Fejér) mean.

**Assumption:** $S_N(f)(x) \to g(x)$ for a.e. $x$, where $g$ is some finite-valu
ed measurable function. We show $g = f$ a.e.

---

### Step 1: Cesàro means inherit the a.e. limit

This is a basic fact from real analysis: if a sequence of real (or complex) numb
ers $a_n \to a$, then its Cesàro means $\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N} a_n \to a$.

Applying this pointwise at every $x$ where $S_N(f)(x) \to g(x)$:

$$\sigma_N(f)(x) = \frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N} S_n(f)(x) \;\longrightarrow\; g(x
) \quad \text{for a.e. } x. \tag{1}$$

---

### Step 2: Fejér means converge to $f$ in $L^1$

The Fejér kernels $F_N$ form an **approximate identity** (non-negative, $\frac{1
}{2\pi}\int F_N = 1$, and $F_N$ concentrate at the origin). A standard theorem i
n harmonic analysis states:

**Fejér's theorem (L¹ version):** For every $f \in L^1(\mathbb{T})$,
$$\|\sigma_N(f) - f\|_{L^1} \to 0 \quad \text{as } N \to \infty. \tag{2}$$

---

### Step 3: Extract an a.e.-convergent subsequence

A standard consequence of $L^1$-convergence (via the Borel–Cantelli argument / R
iesz–Fischer) is:

> If $h_N \to h$ in $L^1$, then there exists a subsequence $h_{N_k}$ such that $
h_{N_k} \to h$ a.e.

Applying this to (2): there exists a subsequence $\sigma_{N_k}(f)$ such that

$$\sigma_{N_k}(f)(x) \to f(x) \quad \text{for a.e. } x. \tag{3}$$

---

### Step 4: Identify the limits

From (1), the **full** sequence $\sigma_N(f)(x) \to g(x)$ a.e. Therefore every s
ubsequence — in particular $\sigma_{N_k}(f)$ — also converges to $g$ a.e.:

$$\sigma_{N_k}(f)(x) \to g(x) \quad \text{for a.e. } x. \tag{4}$$

Combining (3) and (4): at a.e. point $x$, the same numerical sequence $\sigma_{N
_k}(f)(x)$ converges to both $f(x)$ and $g(x)$. By uniqueness of limits,

$$f(x) = g(x) \quad \text{for a.e. } x.$$

---

### Conclusion

Whenever the Fourier series of an $L^1$ function converges almost everywhere, th
e limit **must** be the function itself (a.e.). The key mechanism is:

1. Pointwise convergence of $S_N$ is inherited by the Cesàro means $\sigma_N$ (r
eal-analysis fact).
2. The Cesàro means $\sigma_N$ always converge to $f$ in $L^1$ (Fejér's theorem,
 valid for all $L^1$ functions).
3. $L^1$-convergence yields an a.e.-convergent subsequence, forcing the pointwis
e limit to coincide with $f$.

Therefore, **no** $L^1$ function exists whose Fourier series converges a.e. but
to something other than the function. $\blacksquare$
