## Solution

**Step 1: Simplify the sum.**

Let $S(n) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} \frac{n!}{(n-i)!}$. Substituting $k = n-i$ (so $i=0 \
Rightarrow k=n$, $i=n \Rightarrow k=0$):

$$S(n) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{n!}{k!} = n! \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{1}{k!}.$$

**Step 2: Asymptotic behavior.**

It is a classical fact that $\sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{1}{k!} \to e$ as $n \to \infty
$. Therefore:

$$S(n) = n! \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{1}{k!} \sim e \cdot n! \quad \text{as } n \to \
infty.$$

**Step 3: Define $f$ and verify the limit.**

Set $f(n) = e \cdot n!$. Then:

$$\frac{S(n)}{f(n)} = \frac{n! \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{1}{k!}}{e \cdot n!} = \frac{
\sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{1}{k!}}{e} \longrightarrow \frac{e}{e} = 1 \quad \text{as }
 n \to \infty.$$

So the limit condition is satisfied.

**Step 4: Verify $f(n) \neq S(n)$ for all $n$.**

We need $e \cdot n! \neq n! \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{1}{k!}$, i.e., $e \neq \sum_{k=
0}^{n} \frac{1}{k!}$.

For every finite $n$, the partial sum $\sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{1}{k!}$ is a **ratio
nal number** (a finite sum of rationals). Since $e$ is **irrational**, we have $
e \neq \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{1}{k!}$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Hence $f(n) \neq
 S(n)$ for all $n$.

**Conclusion.**

Yes, such a function exists. One valid definition is:

$$\boxed{f(n) = e \cdot n!}$$

This satisfies $\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{S(n)}{f(n)} = 1$ while $f(n) \neq S(n)
$ for every $n$, since the irrationality of $e$ guarantees the partial sums $\su
m_{k=0}^{n} \frac{1}{k!}$ never equal $e$.
