**Case 1: $a_0 \equiv 2 \pmod{3}$.** The sequence is $a_n = a_0 + 3n$, diverging

**Case 2: $a_0 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$.** We show the sequence always diverges.

Define a **round**: starting from $s \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$, $s > 1$, add $3$ until
reaching a perfect square $p \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$ (which exists since such squares
 $(3j+1)^2, (3j+2)^2$ are unbounded with gaps that are multiples of $3$). Then t

- If $\sqrt{p} \equiv 2 \pmod{3}$: the sequence diverges (Case 1).
- If $\sqrt{p} \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$ and $\sqrt{p} > 1$: set $s' = \sqrt{p}$ and be
gin a new round.

**Claim:** $s' < s$ for all $s \geq 7$.

*Proof of claim:* The perfect squares $\equiv 1 \pmod{3}$ near $s$ are $(3j+1)^2

})$. Since $p$ is the smallest such square $\geq s$, we have $p \leq s + O(\sqrt
{s})$, so $s' = \sqrt{p} \leq \sqrt{s + O(\sqrt{s})} < s$ for $s \geq 7$. $\squa
re$

Since $s \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$ and $s > 1$ implies $s \geq 4$, the strictly decreas
ing sequence of round-starting values (for $s \geq 7$) must eventually reach $s
= 4$. At $s = 4$: since $4 = 2^2$ and $\sqrt{4} = 2 \equiv 2 \pmod{3}$, the sequ
ence diverges.

Therefore **all** $a_0 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$ diverge. ✗
