## Proof

We evaluate

$$
I \;=\; \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos x}{x^{2}}\,dx
$$

by contour integration.

---

### Step 1. Choice of integrand and contour

Set

$$
f(z)=\frac{1-e^{iz}}{z^{2}}.
$$

Near $z=0$ we have $e^{iz}=1+iz-\frac{z^{2}}{2}-\frac{iz^{3}}{6}+\cdots$, so

$$
1-e^{iz}=-iz+\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\frac{iz^{3}}{6}-\cdots,
\qquad
f(z)=-\frac{i}{z}+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{iz}{6}-\cdots.
$$

Thus $f$ has a **simple pole** at $z=0$ with residue $\operatorname{Res}(f,0)=-i
$.

We integrate $f$ over the **indented upper-half-plane contour** $\Gamma$ consist
ing of (traversed counterclockwise):

- the real segment $[\varepsilon,R]$,
- the large semicircle $C_{R}:\,z=Re^{i\theta},\,0\le\theta\le\pi$,
- the real segment $[-R,-\varepsilon]$,
- the small semicircle $C_{\varepsilon}:\,z=\varepsilon e^{i\theta},\,\theta:\pi
\to0$ (traversed clockwise, from $-\varepsilon$ to $\varepsilon$).

The pole at $0$ lies **outside** $\Gamma$, and $f$ is holomorphic inside $\Gamma
$, so by Cauchy's theorem

$$
\int_{\Gamma}f(z)\,dz=0.
$$

---

### Step 2. The large arc $C_{R}$ vanishes

For $z=x+iy$ with $y\ge0$, $|e^{iz}|=e^{-y}\le1$, hence $|1-e^{iz}|\le2$. Theref
ore

$$
|f(z)|\le\frac{2}{R^{2}}\quad\text{on }C_{R},
$$

and

$$
\left|\int_{C_{R}}f(z)\,dz\right|\le\frac{2}{R^{2}}\cdot\pi R=\frac{2\pi}{R}\xri
ghtarrow[R\to\infty]{}0.
$$

---

### Step 3. The small arc $C_{\varepsilon}$

Using the Laurent expansion $f(z)=-\dfrac{i}{z}+\dfrac{1}{2}+O(z)$,

$$
\int_{C_{\varepsilon}}f(z)\,dz
=
\int_{C_{\varepsilon}}\!\left(-\frac{i}{z}\right)dz
+\int_{C_{\varepsilon}}\!\frac{1}{2}\,dz
+\int_{C_{\varepsilon}}\!O(z)\,dz.
$$

Parametrize $C_{\varepsilon}$ by $z=\varepsilon e^{i\theta}$, $\theta:\pi\to0$,
so $\dfrac{dz}{z}=i\,d\theta$:

$$
\int_{C_{\varepsilon}}\!\left(-\frac{i}{z}\right)dz
=
-i\int_{\pi}^{0}i\,d\theta
=
-i\cdot i\cdot(0-\pi)
=
-\pi.
$$

The remaining terms satisfy $\left|\int_{C_{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{2}dz\right|\le
\frac12\pi\varepsilon\to0$ and $\left|\int_{C_{\varepsilon}}O(z)\,dz\right|=O(\v
arepsilon^{2})\to0$. Hence

$$
\lim_{\varepsilon\to0}\int_{C_{\varepsilon}}f(z)\,dz=-\pi.
$$

---

### Step 4. The real-axis contribution

The real-axis pieces give

$$
\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}\frac{1-e^{ix}}{x^{2}}\,dx+\int_{-R}^{-\varepsilon}\frac{1
-e^{ix}}{x^{2}}\,dx
=
\int_{\varepsilon\le|x|\le R}\frac{1-e^{ix}}{x^{2}}\,dx.
$$

Write $1-e^{ix}=(1-\cos x)-i\sin x$. The imaginary part $\dfrac{-\sin x}{x^{2}}$
 is **odd**, so its integral over the symmetric set $\varepsilon\le|x|\le R$ van
ishes. Thus the real-axis contribution equals, exactly,

$$
\int_{\varepsilon\le|x|\le R}\frac{1-\cos x}{x^{2}}\,dx
=
2\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}\frac{1-\cos x}{x^{2}}\,dx.
$$

---

### Step 5. Take the limits

Combining the pieces in $\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}f=0$:

$$
2\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}\frac{1-\cos x}{x^{2}}\,dx
\;+\;
\int_{C_{R}}f(z)\,dz
\;+\;
\int_{C_{\varepsilon}}f(z)\,dz
=0.
$$

Letting $R\to\infty$ and $\varepsilon\to0$ and using Steps 2 and 3,

$$
2\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos x}{x^{2}}\,dx\;+\;0\;-\;\pi=0.
$$

Therefore

$$
\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos x}{x^{2}}\,dx=\frac{\pi}{2}.
$$
