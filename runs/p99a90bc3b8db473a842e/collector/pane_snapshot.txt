## Solution

We determine the nature of the singular point $z=0$ of

$$f(z)=\frac{1}{2+z^{2}-2\cosh z}.$$

Let $g(z) = 2 + z^2 - 2\cosh z$ be the denominator. The singularities of $f$ occ
ur at the zeros of $g$.

**Step 1. Expand the denominator near $z=0$.**

Recall the Taylor expansion

$$\cosh z = 1 + \frac{z^2}{2!} + \frac{z^4}{4!} + \frac{z^6}{6!} + \cdots = 1 +
\frac{z^2}{2} + \frac{z^4}{24} + \frac{z^6}{720} + \cdots$$

Hence

$$2\cosh z = 2 + z^2 + \frac{z^4}{12} + \frac{z^6}{360} + \cdots$$

and therefore

$$g(z) = 2 + z^2 - 2\cosh z = 2 + z^2 - \left(2 + z^2 + \frac{z^4}{12} + \frac{z
^6}{360} + \cdots\right) = -\frac{z^4}{12} - \frac{z^6}{360} - \cdots$$

**Step 2. Determine the order of the zero of $g$ at $z=0$.**

Factoring out the leading term:

$$g(z) = -\frac{z^4}{12}\left(1 + \frac{z^2}{30} + \cdots\right).$$

The factor in parentheses is an analytic function equal to $1$ at $z=0$, hence n
on-vanishing in a neighborhood of $0$. Therefore $g$ has a **zero of order $4$**
 at $z=0$.

**Step 3. Conclude the nature of the singularity of $f$.**

Since $f(z) = 1/g(z)$ and $g$ has a zero of order $4$ at $z=0$ while the numerat
or (the constant $1$) is non-zero there, $f$ has a **pole of order $4$** at $z=0
$.

Explicitly, the Laurent expansion begins

$$f(z) = \frac{1}{g(z)} = -\frac{12}{z^4}\cdot\frac{1}{1 + z^2/30 + \cdots} = -\
frac{12}{z^4} + \frac{2}{5z^2} - \frac{1}{4200} + \cdots,$$

confirming that the highest negative power is $z^{-4}$, with no $z^{-3}$ or $z^{
-1}$ terms (the function is even), and the principal part has finite order.

**Conclusion.** The point $z=0$ is a **pole of order $4$** of the function $f(z)
=\dfrac{1}{2+z^{2}-2\cosh z}$.
