**Answer: No, $L_x$ is not necessarily continuous in $x$.**

### Proof

**Step 1: Identify $L_x$.** Since $f(x,\cdot)\in C^1(\mathbb{R})$, on any interv
al $[\eta-\delta,\eta+\delta]$ the optimal (infimal) Lipschitz constant is $\sup
_{|y-\eta|\le\delta}|f_y(x,y)|$. Taking the infimum over all $\delta>0$ and usin
g continuity of $f_y(x,\cdot)$ yields
$$L_x = |f_y(x,\eta)|.$$

So the question reduces to: is $x\mapsto |f_y(x,\eta)|$ continuous under the giv
en hypotheses?

**Step 2: Construct a counterexample.** Set $I=[0,1]$, $\eta=0$, $K=1$, and defi

$$f(x,y) = \begin{cases} e^{x\sin(y/x)}, & x>0,\\ 1, & x=0. \end{cases}$$

**Verification of all hypotheses:**

*(a) $f\in C^0(I\times\mathbb{R})$.* For $x>0$, $f$ is continuous. At $x=0$: sin

o 0$ as $x\to 0$, uniformly in $y$. Hence $f$ is jointly continuous. ✓

*(b) $\max_{x\in I}|f(x,\eta)|=M<\infty$.* $f(x,0)=e^{x\sin 0}=1$ for all $x$, s
o $M=1$. ✓

*(c) $f$ differentiable in $y$.* For $x>0$: $f_y(x,y)=\cos(y/x)\,e^{x\sin(y/x)}$
. For $x=0$: $f(0,y)\equiv 1$, so $f_y(0,y)=0$. ✓

*(d) $|f_y(x,y)|\le K|f(x,y)|$.* For $x>0$: $|f_y|=|\cos(y/x)|\cdot|f|\le |f|$,

*(e) $f(x,\cdot)\in C^1(\mathbb{R})$ locally Lipschitz.* For $x>0$, $f_y(x,y)=\c

**Step 3: Compute $L_x$ and show discontinuity.**

For $x>0$:
$$L_x = |f_y(x,0)| = |\cos(0)|\cdot e^{0} = 1.$$

For $x=0$:
$$L_0 = |f_y(0,0)| = 0.$$

Therefore
$$L_x = \begin{cases} 1, & x\in(0,1],\\ 0, & x=0, \end{cases}$$
which is **not continuous** at $x=0$.

**Key insight:** The condition $|f_y|\le K|f|$ with $f$ jointly continuous force
