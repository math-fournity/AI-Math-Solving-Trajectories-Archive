# Answer

**No.** A distribution whose support is a single point is *not* necessarily a sc
aled Dirac delta. The correct statement is that such a distribution is a **finit
e linear combination of the Dirac delta at that point and its derivatives**. The
 derivative of the delta, $\delta'_{x_0}$, already provides a counterexample.

---

## Counterexample: $\delta'_{x_0}$

Let $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}$ and let $\delta'_{x_0} \in \mathcal{D}'(\mathbb{R})$ be
 the distributional derivative of the Dirac delta at $x_0$, defined by
$$\langle \delta'_{x_0},\, \varphi \rangle = -\varphi'(x_0), \qquad \varphi \in
C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R}).$$

### Claim 1: $\operatorname{supp}(\delta'_{x_0}) = \{x_0\}$.

Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be any open set with $x_0 \notin U$. For every $\va
rphi \in C_c^\infty(U)$, the function $\varphi$ (and all its derivatives) vanish
 identically in a neighborhood of $x_0$, so in particular $\varphi'(x_0) = 0$. H
ence $\langle \delta'_{x_0}, \varphi \rangle = 0$, which means $\delta'_{x_0}$ v
anishes on $U$. Since this holds for every open $U$ not containing $x_0$,
$$\operatorname{supp}(\delta'_{x_0}) \subseteq \{x_0\}.$$
Conversely, $\delta'_{x_0}$ does not vanish at $x_0$: choosing $\varphi \in C_c^
\infty(\mathbb{R})$ with $\varphi'(x_0) \neq 0$ gives $\langle \delta'_{x_0}, \v
arphi\rangle \neq 0$. Therefore
$$\operatorname{supp}(\delta'_{x_0}) = \{x_0\}.$$

### Claim 2: $\delta'_{x_0}$ is not a scalar multiple of $\delta_{x_0}$.

Suppose for contradiction that $\delta'_{x_0} = c\,\delta_{x_0}$ for some $c \in
 \mathbb{R}$. Choose $\varphi \in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ with $\varphi(x_0) = 0
$ and $\varphi'(x_0) \neq 0$ (e.g., $\varphi(x) = (x - x_0)\rho(x)$ for a bump $
\rho$ with $\rho(x_0) = 1$). Then
$$\langle c\,\delta_{x_0},\, \varphi\rangle = c\,\varphi(x_0) = 0, \qquad \text{
but} \qquad \langle \delta'_{x_0},\, \varphi\rangle = -\varphi'(x_0) \neq 0.$$
This is a contradiction. Hence $\delta'_{x_0} \neq c\,\delta_{x_0}$ for any scal
ar $c$.

**Conclusion so far:** $\delta'_{x_0}$ is a distribution with support $\{x_0\}$
that is not a scaled Dirac delta. The answer to the question is **no**.

## The Correct Characterization

The precise theorem is:

> **Theorem.** Let $u \in \mathcal{D}'(\mathbb{R})$ with $\operatorname{supp}(u)
 = \{x_0\}$. Then there exist an integer $N \geq 0$ and constants $c_0, c_1, \ld
ots, c_N \in \mathbb{R}$ such that
> $$u = \sum_{k=0}^{N} c_k\, \delta^{(k)}_{x_0},$$
> where $\delta^{(k)}_{x_0}$ is the $k$-th distributional derivative of $\delta_
{x_0}$, i.e., $\langle \delta^{(k)}_{x_0}, \varphi\rangle = (-1)^k \varphi^{(k)}
(x_0)$.

### Proof

**Step 1 ($u$ has finite order near $x_0$).** Since $\operatorname{supp}(u) = \{
x_0\}$ is compact, $u$ has compact support. Every compactly supported distributi
on has finite order: there exists $m \geq 0$ and a constant $C > 0$ such that fo
r all $\varphi \in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ supported in a fixed neighborhood $V$
 of $x_0$,
$$|\langle u, \varphi\rangle| \leq C \sum_{k=0}^{m} \sup_{x \in V} |\varphi^{(k)
}(x)|. \tag{$\star$}$$

**Step 2 (Reduction to the jet at $x_0$).** Let $\chi \in C_c^\infty(V)$ with $\
chi \equiv 1$ in a neighborhood of $x_0$. For any $\varphi \in C_c^\infty(\mathb
b{R})$, the function $(1-\chi)\varphi$ is supported away from $x_0$, so $\langle
 u, (1-\chi)\varphi\rangle = 0$, giving
$$\langle u, \varphi\rangle = \langle u, \chi\varphi\rangle.$$
By Taylor's theorem, $\chi\varphi$ can be written as
$$\chi(x)\,\varphi(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{m} \frac{\varphi^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}\,(x-x_0)^k\
,\chi(x) \;+\; (x-x_0)^{m+1}\,\eta(x),$$
where $\eta \in C_c^\infty(V)$ is a smooth remainder (this uses the fact that $\
chi \equiv 1$ near $x_0$, so the Taylor polynomial of $\chi\varphi$ at $x_0$ coi
ncides with that of $\varphi$ up to the $\chi$ factor).

**Step 3 (The remainder vanishes).** We show $\langle u, (x-x_0)^{m+1}\eta\rangl
e = 0$. Set $\psi := (x-x_0)^{m+1}\eta$, which is supported in $V$ and vanishes
to order $m+1$ at $x_0$. For $\epsilon > 0$, define the rescaled family
$$\psi_\epsilon(x) := \frac{1}{\epsilon^{m+1}}\,\psi\!\big(x_0 + \epsilon(x - x_
0)\big).$$
Since $\psi$ vanishes to order $m+1$ at $x_0$, we have $\psi(x_0 + \epsilon(x-x_
0)) = \epsilon^{m+1}(x-x_0)^{m+1}\eta(x_0 + \epsilon(x-x_0))$, so
$$\psi_\epsilon(x) = (x-x_0)^{m+1}\,\eta\!\big(x_0 + \epsilon(x-x_0)\big).$$
As $\epsilon \to 0$, $\psi_\epsilon(x) \to (x-x_0)^{m+1}\eta(x) = \psi(x)$ in $C
_c^\infty(V)$ (all derivatives converge uniformly and supports stay in a fixed c
ompact set). Hence $\langle u, \psi_\epsilon\rangle \to \langle u, \psi\rangle$.

Now we estimate $|\langle u, \psi_\epsilon\rangle|$ using $(\star)$. The derivat
ives are
$$\psi_\epsilon^{(k)}(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\frac{d^j}{dx^j}\!\big[(x-x
_0)^{m+1}\big]\cdot \epsilon^{k-j}\,\eta^{(k-j)}(x_0+\epsilon(x-x_0)).$$
Each term is $O(\epsilon^{k-j})\cdot O(|x-x_0|^{m+1-j})$, uniformly for $x \in V
$. Summing over $k \leq m$, every term carries at least one factor of $\epsilon$
 (since $j \leq k \leq m$ and the derivative of $(x-x_0)^{m+1}$ of order $j \leq
 m$ still leaves a factor $(x-x_0)^{m+1-j}$, while the $\eta$-part contributes $
\epsilon^{k-j} \geq \epsilon^k$... let me be more careful). Actually the key poi
nt: for $k \leq m$,
$$\sup_{V}|\psi_\epsilon^{(k)}| = O(\epsilon) \quad \text{as } \epsilon \to 0,$$
because $\psi_\epsilon^{(k)}(x_0) = 0$ for $k \leq m$ (since $\psi_\epsilon$ van
ishes to order $m+1$ at $x_0$) and the $\epsilon$-scaling makes the supremum shr
ink. More precisely, $\psi_\epsilon^{(k)}(x) = \epsilon^{k} \epsilon^{-(m+1)} \p
si^{(k)}(x_0+\epsilon(x-x_0))$; since $\psi^{(k)}$ vanishes to order $m+1-k$ at
$x_0$, $|\psi^{(k)}(x_0+\epsilon(x-x_0))| \leq C\,|\epsilon(x-x_0)|^{m+1-k}$, so
 $|\psi_\epsilon^{(k)}(x)| \leq C\,\epsilon^{k-(m+1)}\epsilon^{m+1-k}|x-x_0|^{m+
1-k} = C|x-x_0|^{m+1-k}$, which is bounded but **independent of $\epsilon$** — t
his shows boundedness, not decay.

The correct way to get decay: observe that $\langle u, \psi_\epsilon\rangle$ is
also related to $\langle u, \psi\rangle$ by the support property. Since $\operat
orname{supp}(u)=\{x_0\}$, we may insert a cutoff $\rho$ (equal to 1 near $x_0$,
supported in $|x-x_0|\leq r\subset V$):
$$\langle u, \psi_\epsilon\rangle = \langle u, \rho\,\psi_\epsilon\rangle.$$
On the support of $\rho$, $|x-x_0|\leq r$, so $|\psi_\epsilon^{(k)}(x)| \leq C\,
r^{m+1-k}$, and by $(\star)$:
$$|\langle u, \psi_\epsilon\rangle| \leq C'\sum_{k=0}^m r^{m+1-k}.$$
This is a fixed bound, not going to zero. The issue is that $\psi_\epsilon \to \
psi$ and we want $\langle u, \psi\rangle = 0$, but the bound doesn't shrink.

**Revised Step 3 (using the order bound directly).** Instead of rescaling, use $
(\star)$ directly on $\psi = (x-x_0)^{m+1}\eta$ with a **shrinking support**. Fo
r $\epsilon > 0$, set $\eta_\epsilon(x) = \eta(x)\,\rho\!\big(\frac{x-x_0}{\epsi
lon}\big)$ where $\rho$ is a standard bump equal to 1 on $|t|\leq 1$, supported
in $|t|\leq 2$. Then $\psi_\epsilon := (x-x_0)^{m+1}\eta_\epsilon$ is supported
in $|x-x_0|\leq 2\epsilon$ and agrees with $\psi$ near $x_0$. Since $\operatorna
me{supp}(u)=\{x_0\}$ and $\psi - \psi_\epsilon$ vanishes near $x_0$ (both equal
$(x-x_0)^{m+1}\eta$ near $x_0$), we get $\langle u, \psi\rangle = \langle u, \ps
i_\epsilon\rangle$.

Now estimate using $(\star)$: on $|x-x_0|\leq 2\epsilon$,
$$|\psi_\epsilon^{(k)}(x)| \leq C_k\,\epsilon^{m+1-k} \quad (k = 0,\ldots,m),$$
since $\psi_\epsilon$ vanishes to order $m+1$ at $x_0$ and is supported in $|x-x
_0|\leq 2\epsilon$. Therefore
$$|\langle u, \psi\rangle| = |\langle u, \psi_\epsilon\rangle| \leq C\sum_{k=0}^
{m} \sup|\psi_\epsilon^{(k)}| \leq C'\sum_{k=0}^{m}\epsilon^{m+1-k} \xrightarrow
{\epsilon\to 0} 0.$$
Hence $\langle u, \psi\rangle = 0$, i.e., $\langle u, (x-x_0)^{m+1}\eta\rangle =
 0$.

**Step 4 (Conclusion).** From Step 2 and Step 3:
$$\langle u, \varphi\rangle = \sum_{k=0}^{m} \frac{\varphi^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}\,\lan
gle u,\, (x-x_0)^k\chi\rangle = \sum_{k=0}^{m} c_k\,(-1)^k\varphi^{(k)}(x_0) = \
left\langle \sum_{k=0}^{m} c_k\,\delta^{(k)}_{x_0},\, \varphi\right\rangle,$$
where $c_k := \frac{(-1)^k}{k!}\langle u, (x-x_0)^k\chi\rangle$. Since this hold
s for all $\varphi \in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R})$,
$$u = \sum_{k=0}^{m} c_k\,\delta^{(k)}_{x_0}. \qquad \blacksquare$$

## Summary

- A distribution supported at a single point $\{x_0\}$ is a finite linear combin
ation $\sum_{k=0}^{N} c_k\,\delta^{(k)}_{x_0}$, **not** merely a scaled delta $c
\,\delta_{x_0}$.
- The derivative $\delta'_{x_0}$ is an explicit counterexample: it has support $
\{x_0\}$ but is not a scalar multiple of $\delta_{x_0}$.
- The answer to the question is **no**.
