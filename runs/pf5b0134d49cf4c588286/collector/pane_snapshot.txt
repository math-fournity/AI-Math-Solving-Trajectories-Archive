## Proof

**Claim.** For the unit cube $Q = [0,1]^n \subset \mathbb{R}^n$, the $s$-dimensi
onal Hausdorff measure $\mathcal{H}^s(Q)$ satisfies

$$
\mathcal{H}^s(Q) = \begin{cases} +\infty, & s < n, \\ 1, & s = n, \\ 0, & s > n,
 \end{cases}
$$

(up to the standard normalization convention $\omega_s/2^s$ with $\omega_s = \pi
^{s/2}/\Gamma(s/2+1)$, under which $\mathcal{H}^n$ agrees exactly with Lebesgue
measure on $\mathbb{R}^n$). Hence the dimension at which the Hausdorff measure o
f the $n$-cube is **positive and finite** is precisely $s = n$, and at this valu
e it coincides with the $n$-dimensional Lebesgue measure.

---

### Step 1. $\mathcal{H}^s(Q) = 0$ for $s > n$.

Cover $Q$ by the standard dyadic subdivision into $2^{kn}$ sub-cubes of side len
gth $2^{-k}$, hence diameter $2^{-k}\sqrt{n}$. For any $s > n$,

$$
\mathcal{H}^s_{2^{-k}\sqrt{n}}(Q) \le \sum_{i=1}^{2^{kn}} \bigl(\operatorname{di
am}(\text{sub-cube}_i)\bigr)^s = 2^{kn}\cdot(2^{-k}\sqrt{n})^s = n^{s/2}\, 2^{k(
n-s)} \xrightarrow{k\to\infty} 0,
$$

since $n - s < 0$. Thus $\mathcal{H}^s(Q) = 0$.

### Step 2. $\mathcal{H}^s(Q) = +\infty$ for $s < n$.

We use the mass distribution principle (Frostman-type lower bound). Equip $Q$ wi
th Lebesgue measure $\mu$ restricted to $Q$, so $\mu(Q) = 1$. For any subset $A
\subset Q$ with diameter $d = \operatorname{diam}(A)$, we have $A \subset B(x, d
)$ for some $x$, and since $\mu$ is $n$-dimensional Lebesgue measure,

$$
\mu(A) \le \mu(B(x,d)) \le C_n\, d^n
$$

for a constant $C_n$ depending only on $n$ (the volume of a ball of radius $d$).
 Hence for any cover $\{A_i\}$ of $Q$,

$$
1 = \mu(Q) \le \sum_i \mu(A_i) \le C_n \sum_i (\operatorname{diam} A_i)^n = C_n
\sum_i (\operatorname{diam} A_i)^s \cdot (\operatorname{diam} A_i)^{n-s}.
$$

For covers with $\operatorname{diam}(A_i) \le \delta \to 0$ and $s < n$, the fac
tor $(\operatorname{diam} A_i)^{n-s} \le \delta^{n-s} \to 0$, giving

$$
1 \le C_n\, \delta^{\,n-s} \sum_i (\operatorname{diam} A_i)^s \quad\Longrightarr
ow\quad \sum_i (\operatorname{diam} A_i)^s \ge \frac{1}{C_n\,\delta^{\,n-s}} \xr
ightarrow{\delta\to 0} +\infty.
$$

So $\mathcal{H}^s_\delta(Q) \to +\infty$, i.e. $\mathcal{H}^s(Q) = +\infty$.

### Step 3. $\mathcal{H}^n(Q) = 1$ — coincidence with Lebesgue measure.

This is the fundamental theorem of geometric measure theory (Federer, Mattila):
on $\mathbb{R}^n$, the $n$-dimensional Hausdorff measure $\mathcal{H}^n$ (with t
he normalization $\omega_n/2^n$) agrees exactly with $n$-dimensional Lebesgue me
asure $\mathcal{L}^n$. The proof proceeds by:

- **Upper bound**: the dyadic cover from Step 1 with $s = n$ gives $\mathcal{H}^
n_\delta(Q) \le n^{n/2}\cdot 2^{k(n-n)} = n^{n/2}$, and refining with optimal ba
ll-based covers and the isodiametric inequality (the ball maximizes volume among
 sets of given diameter) yields $\mathcal{H}^n(Q) \le \mathcal{L}^n(Q) = 1$.
- **Lower bound**: the isodiametric inequality states $\mathcal{L}^n(A) \le \ome
ga_n\,(\operatorname{diam}A/2)^n$ for every measurable $A$, so for any cover $\{
A_i\}$,
$$
1 = \mathcal{L}^n(Q) \le \sum_i \mathcal{L}^n(A_i) \le \frac{\omega_n}{2^n}\sum_
i (\operatorname{diam}A_i)^n,
$$
giving $\mathcal{H}^n(Q) \ge 1$.

Combining, $\mathcal{H}^n(Q) = 1 = \mathcal{L}^n(Q)$.

### Conclusion

The Hausdorff dimension of the $n$-cube is $n$, and at $s = n$ the measure is po
sitive and finite (equal to $1$, the Lebesgue volume). For $s < n$ it is infinit
e; for $s > n$ it is zero. The critical dimension is therefore **$s = n$**, wher
e Hausdorff measure and Lebesgue measure coincide.
