Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{c}]$ bein
g a UFD (with $c$ not a perfect square) forces it to be a PID.

**Answer: Yes.** Here is the proof.

---

## Proof

**Key ingredients.** (1) Every UFD is integrally closed. (2) $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{c
}]$ is integrally closed only when it equals the full ring of integers of $\math
bb{Q}(\sqrt{c})$, which forces it to be a Dedekind domain. (3) In a Dedekind dom
ain, UFD $\Longrightarrow$ PID.

---

### Step 1: A UFD is integrally closed

This is a standard theorem. If $R$ is a UFD with field of fractions $K$, and $x
= a/b \in K$ (in lowest terms) is integral over $R$, then $x$ satisfies a monic
polynomial $x^n + r_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + r_0 = 0$ with $r_i \in R$. Multiplyi
ng through by $b^n$ gives $a^n + r_{n-1}a^{n-1}b + \cdots + r_0 b^n = 0$. Since
$\gcd(a,b)=1$ (in the UFD sense), any irreducible factor $p$ of $b$ divides $a^n
$, hence divides $a$—contradiction unless $b$ is a unit. So $x \in R$.

---

### Step 2: Determine when $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{c}]$ is integrally closed

Write $c = d k^2$ where $d$ is the squarefree part of $c$ (with $d \neq 0, 1$ si
nce $c$ is not a perfect square and $c \neq 0$; the case $c=0$ gives $R=\mathbb{
Z}$, trivially a PID, but $0$ is a perfect square so it is excluded). Then $\sqr
t{c} = k\sqrt{d}$, so
$$R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{c}] = \mathbb{Z}[k\sqrt{d}] = \{a + bk\sqrt{d} : a, b \in
 \mathbb{Z}\},$$
and the field of fractions is $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$.

The ring of integers of $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ is the well-known formula:
$$\mathcal{O}_K = \begin{cases} \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{d}] & \text{if } d \equiv 2, 3
\pmod{4}, \\[4pt] \mathbb{Z}\!\left[\dfrac{1+\sqrt{d}}{2}\right] & \text{if } d
\equiv 1 \pmod{4}. \end{cases}$$

Since $\mathcal{O}_K$ is the integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $K$, and $R \su
pseteq \mathbb{Z}$, the integral closure of $R$ in $K$ is also $\mathcal{O}_K$.
If $R$ is a UFD, then by Step 1, $R$ is integrally closed, so $R = \mathcal{O}_K
$.

**Case $d \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$:** Then $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{d
}}{2}]$, which contains $\frac{1+\sqrt{d}}{2}$. But every element of $R = \mathb
b{Z}[k\sqrt{d}]$ has the form $a + bk\sqrt{d}$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$, so th
e "rational part" is always an integer. Since $\frac{1+\sqrt{d}}{2}$ has rationa
l part $\frac{1}{2} \notin \mathbb{Z}$, we have $\frac{1+\sqrt{d}}{2} \notin R$.
 So $R \neq \mathcal{O}_K$, contradicting $R$ being integrally closed. **This ca
se is impossible.**

**Case $d \equiv 2, 3 \pmod{4}$:** Then $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{d}]$.
We need $R = \mathbb{Z}[k\sqrt{d}] = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{d}]$. The element $\sqrt{d
} \in \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{d}]$ must lie in $\mathbb{Z}[k\sqrt{d}]$, so $\sqrt{d} =
a + bk\sqrt{d}$ for some $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$, giving $a = 0$ and $bk = 1$, hen
ce $k = 1$. So $c = d$ is **squarefree**, and $c \equiv 2, 3 \pmod{4}$.

**Conclusion of Step 2:** If $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{c}]$ is a UFD, then $c$ is square
free with $c \equiv 2, 3 \pmod{4}$, and
$$R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{c}] = \mathcal{O}_K,$$
the full ring of integers of the quadratic number field $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{c}
)$.

---

### Step 3: $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a Dedekind domain

The ring of integers of any number field is a Dedekind domain: it is Noetherian
(finitely generated as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module), integrally closed (by definition)
, and every nonzero prime ideal is maximal (Krull dimension 1). In particular, e
very nonzero prime ideal of $R$ has **height 1**.

---

### Step 4: In a Dedekind domain, UFD $\Longrightarrow$ PID

Assume $R$ is a Dedekind domain and a UFD.

**Claim:** Every nonzero prime ideal of $R$ is principal.

*Proof of claim.* Let $\mathfrak{p}$ be a nonzero prime ideal. Since $R$ is a De
dekind domain, $\mathfrak{p}$ has height 1. Pick any nonzero element $\alpha \in
 \mathfrak{p}$. Since $R$ is a UFD, $\alpha = \pi_1 \pi_2 \cdots \pi_r$ where ea
ch $\pi_i$ is irreducible (hence prime, since in a UFD irreducible $=$ prime). S
ince $\mathfrak{p}$ is prime, some $\pi_i \in \mathfrak{p}$. The ideal $(\pi_i)$
 is a nonzero prime ideal (because $\pi_i$ is a prime element), and $(\pi_i) \su
bseteq \mathfrak{p}$. Since $R$ has Krull dimension 1, $(\pi_i)$ is a nonzero pr
ime, hence maximal, so $(\pi_i) = \mathfrak{p}$. Thus $\mathfrak{p}$ is principa
l. $\square$

**Every nonzero ideal is principal.** In a Dedekind domain, every nonzero ideal
$\mathfrak{a}$ factors uniquely as $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{p}_1 \mathfrak{p}_2
 \cdots \mathfrak{p}_s$ (product of nonzero prime ideals). By the claim, each $\
mathfrak{p}_i = (\pi_i)$ is principal, so $\mathfrak{a} = (\pi_1 \pi_2 \cdots \p
i_s)$ is principal.

Together with the zero ideal $(0)$, every ideal of $R$ is principal. Therefore $
R$ is a PID.

---

### Conclusion

We have shown:
1. If $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{c}]$ is a UFD, it must be integrally closed (Step 1).
2. This forces $c$ to be squarefree with $c \equiv 2, 3 \pmod{4}$, making $\math
bb{Z}[\sqrt{c}]$ the full ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$, hence a Dedekind dom
ain (Steps 2–3).
3. A Dedekind domain that is a UFD is necessarily a PID (Step 4).

Therefore, **yes**: if $R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{c}]$ is a UFD (with $c$ not a perfe
ct square), then $R$ is a PID. $\blacksquare$
