# Solution

**Problem.** Determine the range of the largest root of $P_a(x) = x^{a+2} - x^{a
+1} - 1$ as $a \to \infty$.

## Step 1: Existence and uniqueness of the largest root

We have $P_a(x) = x^{a+1}(x-1) - 1$.

- $P_a(1) = 1 \cdot 0 - 1 = -1 < 0$.
- $P_a(x) \to +\infty$ as $x \to +\infty$.

By the Intermediate Value Theorem, there exists a root $r_a > 1$.

For uniqueness on $(1,\infty)$: compute
$$P_a'(x) = x^{a}\bigl[(a+2)x - (a+1)\bigr].$$
For $x > 1$ we have $(a+2)x - (a+1) > (a+2)-(a+1) = 1 > 0$ and $x^a > 0$, so $P_
a'(x) > 0$ on $(1,\infty)$. Thus $P_a$ is strictly increasing on $(1,\infty)$, a
nd the root $r_a > 1$ is **unique**. This is the largest real root.

## Step 2: The root $r_a$ satisfies a key equation

Since $r_a$ is a root:
$$r_a^{a+1}(r_a - 1) = 1, \qquad r_a > 1. \tag{$\star$}$$

## Step 3: $r_a \to 1$ as $a \to \infty$

We already know $r_a > 1$ for every $a$. Suppose for contradiction that $r_a \no
t\to 1$. Then there exist $\delta > 0$ and a sequence $a_k \to \infty$ with $r_{
a_k} \geq 1 + \delta$ for all $k$.

From $(\star)$:
$$1 = r_{a_k}^{\,a_k+1}(r_{a_k}-1) \geq (1+\delta)^{a_k+1}\cdot \delta \xrightar
row{k\to\infty} +\infty,$$
a contradiction. Therefore:
$$\boxed{\lim_{a\to\infty} r_a = 1.}$$

## Step 4: Monotonicity and the full range

If $a' > a \geq 0$, then $r_{a'} < r_a$. Indeed, if $r_{a'} \geq r_a > 1$, then
$$1 = r_{a'}^{\,a'+1}(r_{a'}-1) \geq r_a^{\,a'+1}(r_a - 1) > r_a^{\,a+1}(r_a-1)
= 1,$$
a contradiction (the strict inequality uses $r_a > 1$ and $a'+1 > a+1$). So $(r_
a)$ is **strictly decreasing**.

At $a = 0$: $P_0(x) = x^2 - x - 1$, giving $r_0 = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2} = \varphi
$ (the golden ratio).

Since $r_a$ is strictly decreasing with $r_0 = \varphi$ and $r_a \to 1^+$, the r
ange of the largest root over all $a \geq 0$ is
$$\{r_a : a \geq 0\} \subset \bigl(1,\; \varphi\bigr], \qquad \varphi = \tfrac{1
+\sqrt{5}}{2},$$
and as $a \to \infty$ this range collapses to the single limit point $\{1\}$.

## Step 5: Asymptotic rate (optional refinement)

Setting $r_a = 1 + \varepsilon_a$ with $\varepsilon_a \to 0^+$, equation $(\star
)$ becomes
$$(1+\varepsilon_a)^{a+1}\,\varepsilon_a = 1.$$
Taking logs: $(a+1)\ln(1+\varepsilon_a) = -\ln\varepsilon_a$. For small $\vareps
ilon_a$, $\ln(1+\varepsilon_a)\approx \varepsilon_a$, giving $(a+1)\varepsilon_a
 \approx -\ln\varepsilon_a$, i.e., $\varepsilon_a\, e^{(a+1)\varepsilon_a} \appr
ox 1$. Setting $t = (a+1)\varepsilon_a$ yields $t\,e^t \approx a+1$, so
$$\varepsilon_a \sim \frac{W(a+1)}{a+1} \sim \frac{\ln(a+1)}{a+1} \to 0,$$
where $W$ is the Lambert $W$-function. This confirms $r_a = 1 + O\!\left(\frac{\
ln a}{a}\right) \to 1$.

## Conclusion

The largest root of $x^{a+2} - x^{a+1} - 1$ is a strictly decreasing function of
 $a$, starting at the golden ratio $\varphi = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}$ when $a=0$,
and **converging to $1$ from above** as $a \to \infty$. The range of the largest
 root as $a$ approaches infinity is the single point $\{1\}$; equivalently, $\li
m_{a\to\infty} r_a = 1$.
