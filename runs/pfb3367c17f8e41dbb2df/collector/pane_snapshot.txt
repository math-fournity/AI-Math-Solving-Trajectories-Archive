## Solution

**Setup.** The true password registered in the system is $2469$. Two distinct bu
ttons $a$ and $b$ are broken: pressing $a$ registers $b$ and pressing $b$ regist
ers $a$; all other buttons register themselves. If Andy presses the sequence $P
= p_1p_2p_3p_4$, the system registers $\sigma_{ab}(P)$, where $\sigma_{ab}$ is t
he transposition swapping $a$ and $b$. The lock opens iff the registered sequenc
e equals $2469$, i.e., iff
$$P = \sigma_{ab}(2469).$$
So for each possible broken pair $\{a,b\}$ there is a unique "correct" pressing
sequence $\sigma_{ab}(2469)$, and Andy succeeds on an attempt iff his pressed se
quence equals the correct one for the actual broken pair.

**Counting distinct correct sequences.** Let $D = \{2,4,6,9\}$ be the set of dig
its appearing in the password. Partition the $\binom{10}{2}=45$ possible broken
pairs by how many of $a,b$ lie in $D$:

- **Neither in $D$** ($a,b \in \{0,1,3,5,7,8\}$): $\binom{6}{2}=15$ pairs, all g
iving $\sigma_{ab}(2469)=2469$. This yields **1** distinct sequence.

- **Exactly one in $D$** ($a\in D,\; b\notin D$): $4\times 6 = 24$ pairs. The sw
ap replaces the single occurrence of $a$ in $2469$ with $b\notin D$. The resulti
ng sequence contains exactly one digit outside $D$ (namely $b$) at the position
of $a$. Two such sequences coincide only if both $a$ and $b$ coincide, so all **
24** are distinct (and differ from $2469$ and from case-3 sequences since they c
ontain a digit outside $D$).

- **Both in $D$** ($\{a,b\}\subset D$): $\binom{4}{2}=6$ pairs, swapping two dig
its of $2469$:
$$4269,\; 6429,\; 9462,\; 2649,\; 2964,\; 2496.$$
These **6** sequences are all distinct (each permutes a different pair of positi
ons) and contain only digits of $D$, so they do not collide with case-1 or case-
2 sequences.

The total number of distinct correct pressing sequences is
$$|S| = 1 + 24 + 6 = 31.$$

**Lower bound (at least 31 attempts).** Each attempt is a single 4-digit code; i
t opens the lock only if it equals the correct sequence for the actual broken pa
ir, which is always some element of $S$. If Andy makes at most $30$ attempts, at
 least one element of $S$ is never tried. If the actual broken pair happens to b
e one whose correct sequence is that untried element, no attempt succeeds. Hence
 $30$ attempts cannot guarantee opening; at least $\mathbf{31}$ are necessary.

**Upper bound (31 attempts suffice).** Andy simply tries all $31$ sequences in $
S$. Whatever the broken pair is, its correct pressing sequence is among them, so
 one attempt opens the lock. Thus $31$ attempts suffice.

**Conclusion.** The least number of attempts Andy needs to surely open the lock
is

$$\boxed{31}.$$
