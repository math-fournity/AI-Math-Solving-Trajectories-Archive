# Plemelj Formulae for the Cauchy Type Integral

## Setup

Let $L$ be a smooth closed (or open) curve in the complex plane, and let $\varph
i(\tau)$ satisfy the Hölder condition on $L$. Define the Cauchy type integral

$$\Phi(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_L \frac{\varphi(\tau)}{\tau - z}\,d\tau, \qquad
 z\notin L.$$

For a **closed** curve $L$, $\Phi(z)$ is analytic in $\mathbb{C}\setminus L$. We
 denote the two sides of $L$ as $D^+$ (the left/positive side, traversed so that
 $D^+$ is on the left when $L$ is oriented positively) and $D^-$ (the right/nega
tive side).

For a point $t\in L$ (not an endpoint, if $L$ is open), we define the **principa
l value** (Cauchy principal value) integral

$$\Phi^*(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\,\text{P.V.}\!\int_L \frac{\varphi(\tau)}{\tau -
t}\,d\tau := \lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^+}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L\setminus L_\vareps
ilon}\frac{\varphi(\tau)}{\tau-t}\,d\tau,$$

where $L_\varepsilon$ is the arc of $L$ cut out by a small circle $|\tau - t|<\v
arepsilon$.

---

## The Plemelj Formulae (Statement)

As $z\to t\in L$ (with $t$ not an endpoint) from the $+$ side and from the $-$ s
ide respectively, the boundary values of $\Phi(z)$ exist and are given by:

$$\boxed{\Phi^+(t) = \frac{1}{2}\varphi(t) + \frac{1}{2\pi i}\,\text{P.V.}\!\int
_L \frac{\varphi(\tau)}{\tau - t}\,d\tau = \frac{1}{2}\varphi(t) + \Phi^*(t),}$$

$$\boxed{\Phi^-(t) = -\frac{1}{2}\varphi(t) + \frac{1}{2\pi i}\,\text{P.V.}\!\in
t_L \frac{\varphi(\tau)}{\tau - t}\,d\tau = -\frac{1}{2}\varphi(t) + \Phi^*(t).}
$$

Equivalently, adding and subtracting:

$$\Phi^+(t) - \Phi^-(t) = \varphi(t), \qquad \Phi^+(t) + \Phi^-(t) = \frac{1}{\p
i i}\,\text{P.V.}\!\int_L \frac{\varphi(\tau)}{\tau - t}\,d\tau = 2\,\Phi^*(t).$
$

The first relation is the **jump relation**; the second identifies the principal
 value as the average of the two boundary values.

---

## Proof

### Step 1. Decomposition of the integral

Fix $t\in L$ (not an endpoint). Write $\varphi(\tau) = \varphi(t) + \psi(\tau)$,
 where $\psi(\tau) = \varphi(\tau) - \varphi(t)$. By the Hölder condition, $|\ps
i(\tau)|\le A|\tau - t|^\alpha$ for some $A>0$, $0<\alpha\le 1$.

Then

$$\Phi(z) = \frac{\varphi(t)}{2\pi i}\int_L \frac{d\tau}{\tau - z} + \frac{1}{2\
pi i}\int_L \frac{\psi(\tau)}{\tau - z}\,d\tau =: \varphi(t)\,I(z) + \Psi(z).$$

### Step 2. The constant-density term $I(z)$

For a **closed** curve $L$:

$$I(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_L \frac{d\tau}{\tau - z} = \begin{cases} 1, & z\in
 D^+,\\ 0, & z\in D^-. \end{cases}$$

This is the standard winding-number / index result (the integral counts the wind
ing of $L$ around $z$).

As $z\to t\in L$:
- From $D^+$: $I(z)\to 1$.
- From $D^-$: $I(z)\to 0$.

So the contribution of the constant-density part to the boundary values is $\var
phi(t)$ from the $+$ side and $0$ from the $-$ side. This already gives the "$\p
m\frac{1}{2}\varphi(t)$" once we account for the principal value (see Step 4).

### Step 3. The remainder $\Psi(z)$ is continuous across $L$

We claim $\Psi(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_L \frac{\psi(\tau)}{\tau-z}\,d\tau$ ext
ends continuously to $t\in L$, with boundary value

$$\Psi(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\,\text{P.V.}\!\int_L \frac{\psi(\tau)}{\tau - t}\,d
\tau.$$

**Proof of continuity.** It suffices to show $\Psi(z)\to \Psi(t)$ as $z\to t$ (w
ith $z$ approaching from either side, non-tangentially). Consider $z$ approachin
g $t$ along the normal to $L$ at $t$. Set $z = t + \delta\, n$ where $n$ is the
unit normal and $\delta\to 0$ (sign of $\delta$ determines the side).

Compute the difference:

$$\Psi(z) - \Psi(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_L \psi(\tau)\left[\frac{1}{\tau - z}
- \frac{1}{\tau - t}\right]d\tau = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_L \frac{\psi(\tau)(z-t)}
{(\tau-z)(\tau-t)}\,d\tau.$$

Split $L$ into $L_\varepsilon = \{|\tau - t|<\varepsilon\}$ and $L\setminus L_\v
arepsilon$.

- On $L\setminus L_\varepsilon$: $|\tau - t|\ge\varepsilon$, so the integrand is
 $O(|z-t|/\varepsilon^2)$, and the integral over this part $\to 0$ as $z\to t$ (
for fixed $\varepsilon$).

- On $L_\varepsilon$: near $t$, parametrize $L$ by arclength: $\tau = t + s\,e^{
i\theta(t)} + O(s^2)$ where $\theta(t)$ is the tangent angle. Then $|\psi(\tau)|
\le A|s|^\alpha$, $|\tau - t|\approx |s|$, $|\tau - z|\approx |s - i\delta|$ (in
 local coordinates where $L$ is the real axis). The integral over $L_\varepsilon
$ is bounded by

$$C\int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}\frac{|\delta|\,|s|^\alpha}{|s - i\delta|\,|
s|}\,ds \le C|\delta|\int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}\frac{|s|^{\alpha-1}}{|s-i
\delta|}\,ds.$$

By the substitution $s = |\delta| u$:

$$= C|\delta|\cdot|\delta|^{\alpha-1}\int_{-\varepsilon/|\delta|}^{\varepsilon/|
\delta|}\frac{|u|^{\alpha-1}}{|u - i\,\text{sgn}(\delta)|}\,du \le C'|\delta|^\a
lpha \cdot C'' \to 0$$

as $\delta\to 0$, since $\alpha>0$ and the integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\fra
c{|u|^{\alpha-1}}{\sqrt{u^2+1}}\,du$ converges (it converges at $u=0$ since $\al
pha-1>-1$, and at $\infty$ since $\alpha - 2 < -1$).

Hence $\Psi(z)\to\Psi(t)$, and $\Psi$ is continuous across $L$ at $t$. The princ
ipal value integral $\text{P.V.}\!\int_L \frac{\psi(\tau)}{\tau-t}\,d\tau$ exist
s because $\psi(\tau)/(\tau-t) = O(|\tau-t|^{\alpha-1})$ with $\alpha-1>-1$, so
the singularity is integrable.

### Step 4. Assembling the boundary values

From Step 2, the constant-density part contributes $\varphi(t)\cdot I(z)$, which
 tends to $\varphi(t)$ from $D^+$ and to $0$ from $D^-$.

However, the principal value of the constant-density integral is:

$$\text{P.V.}\!\int_L \frac{d\tau}{\tau - t} = \lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{L\se
tminus L_\varepsilon}\frac{d\tau}{\tau-t}.$$

For a closed curve, by the residue/index computation (or directly: the symmetric
 excision gives half the jump), one obtains:

$$\frac{1}{2\pi i}\,\text{P.V.}\!\int_L \frac{d\tau}{\tau - t} = \frac{1}{2}.$$

This can be seen as follows: the integral $\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_L\frac{d\tau}{\t
au - z}$ equals $1$ inside and $0$ outside. The average of the two boundary valu
es is $\frac{1}{2}(1+0)=\frac{1}{2}$, and by continuity of the remainder (Step 3
 applied with $\psi\equiv 1$, which is Hölder), this average equals the principa
l value. Hence $\frac{1}{2\pi i}\,\text{P.V.}\!\int_L\frac{d\tau}{\tau-t}=\frac{
1}{2}$.

Now combine. The full principal value is:

$$\Phi^*(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\,\text{P.V.}\!\int_L\frac{\varphi(\tau)}{\tau-t}\
,d\tau = \frac{\varphi(t)}{2\pi i}\,\text{P.V.}\!\int_L\frac{d\tau}{\tau-t} + \f
rac{1}{2\pi i}\,\text{P.V.}\!\int_L\frac{\psi(\tau)}{\tau-t}\,d\tau = \frac{1}{2
}\varphi(t) + \Psi(t).$$

The boundary values are:

$$\Phi^+(t) = \varphi(t)\cdot 1 + \Psi(t) = \varphi(t) + \Psi(t) = \varphi(t) +
\Phi^*(t) - \tfrac{1}{2}\varphi(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\varphi(t) + \Phi^*(t).$$

$$\Phi^-(t) = \varphi(t)\cdot 0 + \Psi(t) = \Psi(t) = \Phi^*(t) - \tfrac{1}{2}\v
arphi(t) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\varphi(t) + \Phi^*(t).$$

This establishes the Plemelj formulae. $\blacksquare$

## How the Plemelj Formulae Are Used in Practice

### 1. Riemann–Hilbert boundary value problems

The central application. One seeks a function $\Phi(z)$, sectionally analytic on
 $\mathbb{C}\setminus L$, satisfying the **linear conjugacy (Riemann–Hilbert) bo
undary condition**:

$$\Phi^+(t) = G(t)\,\Phi^-(t) + g(t), \qquad t\in L,$$

where $G, g$ are given Hölder-continuous functions on $L$ and $G(t)\neq 0$.

**Method.** Write $\Phi = \Phi^* + \frac{1}{2}\varphi$ on $+$ and $\Phi^* - \fra
c{1}{2}\varphi$ on $-$, where $\varphi = \Phi^+ - \Phi^-$ is the unknown jump. T
he Plemelj formulae convert the boundary condition into a **singular integral eq
uation** for $\varphi$:

$$\varphi(t) + \frac{1}{\pi i}\,\text{P.V.}\!\int_L \frac{G(t)-G(\tau)}{G(t)}\cd
ot\frac{\varphi(\tau)}{\tau-t}\,d\tau = \frac{2\,g(t)}{G(t)}\quad(\text{after ma
nipulation}),$$

or, more cleanly, one uses the **canonical function** $X(z) = \exp\!\big(\Gamma(
z)\big)$ where $\Gamma(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_L\frac{\ln G(\tau)}{\tau-z}\,d\ta
u$, reducing the problem to a Cauchy integral for the inhomogeneous part. The Pl
emelj formulae are what make this reduction work — they tell us the jump of $X$
across $L$ is exactly $G(t)$.

### 2. Singular integral equations

Equations of the form

$$a(t)\,\varphi(t) + \frac{b(t)}{\pi i}\,\text{P.V.}\!\int_L\frac{\varphi(\tau)}
{\tau-t}\,d\tau = f(t)$$

are solved by recognizing them as the boundary condition of a Riemann–Hilbert pr
oblem. Setting $\Phi(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_L\frac{\varphi(\tau)}{\tau-z}\,d\ta
u$ and applying Plemelj, the equation becomes $\Phi^+(t) = \frac{a+b}{a-b}\Phi^-
(t) + \frac{f}{a-b}$, reducing the integral equation to a boundary value problem
.

### 3. Discontinuity / jump problems in aerodynamics and elasticity

In thin-airfoil theory (Prandtl), the vortex sheet strength $\gamma(x)$ is recov
ered from the boundary condition of tangential flow via a Cauchy integral. The P
lemelj formulae relate the jump in velocity potential across the airfoil to the
vortex density, yielding the integral equation of the airfoil.

In crack problems in 2D elasticity, the displacement discontinuity across a crac
k is the jump $\varphi = \Phi^+ - \Phi^-$, and the Plemelj jump relation $\Phi^+
-\Phi^-=\varphi$ directly links the complex potential's jump to the physical cra
ck opening.

### 4. Inversion of the Cauchy integral

The Plemelj formulae immediately give the **inversion formula**: if $\varphi(t)
= \frac{1}{\pi i}\,\text{P.V.}\!\int_L\frac{\psi(\tau)}{\tau-t}\,d\tau$, then $\
psi(t) = \frac{1}{\pi i}\,\text{P.V.}\!\int_L\frac{\varphi(\tau)}{\tau-t}\,d\tau
$ (up to solutions of the homogeneous problem, governed by the index). This is t
he Hilbert-inversion on $L$, fundamental in harmonic analysis on curves.

### 5. Summary of the practical role

The Plemelj formulae serve as the **bridge** between:
- the **interior/exterior analytic** viewpoint (a function $\Phi(z)$ analytic of
f $L$), and
- the **boundary singular-integral** viewpoint (a principal-value equation on $L
$).

Every problem that can be formulated as "find a sectionally analytic function wi
th prescribed jump or conjugacy on $L$" is, via Plemelj, equivalent to a singula
r integral equation on $L$, and vice versa. This duality is the foundation of th
e Muskhelishvili–Vekua theory of singular integral equations and its application
s in continuum mechanics.
