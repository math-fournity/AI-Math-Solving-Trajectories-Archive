## Proof

**Goal.** Show that an entire function taking every complex value at most three
times must be a polynomial of degree at most three, and conversely.

### Step 1. Classify the singularity at infinity

Let $f$ be entire. The behavior of $f$ at infinity is determined by the isolated
 singularity of $g(w) = f(1/w)$ at $w = 0$. There are three cases:

- **Removable singularity at infinity:** $f$ is bounded, hence constant by Liouv
ille's theorem. A constant takes one value infinitely many times — this satisfie
s "at most three times" only vacuously if we interpret it as "at most 3 distinct
 preimages," but a constant takes its value at *every* point, so it fails the co
ndition. (If the constant is excluded, we move on.) Actually, a constant $f \equ
iv c$ takes the value $c$ at infinitely many points, violating "no more than thr
ee times." So non-constant $f$ is needed.

- **Pole at infinity:** $f$ is a polynomial. If $\deg f = n$, then by the Fundam
ental Theorem of Algebra, for every $w \in \mathbb{C}$, the equation $f(z) = w$
has exactly $n$ solutions counting multiplicity, hence at most $n$ distinct solu
tions. The condition "at most three times" forces $n \leq 3$.

- **Essential singularity at infinity:** $f$ is transcendental entire. We show t
his case is impossible.

### Step 2. The transcendental case is impossible (Great Picard)

Suppose $f$ is transcendental entire. Then infinity is an essential singularity
of $f$.

By the **Great Picard Theorem**: in every punctured neighborhood of an essential
 singularity, the function assumes every complex value, with at most one excepti
on, **infinitely often**.

Concretely, for any $R > 0$, the region $\{z : |z| > R\}$ is a punctured neighbo
rhood of infinity. Applying Great Picard to $g(w) = f(1/w)$ near $w=0$: for ever
y $w_0 \in \mathbb{C}$, with at most one exception, the equation $f(z) = w_0$ ha
s infinitely many solutions in $\{|z| > R\}$.

Since $R$ was arbitrary, these infinitely many solutions are genuine (distinct)
preimages of $w_0$ under $f$. Thus $f$ takes all but at most one value **infinit
ely many times**.

This directly contradicts the hypothesis that $f$ takes **every** value no more
than three times. (Even allowing Picard's single exceptional value, all other va
lues are taken infinitely often — far more than three times.)

Therefore, $f$ cannot be transcendental entire.

### Step 3. $f$ must be a polynomial of degree $\leq 3$

Since the only remaining possibility is a pole at infinity, $f$ is a polynomial.
 Let $\deg f = n$. By the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, for each $w \in \mathb
b{C}$, the equation $f(z) = w$ has exactly $n$ roots in $\mathbb{C}$ counting mu
ltiplicity, and for a generic (non-critical) value $w$, these $n$ roots are dist
inct. Hence $f$ takes generic values exactly $n$ times (as distinct preimages).

The hypothesis "every value no more than three times" requires $n \leq 3$.

### Step 4. Converse

Conversely, if $f$ is a polynomial of degree $n \leq 3$, then for every $w \in \
mathbb{C}$, the equation $f(z) = w$ has at most $n \leq 3$ solutions (counting o
r not counting multiplicity). Thus $f$ takes every value no more than three time
s.

### Conclusion

An entire function that takes every value no more than three times is precisely
a **polynomial of degree at most three**:

$$\boxed{f(z) = az^3 + bz^2 + cz + d, \quad a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{C},}$$

where $a$ may be zero (giving degree $\leq 2$), and the non-constant requirement
 excludes the case $a = b = c = 0$.

The essential point is that the singularity at infinity cannot be essential (Gre
at Picard would force infinitely many preimages for all but one value), and cann
ot be removable for a non-constant function (Liouville), so it must be a pole —
making $f$ a polynomial — and the degree bound comes from the Fundamental Theore
m of Algebra.
