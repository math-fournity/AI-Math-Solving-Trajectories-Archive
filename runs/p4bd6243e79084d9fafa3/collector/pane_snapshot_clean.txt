## Proof

**Answer: Yes.** For any $p \geq 1$, any $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}$, and any open set
$I \ni x_0$, such a function exists.

### Construction

Without loss of generality, translate so that $x_0 = 0$ (the general case follow
s by replacing $x$ with $x - x_0$). Since $I$ is open and $0 \in I$, there exist
s $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \subseteq I$.

Define
$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} \left(1 - \dfrac{x^2}{\varepsilon^2}\right)^{p+1}, & |x| \l
eq \varepsilon, \\[6pt] 0, & |x| > \varepsilon. \end{cases}
$$

### Verification of all required properties

**1. $f \in C^p(\mathbb{R})$:** On the open intervals $(-\infty, -\varepsilon)$,
 $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$, and $(\varepsilon, \infty)$, $f$ is a polynomial
 (hence $C^\infty$) or identically zero. The only points to check are $x = \pm \
varepsilon$.

Near $x = \varepsilon$, set $u = x/\varepsilon$. The function is $\phi(u) = (1 -
 u^2)^{p+1} = \bigl((1-u)(1+u)\bigr)^{p+1}$. The factor $(1-u)^{p+1}$ has a zero
 of order $p+1$ at $u = 1$, so $\phi^{(k)}(1^-) = 0$ for all $0 \leq k \leq p$.
Since $\phi \equiv 0$ for $u > 1$, we also have $\phi^{(k)}(1^+) = 0$. Thus all
derivatives up to order $p$ match at $u = 1$, so $f$ is $C^p$ at $x = \varepsilo
n$. The argument at $x = -\varepsilon$ is identical by symmetry. Hence $f \in C^
p(\mathbb{R})$.

**2. $f$ vanishes at infinity:** $f$ has compact support $[-\varepsilon, \vareps
ilon]$, so $f(x) = 0$ for all $|x| > \varepsilon$. In particular $\lim_{|x|\to\i
nfty} f(x) = 0$.

**3. $\operatorname{supp}(f) \subseteq I$:** The set $\{x : f(x) \neq 0\} = (-\v
arepsilon, \varepsilon)$, so $\operatorname{supp}(f) = \overline{(-\varepsilon,
\varepsilon)} = [-\varepsilon, \varepsilon] \subseteq (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon
) \subseteq I$. (In fact $[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon] \subseteq I$ since $(-\vare
psilon,\varepsilon)\subseteq I$ and $I$ is open—actually we need $[-\varepsilon,
\varepsilon]\subseteq I$; since $(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\subseteq I$ and $I$
is open, we can choose $\varepsilon$ small enough that $[-\varepsilon,\varepsilo
n]\subseteq I$, e.g., pick $\varepsilon$ with $(-2\varepsilon, 2\varepsilon)\sub
seteq I$.)

**4. $f(x_0) = 1$:** $f(0) = (1 - 0)^{p+1} = 1$.

**5. $\sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} |f(x)| \leq 1$:** For $|x| \leq \varepsilon$, we h
ave $0 \leq x^2/\varepsilon^2 \leq 1$, so $0 \leq 1 - x^2/\varepsilon^2 \leq 1$,
 giving $0 \leq f(x) \leq 1$. For $|x| > \varepsilon$, $f(x) = 0$. Therefore $\s
up_{x \in \mathbb{R}} |f(x)| = 1 \leq 1$.

### Conclusion

For every natural number $p \geq 1$, every $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}$, and every open
set $I$ containing $x_0$, there exists $f \in C_0^p(\mathbb{R})$ with $\operator
name{supp}(f) \subseteq I$, $f(x_0) = 1$, and $\sup |f| \leq 1$. The key ingredi
ent is that the polynomial $(1 - t^2)^{p+1}$ has a zero of order $p+1$ at $t = \
pm 1$, which is one order more than $p$, ensuring $C^p$ smoothness at the bounda
ry of the support.
