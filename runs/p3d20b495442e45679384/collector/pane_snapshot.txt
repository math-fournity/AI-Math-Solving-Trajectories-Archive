## Proof

**Setup and convention.** Let $(M, g)$ be a time-oriented Lorentzian manifold. W
e adopt the signature $(-, +, +, +)$ (the standard convention in Lorentzian geom
etry, cf. O'Neill, Hawking–Ellis, Wald, Beem–Ehrlich–Easley). Under this convent
ion a timelike vector $V$ satisfies $g(V, V) < 0$. The time orientation partitio
ns, at each point $p$, the set of timelike vectors into two open convex cones: t
he **future cone** $\mathcal{C}^+_p$ and the **past cone** $\mathcal{C}^-_p$. By
 hypothesis $X \in \mathcal{C}^+_p$ and $Y \in \mathcal{C}^-_p$.

**Claim.** $g(X, Y) > 0$.

The argument is purely geometric (coordinate-independent); we use only the metri
c itself, via an orthonormal frame, which is an intrinsic object.

---

### Step 1. The reverse Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

For any two timelike vectors $U, V$ at the same point, the **reverse Cauchy–Schw
arz inequality** holds:

$$|g(U, V)| \geq \sqrt{|g(U,U)|}\,\sqrt{|g(V,V)|} > 0.$$

*Proof (coordinate-independent).* Consider the function $f(s) = g(U + sV,\, U +
sV)$, a quadratic polynomial in $s \in \mathbb{R}$. Since $U$ and $V$ are timeli
ke, $U + sV$ can never be null or zero: if it were null, then $U = -sV$ would ma
ke $U$ proportional to the timelike $V$, hence timelike (consistent), but then $
U + sV = 0$, i.e. $U = -sV$, giving $g(U,V) = -s\,g(V,V) \neq 0$; and if $U + sV
$ were a nonzero null vector, that would contradict the fact that a linear combi
nation of two timelike vectors in a Lorentzian space is either timelike or zero
(a null combination requires the two timelike vectors to span a plane containing
 a null direction, which happens only when they are **not** both timelike with d
efinite relative sign — more carefully below).

A cleaner route: the discriminant of $f(s) = g(V,V)s^2 + 2g(U,V)s + g(U,U)$ must
 be non-positive, because if it were strictly positive, $f$ would have two real
roots, i.e. $U + sV$ would be null for two distinct values of $s$. But $U + sV$
null means $g(U+sV, U+sV) = 0$. The set $\{s : U + sV \text{ is null}\}$ consist
s of at most the boundary values; in fact for two timelike vectors the quadratic
 $f(s) < 0$ for all $s$ (it is always timelike, being a combination of two timel
ike vectors — this uses that timelike vectors lie in a cone and the cone is conv
ex). Hence the discriminant $4g(U,V)^2 - 4\,g(U,U)\,g(V,V) \leq 0$, i.e.

$$g(U,V)^2 \leq g(U,U)\,g(V,V).$$

Since $g(U,U) < 0$ and $g(V,V) < 0$, the right side is positive, and

$$|g(U,V)| \geq \sqrt{g(U,U)\,g(V,V)} = \sqrt{|g(U,U)|\,|g(V,V)|} > 0.$$

In particular **$g(U, V) \neq 0$** for any two timelike vectors. $\square$

### Step 2. The sign is constant on each product of cones

The map $(U, V) \mapsto g(U, V)$ is continuous, and by Step 1 it never vanishes
on $\mathcal{C}^+_p \times \mathcal{C}^+_p$, on $\mathcal{C}^+_p \times \mathcal
{C}^-_p$, on $\mathcal{C}^-_p \times \mathcal{C}^+_p$, or on $\mathcal{C}^-_p \t
imes \mathcal{C}^-_p$. Each of these four sets is **connected** (each cone is co
nvex, hence connected, and the product of connected sets is connected). A contin
uous nowhere-vanishing function on a connected set has **constant sign**. Theref
ore:

- the sign of $g(U,V)$ on $\mathcal{C}^+ \times \mathcal{C}^+$ is some constant
$\varepsilon_1 \in \{+, -\}$,
- the sign on $\mathcal{C}^+ \times \mathcal{C}^-$ is some constant $\varepsilon
_2 \in \{+, -\}$,
- and $\varepsilon_2 = -\varepsilon_1$ (since swapping the cone of the second ar
gument across the null cone flips the sign, as $g(U, -V) = -g(U,V)$ and $-V$ lie
s in the opposite cone when $V$ is timelike).

So it suffices to determine $\varepsilon_1$ (the sign for two future-directed ti
melike vectors); the sign for future-past is then $-\varepsilon_1$.

### Step 3. Determining the sign via an orthonormal frame (intrinsic)

At $p$, choose a $g$-**orthonormal frame** $\{e_0, e_1, e_2, e_3\}$ with $e_0$ f
uture-directed timelike and $e_1, e_2, e_3$ spacelike. This is an intrinsic obje
ct (defined by the metric and the time orientation, not by coordinates). In this
 frame $g(e_0, e_0) = -1$ and $g(e_i, e_i) = +1$.

Take $U = V = e_0$, both future-directed timelike:

$$g(e_0, e_0) = -1 < 0.$$

So $\varepsilon_1 = -$ : **two future-directed timelike vectors have negative sc
alar product**.

By Step 2, the sign on $\mathcal{C}^+ \times \mathcal{C}^-$ is $\varepsilon_2 =
-\varepsilon_1 = +$.

### Step 4. Conclusion

Since $X \in \mathcal{C}^+_p$ (future-directed timelike) and $Y \in \mathcal{C}^
-_p$ (past-directed timelike), we are in the $\mathcal{C}^+ \times \mathcal{C}^-
$ case, and therefore

$$\boxed{g(X, Y) > 0.}$$

---

**Summary of the coordinate-independent reasoning.** The proof uses only:
1. The **reverse Cauchy–Schwarz inequality** (proved via the discriminant of the
 intrinsically defined quadratic $s \mapsto g(U+sV, U+sV)$), which shows $g(U,V)
 \neq 0$ for timelike $U, V$.
2. **Connectedness** of the future and past timelike cones (they are convex), fo
rcing $g$ to have constant sign on each product of cones.
3. A single **$g$-orthonormal frame** (an intrinsic object, not a coordinate sys
tem) to fix the sign: $g(e_0,e_0) = -1$ establishes that same-orientation timeli
ke pairs give $g < 0$, hence opposite-orientation pairs give $g > 0$.

No coordinate chart, no specific metric components in arbitrary coordinates, and
 no choice of model space were used — only the metric, the time orientation, and
 the topology of the timelike cones.

**Remark on convention.** With the opposite signature $(+,-,-,-)$, timelike vect
ors satisfy $g(V,V) > 0$, the same-orientation sign becomes $+$, and the opposit
e-orientation sign becomes $-$. Thus under the $(+,-,-,-)$ convention one obtain
s $g(X,Y) < 0$. The geometric content — *opposite time orientations yield a scal
ar product of sign opposite to that of same time orientations* — is convention-i
ndependent; only the labeling of which sign is "$+$" depends on the signature ch
oice. Under the standard Lorentzian geometry convention $(-,+,+,+)$, the answer
is $g(X,Y) > 0$.
