## Example: $\mathbb{R}$ with the standard (Euclidean) topology

**Claim.** The real line $\mathbb{R}$ equipped with its usual topology is Fréche
t–Urysohn but is not countable.

---

### 1. $\mathbb{R}$ is not countable

This is Cantor's classical diagonal argument. Suppose $\mathbb{R}$ were countabl
e, so $\mathbb{R} = \{r_1, r_2, r_3, \dots\}$. Choose a decimal expansion $r_n =
 \pm d^{(n)}_1 d^{(n)}_2 d^{(n)}_3 \cdots$ for each $r_n$. Construct a real numb
er $x = 0.e_1 e_2 e_3 \cdots$ where $e_n \in \{1,2\}$ and $e_n \neq d^{(n)}_n$ (
choosing digits from $\{1,2\}$ avoids the $0.999\ldots = 1$ ambiguity). Then $x$
 differs from every $r_n$ in the $n$-th decimal place, so $x \notin \{r_1, r_2,
\dots\}$, contradicting the enumeration. Hence $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable. $\sq
uare$

### 2. $\mathbb{R}$ is Fréchet–Urysohn

Recall that a space $X$ is **Fréchet–Urysohn** if for every $A \subseteq X$ and
every $x \in \overline{A}$, there exists a sequence $(a_n)$ in $A$ with $a_n \to
 x$.

It suffices to show $\mathbb{R}$ is **first-countable**, since every first-count
able space is Fréchet–Urysohn:

> **Lemma.** If $X$ is first-countable, then $X$ is Fréchet–Urysohn.
>
> *Proof.* Let $x \in \overline{A}$. First-countability gives a countable neighb
orhood base $\{U_n : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$ at $x$. WLOG $U_1 \supseteq U_2 \supset
eq \cdots$ (replace $U_n$ by $U_1 \cap \cdots \cap U_n$). Since $x \in \overline
{A}$, each $U_n$ meets $A$; pick $a_n \in U_n \cap A$. For any neighborhood $V$
of $x$, some $U_N \subseteq V$, so $a_n \in V$ for all $n \geq N$. Thus $a_n \to
 x$. $\square$

Now $\mathbb{R}$ is first-countable: at each point $x \in \mathbb{R}$, the famil
y
$$\left\{\left(x - \tfrac{1}{n},\; x + \tfrac{1}{n}\right) : n \in \mathbb{N}\ri
ght\}$$
is a countable neighborhood base. Indeed, every open neighborhood of $x$ contain
s some $\varepsilon$-ball $(x-\varepsilon, x+\varepsilon)$, and choosing $n > 1/
\varepsilon$ gives $(x - \tfrac{1}{n}, x + \tfrac{1}{n}) \subseteq (x-\varepsilo
n, x+\varepsilon)$.

By the lemma, $\mathbb{R}$ is Fréchet–Urysohn. $\square$

---

### Conclusion

$\mathbb{R}$ with the Euclidean topology is **first-countable** (hence Fréchet–U
rysohn) and **uncountable** (by Cantor's theorem). Therefore it is a topological
 space that is Fréchet–Urysohn but not countable.
