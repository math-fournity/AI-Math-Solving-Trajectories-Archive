## Evaluation of $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{\cos x}{x^2+1}\,dx$

### Step 1. Set up the contour integral

Consider the meromorphic function

$$f(z) = \frac{e^{iz}}{z^2+1}.$$

Its only singularities are simple poles at $z = \pm i$ (the roots of $z^2+1=0$).

Let $C_R$ be the closed contour consisting of:
- the real segment $[-R, R]$, and
- the upper semicircular arc $\Gamma_R = \{Re^{i\theta} : 0 \le \theta \le \pi\}
$,

chosen with $R > 1$ so that the pole $z = i$ lies strictly inside $C_R$ (the pol
e $z = -i$ lies in the lower half-plane and is excluded).

### Step 2. Apply the Residue Theorem

The only pole enclosed by $C_R$ is $z = i$. Since $z^2+1 = (z-i)(z+i)$,

$$\operatorname{Res}(f, i) = \lim_{z \to i} (z-i)\,\frac{e^{iz}}{(z-i)(z+i)} = \
frac{e^{i\cdot i}}{2i} = \frac{e^{-1}}{2i}.$$

By the Residue Theorem,

$$\oint_{C_R} f(z)\,dz = 2\pi i \cdot \frac{e^{-1}}{2i} = \frac{\pi}{e}.$$

Equivalently,

$$\int_{-R}^{R} \frac{e^{ix}}{x^2+1}\,dx + \int_{\Gamma_R} \frac{e^{iz}}{z^2+1}\
,dz = \frac{\pi}{e}. \tag{1}$$

### Step 3. Show the arc integral vanishes (Jordan's Lemma)

On $\Gamma_R$, write $z = Re^{i\theta} = R(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$ with $0 \l
e \theta \le \pi$. Then

$$|e^{iz}| = e^{-R\sin\theta} \le 1 \quad (\text{since } \sin\theta \ge 0 \text{
 on } [0,\pi]),$$

and

$$|z^2+1| \ge |z|^2 - 1 = R^2 - 1.$$

Hence

$$\left|\int_{\Gamma_R} \frac{e^{iz}}{z^2+1}\,dz\right| \le \frac{1}{R^2-1}\int_
{\Gamma_R} e^{-R\sin\theta}\,|dz|.$$

By **Jordan's Lemma** (applicable since $e^{iz}$ has the decaying factor $e^{-R\
sin\theta}$ in the upper half-plane and $\frac{1}{z^2+1} \to 0$ as $|z|\to\infty
$),

$$\int_{\Gamma_R} \frac{e^{iz}}{z^2+1}\,dz \to 0 \quad \text{as } R \to \infty.$
$

(A direct estimate also works: $\int_0^\pi e^{-R\sin\theta}R\,d\theta \le 2R\int
_0^{\pi/2} e^{-2R\theta/\pi}\,d\theta = \frac{\pi(1-e^{-R})}{1} \to$ bounded, an
d dividing by $R^2-1$ gives $0$.)

### Step 4. Take the limit $R \to \infty$

Letting $R \to \infty$ in (1):

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{e^{ix}}{x^2+1}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{e}.$$

Taking real parts of both sides (the imaginary part involves $\sin x$, an odd fu
nction over a symmetric interval, contributing $0$):

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\cos x}{x^2+1}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{e}.$$

### Step 5. Exploit evenness

The integrand $\frac{\cos x}{x^2+1}$ is an even function of $x$. Therefore

$$\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{\cos x}{x^2+1}\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty
} \frac{\cos x}{x^2+1}\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\pi}{e} = \frac{\pi}{2e}.$$

### Conclusion

$$\boxed{\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{\cos x}{x^2+1}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{2e}.}$$
