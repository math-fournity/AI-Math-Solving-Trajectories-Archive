## Proof

**Answer: Yes, such a vector field always exists.**

We prove a stronger statement: on a compact oriented Riemannian manifold $M$ wit
h nonempty boundary $\partial M$, for *any* smooth function $g \in C^\infty(M)$,
 there exists $X \in \Gamma(TM)$ with $\operatorname{div} X = g$. The claim then
 follows by taking $g = \det df$.

### Construction via the Dirichlet problem

Consider the **Dirichlet boundary value problem** for the Laplace–Beltrami opera
tor:
$$\Delta u = g \quad \text{on } M, \qquad u\big|_{\partial M} = 0,$$
where $\Delta = \operatorname{div} \circ \nabla$ is the Laplace–Beltrami operato
r (geometer's sign convention). If this problem admits a smooth solution $u$, th
en setting $X = \nabla u$ gives
$$\operatorname{div} X = \operatorname{div}(\nabla u) = \Delta u = g,$$
and we are done. It remains to show the Dirichlet problem is always solvable.

### Well-posedness of the Dirichlet problem

**Trivial kernel (maximum principle).** The kernel of $\Delta$ subject to the Di
richlet condition consists of functions $u$ satisfying $\Delta u = 0$ on $M$ and
 $u|_{\partial M} = 0$. Such $u$ is harmonic. By the **maximum principle** for h
armonic functions on compact manifolds with boundary, $u$ attains its maximum an
d minimum on $\partial M$. Since $u|_{\partial M} = 0$, we have $\max_M u = \min
_M u = 0$, hence $u \equiv 0$.

**Invertibility.** The Dirichlet Laplacian $\Delta : H^1_0(M) \cap H^2(M) \to L^
2(M)$ is self-adjoint and has trivial kernel (by the maximum principle above). B
y the elliptic theory on compact manifolds with boundary, its eigenvalues (geome
ter's convention) satisfy $0 > \lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \to -\infty$
, so $0$ is not in the spectrum. Therefore $\Delta$ is an isomorphism from $H^1_
0(M) \cap H^2(M)$ onto $L^2(M)$, and the Dirichlet problem $\Delta u = g$, $u|_{
\partial M} = 0$ admits a unique weak solution $u \in H^1_0(M)$ for every $g \in
 L^2(M)$.

**Elliptic regularity.** By standard elliptic regularity for the Dirichlet probl
em on compact manifolds with boundary (cf. Gilbarg–Trudinger, Ch. 8; or Evans, P
DE, §6.3), if $g \in C^\infty(M)$, then the weak solution $u$ is smooth: $u \in
C^\infty(M)$.

### Why the boundary is essential

On a **closed** manifold ($\partial M = \emptyset$), the divergence theorem forc
es $\int_M \operatorname{div} X \, dV = 0$ for all $X$, so a necessary condition
 for $\operatorname{div} X = g$ is $\int_M g \, dV = 0$. This obstruction genera
lly fails for $g = \det df$.

On a manifold **with boundary**, the divergence theorem gives $\int_M \operatorn
ame{div} X \, dV = \int_{\partial M} \langle X, \nu\rangle \, dS$, which is *not
* constrained to vanish — the boundary flux is free. This removes the integral o
bstruction. Concretely, the Dirichlet boundary condition $u|_{\partial M} = 0$ e
liminates the constant functions (the kernel of $\Delta$ on a closed manifold),
rendering $\Delta$ invertible and the Poisson equation solvable for *arbitrary*
$g$.

### Conclusion

Given $g = \det df \in C^\infty(M)$, solve the Dirichlet problem
$$\Delta u = \det df, \qquad u\big|_{\partial M} = 0,$$
obtaining a unique $u \in C^\infty(M)$. Set $X = \nabla u \in \Gamma(TM)$. Then
$$\operatorname{div} X = \operatorname{div}(\nabla u) = \Delta u = \det df.$$

Therefore, **yes**, there always exists a vector field $X \in \Gamma(TM)$ with $
\operatorname{div} X = \det df$. $\blacksquare$
