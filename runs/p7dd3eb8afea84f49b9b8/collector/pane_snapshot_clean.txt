## Answer

**No**, a linearly ordered topological space with a countable dense subset need
not be metrizable.

### Counterexample: The Double Arrow Space (Alexandrov's Split Interval)

**Construction.** Let $X = [0,1] \times \{0,1\}$ with the lexicographic order: $
(a,i) < (b,j)$ iff $a < b$, or $a = b$ and $i < j$. Equip $X$ with the order top
ology. This is a linearly ordered topological space (LOTS) with minimum $(0,0)$
and maximum $(1,1)$.

---

**Step 1: $X$ has a countable dense subset.**

Set $D = \{(q,0) : q \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]\} \cup \{(q,1) : q \in \mathbb{Q}
 \cap [0,1]\}$, which is countable. We verify $D$ meets every nonempty basic ope
n set.

- **Open interval** $((a,i),(b,j))$ nonempty with $(a,i) < (b,j)$: If $a < b$, p
ick $q \in \mathbb{Q}$ with $a < q < b$; then $(a,i) < (q,0) < (b,j)$, so $(q,0)
 \in D \cap ((a,i),(b,j))$. If $a = b$, then $i=0, j=1$ is forced, but $((a,0),(
a,1)) = \emptyset$ since $(a,0)$ and $(a,1)$ are consecutive—contradicting nonem
ptiness.

- **Left ray** $\{x : x < (b,j)\}$ nonempty (so $(b,j) > (0,0)$): If $b > 0$, pi
ck $q \in \mathbb{Q}$ with $0 \le q < b$; then $(q,0) \in D$ and $(q,0) < (b,j)$
. If $b=0, j=1$, the ray equals $\{(0,0)\}$ and $(0,0) \in D$.

- **Right ray** $\{x : x > (a,i)\}$ nonempty (so $(a,i) < (1,1)$): If $a < 1$, p
ick $q \in \mathbb{Q}$ with $a < q \le 1$; then $(q,0) \in D$ and $(q,0) > (a,i)
$. If $a=1, i=0$, the ray equals $\{(1,1)\}$ and $(1,1) \in D$.

Thus $D$ is dense. $\checkmark$

---

**Step 2: $X$ is compact Hausdorff.**

$X$ is a complete linear order with endpoints, hence compact in the order topolo
gy. Every LOTS is Hausdorff. $\checkmark$

---

**Step 3: $X$ is not second-countable.**

Consider the subspace $U = \{(a,1) : a \in [0,1]\}$ (the "upper copy"). We show
$U$ is homeomorphic to the **Sorgenfrey line** (lower limit topology on $[0,1]$)
.

Identify $(a,1) \leftrightarrow a$. Take the basic open interval $((a,0),(d,0))$
 in $X$ (with $d > a$) and intersect with $U$:

$$((a,0),(d,0)) \cap U = \{(e,1) : (a,0) < (e,1) < (d,0)\} = \{(e,1) : a \le e <
 d\},$$

since $(a,0) < (e,1)$ iff $e \ge a$, and $(e,1) < (d,0)$ iff $e < d$. Under the
identification, this is $[a,d)$—a basic open set of the Sorgenfrey topology. All
 other basic open sets of $X$ (intervals with $i=1$, rays) intersect $U$ in sets
 that are also Sorgenfrey-open. Hence $U \cong$ Sorgenfrey line.

The Sorgenfrey line is **not second-countable**: for each $a \in [0,1]$, any bas
is must contain some $B_a$ with $a \in B_a \subseteq [a, a+1) \cap [0,1]$. Since
 $a = \min(B_a)$, distinct $a$'s require distinct $B_a$'s, demanding uncountably
 many basis elements.

Since second-countability is hereditary (passes to subspaces), and $U$ is not se
cond-countable, $X$ is not second-countable. $\checkmark$

---

**Step 4: $X$ is not metrizable.**

If $X$ were metrizable, then—being compact (Step 2)—it would be second-countable
 (every compact metric space is second-countable). This contradicts Step 3. $\ch
eckmark$

---

**Conclusion.** The double arrow space $X = [0,1] \times \{0,1\}$ with the lexic
ographic order topology is a linearly ordered topological space that possesses a
 countable dense subset (Step 1) yet is not metrizable (Step 4). Therefore, a li
nearly ordered topological space with a countable dense subset **need not** be m
etrizable.
