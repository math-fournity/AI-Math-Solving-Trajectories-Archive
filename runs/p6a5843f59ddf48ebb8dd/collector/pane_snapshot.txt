## Proof

Define $r(k) = p(k)\,q(k)$. The system becomes the single linear recurrence

$$r(k+2) - 3\,r(k+1) + 2\,r(k) = 0 \qquad (\forall k\in\mathbb{N}).$$

Its characteristic polynomial is $x^{2}-3x+2=(x-1)(x-2)$, so every solution is o
f the form

$$r(k) = A + B\cdot 2^{k}$$

for constants $A,B$. Thus the original problem reduces to:

> Can we choose constants $A,B$ and factorizations $p(k)\,q(k)=A+B\cdot 2^{k}$ w
ith $p(k),q(k)\in\mathbb{N}$ and $p(k),q(k)>2$ for every natural $k$?

**Construction.** Take $A=4,\;B=4$, i.e. $r(k)=4+4\cdot 2^{k}=4\,(1+2^{k})$. Set

$$p(k)=4,\qquad q(k)=1+2^{k}.$$

**Verification of the bounds.** For every natural $k\ge 1$ we have $2^{k}\ge 2$,
 hence $q(k)=1+2^{k}\ge 3>2$, and $p(k)=4>2$. Both are natural numbers.

**Verification of the recurrence.** With $r(k)=p(k)q(k)=4(1+2^{k})$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
r(k+2)-3r(k+1)+2r(k)
&= 4\bigl[(1+2^{k+2})-3(1+2^{k+1})+2(1+2^{k})\bigr] \\
&= 4\bigl[(1-3+2)+2^{k}(4-6+2)\bigr] \\
&= 4\bigl[0+0\bigr]=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore

$$p(k+2)q(k+2)-3p(k+1)q(k+1)+2p(k)q(k)=0\qquad(\forall k\in\mathbb{N}).$$

The pair of sequences $\;p(k)=4,\;q(k)=1+2^{k}\;$ satisfies the system for all $
k$, with every term a natural number strictly greater than $2$.

Hence such a pair of integer sequences **does exist**. $\blacksquare$
