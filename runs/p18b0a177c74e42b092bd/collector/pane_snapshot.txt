## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, $|A_n \Delta B| \to 0$.

**Setup.** Let $\mu$ denote Lebesgue measure on $[0,1]$. The hypothesis is that
for every measurable $C \subseteq [0,1]$,
$$\mu(A_n \cap C) \to \mu(B \cap C).$$
Equivalently, writing $f_n = \mathbf{1}_{A_n}$ and $g = \mathbf{1}_{B}$,
$$\int f_n \mathbf{1}_C\,d\mu \to \int g\,\mathbf{1}_C\,d\mu \quad\text{for ever
y measurable } C.$$

**Step 1: Weak convergence in $L^2$.** By linearity, the convergence extends to
all simple functions $s = \sum_i a_i \mathbf{1}_{C_i}$:
$$\int f_n\, s\,d\mu \to \int g\, s\,d\mu.$$
Since $0 \le f_n \le 1$, we have $\|f_n\|_{L^2} \le 1$, so $\{f_n\}$ is bounded
in $L^2([0,1])$. Simple functions are dense in $L^2([0,1])$. A standard density/
approximation argument then gives
$$\int f_n\, h\,d\mu \to \int g\, h\,d\mu \quad\text{for every } h \in L^2([0,1]
),$$
i.e., $f_n \rightharpoonup g$ weakly in $L^2$.

**Step 2: Norm convergence.** Taking $C = [0,1]$ in the hypothesis:
$$\mu(A_n) = \mu(A_n \cap [0,1]) \to \mu(B \cap [0,1]) = \mu(B).$$
Since $\|f_n\|_{L^2}^2 = \int \mathbf{1}_{A_n}^2\,d\mu = \mu(A_n)$ and $\|g\|_{L
^2}^2 = \mu(B)$, we obtain
$$\|f_n\|_{L^2}^2 \to \|g\|_{L^2}^2.$$

**Step 3: Strong convergence via the Radon–Riesz property.** In a Hilbert space,
 weak convergence $f_n \rightharpoonup g$ together with convergence of norms $\|
f_n\| \to \|g\|$ implies strong (norm) convergence $\|f_n - g\| \to 0$. (Proof:
$\|f_n - g\|^2 = \|f_n\|^2 - 2\langle f_n, g\rangle + \|g\|^2 \to \|g\|^2 - 2\|g
\|^2 + \|g\|^2 = 0$, using weak convergence with test function $h = g$.)

Therefore
$$\|f_n - g\|_{L^2}^2 = \int (\mathbf{1}_{A_n} - \mathbf{1}_B)^2\,d\mu = \mu(A_n
 \Delta B) \to 0.$$

**Conclusion.** The hypothesis implies $\mu(A_n \Delta B) \to 0$. $\blacksquare$
