## Proof

**Goldbach's Conjecture** states: *Every even integer greater than 2 can be expr
essed as the sum of two primes.*

**Claim:** Goldbach's Conjecture implies that for any positive integer $k$, ther
e exists a positive integer $n$ such that all of $2n, 2n+2, \ldots, 2n+2k$ are e
xpressible as the sum of two primes.

**Proof:**

Let $k$ be an arbitrary positive integer. Choose $n = 2$.

Then the numbers in question are:
$$2n,\; 2n+2,\; 2n+4,\; \ldots,\; 2n+2k$$
$$= 4,\; 6,\; 8,\; \ldots,\; 4+2k.$$

These are $k+1$ even integers, each of which is greater than 2 (since the smalle
st is 4).

By Goldbach's Conjecture, **every** even integer greater than 2 is expressible a
s the sum of two primes. Therefore, each of $4, 6, 8, \ldots, 4+2k$ is a sum of
two primes.

Hence, for this choice of $n = 2$, all numbers $2n, 2n+2, \ldots, 2n+2k$ are exp
ressible as the sum of two primes.

Since $k$ was arbitrary, the statement holds for every positive integer $k$. $\b
lacksquare$

**Remark:** The implication is immediate because Goldbach's Conjecture is a *uni
versal* statement — it asserts that *every* even integer $> 2$ is a sum of two p
rimes. The numbers $2n, 2n+2, \ldots, 2n+2k$ (for any $n \geq 2$) are all even i
ntegers $> 2$, so they are all covered by the conjecture simultaneously. No addi
tional work is needed to find such an $n$; indeed, *every* $n \geq 2$ works for
every $k$.
