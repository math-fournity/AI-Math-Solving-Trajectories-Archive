Looking at this problem, I need to find the largest $n$ such that $n!$ equals th
e product of $n-3$ consecutive positive integers.

**Answer leak check:** The problem only gives $6! = 8 \times 9 \times 10$ as an
example. No solution is leaked. Proceeding.

## Setup

Let the product of $n-3$ consecutive positive integers starting from $a$ equal $
n!$:

$$n! = a(a+1)(a+2)\cdots(a+n-4).$$

Writing this with $k = a - 1 \geq 0$:

$$n! \cdot k! = (n + k - 3)!.$$

Setting $r = n - 3 \geq 1$ and $s = k \geq 0$, this becomes:

$$(s+1)(s+2)\cdots(s+r) = (r+3)! \tag{$\star$}$$

where the LHS is a product of $r$ consecutive integers starting from $s+1$, and
we seek $n = r + 3$.

---

## Case Analysis

### Case 1: $s + r \leq r + 3$, i.e., $s \leq 3$

Then every factor on the LHS is $\leq r+3$, and the LHS divides $(r+3)!$ but is
missing the factors $1, 2, \ldots, s$. Specifically:
- $s=0$: LHS $= r!$, need $r! = (r+3)!$, impossible.
- $s=1$: LHS $= (r+1)!/1$, need $(r+1)! = (r+3)!$, impossible.
- $s=2$: LHS $= (r+2)!/2$, need $(r+2)!/2 = (r+3)!$, impossible.
- $s=3$: LHS $= (r+3)!/6$, need $1/6 = 1$, impossible.

**No solutions.**

### Case 3: $s + 1 > r + 3$, i.e., $s \geq r + 3$

All LHS factors exceed $r+3$, so LHS $\geq (r+4)^r$. We need $(r+4)^r \leq (r+3)
!$.

- $r = 1$: $(s+1) = 4! = 24 \Rightarrow s = 23$. **Solution: $n = 4$.** ✓ ($4! =
 24$)
- $r = 2$: $(s+1)(s+2) = 120$. Checking: $10 \times 11 = 110$, $11 \times 12 = 1
32$. No.
- $r = 3$: $(s+1)(s+2)(s+3) = 720$. $8 \times 9 \times 10 = 720 \Rightarrow s =
7$. **Solution: $n = 6$.** ✓ ($6! = 8 \cdot 9 \cdot 10$)
- $r = 4$: $(s+1)\cdots(s+4) = 5040$. Minimum at $s=7$: $8 \times 9 \times 10 \t
imes 11 = 7920 > 5040$. No.
- $r \geq 5$: $(r+4)^r > (r+3)!$ (verified: $r=5$ gives $9^5 = 59049 > 40320 = 8
!$; ratio worsens for larger $r$). No.

### Case 2: $4 \leq s \leq r + 2$

Define the ratio $R(r) = \frac{(s+r)!}{s!\,(r+3)!}$. We need $R(r) = 1$. Note:

$$R(r+1) = R(r) \cdot \frac{s+r+1}{r+4}.$$

For $s \geq 8$ and $r \geq s-2 \geq 6$: $\frac{s+r+1}{r+4} \geq \frac{15}{10} =
1.5 > 1$, and $R(s-2) > 1$ (checked at $s=8, r=6$: $R = 2162160/362880 \approx 5
.96$). So $R$ is increasing and already $>1$ — **no solutions for $s \geq 8$.**

For $s = 7, r \geq 5$: $R(5) = 12!/(7!\cdot 8!) \approx 2.36 > 1$ and increasing
. **No solutions.**

For $s = 6$: $R(r) = \frac{(r+6)(r+5)(r+4)}{720}$. Setting $R = 1$: $(r+6)(r+5)(
r+4) = 720 = 8\cdot9\cdot10$, giving $r = 4$. Strictly increasing, so **unique s
olution: $r=4$, $n = 7$.** ✓ ($7! = 7\cdot8\cdot9\cdot10$)

For $s = 5$: $R(r) = \frac{(r+5)(r+4)}{120}$. Setting $R=1$: $(r+5)(r+4) = 120$.
 But $10\times11 = 110$ and $11\times 12 = 132$ — **no integer solution.** (Conf
irmed: $R(6) = 11/12 < 1$, $R(7) = 11/10 > 1$, ratio jumps over 1.)

For $s = 4$: $R(r) = \frac{r+4}{24}$. Setting $R = 1$: $r + 4 = 24$, so $r = 20$
. Strictly increasing, so **unique solution: $r = 20$, $n = 23$.** ✓

**Verification of $n = 23$:** $23! = 5 \times 6 \times 7 \times \cdots \times 24
 = \frac{24!}{4!} = \frac{24!}{24} = 23!$. The product has $24 - 5 + 1 = 20 = 23
 - 3$ consecutive factors. ✓

## Summary of All Solutions

| $n$ | Product | Verification |
|-----|---------|-------------|
| $4$ | $24$ | $4! = 24$ ✓ |
| $6$ | $8 \times 9 \times 10$ | $6! = 720$ ✓ |
| $7$ | $7 \times 8 \times 9 \times 10$ | $7! = 5040$ ✓ |
| $23$ | $5 \times 6 \times \cdots \times 24$ | $23! = 24!/4!$ ✓ |

The analysis is exhaustive: Case 1 yields nothing, Case 3 yields $n \in \{4, 6\}
$, and Case 2 yields $n \in \{7, 23\}$, with all remaining values of $s$ ruled o
ut by monotonicity of the ratio $R(r)$.

## Answer

The largest positive integer $n$ for which a Perfect Sequence exists is $\boxed{
23}$.
