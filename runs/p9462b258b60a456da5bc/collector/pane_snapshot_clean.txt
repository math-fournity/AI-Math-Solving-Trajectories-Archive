Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether a two-sided sequence $\{a_n
\}_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}$ not in $l^2$, whose Fourier series $\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\in
fty} a_n e^{inx}$ converges to zero a.e., must be identically zero.

This is a classical question in the uniqueness theory of trigonometric series. L
et me reason through it.

**Answer: No, $\{a_n\}$ need not be zero.**

## Proof

### Step 1: Uniqueness holds in the $l^2$ case

Suppose $\{a_n\} \in l^2(\mathbb{Z})$. By the **Riesz–Fischer theorem**, there e
xists a function $f \in L^2(\mathbb{T})$ such that $a_n = \hat{f}(n)$ for all $n
 \in \mathbb{Z}$.

The partial sums $S_N(x) = \sum_{|n| \le N} a_n e^{inx}$ satisfy:
- $S_N \to f$ in $L^2(\mathbb{T})$ (standard $L^2$ convergence of Fourier series
).
- $S_N(x) \to 0$ for a.e. $x$ (by hypothesis).

From $L^2$-convergence, a subsequence $\{S_{N_k}\}$ converges to $f$ a.e. But by
 hypothesis, $S_{N_k}(x) \to 0$ a.e. as well. Therefore $f = 0$ a.e., which forc
es $\hat{f}(n) = a_n = 0$ for all $n$.

**Conclusion:** If $\{a_n\} \in l^2$ and the series converges to 0 a.e., then $\
{a_n\} \equiv 0$. Uniqueness holds in the $l^2$ regime.

### Step 2: Menshov's counterexample (1916)

In 1916, **Menshov** proved the following landmark result:

> *There exists a trigonometric series $\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n e^{inx}$ t
hat is not identically zero (i.e., not all $c_n = 0$) but converges to $0$ almos
t everywhere on $[0, 2\pi)$.*

This is a **trigonometric series** in the general sense — it is not assumed to b
e the Fourier series of any integrable function. The coefficients $\{c_n\}$ are
just a two-sided sequence of complex numbers.

### Step 3: The counterexample coefficients are necessarily not in $l^2$

Suppose, for contradiction, that the coefficients $\{c_n\}$ of Menshov's series
were in $l^2(\mathbb{Z})$. Then by Step 1, the a.e. convergence to 0 would force
 all $c_n = 0$, contradicting the nontriviality of the series.

Therefore $\{c_n\} \notin l^2(\mathbb{Z})$.

### Step 4: Conclusion

Menshov's counterexample provides a two-sided sequence $\{c_n\}$ with:
- $\{c_n\} \notin l^2(\mathbb{Z})$,
- $\sum_{n} c_n e^{inx} = 0$ for a.e. $x$,
- $\{c_n\}$ is **not** identically zero.

This directly demonstrates that the condition $\{a_n\} \notin l^2$ does **not**
force $\{a_n\}$ to be zero. The uniqueness theorem for trigonometric series, whi
ch holds when $\{a_n\} \in l^2$ (via Riesz–Fischer), **fails** in the non-$l^2$
regime. The almost-everywhere convergence to zero is insufficient to guarantee u
niqueness of the coefficients when those coefficients are not square-summable.

**Remark.** This stands in contrast to **Cantor's uniqueness theorem** (1870), w
hich guarantees uniqueness when the series converges to zero *everywhere* (point
wise for all $x$), with no $l^2$ assumption needed. The passage from "everywhere
" to "almost everywhere" is precisely where uniqueness breaks down, as Menshov's
 construction shows. The $l^2$ condition is one sufficient condition that restor
es uniqueness even in the a.e. setting, but without it, uniqueness can fail.
