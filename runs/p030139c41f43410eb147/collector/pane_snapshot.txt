## Proof

We are given:
- $g_n(x) \to 0$ pointwise as $n \to \infty$, i.e., for every $x$, $\lim_{n\to\i
nfty} g_n(x) = 0$.
- $\int g_n(x)\,dx = 2$ for all $n$.

We are asked to find $\int \lim_{n\to\infty} g_n(x)\,dx$.

**Step 1: Identify the pointwise limit.** By hypothesis, $g_n(x) \to 0$ pointwis
e. Therefore the limit function is
$$\lim_{n\to\infty} g_n(x) = 0 \quad \text{for every } x.$$

**Step 2: Compute the integral of the limit.** Since the limit function is ident
ically zero,
$$\int \lim_{n\to\infty} g_n(x)\,dx = \int 0\,dx = 0.$$

**Step 3: Reconcile with the given condition.** The condition $\int g_n(x)\,dx =
 2$ for all $n$ does **not** contradict the result above. The equality
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\int g_n(x)\,dx = \int \lim_{n\to\infty} g_n(x)\,dx$$
requires additional hypotheses (e.g., dominated convergence, uniform integrabili
ty, or uniform convergence). Pointwise convergence alone is insufficient to inte
rchange the limit and the integral.

A concrete illustration: let $g_n(x) = 2n\cdot\mathbf{1}_{[0,1/n]}(x)$. Then $g_
n(x) \to 0$ pointwise (for any fixed $x>0$, eventually $x>1/n$; and at $x=0$, $g
_n(0)=2n\to\infty$ — so to get pointwise convergence to $0$ everywhere, shift sl
ightly, e.g. $g_n(x)=2n\cdot\mathbf{1}_{(0,1/n]}(x)$, giving $g_n(0)=0$ and $g_n
(x)\to 0$ for all $x$). Yet $\int g_n = 2n\cdot\tfrac{1}{n}=2$ for every $n$. Th
e mass escapes to a set of measure zero, so the limit function carries none of i
t.

**Conclusion.** The integral of the pointwise limit is determined solely by the
limit function, which is $0$:

$$\int \lim_{n\to\infty} g_n(x)\,dx = 0.$$
