The statement is **true**.

**Proof.**

Let $f$ be a (sufficiently regular, say $2\pi$-periodic) function with Fourier c
oefficients $\hat{f}(n)$, $n \in \mathbb{Z}$. The Fourier coefficients of the de
rivative $f'$ are related to those of $f$ by

$$\widehat{f'}(n) = in\,\hat{f}(n), \qquad n \in \mathbb{Z}$$

(with $\widehat{f'}(0) = 0$).

**Hypothesis.** $\{\widehat{f'}(n)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} \in l_2$, i.e.

$$\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} |\widehat{f'}(n)|^2 < \infty.$$

Substituting $\widehat{f'}(n) = in\,\hat{f}(n)$, this is equivalent to

$$\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} n^2\,|\hat{f}(n)|^2 < \infty. \tag{1}$$

**Goal.** Show $\{\hat{f}(n)\} \in l_1$, i.e. $\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} |\hat{f}(n)
| < \infty$.

We separate the $n=0$ term (a single finite value, irrelevant to convergence) an
d apply the **Cauchy–Schwarz inequality** to the remaining terms, writing $|\hat
{f}(n)| = \frac{1}{|n|}\cdot|n\,\hat{f}(n)|$:

$$\sum_{n\neq 0} |\hat{f}(n)| \;=\; \sum_{n\neq 0} \frac{1}{|n|}\,|n\,\hat{f}(n)
| \;\le\; \left(\sum_{n\neq 0} \frac{1}{n^2}\right)^{\!1/2}\!\left(\sum_{n\neq 0
} n^2\,|\hat{f}(n)|^2\right)^{\!1/2}.$$

Now examine each factor on the right-hand side:

- **First factor.** $\displaystyle\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{1}{n^2} = 2\sum_{n=1}^{\in
fty}\frac{1}{n^2} = 2\cdot\frac{\pi^2}{6} = \frac{\pi^2}{3} < \infty.$

- **Second factor.** $\displaystyle\sum_{n\neq 0} n^2\,|\hat{f}(n)|^2 \le \sum_{
n\in\mathbb{Z}} n^2\,|\hat{f}(n)|^2 < \infty$ by the hypothesis (1).

Both factors are finite, so their product is finite. Therefore

$$\sum_{n\neq 0} |\hat{f}(n)| < \infty,$$

and adding the single term $|\hat{f}(0)|$ gives

$$\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} |\hat{f}(n)| < \infty.$$

Hence $\{\hat{f}(n)\} \in l_1$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The key mechanism is that the hypothesis places $\hat{f}(n)$ in a *w
eighted* $l_2$ space with weight $n^2$, and the weight sequence $\{1/n\}$ is its
elf in $l_2$. Cauchy–Schwarz then transfers the weighted-$l_2$ summability into
unweighted $l_1$ summability. This is the discrete analogue of the Sobolev embed
ding $H^1(\mathbb{T}) \hookrightarrow C(\mathbb{T})$ (in one dimension), which g
uarantees absolute convergence of the Fourier series and hence continuity of $f$
.
