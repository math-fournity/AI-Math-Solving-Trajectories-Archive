## Solution to Exercise 2.5: Modified Bareiss' Algorithm for Rational Matrices

### 1. Recap of Bareiss' Algorithm (Integer Case)

Given an $n \times n$ integer matrix $A = (a_{ij})$, Bareiss' fraction-free Gaus
sian elimination computes the determinant using the recurrence:

$$a_{ij}^{(k)} = \frac{a_{kk}^{(k-1)} \cdot a_{ij}^{(k-1)} - a_{ik}^{(k-1)} \cdo
t a_{kj}^{(k-1)}}{a_{k-1,k-1}^{(k-2)}}, \qquad 1 \le k \le n-1$$

with the conventions $a_{ij}^{(0)} = a_{ij}$ and $a_{0,0}^{(-1)} = 1$.

**Key properties (Bareiss, 1968):**
- Every division is **exact** (the divisor $a_{k-1,k-1}^{(k-2)}$ divides the num
erator exactly in $\mathbb{Z}$), so all intermediates remain integers.
- The intermediate $a_{ij}^{(k)}$ equals (up to sign) the $(k{+}1) \times (k{+}1
)$ minor of $A$ formed by rows $\{1,\ldots,k,i\}$ and columns $\{1,\ldots,k,j\}$
. This gives the **Hadamard bound**:

$$|a_{ij}^{(k)}| \le \bigl(\sqrt{k+1}\cdot \beta\bigr)^{k+1}, \qquad \beta = \ma
x_{ij}|a_{ij}|.$$

- The determinant is $\det(A) = a_{nn}^{(n-1)}$.

### 2. The Modification for Rational Inputs

**Input:** $A = (a_{ij}) \in \mathbb{Q}^{n \times n}$, where each entry $a_{ij}
= p_{ij}/q_{ij}$ in lowest terms.

**Step 1 — Row-wise denominator clearing (per the hint).**
For each row $i$, compute the common denominator:
$$d_i = \operatorname{lcm}(q_{i1}, q_{i2}, \ldots, q_{in}).$$
Define the integer matrix $B = (b_{ij})$ by $b_{ij} = d_i \cdot a_{ij} \in \math
bb{Z}$. Equivalently, $B = D \cdot A$ where $D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1, \ldots
, d_n)$.

**Step 2 — Compute the total denominator factor.**
$$\Delta = \prod_{i=1}^{n} d_i.$$

**Step 3 — Apply (unmodified) Bareiss to $B$.**
Since $B$ has integer entries, Bareiss' algorithm runs with exact integer divisi
ons and produces $\det(B) \in \mathbb{Z}$.

**Step 4 — Recover the determinant.**
$$\det(A) = \frac{\det(B)}{\Delta}.$$

This is a single exact rational division at the very end.

### 3. Correctness Proof

**Theorem.** *The modified algorithm returns $\det(A)$ exactly.*

*Proof.* Since $B = D \cdot A$ with $D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1, \ldots, d_n)$,
 by the multiplicativity of the determinant:

$$\det(B) = \det(D) \cdot \det(A) = \left(\prod_{i=1}^n d_i\right) \cdot \det(A)
 = \Delta \cdot \det(A).$$

All $d_i \ge 1$ are positive integers (being least common multiples of positive
denominators), so $\Delta \ge 1$ and $\Delta \neq 0$. By the correctness of Bare
iss' algorithm on the integer matrix $B$, Step 3 yields the exact integer $\det(
B)$. Therefore:

$$\det(A) = \frac{\det(B)}{\Delta}$$

is computed exactly as a rational number. $\square$

### 4. Why Row-Wise Clearing (the Hint) Is Better Than Global Clearing

One could alternatively find a single global common denominator $d = \operatorna
me{lcm}_{i,j}(q_{ij})$ and set $B' = d \cdot A$ (every entry multiplied by $d$),
 yielding $\det(A) = \det(B')/d^n$. However, this is inferior:

| | Row-wise clearing | Global clearing |
|---|---|---|
| Denominator size | $\|d_i\| \le nL$ bits (lcm of $n$ denominators) | $\|d\| \l
e n^2 L$ bits (lcm of $n^2$ denominators) |
| Entry size of $B$ | $\|b_{ij}\| \le (n{+}1)L$ bits | $\|b'_{ij}\| \le (n^2{+}1
)L$ bits |
| Final divisor | $\Delta = \prod d_i$, $\|\Delta\| \le n^2 L$ bits | $d^n$, $\|
d^n\| \le n^3 L$ bits |

Here $L = \max_{ij} \max(\log|p_{ij}|, \log q_{ij})$ is the input bit-size. Row-
wise clearing keeps entries and intermediates dramatically smaller, directly red
ucing the cost of every arithmetic operation in Bareiss.

#### 5.1 Experimental Design

- **(M2) Rational Gaussian elimination with GCD reduction** at each step.

- **(M4) Modified Bareiss** (row-clearing + integer Bareiss, as described above)

Measure: (a) maximum bit-size of any intermediate value, (b) total running time,

#### 5.2 Expected Results and Analysis

**Bit-size growth of intermediates:**

In Gaussian elimination (with or without pivoting), the Schur complement entry a
t step $k$ satisfies:
$$a_{ij}^{(k)} = \frac{\det(B[1..k, i;\; 1..k, j])}{\det(B[1..k;\; 1..k])},$$
i.e., it is a ratio of two $(k{+}1)\times(k{+}1)$ minors of $B$. When fully redu
ced, both numerator and denominator have bit-size $O(knL)$.

- **(M1) No reduction:** The numerator at step $k$ is formed by cross-multiplyin
g two fractions from step $k{-}1$, roughly **doubling** the bit-size at each ste
p. After $k$ steps, the unreduced numerator has bit-size $\Theta(2^k \cdot L)$ —
 **exponential blowup**. This makes M1 impractical for $n \gtrsim 20$.

- **(M2) With GCD reduction:** After reduction, intermediates have bit-size $O(k
nL)$, matching the minor bound. However, each step requires expensive GCD comput
ations on $O(knL)$-bit numbers (cost $O((knL)^2)$ via Euclid, or $O(knL \cdot \l
og(knL))$ via fast GCD), and both numerator and denominator must be stored and o
perated on — **doubling the data**.

- **(M3) Bareiss on rationals:** The Bareiss recurrence involves products of fou
r rational numbers in the numerator and one in the denominator. The "exact divis
ion" property that makes integer Bareiss work **does not help in $\mathbb{Q}$**
— every division is trivially exact in $\mathbb{Q}$, so there is no structural c
ancellation. Without reduction, the rational intermediates blow up exponentially
 (same as M1). With reduction, it degenerates to M2 with extra overhead. **M3 of
fers no advantage over M2.**

- **(M4) Modified Bareiss:** Intermediates are **integers** equal to minors of $
B$, with bit-size $O(knL)$ by the Hadamard bound. No denominators are carried du
ring elimination. The exact integer division in Bareiss is cheaper than general
rational division + GCD. Only **one** rational division occurs (at the very end)
. This is the most efficient.

**Summary table (asymptotic bit complexity for $n \times n$ matrix, entry bit-si
ze $L$):**

| Method | Max intermediate bit-size | GCD cost | Total bit complexity |
|--------|--------------------------|----------|---------------------|
| M1 (naive rational) | $\Theta(2^n L)$ — **exponential** | None | Exponential |
| M2 (rational + GCD) | $O(n^2 L)$ | $O(n^3 \cdot (nL)^2)$ | $O(n^5 L^2 + n^5 L^
2)$ = $O(n^5 L^2)$ |
| M3 (Bareiss on $\mathbb{Q}$) | Exponential (no reduction) / $O(n^2 L)$ (with r
eduction) | Same as M2 | No better than M2 |
| **M4 (modified Bareiss)** | $O(n^2 L)$ | **None** (exact division only) | $O(n
^5 L^2)$ with **smaller constants** |

**Why M4 has smaller constants than M2:**

1. **No GCD computations:** Bareiss' exact division (integer division where the
result is guaranteed to be an integer) costs $O(M(n^2 L))$ where $M$ is multipli
cation cost — cheaper than GCD which requires a full Euclidean algorithm.

2. **Single representation:** M4 stores only integers ($O(n^2 L)$ bits each); M2
 stores rationals (numerator + denominator, $2 \times O(n^2 L)$ bits each).

3. **No fraction reduction overhead:** M2 must compute $\gcd(\text{num}, \text{d
en})$ and divide both at every step — $O(n^3)$ GCD operations total. M4 performs
 zero GCDs.

4. **Single final division:** M4 performs exactly one rational division ($\det(B
)/\Delta$) at the end; M2 performs $O(n^3)$ rational operations.

Empirically, M4 is expected to be **2–5× faster** than M2 and **dramatically fas
ter** (by exponential factors) than M1 and M3 for moderate $n$.
