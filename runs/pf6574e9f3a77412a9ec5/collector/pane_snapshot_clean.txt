Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the Lagrange (mean-value) f
orm of the second-order Taylor expansion holds for a twice Gâteaux differentiabl
e scalar-valued function on a normed space.

## Proof

**Yes**, such a Taylor expansion is possible. The key insight is that although G
âteaux differentiability is weaker than Fréchet differentiability, the Lagrange
remainder form with $\theta \in (0,1)$ only requires information along the line
segment from $\bar{u}$ to $u$. Along this segment, the problem reduces to a one-
dimensional Taylor theorem, and Gâteaux differentiability—being precisely direct
ional differentiability—is exactly sufficient for this reduction.

**Step 1: Reduce to one dimension.**

Define $g:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ by
$$g(t) = f\bigl(\bar{u} + t(u-\bar{u})\bigr).$$

**Step 2: Compute $g'$ and $g''$.**

Since $f$ is Gâteaux differentiable, $g$ is differentiable on $(0,1)$ with
$$g'(t) = f'\bigl(\bar{u}+t(u-\bar{u})\bigr)(u-\bar{u}),$$
where $f'(x)$ denotes the Gâteaux derivative (a continuous linear functional on
$\mathbb{X}$) evaluated at the direction $(u-\bar{u})$.

Since $f$ is twice Gâteaux differentiable, $f'$ is Gâteaux differentiable. There
fore $g'$ is differentiable on $(0,1)$ with
$$g''(t) = \lim_{s\to 0}\frac{f'(\bar{u}+(t+s)(u-\bar{u}))(u-\bar{u}) - f'(\bar{
u}+t(u-\bar{u}))(u-\bar{u})}{s} = f''\bigl(\bar{u}+t(u-\bar{u})\bigr)(u-\bar{u},
\, u-\bar{u}),$$
i.e., $g''(t) = f''(\bar{u}+t(u-\bar{u}))(u-\bar{u})^2$, the bilinear form $f''$
 evaluated at the pair $(u-\bar{u}, u-\bar{u})$.

**Step 3: Verify continuity of $g$ and $g'$ on $[0,1]$.**

- $g$ is differentiable on $(0,1)$, hence continuous on $(0,1)$. At the endpoint
s: Gâteaux differentiability of $f$ at $\bar{u}$ (resp. $u$) implies $f$ is cont
inuous along the direction $(u-\bar{u})$ at $\bar{u}$ (resp. $u$), so $g(t)\to g
(0)$ as $t\to 0^+$ and $g(t)\to g(1)$ as $t\to 1^-$. Thus $g$ is continuous on $
[0,1]$.

- $g'$ is differentiable on $(0,1)$ (since $g''$ exists there), hence continuous
 on $(0,1)$. At the endpoints: Gâteaux differentiability of $f'$ at $\bar{u}$ gi
ves
$$\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{f'(\bar{u}+s(u-\bar{u})) - f'(\bar{u})}{s} = f''(\bar{u})(u
-\bar{u}),$$
which in particular implies $f'(\bar{u}+s(u-\bar{u}))\to f'(\bar{u})$ as $s\to 0
$, so $g'(t)\to g'(0)$ as $t\to 0^+$. The same argument at $u$ gives $g'(t)\to g
'(1)$ as $t\to 1^-$. Thus $g'$ is continuous on $[0,1]$.

**Step 4: Apply the one-dimensional Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder.**

We have established:
- $g$ and $g'$ are continuous on $[0,1]$,
- $g'$ is differentiable on $(0,1)$ (equivalently, $g''$ exists on $(0,1)$).

By the classical Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder ($n=1$), there exists $\
theta\in(0,1)$ such that
$$g(1) = g(0) + g'(0)(1-0) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,g''(\theta)(1-0)^2.$$

*Proof of this step (for completeness):* Set $M = \frac{g(1)-g(0)-g'(0)}{1/2}$ a
nd define
$$h(t) = g(1) - g(t) - g'(t)(1-t) - \tfrac{M}{2}(1-t)^2.$$
Then $h(0)=0$ (by the choice of $M$) and $h(1)=0$. Since $h$ is continuous on $[
0,1]$ and differentiable on $(0,1)$, Rolle's theorem yields $\theta\in(0,1)$ wit
h $h'(\theta)=0$. Computing:
$$h'(t) = -g'(t) - g''(t)(1-t) + g'(t) + M(1-t) = (1-t)\bigl(M - g''(t)\bigr).$$
Since $1-\theta \neq 0$, we get $M = g''(\theta)$, giving the stated formula. $\
square$

**Step 5: Substitute back.**

Substituting the expressions for $g$, $g'$, $g''$ into the one-dimensional resul
t:
$$f(u) = f(\bar{u}) + f'(\bar{u})(u-\bar{u}) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,f''\bigl(\bar{u}+\t
heta(u-\bar{u})\bigr)(u-\bar{u})^2,$$
for some $\theta\in(0,1)$.

**Why Gâteaux suffices (and the role of scalar-valuedness).** The Lagrange form
with a single $\theta$ is special to **scalar-valued** functions. For vector-val
ued maps $f:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}$, the mean value theorem in Lagrange form fa
ils (one cannot generally find a single intermediate point). Here, since $f:\mat
hbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}$, the restriction $g(t)=f(\bar{u}+t(u-\bar{u}))$ is a real-v
alued function of one real variable, and the classical one-dimensional Lagrange
remainder applies. Gâteaux differentiability—being exactly differentiability alo
ng lines—provides everything needed: the existence of $g'$ and $g''$ along the s
egment, and the directional continuity of $f$ and $f'$ at the endpoints. No Fréc
het differentiability is required. $\blacksquare$
