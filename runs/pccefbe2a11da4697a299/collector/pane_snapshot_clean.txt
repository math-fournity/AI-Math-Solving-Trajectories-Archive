## Proof

**Setup.** Since $S^n$ is compact and $f$ is continuous, $f(S^n)$ is a compact c
onnected subset of $\mathbb{R}$, hence a closed interval $[m, M]$ where $m = \mi
n_{S^n} f$ and $M = \max_{S^n} f$.

**Key topological fact.** For $n \ge 2$, the space $S^n \setminus F$ is (path-)c
onnected for every finite set $F \subset S^n$.

*Proof of the fact.* If $F = \{p_1, \dots, p_k\}$ is nonempty, stereographic pro
jection from $p_1$ gives a homeomorphism $S^n \setminus \{p_1\} \cong \mathbb{R}
^n$, under which $S^n \setminus F \cong \mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{k-1 \text{ poin
ts}\}$. Since $n \ge 2$, any two points in $\mathbb{R}^n$ can be joined by a pat
h avoiding a prescribed finite set (there is enough room to route around finitel
y many points), so this space is path-connected. If $F = \emptyset$, then $S^n$
itself is path-connected for $n \ge 1$. ∎

**Main argument.** Let $t \in A$, so $f^{-1}(t) = \{p_1, \dots, p_k\}$ is finite
. Consider the set
$$U = S^n \setminus \{p_1, \dots, p_k\}.$$
By the fact above, $U$ is connected (since $n \ge 2$). The restriction $f|_U : U
 \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous, and by construction $f(x) \neq t$ for all $x \in
 U$. Therefore
$$f(U) \subseteq \mathbb{R} \setminus \{t\} = (-\infty, t) \cup (t, +\infty).$$
Since $U$ is connected and $f|_U$ is continuous, $f(U)$ is a connected subset of
 $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{t\}$. The set $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{t\}$ has exactly
two connected components, $(-\infty, t)$ and $(t, +\infty)$, so $f(U)$ lies enti
rely in one of them.

- **Case 1:** $f(U) \subseteq (t, +\infty)$, i.e., $f(x) > t$ for all $x \in U$.
 Then $f(x) \ge t$ for all $x \in S^n$, with equality exactly at $p_1, \dots, p_
k$. Hence $t = \min_{S^n} f = m$.

- **Case 2:** $f(U) \subseteq (-\infty, t)$, i.e., $f(x) < t$ for all $x \in U$.
 Then $f(x) \le t$ for all $x \in S^n$, with equality exactly at $p_1, \dots, p_
k$. Hence $t = \max_{S^n} f = M$.

In either case, $t \in \{m, M\}$. Since $t \in A$ was arbitrary,
$$A \subseteq \{m, M\},$$
and therefore $|A| \le 2$. $\blacksquare$
