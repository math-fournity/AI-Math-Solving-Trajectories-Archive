No. Here is a counterexample.

Take the topological space $X = \mathbb{R}^2$ (which has exactly one connected c
omponent, hence finitely many). Let

$$D_1 = \overline{B((0,0),1)}, \qquad D_2 = \overline{B((4,0),1)}$$

be two disjoint closed unit disks, and let $L = \{(t,0) : 1 \le t \le 3\}$ be th
e closed line segment joining the point $(1,0) \in D_1$ to the point $(3,0) \in
D_2$. Set

$$A = D_1 \cup L \cup D_2.$$

**$A$ is closed.** It is a finite union of closed subsets of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**$A$ is connected.** $D_1$, $L$, and $D_2$ are each connected (a closed disk is
 convex hence connected; a segment is connected). Moreover $D_1 \cap L \ni (1,0)
 \neq \emptyset$ and $L \cap D_2 \ni (3,0) \neq \emptyset$. A union of connected
 sets with nonempty successive intersections is connected, so $A$ is connected.

**$\operatorname{int}(A)$ is not connected.** The segment $L$ is one-dimensional
, so every point of $L \setminus (D_1 \cup D_2)$ has a neighborhood in $\mathbb{
R}^2$ that is not contained in $A$; hence such points are not interior points. P
oints of $D_1 \setminus L$ and $D_2 \setminus L$ that lie on the boundary circle
 are likewise not interior. The only interior points are those in the open disks
 $B((0,0),1)$ and $B((4,0),1)$. Thus

$$\operatorname{int}(A) = B((0,0),1) \;\cup\; B((4,0),1),$$

a disjoint union of two nonempty open sets. These two open disks are separated (
distance $2$ apart), so $\operatorname{int}(A)$ is **not connected**.

Therefore, even in a topological space with finitely many connected components (
here, just one), the interior of a closed and connected set need not be connecte
d.
