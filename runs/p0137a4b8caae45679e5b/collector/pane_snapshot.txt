# Solution

The answer is **No**: $g$ is not necessarily $k$ times continuously differentiab
le on $(-1,1)$.

## Counterexample

Let $n = 2$ (so $S_{n-1} = S_1$, the unit circle) and $k = 1$. Define:

$$a(t, x) = 1 \quad \text{for all } (t, x) \in [-1,1] \times S_1,$$
$$b(t, x) = t \quad \text{for all } (t, x) \in [-1,1] \times S_1,$$
$$f(t) = \max(t, 0).$$

## Verification of hypotheses

**$a$ and $b$ map into $[-1,1]$:** $a$ is the constant $1 \in [-1,1]$; $b(t,x) =
 t \in [-1,1]$. ✓

**$C^k$ in $t$ for each fixed $x$:** For every $x \in S_1$, the map $t \mapsto a
(t,x) = 1$ is $C^\infty$ on $(-1,1)$, and $t \mapsto b(t,x) = t$ is $C^\infty$ o
n $(-1,1)$. ✓

**$f$ is a.e. $C^k$:** $f(t) = \max(t,0)$ is $C^\infty$ on $(-1,0) \cup (0,1)$.
The exceptional set is $B = \{0\}$, which has Lebesgue measure zero, and $f$ is
$C^\infty$ on a neighborhood of every $t \notin B$. ✓

## Computation of $g$

Since $a(t,X) = 1$ and $b(t,X) = t$ do not depend on $X$:

$$g(t) = \mathbb{E}_X\bigl[f(a(t,X))\,f(b(t,X))\bigr] = \mathbb{E}_X\bigl[f(1)\c
dot f(t)\bigr] = f(1)\cdot f(t).$$

Now $f(1) = \max(1, 0) = 1 \neq 0$, so:

$$g(t) = \max(t, 0).$$

## $g$ is not $C^1$

The function $\max(t, 0)$ has a left derivative of $0$ and a right derivative of
 $1$ at $t = 0$, so it is not differentiable at $t = 0 \in (-1, 1)$. Hence $g$ i
s not $C^1$ on $(-1,1)$, and therefore not $C^k$ for any $k \geq 1$.

## Generality of the counterexample

This counterexample is independent of the dimension $n$: since $a$ and $b$ do no
t depend on $x \in S_{n-1}$, the expectation over $X$ is trivial for any $n \geq
 2$. It also works for every $k \geq 1$, since $\max(t,0)$ fails to be $C^1$ (an
d hence fails to be $C^k$ for all $k \geq 1$).

## Conclusion

There exist valid choices of $a$, $b$, and $f$ satisfying all the stated hypothe
ses for which $g$ is not $k$ times continuously differentiable on $(-1,1)$. Ther
efore, $g$ is **not** necessarily $k$ times continuously differentiable.
