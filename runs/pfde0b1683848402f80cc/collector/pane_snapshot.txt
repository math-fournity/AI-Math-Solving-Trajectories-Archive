## Proof

**Claim:** No, a one-to-one correspondence between $[0,1]$ and $\mathbb{N}$ can
be established by the proposed digit-concatenation method, and indeed no bijecti
on between $[0,1]$ and $\mathbb{N}$ exists at all.

We give two independent arguments. The first shows the *specific* method fails;
the second (Cantor's diagonal argument) shows the *goal* is impossible by any me
thod.

---

### Argument 1: The concatenation method is not even well-defined as a map into
$\mathbb{N}$

Every natural number has a **finite** decimal representation: $n \in \mathbb{N}$
 has at most $\lfloor \log_{10} n \rfloor + 1$ digits. In particular, the set of
 digits of any natural number is finite.

On the other hand, every $x \in [0,1]$ (except $0$) has a decimal expansion $0.d
_1 d_2 d_3 \dots$ with **infinitely many** digits after the decimal point. (Even
 terminating decimals like $0.5$ are written as $0.5000\dots$ or equivalently $0
.4999\dots$, both infinite.) Concatenating the digits $d_1 d_2 d_3 \cdots$ produ
ces an **infinite** string of digits.

An infinite string of digits is **not** a natural number, since natural numbers
are precisely those non-negative integers expressible by a finite digit string.
Hence the proposed "concatenation" does not land in $\mathbb{N}$ at all; the map
 is undefined as a function $[0,1] \to \mathbb{N}$.

Moreover, even ignoring finiteness, the map would not be well-defined (injective
): $0.5000\dots = 0.4999\dots$ are the same real number but would yield differen
t digit strings "$5000\dots$" and "$4999\dots$". So the method is ambiguous on i
ts domain before one even asks about bijectivity.

---

### Argument 2: No bijection $[0,1] \leftrightarrow \mathbb{N}$ exists (Cantor's
 diagonal argument)

Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists a bijection $f : \mathbb{N} \to [0
,1]$. List each value as a decimal expansion, choosing the non-terminating form
(ending in $999\dots$ rather than $000\dots$) to ensure uniqueness:

$$
f(1) = 0.d_{11} d_{12} d_{13} \cdots \\
f(2) = 0.d_{21} d_{22} d_{23} \cdots \\
f(3) = 0.d_{31} d_{32} d_{33} \cdots \\
\vdots
$$

Construct a real number $y = 0.e_1 e_2 e_3 \cdots$ by setting, for each $n \geq
1$,

$$
e_n = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } d_{nn} \neq 1, \\ 2 & \text{if } d_{nn} = 1.
\end{cases}
$$

(Any two distinct digits avoiding the diagonal value work; this choice also side
steps the $0.999\dots = 1.000\dots$ ambiguity because $y$'s digits are only $1$s
 and $2$s, never $0$ or $9$.)

Then $y \in [0,1]$, and for every $n \geq 1$, $y$ differs from $f(n)$ in the $n$
-th decimal digit ($e_n \neq d_{nn}$). Hence $y \neq f(n)$ for all $n$, so $y \n
otin \operatorname{range}(f)$. This contradicts the assumption that $f$ is surje
ctive.

Therefore no surjection $\mathbb{N} \to [0,1]$ exists, hence no bijection exists
. The interval $[0,1]$ is **uncountable**.

---

### Conclusion

The proposed digit-concatenation method fails immediately because it produces in
finite digit strings, which are not natural numbers (and is not even well-define
d due to decimal non-uniqueness). More fundamentally, Cantor's diagonal argument
 proves that $[0,1]$ is uncountable, so **no** one-to-one correspondence with $\
mathbb{N}$ can be established by any method whatsoever.
