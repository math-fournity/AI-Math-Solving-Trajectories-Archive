## Proof

**Setup.** We have $R = \mathbb{Z} + X\mathbb{Q}[X] \subset \mathbb{Q}[X]$, the
subring of polynomials with integer constant term and rational higher coefficien
ts. The set $I = X\mathbb{Q}[X]$ is the ideal of polynomials with zero constant
term (and rational coefficients), and is an $R$-submodule of $R$.

**Goal.** Show $I$ is not finitely generated as an $R$-module.

**Key observation: the $X$-coefficient map.** Define $\varphi: I \to \mathbb{Q}$
 by $\varphi(f) = $ the coefficient of $X$ in $f$. This is well-defined since ev
ery $f \in I$ has the form $a_1 X + a_2 X^2 + \cdots$ with $a_i \in \mathbb{Q}$,
 and $\varphi$ is surjective onto $\mathbb{Q}$ (since $\frac{q}{1}X \in I$ for e
very $q \in \mathbb{Q}$).

**Lemma (structure of $\varphi$ on $R$-linear combinations).** Let $f_1, \ldots,
 f_k \in I$ and $r_1, \ldots, r_k \in R$. Write $f_i = a_{i1}X + a_{i2}X^2 + \cd
ots$ (with $a_{ij} \in \mathbb{Q}$) and $r_i = b_{i0} + b_{i1}X + \cdots$ (with
$b_{i0} \in \mathbb{Z}$, $b_{ij} \in \mathbb{Q}$ for $j \geq 1$). Then the coeff
icient of $X$ in the product $r_i f_i$ is $b_{i0} \cdot a_{i1}$, since the only
way to obtain $X^1$ in the product is (constant term of $r_i$) $\times$ ($X$-ter
m of $f_i$). Therefore:

$$\varphi\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^k r_i f_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^k b_{i0}\, a_{i1}, \qu
ad \text{where each } b_{i0} \in \mathbb{Z}.$$

In particular, the set of $X$-coefficients achievable by $R$-linear combinations
 of $f_1, \ldots, f_k$ is exactly the $\mathbb{Z}$-linear span of $\{a_{11}, \ld
ots, a_{k1}\}$:

$$\varphi\!\left(\sum_i R\, f_i\right) = \mathbb{Z}\text{-span}(a_{11}, \ldots,
a_{k1}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}.$$

**Lemma ($\mathbb{Q}$ is not finitely generated as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module).** Sup
pose $q_1, \ldots, q_k \in \mathbb{Q}$. Write them with a common denominator $d
> 0$: $q_i = p_i / d$ with $p_i \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then every $\mathbb{Z}$-linear
combination $\sum n_i q_i = \frac{\sum n_i p_i}{d} \in \frac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$. B
ut $\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z} \subsetneq \mathbb{Q}$, since e.g. $\frac{1}{d+1} \not
in \frac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$ (as $\frac{d}{d+1} \notin \mathbb{Z}$). Hence no finit
e set generates $\mathbb{Q}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$. $\square$

**Main argument.** Suppose for contradiction that $I$ is finitely generated as a
n $R$-module, say $I = R f_1 + \cdots + R f_k$ with $f_i \in I$. Write $f_i = a_
{i1} X + (\text{higher terms})$.

Since $\varphi$ is surjective onto $\mathbb{Q}$ and $I = \sum R f_i$, we have:

$$\mathbb{Q} = \varphi(I) = \varphi\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^k R f_i\right) = \mathbb{Z
}\text{-span}(a_{11}, \ldots, a_{k1}).$$

But by the lemma above, $\mathbb{Z}\text{-span}(a_{11}, \ldots, a_{k1})$ is a fi
nitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-submodule of $\mathbb{Q}$, hence of the form $\fra
c{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$ for some $d$, which is a **proper** subset of $\mathbb{Q}$. T
his contradicts $\mathbb{Q} = \mathbb{Z}\text{-span}(a_{11}, \ldots, a_{k1})$.

**Conclusion.** The contradiction shows that $I$ cannot be finitely generated as
 an $R$-module. $\blacksquare$
