# Proof: The Product of Two Measure-Preserving Transformations Is Measure-Preser
ving

## Setup

Let $(X, \mathcal{B}, \mu)$ be a measure space. A transformation $T: X \to X$ is
 **measure-preserving** if $T$ is measurable and

$$\mu(T^{-1}(A)) = \mu(A) \quad \text{for all } A \in \mathcal{B}.$$

The term "product of two transformations" admits two standard readings in ergodi
c theory. I prove the result for **both** interpretations; in each case the answ
er is **yes**.

---

## Interpretation 1: Product = Composition (same space)

Let $S, T: X \to X$ both be measure-preserving, and consider the composition $S
\circ T$ (i.e., $(S \circ T)(x) = S(T(x))$).

**Measurability.** Since $S$ and $T$ are measurable, $S \circ T$ is measurable (
composition of measurable maps).

**Measure preservation.** For any $A \in \mathcal{B}$,

$$(S \circ T)^{-1}(A) = T^{-1}\!\big(S^{-1}(A)\big).$$

Set $B := S^{-1}(A) \in \mathcal{B}$ (measurable because $S$ is measurable). The
n

$$\mu\!\big((S \circ T)^{-1}(A)\big) = \mu\!\big(T^{-1}(B)\big).$$

Since $T$ is measure-preserving, $\mu(T^{-1}(B)) = \mu(B) = \mu(S^{-1}(A))$. Sin
ce $S$ is measure-preserving, $\mu(S^{-1}(A)) = \mu(A)$. Chaining these equaliti
es:

$$\mu\!\big((S \circ T)^{-1}(A)\big) = \mu(B) = \mu(S^{-1}(A)) = \mu(A).$$

Hence $S \circ T$ is measure-preserving. $\square$

**Remark.** This shows the collection of measure-preserving transformations of $
(X, \mathcal{B}, \mu)$ is closed under composition — it forms a semigroup (and,
with inverses when they exist as measure-preserving maps, a group).

---

## Interpretation 2: Product = Product transformation (product space)

Let $S$ be measure-preserving on $(X, \mathcal{B}, \mu)$ and $T$ measure-preserv
ing on $(Y, \mathcal{C}, \nu)$. The **product transformation** $S \times T$ acts
 on $(X \times Y,\, \mathcal{B} \otimes \mathcal{C},\, \mu \otimes \nu)$ by

$$(S \times T)(x, y) = (Sx, Ty).$$

**Measurability.** $S \times T$ is measurable because each coordinate map is mea
surable.

**Measure preservation.** For a measurable rectangle $A \times B$ with $A \in \m
athcal{B}$, $B \in \mathcal{C}$:

$$(S \times T)^{-1}(A \times B) = S^{-1}(A) \times T^{-1}(B),$$

so

$$(\mu \otimes \nu)\!\big((S \times T)^{-1}(A \times B)\big) = \mu(S^{-1}(A))\,\
nu(T^{-1}(B)) = \mu(A)\,\nu(B) = (\mu \otimes \nu)(A \times B).$$

Thus $S \times T$ preserves the measure of every measurable rectangle. The colle
ction

$$\mathcal{R} := \{A \times B : A \in \mathcal{B},\, B \in \mathcal{C}\}$$

is a $\pi$-system generating $\mathcal{B} \otimes \mathcal{C}$, and the two meas
ures

$$E \mapsto (\mu \otimes \nu)\!\big((S \times T)^{-1}(E)\big) \quad\text{and}\qu
ad E \mapsto (\mu \otimes \nu)(E)$$

agree on $\mathcal{R}$ and are both probability measures (or both $\sigma$-finit
e, agreeing on a generating $\pi$-system with a common finite-measure exhausting
 sequence). By the **uniqueness theorem** (Dynkin's $\pi$-$\lambda$ theorem / Ca
rathéodory extension uniqueness), they agree on all of $\mathcal{B} \otimes \mat
hcal{C}$.

Hence $S \times T$ is measure-preserving on the product space. $\square$

---

## Conclusion

Under either standard meaning of "product" — composition on a single space, or t
he product transformation on a product space — **the product of two measure-pres
erving transformations is again measure-preserving.** The key mechanism is the s
ame in both cases: preimage distributes over the operation, and measure preserva
tion is checked entirely through preimages, so the two preservation equalities c
hain together directly.
