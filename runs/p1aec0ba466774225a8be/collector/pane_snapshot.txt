**Claim.** Yes. Take $g = \mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{N}}$. There are infinitely many $
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$ with $f\circ f = g$.

**Proof.**

A function $f$ satisfying $f\circ f = \mathrm{id}$ is an *involution*. We exhibi
t infinitely many distinct involutions on $\mathbb{N}$.

Write $\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}$ (the argument is identical for $\{1,2,3,\dots
\}$). For each integer $m\ge 0$ define $f_m:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$ by

$$
f_m(n)=\begin{cases}
2m+1 & \text{if } n=2m,\\
2m   & \text{if } n=2m+1,\\
n    & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
$$

So $f_m$ transposes the pair $\{2m,2m+1\}$ and fixes every other element.

*Involution check.* For $n=2m$: $f_m(f_m(2m))=f_m(2m+1)=2m=n$. For $n=2m+1$: $f_
m(f_m(2m+1))=f_m(2m)=2m+1=n$. For $n\notin\{2m,2m+1\}$: $f_m(f_m(n))=f_m(n)=n$.
Hence $f_m\circ f_m=\mathrm{id}=g$ for every $m$.

*Distinctness.* If $m\neq k$, then $f_m(2m)=2m+1\neq 2m=f_k(2m)$, so $f_m\neq f_
k$.

Thus $\{f_m:m\ge 0\}$ is an infinite (in fact countably infinite; one can even g
et uncountably many by choosing arbitrary subsets of pairs to swap) family of fu
nctions with $f\circ f=g$.

Therefore a function $g$ with the required property exists — namely $g=\mathrm{i
d}_{\mathbb{N}}$. $\quad\blacksquare$
