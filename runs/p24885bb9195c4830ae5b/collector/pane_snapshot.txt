## Counterexample

Define $f: (-1,1)^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ by

$$f(x, y) = \begin{cases} e^{-y^2/x^2} & \text{if } x \neq 0, \\ 0 & \text{if }
x = 0,\; y \neq 0, \\ 1 & \text{if } x = 0,\; y = 0. \end{cases}$$

### Verification of the hypotheses

**Condition 1: For every fixed $y \in (-1,1)$, $f(\cdot, y)$ is $C^\infty$.**

- **Case $y = 0$:** For $x \neq 0$, $f(x,0) = e^{0} = 1$, and $f(0,0) = 1$. So $
f(\cdot, 0) \equiv 1$, which is $C^\infty$. ✓

- **Case $y \neq 0$:** Set $c = y^2 > 0$. For $x \neq 0$, $f(x,y) = e^{-c/x^2}$,
 and $f(0,y) = 0$. The function $g(x) = e^{-c/x^2}$ for $x \neq 0$, $g(0) = 0$,
is the classical flat function: for every $n \geq 0$, $g^{(n)}(x) = P_n(1/x)\, e
^{-c/x^2}$ for $x \neq 0$ (where $P_n$ is a polynomial), and $g^{(n)}(x) \to 0$
as $x \to 0$ since the exponential $e^{-c/x^2}$ decays faster than any polynomia
l in $1/|x|$ grows. By induction, $g^{(n)}(0) = 0$ for all $n$, so $g \in C^\inf
ty$. ✓

**Condition 2: For every fixed $x \in (-1,1)\setminus\{0\}$, $f(x, \cdot)$ is $C
^k$.**

For $x \neq 0$, $f(x, y) = e^{-y^2/x^2}$, which is $C^\infty$ in $y$ (compositio
n of the smooth exponential with the polynomial $-y^2/x^2$). Hence it is $C^k$ f
or every $k$. ✓

### $f(0, \cdot)$ is not $C^k$

We have
$$f(0, y) = \begin{cases} 0 & y \neq 0, \\ 1 & y = 0. \end{cases}$$

This function is **discontinuous** at $y = 0$, since $\lim_{y \to 0} f(0, y) = 0
 \neq 1 = f(0, 0)$. Therefore $f(0, \cdot) \notin C^0$, and consequently $f(0, \
cdot) \notin C^k$ for any $k \geq 0$.

---

## Conclusion

The function $f$ defined above satisfies both hypotheses (smooth in $x$ for each
 fixed $y$; $C^k$ in $y$ for each fixed $x \neq 0$), yet $f(0, \cdot)$ fails to
be even continuous. The key mechanism is that $e^{-y^2/x^2}$ converges pointwise
 to a discontinuous function as $x \to 0$: it equals $1$ when $y = 0$ and tends
to $0$ for every $y \neq 0$, while the smoothness-in-$x$ condition is preserved
because $e^{-c/x^2}$ is flat at $x = 0$ for each fixed $c > 0$.

Therefore, **$f(0, \cdot)$ need not be $C^k$**, and the answer is **No**.
