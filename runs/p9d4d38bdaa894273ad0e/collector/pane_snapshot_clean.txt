**Answer: Yes, $A$ must be bounded.**

## Proof

**Step 1: Openness forces surjectivity.**

Since $A$ is open, it maps open subsets of $X$ to open subsets of $Y$. The space
 $X$ itself is open in $X$, so $A(X)$ is open in $Y$. But $A(X)$ is a linear sub
space of $Y$. The only open linear subspace of a normed space is the whole space
: indeed, if $A(X)$ is open and nonempty, it contains a ball $B(y_0, r)$ for som
e $y_0 \in A(X)$ and $r > 0$; by linearity (translating by $-y_0$ and scaling),
$A(X)$ contains $B(0, r)$, and then for any $y \in Y$, the vector $\frac{r}{2\|y
\|}y \in B(0,r) \subseteq A(X)$, so $y \in A(X)$. Thus $A(X) = Y$.

**Step 2: $A$ is a bijection.**

Since $A$ is injective (given) and surjective (Step 1), $A: X \to Y$ is a linear
 bijection.

**Step 3: Openness of $A$ gives continuity of $A^{-1}$.**

A bijective map is open if and only if its inverse is continuous. Since $A$ is a
n open bijection, $A^{-1}: Y \to X$ is continuous, i.e., $A^{-1}$ is a bounded l
inear operator.

**Step 4: Apply the Bounded Inverse Theorem.**

We now have $A^{-1}: Y \to X$, which is a **bounded linear bijection** between B
anach spaces ($Y$ and $X$ are both complete). By the **Bounded Inverse Theorem**
 (a corollary of the Open Mapping Theorem), the inverse of a bounded linear bije
ction between Banach spaces is bounded. Therefore:

$$(A^{-1})^{-1} = A : X \to Y$$

is bounded.

## Conclusion

A linear injective open operator $A: X \to Y$ between Banach spaces must be boun
ded. The key steps are: (1) openness forces the range to be all of $Y$ (since th
e only open subspace of a normed space is the space itself), giving surjectivity
 and hence bijectivity; (2) an open bijection has a continuous inverse; (3) appl
ying the Bounded Inverse Theorem to $A^{-1}$ (a bounded bijection between Banach
 spaces) yields that $A$ itself is bounded.
