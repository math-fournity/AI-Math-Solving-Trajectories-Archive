## Answer

**Yes.** Under the stated hypotheses, $e^{tA}$ is bounded on $L^p(X,\mu)$ for ev
ery $1 \le p \le \infty$.

### Proof

Let $M_\infty := \|e^{tA}\|_{L^\infty \to L^\infty}$. We are given that $\|e^{tA
}\|_{L^2 \to L^2} \le 1$ (contraction semigroup) and that $e^{tA}$ is bounded on
 $L^\infty$. Since $(X,\mu)$ is a **finite** measure space, we have the continuo
us inclusions
$$L^\infty(X,\mu) \hookrightarrow L^2(X,\mu) \hookrightarrow L^1(X,\mu),$$
and in particular $L^\infty \cap L^2 = L^\infty$ and $L^1 \cap L^2$ is dense in
$L^1$.

---

**Step 1 — Boundedness on $L^1$ via duality and self-adjointness.**

Because $A$ is self-adjoint on $L^2$, the operator $e^{tA}$ is self-adjoint on $
L^2$:
$$\langle e^{tA}u,\, v\rangle_{L^2} = \langle u,\, e^{tA}v\rangle_{L^2}, \qquad
u,v\in L^2.$$

Take $f \in L^1 \cap L^2$ and $h \in L^\infty$. Since $\mu(X)<\infty$, we have $
h \in L^2$ as well, so $f,h \in L^2$ and $e^{tA}f, e^{tA}h \in L^2 \subset L^1$.
 Using self-adjointness on $L^2$ and writing the $L^2$ inner product as an integ
ral,
$$\int_X (e^{tA}f)\,\overline{h}\,d\mu = \int_X f\,\overline{(e^{tA}h)}\,d\mu.$$
Taking absolute values and using Hölder,
$$\left|\int_X (e^{tA}f)\,\overline{h}\,d\mu\right| \le \|f\|_{L^1}\,\|e^{tA}h\|
_{L^\infty} \le M_\infty\,\|f\|_{L^1}\,\|h\|_{L^\infty}.$$

Now $(L^1)^* = L^\infty$ (for $\sigma$-finite, in particular finite, measure spa
ces), so
$$\|e^{tA}f\|_{L^1} = \sup_{\|h\|_{L^\infty}\le 1}\left|\int_X (e^{tA}f)\,\overl
ine{h}\,d\mu\right| \le M_\infty\,\|f\|_{L^1}.$$

This holds for every $f \in L^1\cap L^2$, which is dense in $L^1$. Since $e^{tA}
f \in L^2 \subset L^1$ and the estimate is uniform in the $L^1$-norm, $e^{tA}$ e
xtends uniquely to a bounded operator on $L^1$ with
$$\|e^{tA}\|_{L^1 \to L^1} \le M_\infty.$$

---

**Step 2 — Interpolation.**

We now have boundedness on three endpoints:

| Space | Operator norm |
|---|---|
| $L^1$ | $\le M_\infty$ |
| $L^2$ | $\le 1$ |
| $L^\infty$ | $\le M_\infty$ |

Apply the **Riesz–Thorin interpolation theorem**:

- Interpolating between $L^1$ and $L^2$ gives boundedness on $L^p$ for every $1
\le p \le 2$, with
$$\|e^{tA}\|_{L^p\to L^p} \le M_\infty^{\,\theta}\cdot 1^{\,1-\theta}, \qquad \t
frac{1}{p} = \tfrac{\theta}{1} + \tfrac{1-\theta}{2}.$$

- Interpolating between $L^2$ and $L^\infty$ gives boundedness on $L^p$ for ever
y $2 \le p \le \infty$, with
$$\|e^{tA}\|_{L^p\to L^p} \le 1^{\,\theta}\cdot M_\infty^{\,1-\theta}, \qquad \t
frac{1}{p} = \tfrac{\theta}{2} + \tfrac{1-\theta}{\infty}.$$

Combining the two ranges, $e^{tA}$ is bounded on $L^p(X,\mu)$ for **all** $1 \le
 p \le \infty$. $\blacksquare$

---

**Remark on the role of each hypothesis.** The self-adjointness of $A$ on $L^2$
is what allows us to "reflect" $L^\infty$-boundedness into $L^1$-boundedness thr
ough the $L^2$ duality pairing. The finiteness of $\mu$ is used to ensure $L^\in
fty \subset L^2$, so that every $L^\infty$ test function is admissible in the se
lf-adjointness identity, and that $L^1\cap L^2$ is dense in $L^1$. Riesz–Thorin
then fills in the intermediate $p$. Without self-adjointness (or some substitute
 such as $e^{tA}$ being symmetric / positivity-preserving and $L^\infty$-bounded
 with a duality argument), $L^1$-boundedness need not follow from $L^2$- and $L^
\infty$-boundedness alone.
