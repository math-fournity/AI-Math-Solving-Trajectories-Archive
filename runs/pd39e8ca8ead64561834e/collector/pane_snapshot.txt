**Answer: Yes**, for every $\varepsilon > 0$ such a polynomial exists.

**Proof.**

**Substitution.** Let $u = x^4$. As $x$ ranges over $[0,1]$, so does $u$, with $
x = u^{1/4}$ and $x^2 = u^{1/2}$. Writing $p(u) = a_1 u + a_2 u^2 + \cdots + a_n
 u^n$, the condition becomes
$$\sup_{u \in [0,1]} \left| p(u)\, u^{1/2} - u^{1/4} \right| < \varepsilon.$$
Since $p(u)\,u^{1/2} = \sum_{k=1}^n a_k\, u^{k+1/2}$, we need to uniformly appro
ximate $f(u) = u^{1/4}$ on $[0,1]$ by finite linear combinations of the function
s
$$u^{3/2},\; u^{5/2},\; u^{7/2},\; \ldots$$

**Müntz–Szász theorem.** Let $0 < \lambda_1 < \lambda_2 < \cdots$ with $\lambda_
k \to \infty$. The linear span of $\{u^{\lambda_k}\}_{k \geq 1}$ is dense in the
 subspace $\{f \in C[0,1] : f(0) = 0\}$ (sup norm) if and only if
$$\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\lambda_k} = \infty.$$

**Application.** Here $\lambda_k = k + \tfrac{1}{2}$ for $k = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$,
giving exponents $\tfrac{3}{2}, \tfrac{5}{2}, \tfrac{7}{2}, \ldots$. Then
$$\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\lambda_k} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{2}{2k+1}.$
$
Since $\dfrac{2}{2k+1} \geq \dfrac{1}{2k}$ for all $k \geq 1$ and $\sum \frac{1}
{2k} = \infty$, this series **diverges**.

The target function $f(u) = u^{1/4}$ is continuous on $[0,1]$ and satisfies $f(0
) = 0$. By the Müntz–Szász theorem, $f$ lies in the closure of $\operatorname{sp
an}\{u^{k+1/2} : k \geq 1\}$ in $C[0,1]$. Hence for every $\varepsilon > 0$, the
re exist $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and coefficients $a_1, \ldots, a_n$ such that
$$\sup_{u \in [0,1]} \left| \sum_{k=1}^n a_k\, u^{k+1/2} - u^{1/4} \right| < \va
repsilon.$$

**Reverting to $x$.** Substituting $u = x^4$ back (a bijection $[0,1] \to [0,1]$
 preserving the sup norm):
$$\sup_{x \in [0,1]} \left| \sum_{k=1}^n a_k\, x^{4k+2} - x \right| = \sup_{x \i
n [0,1]} \left| p(x^4)\,x^2 - x \right| < \varepsilon.$$

Therefore, for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a polynomial $p(x^4) = a_1
x^4 + a_2 x^8 + \cdots + a_n x^{4n}$ satisfying $\|p(x^4)x^2 - x\| < \varepsilon
$ on $[0,1]$. $\blacksquare$
