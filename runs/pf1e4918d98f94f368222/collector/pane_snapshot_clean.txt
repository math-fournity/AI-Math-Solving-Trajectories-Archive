## Proof

**Answer:** Yes. For every $n \geq 2$ and every pair of ordered $k$-tuples of di
stinct points $(a_1,\dots,a_k)$, $(b_1,\dots,b_k)$ in $S^n$, there exists a home
omorphism $f:S^n\to S^n$ with $f(a_i)=b_i$ for all $1\le i\le k$.

(Since $X=\{a_1,\dots,a_k\}$ and $Y=\{b_1,\dots,b_k\}$ are *sets* of size $k$, t
he $a_i$ are pairwise distinct and the $b_i$ are pairwise distinct, so the only
necessary condition—injectivity of the matching—is automatically satisfied.)

### Two standard facts

**Fact 1 (Path-connectedness).** *For $n\ge 2$, $S^n$ minus finitely many points
 is path-connected.*

*Proof.* Let $x,y\in S^n\setminus\{p_1,\dots,p_m\}$. Since $S^n$ is path-connect
ed, there is a path $\gamma$ from $x$ to $y$. If $\gamma$ passes through some $p
_i$, take a small ball $B_i$ around $p_i$ (disjoint from the other $p_j$ and fro
m $x,y$). The path enters and exits $B_i$; since $\partial B_i\cong S^{n-1}$ is
path-connected for $n\ge 2$, replace the segment of $\gamma$ inside $B_i$ by a p
ath along $\partial B_i$. Repeating for each $p_i$ on $\gamma$ yields a path fro
m $x$ to $y$ avoiding all removed points. $\square$

**Fact 2 (Ball homogeneity).** *For $n\ge 1$, let $B$ be a closed $n$-ball and $
x,y\in\operatorname{int}(B)$. There exists a homeomorphism $h:B\to B$ fixing $\p
artial B$ pointwise with $h(x)=y$.*

*Proof.* Identify $B\cong D^n$. The quotient $D^n/\partial D^n\cong S^n$ collaps
es $\partial D^n$ to a point $\infty$, so $D^n\setminus\partial D^n\cong\mathbb{
R}^n$. Under this identification, $x,y$ correspond to points of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
A translation $T:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n$ mapping $x$ to $y$ extends to a ho
meomorphism of the one-point compactification $S^n=\mathbb{R}^n\cup\{\infty\}$ f
ixing $\infty$. This descends to a homeomorphism of $D^n$ fixing $\partial D^n$
and mapping $x$ to $y$. $\square$

### Key Lemma

**Lemma.** *Let $n\ge 2$, let $p_1,\dots,p_m$ be distinct points in $S^n$, and l
et $p,q\in S^n\setminus\{p_1,\dots,p_m\}$ with $p\ne q$. Then there exists a hom
eomorphism $g:S^n\to S^n$ fixing each $p_i$ with $g(p)=q$.*

*Proof.* By Fact 1, there is a path $\gamma:[0,1]\to S^n\setminus\{p_1,\dots,p_m
\}$ from $p$ to $q$. Cover the compact image $\gamma([0,1])$ by finitely many op
en balls $B_1,\dots,B_N$ in $S^n\setminus\{p_1,\dots,p_m\}$, each homeomorphic t
o an open $n$-ball, such that $\gamma$ traverses them in order: there exist $0=t
_0<t_1<\cdots<t_N=1$ with $\gamma(t_{i-1}),\gamma(t_i)\in B_i$.

Set $x_i=\gamma(t_i)$, so $x_0=p$ and $x_N=q$. For each $i$, choose a closed $n$
-ball $C_i\subset B_i$ with $x_{i-1},x_i\in\operatorname{int}(C_i)$. Since $C_i\
cap\{p_1,\dots,p_m\}=\varnothing$, any homeomorphism supported in $C_i$ automati
cally fixes all $p_j$.

By Fact 2, for each $i$ there is a homeomorphism $g_i:S^n\to S^n$, supported in
$C_i$ (identity outside $C_i$), with $g_i(x_{i-1})=x_i$. Define

$$g = g_N\circ g_{N-1}\circ\cdots\circ g_1.$$

- **Fixes each $p_j$:** Each $g_i$ is the identity outside $C_i\subset S^n\setmi
nus\{p_1,\dots,p_m\}$, so each $g_i$ fixes every $p_j$; hence so does their comp
osition.
- **Maps $p$ to $q$:** $g_1$ sends $x_0=p$ to $x_1$; $g_2$ sends $x_1$ to $x_2$;
 $\dots$; $g_N$ sends $x_{N-1}$ to $x_N=q$. Thus $g(p)=q$. $\square$

### Induction

We prove by induction on $k$ that for any two ordered $k$-tuples of distinct poi
nts in $S^n$ ($n\ge 2$), the desired homeomorphism exists.

**Base case ($k=1$):** A rotation of $S^n\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ maps $a_1$ to
$b_1$.

**Inductive step:** Assume the result for $k-1$. There exists a homeomorphism $h
:S^n\to S^n$ with $h(a_i)=b_i$ for $i=1,\dots,k-1$. Set $p=h(a_k)$ and $q=b_k$.
Since $h$ is injective and $a_k\notin\{a_1,\dots,a_{k-1}\}$, we have $p\notin\{b
_1,\dots,b_{k-1}\}$; and $q\notin\{b_1,\dots,b_{k-1}\}$ since the $b_i$ are dist
inct. By the Lemma (with $m=k-1$, $p_i=b_i$), there exists a homeomorphism $g:S^
n\to S^n$ fixing $b_1,\dots,b_{k-1}$ with $g(p)=q$. Then

$$f = g\circ h$$

satisfies $f(a_i)=g(b_i)=b_i$ for $i<k$ and $f(a_k)=g(p)=q=b_k$. $\square$

---

**Remark on necessity of $n\ge 2$.** The hypothesis $n\ge 2$ is essential. For $
n=1$ ($S^1$), every homeomorphism of the circle preserves or reverses cyclic ord
er, so a matching $(a_i\mapsto b_i)$ is realizable only if it preserves or rever
ses the cyclic ordering of the points—far more restrictive.
