## Proof

Let g be a polynomial of degree d ≥ 2.

**Step 1: Degree analysis of g(x³) = (g(x))³.**

- g(x³) has degree 3d (substituting x³ into a degree-d polynomial).
- (g(x))³ has degree 3d.

These match for any d, so this equation alone imposes no restriction on d.

**Step 2: Degree analysis of g(x³) = g(g(g(x))).**

- g(x³) has degree 3d.
- g(g(g(x))) has degree d³ (composing three degree-d polynomials multiplies degr
ees: d·d·d = d³).

For these two polynomials to be identically equal, their degrees must match:

$$3d = d^3 \implies d(d^2 - 3) = 0 \implies d = 0 \text{ or } d = \sqrt{3}.$$

Since d must be a positive integer (the degree of a polynomial) with d ≥ 2, neit
her solution is admissible:
- d = 0 is excluded (degree must be ≥ 2).
- d = √3 is not an integer.

**Step 3: Conclusion.**

No integer d ≥ 2 satisfies the degree constraint 3d = d³. Therefore, there is no
 polynomial function g of degree at least 2 satisfying g(x³) = (g(x))³ = g(g(g(x
))) for all real x.

(For completeness: d = 1 gives 3·1 = 1³ = 1, i.e., 3 ≠ 1, so degree 1 also fails
 the g(x³) = g(g(g(x))) equation — except one can verify g(x) = x works, but it
has degree 1 and is excluded.)
