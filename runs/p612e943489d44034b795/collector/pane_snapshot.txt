Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether there exists a sequence of
complex numbers where the power sums converge exactly for non-prime exponents.

**Answer: Yes.**

## Construction

Let $f(j) = \lfloor \sqrt{\ln(j+1)} \rfloor$. Note that $f(j) \to \infty$ and $f
(j) = o(\ln j)$.

For each $j \geq 1$ and each prime $q \leq f(j)$, form the **block** $B(q,j)$ co
nsisting of the $q$ numbers:
$$j^{-1/q}\, e^{2\pi i k/q}, \quad k = 0, 1, \dots, q-1.$$
These are all $q$-th roots of $j^{-1}$.

The sequence $(a_n)$ is the concatenation of all blocks $B(q,j)$, ordered by inc
reasing $j$, then increasing $q$, then increasing $k$.

## Key Identity

For any positive integer $p$, the contribution of a complete block $B(q,j)$ to $
\sum a_n^p$ is:
$$\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\left(j^{-1/q} e^{2\pi i k/q}\right)^p = j^{-p/q}\sum_{k=0}^{q
-1} e^{2\pi i kp/q} = \begin{cases} q\, j^{-p/q} & \text{if } q \mid p,\\ 0 & \t
ext{if } q \nmid p. \end{cases}$$

This is the standard orthogonality relation for roots of unity.

## Analysis by Cases

**Case $p = 1$:** No prime divides $1$, so every block contributes $0$. The bloc
k-complete sum is $0$.

**Case $p$ prime, say $p = q_0$:** The only prime dividing $q_0$ is $q_0$ itself
. Since $f(j) \to \infty$, there exists $j_0$ such that $f(j) \geq q_0$ for all
$j \geq j_0$. The block-complete sum is:
$$\sum_{j=j_0}^{\infty} q_0 \cdot j^{-q_0/q_0} = q_0 \sum_{j=j_0}^{\infty} \frac
{1}{j} = +\infty.$$

**Case $p$ composite:** Every prime divisor $q$ of $p$ satisfies $q \leq p/2$ (s
ince $p$ is composite), hence $p/q \geq 2 > 1$. The block-complete sum is:
$$\sum_{\substack{q \mid p \\ q \text{ prime}}} q \sum_{j=j_0(q)}^{\infty} j^{-p
/q},$$
which is a **finite** sum of convergent $p$-series (each with exponent $p/q \geq
 2$). So the sum converges.

## Actual Partial Sums Converge/Diverge Correctly

The actual partial sums (which may cut through incomplete blocks) differ from th
e block-complete sums by the **incomplete-block deviation**. At level $j$, a par
tial block $B(q,j)$ contributes at most $q \cdot j^{-p/q}$ in absolute value (at
 most $q$ terms, each of modulus $j^{-p/q}$). The total deviation at level $j$ i
s:

$$D_j(p) = \sum_{\substack{q \leq f(j) \\ q \text{ prime}}} q\, j^{-p/q}.$$

**For composite $p \geq 4$:** Since $q \leq f(j) = \sqrt{\ln(j+1)}$, we have $p/
q \geq p/f(j)$, so:
$$j^{-p/q} \leq j^{-p/f(j)} = e^{-p\ln j/\sqrt{\ln(j+1)}} \leq e^{-p\sqrt{\ln j}
/2} \quad \text{(for large } j\text{)}.$$
Therefore:
$$D_j(p) \leq e^{-p\sqrt{\ln j}/2}\sum_{q \leq f(j)} q \leq e^{-p\sqrt{\ln j}/2}
\cdot f(j)^2 \leq \ln(j+1)\cdot e^{-p\sqrt{\ln j}/2} \longrightarrow 0.$$

So the actual partial sums converge for every composite $p$.

**For prime $p = q_0$:** The block-complete partial sums grow like $q_0 \ln J \t
o +\infty$, while the incomplete-block deviations $D_j(q_0) \to 0$. Hence the ac
tual partial sums diverge to $+\infty$.

**For $p = 1$:** The block-complete sums are $0$, and the same estimate gives $D
_j(1) \leq \ln(j+1)\cdot e^{-\sqrt{\ln(j+1)}} \to 0$. So the actual partial sums
 converge to $0$.

## The Sequence Tends to Zero

The maximum modulus of any term at level $j$ is:
$$j^{-1/f(j)} = e^{-\ln j / \sqrt{\ln(j+1)}} \leq e^{-\sqrt{\ln j}/2} \longright
arrow 0,$$
so $a_n \to 0$, which is necessary for $\sum a_n$ to converge.

## Conclusion

The sequence $(a_n)$ constructed above satisfies:
- $\sum a_n^p$ **converges** for $p = 1$ and for every composite $p$,
- $\sum a_n^p$ **diverges** for every prime $p$.

Therefore, such a sequence exists. $\blacksquare$
