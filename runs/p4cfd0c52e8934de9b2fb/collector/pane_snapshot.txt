## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. A non-zero entire function with infinitely many zeros is always
transcendental.

**Proof.**

Recall that an entire function is *transcendental* if it is not a polynomial. So
 it suffices to show that no non-zero polynomial can have infinitely many zeros.

**Key fact (Fundamental Theorem of Algebra).** Every non-constant polynomial $p(
z) = a_n z^n + \cdots + a_0$ with $a_n \neq 0$ and $n \geq 1$ has exactly $n$ ze
ros in $\mathbb{C}$, counted with multiplicity. In particular, a non-zero polyno
mial has at most $\deg(p)$ zeros (counting multiplicity), hence only finitely ma
ny distinct zeros.

A non-zero constant polynomial has no zeros at all.

Therefore, **every non-zero polynomial has only finitely many zeros.**

Now let $f$ be a non-zero entire function with infinitely many zeros. If $f$ wer
e a polynomial, then by the above it would have only finitely many zeros — a con
tradiction. Hence $f$ is not a polynomial, which is precisely the definition of
$f$ being a **transcendental** entire function.

**Remark on consistency.** The hypothesis is not vacuous: non-zero transcendenta
l entire functions with infinitely many zeros do exist, e.g. $f(z) = \sin(\pi z)
$, which has zeros at every integer $n \in \mathbb{Z}$. Note also that by the id
entity theorem (isolated zeros principle), the zeros of a non-zero entire functi
on cannot accumulate at any finite point of $\mathbb{C}$; so the infinitely many
 zeros must escape to infinity, which is exactly what happens for $\sin(\pi z)$.

**Conclusion.** A non-zero entire function with infinitely many zeros must be tr
anscendental, since every non-zero polynomial has only finitely many zeros by th
e Fundamental Theorem of Algebra.
