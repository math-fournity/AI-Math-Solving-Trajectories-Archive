## Solution

The statement is **FALSE**.

### Key Observation

In any metric space (which is $T_1$), every finite set is closed: singletons are
 closed, and a finite union of closed sets is closed. Therefore, if $D$ is a **f
inite** subset of a metric space $X$, then
$$\overline{D} = D.$$
Consequently, $D$ is dense in $X$ (i.e., $\overline{D} = X$) **if and only if**
$D = X$, which means $X$ itself is finite.

So: **a metric space contains a finite dense subset if and only if it is finite.
**

### The Forward Direction Holds (trivially)

If $X$ has a finite dense subset $D$, then for every $\varepsilon > 0$, the coll
ection $\{B(d, \varepsilon) : d \in D\}$ covers $X$ (since $D$ is dense, every $
x \in X$ is within $\varepsilon$ of some $d \in D$). This is a finite $\varepsil
on$-net, so $X$ is totally bounded. ✓

### The Reverse Direction Fails

Total boundedness does **not** imply finiteness. The standard construction from
total boundedness yields only a **countable** dense subset: for each $n \geq 1$,
 take a finite $\frac{1}{n}$-net $F_n$; then $\bigcup_n F_n$ is a countable dens
e subset. There is no guarantee this can be made finite.

### Counterexample

Take $X = [0,1]$ with the usual metric.

- **$X$ is totally bounded:** For any $\varepsilon > 0$, the points $\{0, \varep
silon/2, \varepsilon, 3\varepsilon/2, \dots, 1\}$ form a finite $\varepsilon$-ne
t. (Equivalently, $[0,1]$ is compact, hence totally bounded.)

- **$X$ has no finite dense subset:** Suppose $D \subseteq [0,1]$ is finite. Sin
ce $[0,1]$ is a metric space, $D$ is closed, so $\overline{D} = D \neq [0,1]$ (a
s $[0,1]$ is uncountable and $D$ is finite). Hence $D$ is not dense.

Therefore $[0,1]$ is totally bounded but contains no finite dense subset, contra
dicting the "only if" direction of the statement.

### Conclusion

The implication "finite dense subset $\Rightarrow$ totally bounded" is true, but
 the converse "totally bounded $\Rightarrow$ finite dense subset" is false. The
statement claims equivalence, so it is **false**. The error is that total bounde
dness guarantees a finite $\varepsilon$-net for each fixed $\varepsilon$, but th
e union over all $\varepsilon = 1/n$ is only countable—not finite. A finite dens
e subset exists precisely when the space itself is finite.
