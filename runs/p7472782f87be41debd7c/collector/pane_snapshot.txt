## Solution

**Fourier cosine transform.** Define

$$\mathcal{F}_c\{g\}(\omega) = G_c(\omega) = \int_0^\infty g(x)\cos(\omega x)\,d
x, \qquad g(x) = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\infty G_c(\omega)\cos(\omega x)\,d\omega.$
$

**Transform of $y''$.** Integrating by parts twice and using $y(x)\to 0$, $y'(x)
\to 0$ as $x\to\infty$:

$$\mathcal{F}_c\{y''\}(\omega) = \big[f'(x)\cos(\omega x)\big]_0^\infty + \omega
\int_0^\infty y'(x)\sin(\omega x)\,dx = -y'(0) - \omega^2 Y_c(\omega).$$

With $y'(0)=1$:

$$\mathcal{F}_c\{y''\}(\omega) = -1 - \omega^2 Y_c(\omega).$$

**Transform the ODE.** Applying $\mathcal{F}_c$ to $y''-k^2 y = f(x)$:

$$-1 - \omega^2 Y_c(\omega) - k^2 Y_c(\omega) = F_c(\omega),$$

so

$$Y_c(\omega) = -\frac{F_c(\omega)+1}{\omega^2+k^2}.$$

**Inversion.** Using the linearity of the inverse cosine transform:

$$y(x) = -\frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\infty \frac{F_c(\omega)\cos(\omega x)}{\omega^2+k
^2}\,d\omega \;-\; \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\infty \frac{\cos(\omega x)}{\omega^2+k^2
}\,d\omega.$$

The second integral is the classical result (for $k>0$, $x>0$):

$$\int_0^\infty \frac{\cos(\omega x)}{\omega^2+k^2}\,d\omega = \frac{\pi}{2k}e^{
-kx},$$

so the second term contributes $-\dfrac{1}{k}e^{-kx}$.

For the first term, note that

$$\mathcal{F}_c^{-1}\!\left\{\frac{1}{\omega^2+k^2}\right\}(x) = \frac{2}{\pi}\c
dot\frac{\pi}{2k}e^{-kx} = \frac{1}{k}e^{-kx} \;=:\; g(x).$$

By the **cosine-convolution (Faltung) theorem**,

$$\mathcal{F}_c^{-1}\{F_c(\omega)\,G_c(\omega)\}(x) = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^\infty f
(t)\big[g(|x-t|)+g(x+t)\big]\,dt.$$

With $g(t)=\frac{1}{k}e^{-kt}$, the first term becomes

$$-\frac{1}{2}\int_0^\infty f(t)\cdot\frac{1}{k}\big[e^{-k|x-t|}+e^{-k(x+t)}\big
]\,dt = -\frac{1}{2k}\int_0^\infty f(t)\big[e^{-k|x-t|}+e^{-k(x+t)}\big]\,dt.$$

**Final solution.**

$$\boxed{\,y(x) = -\frac{1}{2k}\int_0^\infty f(t)\Big[e^{-k|x-t|}+e^{-k(x+t)}\Bi
g]\,dt \;-\; \frac{1}{k}e^{-kx}\,}$$

**Verification of boundary conditions.**

*Decay at infinity.* Every exponential in the integrand is bounded by $e^{-kx}$
(since $|x-t|\ge x-t$ gives $e^{-k|x-t|}\le e^{k(t-x)}$, and combined with $e^{-
k(x+t)}\le e^{-kx}$; using $f\in L^1$, the integral is $O(e^{-kx})$). Hence $y(x
)\to 0$ and $y'(x)\to 0$ as $x\to\infty$. ✓

*Neumann condition $y'(0)=1$.* Differentiating under the integral:

$$y'(x) = -\frac{1}{2k}\int_0^\infty f(t)\big[-k\,\mathrm{sgn}(x-t)\,e^{-k|x-t|}
-k\,e^{-k(x+t)}\big]\,dt + e^{-kx}.$$

At $x=0$, $\mathrm{sgn}(0-t)=-1$ for $t>0$, so $-k\,\mathrm{sgn}(0-t)\,e^{-k|0-t
|}=+k\,e^{-kt}$, and the bracket becomes $k\,e^{-kt}-k\,e^{-kt}=0$. Thus

$$y'(0) = -\frac{1}{2k}\int_0^\infty f(t)\cdot 0\,dt + 1 = 1. \checkmark$$

*The ODE.* The kernel $G(x,t)=-\frac{1}{2k}\big[e^{-k|x-t|}+e^{-k(x+t)}\big]$ is
 the Neumann Green's function on the half-line (method of images). Since $(d^2/d
x^2-k^2)e^{-k|x-t|}=-2k\,\delta(x-t)$ while $e^{-k(x+t)}$ is smooth and satisfie
s the homogeneous equation on $(0,\infty)$,

$$\Big(\tfrac{d^2}{dx^2}-k^2\Big)G(x,t)=\delta(x-t),$$

so $\int_0^\infty f(t)G(x,t)\,dt$ yields $f(x)$. The remaining term $-\frac{1}{k
}e^{-kx}$ is a homogeneous solution ($y''-k^2y=0$), so it does not alter the ODE
. Hence $y''-k^2y=f(x)$. ✓

All conditions are satisfied, completing the proof.
