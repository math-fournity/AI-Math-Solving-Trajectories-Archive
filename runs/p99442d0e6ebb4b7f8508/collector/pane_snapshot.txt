## Answer

**No.** $X$ need not be homeomorphic to $S^n$. Below is an explicit counterexamp
le (with $n=4$).

---

### Key lemma

For any space $Y$,
$$\Sigma Y \cong S^{k+1} \;\Longrightarrow\; Y\times\mathbb{R} \cong S^{k}\times
\mathbb{R}.$$

*Proof of lemma.* The (unreduced) suspension $\Sigma Y=(Y\times[-1,1])/(Y\times\
{-1\}\text{ collapsed},\,Y\times\{1\}\text{ collapsed})$ has two distinguished *
suspension vertices* $N,S$. Removing them leaves
$$\Sigma Y\setminus\{N,S\}\;\cong\;Y\times(-1,1)\;\cong\;Y\times\mathbb{R}.$$
On the other hand $S^{k+1}$, viewed as $\Sigma S^{k}$, has its two suspension ve
rtices; removing them gives $S^{k}\times(-1,1)\cong S^{k}\times\mathbb{R}$. Henc
e a homeomorphism $\Sigma Y\cong S^{k+1}$ (sending vertices to vertices, which m
ay be arranged) yields $Y\times\mathbb{R}\cong S^{k}\times\mathbb{R}$. $\square$

---

### The counterexample

Let $H$ be a nontrivial homology $3$-sphere, e.g. the **Poincaré homology sphere
**. Then
- $H_{*}(H)\cong H_{*}(S^{3})$ (it is a homology sphere),
- $\pi_{1}(H)\cong$ the binary icosahedral group (order $120$), so $H\not\cong S
^{3}$.

Set $X:=\Sigma H$ (single suspension), a $4$-dimensional space.

**Step 1 — $X\times\mathbb{R}\cong S^{4}\times\mathbb{R}$.**
By the **Cannon–Edwards double suspension theorem** (1979), the double suspensio
n of any homology sphere is a topological sphere:
$$\Sigma^{2}H=\Sigma(\Sigma H)=\Sigma X\cong S^{5}.$$
Applying the lemma with $Y=X=\Sigma H$ and $k+1=5$ (so $k=4$):
$$X\times\mathbb{R}\;\cong\;S^{4}\times\mathbb{R}.$$

**Step 2 — this is in fact a diffeomorphism.**
$X\times\mathbb{R}$ is homeomorphic to $S^{4}\times\mathbb{R}$, hence is a (non-
compact, boundaryless) topological $5$-manifold. By smoothing theory / Kirby–Sie
benmann, **open topological manifolds of dimension $\geqslant 5$ admit a smooth
structure, and that smooth structure is unique up to diffeomorphism** (concordan
ce classes of smoothings are trivial for open manifolds). Since $S^{4}\times\mat
hbb{R}$ carries the standard smooth structure, the induced smooth structure on $
X\times\mathbb{R}$ is diffeomorphic to it:
$$X\times\mathbb{R}\;\cong_{\mathrm{diff}}\;S^{4}\times\mathbb{R}.$$

**Step 3 — $X\not\cong S^{4}$.**
$S^{4}$ is a topological $4$-manifold. But $X=\Sigma H$ is **not** a manifold: t
he link of either suspension vertex is $H$ itself, and $H\not\cong S^{3}$, so th
e suspension vertices fail to have Euclidean neighborhoods. Therefore $X$ cannot
 be homeomorphic to the manifold $S^{4}$.

---

### Conclusion

With $H$ the Poincaré homology sphere and $X=\Sigma H$, we have
$$X\times\mathbb{R}\;\cong_{\mathrm{diff}}\;S^{4}\times\mathbb{R},\qquad\text{ye
t}\qquad X\not\cong S^{4}.$$
So the hypothesis "$X\times\mathbb{R}$ is diffeomorphic to $S^{n}\times\mathbb{R
}$" does **not** force $X$ to be homeomorphic to $S^{n}$. The cancellation $-\ti
mes\mathbb{R}$ fails in general; the obstruction here is precisely the existence
 of nontrivial homology spheres combined with the double-suspension theorem, whi
ch "smooths out" the singularity only after crossing with an extra suspension (e
quivalently, after taking the product with $\mathbb{R}$).
