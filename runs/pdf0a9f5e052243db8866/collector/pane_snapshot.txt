## Proof

Let $G = (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$, which is cyclic of order $p-1$. The q
uadratic residues modulo $p$ form the unique subgroup $QR \subseteq G$ of index
$2$, so $QR$ is cyclic of order $m = \frac{p-1}{2}$.

Every perfect square not divisible by $p$ has residue in $QR$. Distinct perfect
square integers may share the same residue in $QR$, so a "set of $n$ perfect squ
ares" yields a **multiset** of $n$ elements from $QR$.

We seek the least $n$ such that every multiset of $n$ elements from $QR$ contain
s a nonempty sub-multiset with product $= 1$ (the identity).

---

### Lower bound: $n \geq m$

Let $g$ be a generator of $QR$ (order $m$). We construct $m-1$ distinct perfect
squares, all with residue $g \bmod p$, such that no nonempty subset has product
$1$.

Since $g \in QR$, there exists $b$ with $b^2 \equiv g \pmod{p}$. For $k = 0, 1,
\dots, m-2$, the numbers $(kp + b)^2$ are distinct perfect squares, each $\equiv
 b^2 \equiv g \pmod{p}$, and none divisible by $p$.

Any nonempty subset of these $m-1$ squares has product $g^j$ for some $1 \leq j
\leq m-1$. Since $g$ has order $m$, we have $g^j \not\equiv 1 \pmod{p}$ for $1 \
leq j \leq m-1$.

Thus $m-1$ perfect squares can avoid having any nonempty subset with product $1$
, so $n \geq m$.

---

### Upper bound: $n \leq m$

We show any $m$ perfect squares (not divisible by $p$) contain a nonempty subset
 with product $\equiv 1 \pmod{p}$.

Let the residues be $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_m \in QR$. Define the partial products:
$$P_0 = 1, \quad P_k = a_1 a_2 \cdots a_k \pmod{p}, \quad k = 1, \dots, m.$$

Each $P_k \in QR$ (since $QR$ is closed under multiplication). We have $m+1$ par
tial products $P_0, P_1, \dots, P_m$ but only $m = |QR|$ possible values. By the
 **pigeonhole principle**, there exist $0 \leq i < j \leq m$ with $P_i = P_j$.

Then:
$$a_{i+1} \cdot a_{i+2} \cdots a_j = P_j \cdot P_i^{-1} = 1 \pmod{p}.$$

This is a nonempty subset (the squares corresponding to $a_{i+1}, \dots, a_j$) w
ith product $1 \pmod{p}$.

---

### Conclusion

Combining both bounds, the least $n$ is:

$$\boxed{n = \dfrac{p-1}{2}}$$
