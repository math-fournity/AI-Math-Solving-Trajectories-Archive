Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether a continuous function on [0
,1] can have uncountably many zeros, with every zero being a limit point of both
 the positive and negative sets.

**Answer: Yes, such a function exists.**

**Proof.**

Let $C \subset [0,1]$ be the middle-thirds Cantor set. Its complement $[0,1]\set
minus C$ is the disjoint union of countably many open intervals $I_{n,k}$, where
 $I_{n,k}$ is the $k$-th interval (left to right) removed at stage $n$ of the Ca
ntor construction ($n \ge 1$, $1 \le k \le 2^{n-1}$). Each $I_{n,k} = (a_{n,k},
b_{n,k})$ has length $3^{-n}$, with endpoints in $C$.

Define $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ by:
- $f(x) = 0$ for $x \in C$.
- For $x \in I_{n,k}$: $\displaystyle f(x) = (-1)^{n+1}\cdot 3^{-n}\cdot\sin\!\l
eft(\frac{\pi(x-a_{n,k})}{b_{n,k}-a_{n,k}}\right).$

So on odd-stage intervals $f$ is a positive bump, on even-stage intervals a nega
tive bump, with amplitude $3^{-n}$ at stage $n$.

**Continuity.** On each $I_{n,k}$, $f$ is continuous and vanishes at the endpoin
ts $a_{n,k},b_{n,k}\in C$. On $C$, $f=0$. To verify continuity at any $x\in C$,
let $x_m\to x$. If $x_m\in C$, then $f(x_m)=0$. If $x_m\in I_{n_m,k_m}$, then $|
f(x_m)|\le 3^{-n_m}$. Either $n_m\to\infty$ (so $3^{-n_m}\to 0$), or $n_m$ is bo
unded—then $x_m$ eventually lies in a single fixed interval $I_{n_0,k_0}$, forci
ng $x$ to be an endpoint of that interval, so $f(x_m)\to 0$ since the sine vanis
hes at endpoints. In all cases $f(x_m)\to 0 = f(x)$. Hence $f$ is continuous on
$[0,1]$.

**Zero set is uncountable.** On each $I_{n,k}$, the sine bump is nonzero in the
interior and vanishes only at the endpoints (which belong to $C$). Therefore $\{
f=0\}=C$, which is uncountable.

**Sign condition at every zero.** Let $x\in C$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ w
ith $3^{-N}<\varepsilon$. For each $n\ge N$, the stage-$n$ surviving interval co
ntaining $x$ has length $3^{-n}<\varepsilon$, and the stage-$(n{+}1)$ complement
ary interval removed from its interior lies within distance $3^{-n}<\varepsilon$
 of $x$. This interval $I_{n+1,k}$ carries sign $(-1)^{(n+1)+1}=(-1)^{n+2}$: **p
ositive** when $n+1$ is odd, **negative** when $n+1$ is even. Since both paritie
s of $n+1$ occur for $n\ge N$, the neighborhood $(x-\varepsilon, x+\varepsilon)$
 contains points $a$ with $f(a)>0$ and points $b$ with $f(b)<0$.

This holds for every $x\in C$ and every $\varepsilon>0$, so the required conditi
on is satisfied. $\blacksquare$
