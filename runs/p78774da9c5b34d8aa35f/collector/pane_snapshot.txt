## Proof

**Theorem.** If $L$ is a field with $\mathbb{C} \subseteq L$ and $[L:\mathbb{C}]
 < \infty$, then $L = \mathbb{C}$.

The statement is **true**. We prove it by first establishing (with a self-contai
ned argument) that $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed, then deducing the resul
t.

### Lemma (Fundamental Theorem of Algebra): $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed
.

**Proof of Lemma.** We use two elementary facts:

- **(F1)** Every non-constant polynomial $p(x)\in\mathbb{R}[x]$ of *odd* degree
has a real root.
  *Reason:* As $x\to+\infty$, $p(x)\to\pm\infty$ (sign of leading coefficient),
and as $x\to-\infty$, $p(x)\to\mp\infty$ (opposite sign). By the Intermediate Va
lue Theorem, $p$ has a real root.

- **(F2)** Every $z\in\mathbb{C}$ has a square root in $\mathbb{C}$.
  *Reason:* For $z=a+bi$, set $r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$, $x=\sqrt{(r+a)/2}$, $y=\operat
orname{sgn}(b)\sqrt{(r-a)/2}$ (both radicands are $\geq 0$ since $r\geq|a|$). Th
en $(x+yi)^2 = z$.

It suffices to show every non-constant $f(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]$ has a root in $\ma
thbb{C}$. Given such $f$, form $g(x)=f(x)\overline{f}(x)\in\mathbb{R}[x]$, where
 $\overline{f}$ has conjugated coefficients. If $g$ has a root $\alpha\in\mathbb
{C}$, then $f(\alpha)=0$ or $\overline{f}(\alpha)=0$; in the latter case $f(\ove
rline{\alpha})=\overline{\overline{f}(\alpha)}=0$. So it suffices to show every
non-constant **real** polynomial has a root in $\mathbb{C}$.

Let $g(x)\in\mathbb{R}[x]$ be non-constant, and let $E$ be its splitting field o
ver $\mathbb{R}$. Then $E/\mathbb{R}$ is finite Galois. Set $G=\operatorname{Gal
}(E/\mathbb{R})$, $|G|=m$. Write $m=2^k\cdot q$ with $q$ odd, and let $H\leq G$
be a 2-Sylow subgroup ($|H|=2^k$). Set $F=E^H$; then $[F:\mathbb{R}]=q$.

**Claim:** $q=1$. If $q>1$, pick $\beta\in F\setminus\mathbb{R}$. Its minimal po
lynomial over $\mathbb{R}$ has odd degree (dividing $q$). By (F1) it has a real
root; being irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$, it must be linear — so $\beta\in\math
bb{R}$, contradiction. Thus $q=1$ and $|G|=2^k$.

Now $G$ is a 2-group. If $k\geq 1$, $G$ has a subgroup $H'$ of index $2$ (2-grou
ps have subgroups of every order $2^j\mid |G|$). Set $F'=E^{H'}$; then $[F':\mat
hbb{R}]=2$, so $F'=\mathbb{R}(\sqrt{d})$ for some $d\in\mathbb{R}$. If $d\geq 0$
 then $\sqrt{d}\in\mathbb{R}$ and $F'=\mathbb{R}$, contradicting $[F':\mathbb{R}
]=2$. Hence $d<0$ and $F'=\mathbb{R}(i)=\mathbb{C}$.

If $k\geq 2$, take $H''$ with $H'\leq H''\leq G$ and $[H'':H']=2$. Then $[E^{H''
}:F']=2$, i.e., $E^{H''}$ is a degree-2 extension of $\mathbb{C}$. Any degree-2
extension of $\mathbb{C}$ is $\mathbb{C}(\sqrt{z})$ for some $z\in\mathbb{C}$, w
hich equals $\mathbb{C}$ by (F2) — contradiction. So $k=1$, $[E:\mathbb{R}]=2$,
and $E=F'=\mathbb{C}$.

Therefore $g$ splits over $\mathbb{C}$, and hence $f$ has a root in $\mathbb{C}$
. $\quad\square$

### Main Theorem

Let $[L:\mathbb{C}]=n<\infty$. Then $L/\mathbb{C}$ is a finite extension, hence
**algebraic**: every $\alpha\in L$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{C}$.

Let $\alpha\in L$ and let $m_\alpha(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]$ be its minimal polynomia
l over $\mathbb{C}$. By the Lemma, $m_\alpha$ (being non-constant) has a root in
 $\mathbb{C}$. Since $m_\alpha$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{C}$ and has a root
in $\mathbb{C}$, it must be **linear** (degree $1$). Therefore $\alpha\in\mathbb
{C}$.

Since $\alpha$ was arbitrary, $L\subseteq\mathbb{C}$. Combined with the hypothes
is $\mathbb{C}\subseteq L$, we conclude

$$L = \mathbb{C}. \qquad \blacksquare$$
