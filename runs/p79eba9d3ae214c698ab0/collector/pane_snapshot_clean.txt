## Extension of Dulac's Test and Bendixson's Negative Criterion to Cases with Is
olated Zero Sets

### Setup and Recall

Consider the planar system $\dot{x} = X(x,y)$, $\dot{y} = Y(x,y)$ in a simply co
nnected region $D$. Both Bendixson's negative criterion and Dulac's test rule ou
t closed orbits in $D$ by exploiting Green's theorem.

**Bendixson's criterion** states: if $\frac{\partial X}{\partial x} + \frac{\par
tial Y}{\partial y}$ does not change sign and is not identically zero in $D$, th
en $D$ contains no closed orbit.

**Dulac's test** generalizes this: if there exists a $C^1$ function $\rho(x,y) >
 0$ (the Dulac function) such that
$$\Delta := \frac{\partial(\rho X)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial(\rho Y)}{\parti
al y}$$
does not change sign and is not identically zero in $D$, then $D$ contains no cl
osed orbit.

The proof of both rests on one identity. Suppose $\Gamma$ is a closed orbit encl
osing a region $S \subseteq D$. Along $\Gamma$, $dx = X\,dt$ and $dy = Y\,dt$, s
o
$$\oint_\Gamma \bigl(\rho X\,dy - \rho Y\,dx\bigr) = \oint_\Gamma \rho\bigl(XY -
 YX\bigr)\,dt = 0.$$
By Green's theorem this equals
$$\oint_\Gamma \bigl(\rho X\,dy - \rho Y\,dx\bigr) = \iint_S \left[\frac{\partia
l(\rho X)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial(\rho Y)}{\partial y}\right] dx\,dy = \ii
nt_S \Delta\, dA.$$
Hence $\iint_S \Delta\, dA = 0$. If $\Delta$ is of one sign and not identically
zero on $S$, this integral cannot vanish — contradiction. So no closed orbit exi
sts.

### The Extension: $\Delta$ of One Sign Except on Isolated Points or Curves

Now suppose $\Delta \geq 0$ (the case $\Delta \leq 0$ is identical) throughout $
D$, but $\Delta = 0$ only on a collection of **isolated points** or **curves** (
i.e., a set $Z \subset D$ of Lebesgue measure zero). We claim the conclusion sti
ll holds: **no closed orbit lies entirely in $D$.**

**Key observation — measure-zero sets do not affect the integral.** Isolated poi
nts have zero area. A (piecewise smooth) curve in the plane also has zero two-di
mensional Lebesgue measure. Therefore $Z$ has measure zero, and
$$\iint_S \Delta\, dA = \iint_{S \setminus Z} \Delta\, dA.$$
On $S \setminus Z$ we have $\Delta > 0$ (strictly, by hypothesis). Since $S \set
minus Z$ is $S$ minus a measure-zero set, it has the same positive area as $S$ (
provided $S$ is a nontrivial region, which it is — it is the interior of a close
d orbit). Moreover $\Delta$ is continuous (being a sum of partial derivatives of
 $C^1$ functions), so $\Delta > 0$ on the open set $S \setminus Z$, and the inte
gral over any region of positive area where a continuous function is strictly po
sitive is itself strictly positive. Thus
$$\iint_S \Delta\, dA > 0,$$
contradicting the Green's theorem identity $\iint_S \Delta\, dA = 0$. Hence no c
losed orbit can exist.

### Handling the Case Where $\Delta$ Vanishes on Curves: Splitting the Region

When $\Delta = 0$ on a curve $C$ (rather than just isolated points), one might w
orry that $\rho X$ or $\rho Y$ fails to be $C^1$ across $C$, threatening the dir
ect application of Green's theorem. The remedy is to **split $S$ along $C$**.

Suppose $C$ divides $S$ into subregions $S_1, S_2, \ldots, S_k$ (a finite number
 if $C$ consists of finitely many piecewise smooth arcs). Apply Green's theorem
to each $S_i$ separately:
$$\iint_{S_i} \Delta\, dA = \oint_{\partial S_i} \bigl(\rho X\,dy - \rho Y\,dx\b
igr).$$
Summing over $i$, the boundary integrals along the internal arcs (the portions o
f $C$ inside $S$) cancel pairwise because adjacent subregions induce opposite or
ientations on the shared boundary. What remains is exactly the integral over the
 outer boundary $\Gamma$:
$$\sum_i \iint_{S_i} \Delta\, dA = \oint_\Gamma \bigl(\rho X\,dy - \rho Y\,dx\bi
gr) = 0.$$
But each $\iint_{S_i} \Delta\, dA \geq 0$ (since $\Delta \geq 0$ everywhere), an
d at least one subregion has $\Delta > 0$ on a set of positive measure (because
$\Delta$ is not identically zero and vanishes only on the measure-zero set $Z$).
 Hence the sum is strictly positive — contradiction.

This splitting argument shows that the presence of curves where $\Delta$ vanishe
s causes no difficulty: the integral is still strictly of one sign, and Green's
theorem (applied piecewise) still forces it to be zero.

### Summary

The extension rests on two facts:

1. **Isolated points and curves have two-dimensional Lebesgue measure zero**, so
 they do not contribute to the area integral $\iint_S \Delta\, dA$. The integral
 is determined entirely by the values of $\Delta$ on the complement, where $\Del
ta$ is strictly of one sign. A continuous function that is nonnegative everywher
e and strictly positive on a set of positive measure integrates to a strictly po
sitive value.

2. **When $\Delta$ vanishes on curves, Green's theorem is applied piecewise** by
 cutting $S$ along those curves. The internal boundary contributions cancel upon
 summation, recovering the original identity, while each piece contributes a non
negative integral with at least one piece strictly positive.

In both cases the Green's theorem identity $\iint_S \Delta\, dA = 0$ (forced by
the existence of a hypothetical closed orbit) contradicts the strict positivity
(or negativity) of the integral. Therefore **Dulac's test and Bendixson's negati
ve criterion extend unchanged in their conclusion**: if $\Delta = \frac{\partial
(\rho X)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial(\rho Y)}{\partial y}$ is of one sign thro
ughout a simply connected region $D$ and vanishes only on isolated points or cur
ves (a set of measure zero), then $D$ contains no closed orbit of the system.
