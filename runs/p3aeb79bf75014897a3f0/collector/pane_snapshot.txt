## Solution

The two-dimensional wave equation is

$$a^2(u_{xx}+u_{yy})=u_{tt},\qquad (x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2,\; t>0.$$

### 1. General form via Poisson's formula

We solve the Cauchy problem with initial data

$$u(x,y,0)=\varphi(x,y),\qquad u_t(x,y,0)=\psi(x,y).$$

Fix a point $(x,y)$ and time $t$. Introduce polar coordinates centered at $(x,y)
$:

$$\xi=x+r\cos\theta,\qquad \eta=y+r\sin\theta,\qquad r\ge 0,\;0\le\theta\le 2\pi
.$$

Let $P(r,t)$ denote the circular mean of the initial data over the circle of rad
ius $r$ centered at $(x,y)$:

$$P(r,t)\;\text{with}\; P(r,0)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\varphi(x+r\cos\thet
a,\,y+r\sin\theta)\,d\theta,$$

$$P_t(r,0)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\psi(x+r\cos\theta,\,y+r\sin\theta)\,d\t
heta.$$

Averaging the PDE over the circle reduces the 2D wave equation to the **Euler–Po
isson–Darboux equation** satisfied by $P(r,t)$:

$$P_{tt}=a^2\!\left(P_{rr}+\frac{1}{r}P_r\right),\qquad P_{rt}\big|_{r=0}=0,\;\;
 P_r\big|_{r=0}=0.$$

The key observation (Hadamard's descent / method of descent from 3D) is that the
 quantity $r^{1/2}P(r,t)$, extended oddly to $r<0$, satisfies the **one-dimensio
nal wave equation** in the variable $r$. Applying d'Alembert's formula to this o
dd extension and then taking the limit $r\to 0^{+}$ (which recovers $u(x,y,t)=P(
0,t)$), one obtains after a careful integration by parts the **Poisson formula f
or the 2D wave equation**:

$$\boxed{\;u(x,y,t)=\frac{1}{2\pi a}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\iint_{D_{at}}\!\
frac{\varphi(\xi,\eta)}{\sqrt{a^2t^2-(\xi-x)^2-(\eta-y)^2}}\,d\xi\,d\eta+\frac{1
}{2\pi a}\iint_{D_{at}}\!\frac{\psi(\xi,\eta)}{\sqrt{a^2t^2-(\xi-x)^2-(\eta-y)^2
}}\,d\xi\,d\eta\;}$$

where $D_{at}$ is the disk $\{(\xi,\eta):(\xi-x)^2+(\eta-y)^2\le a^2t^2\}$.

This is the **general form of the solution** to the Cauchy problem. Equivalently
, using the circle-mean notation,

$$u(x,y,t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\left[\frac{1}{2\pi a}\int_{0}^{at}\!\fr
ac{r\,P_\varphi(r)}{\sqrt{a^2t^2-r^2}}\,dr\right]+\frac{1}{2\pi a}\int_{0}^{at}\
!\frac{r\,P_\psi(r)}{\sqrt{a^2t^2-r^2}}\,dr,$$

with $P_\varphi,P_\psi$ the circular means of $\varphi,\psi$ of radius $r$ about
 $(x,y)$.

**Physical meaning (Huygens' principle in 2D).** Unlike the 3D case where signal
s propagate sharply on the sphere $r=at$ (no tail), in 2D the solution at $(x,y,
t)$ depends on **all** initial data inside the disk $D_{at}$, not just on the bo
undary circle. This is the "tail" or "reverberation" effect: a 2D wave leaves a
lingering wake. This is precisely why sound in 2D (e.g. on a membrane) is not as
 crisp as in 3D.

### 2. Conditions for a unique solution

For the Cauchy problem on $\mathbb{R}^2\times(0,\infty)$, a unique solution is d
etermined by:

1. **Initial displacement:** $u(x,y,0)=\varphi(x,y)$ prescribed for all $(x,y)\i
n\mathbb{R}^2$.
2. **Initial velocity:** $u_t(x,y,0)=\psi(x,y)$ prescribed for all $(x,y)\in\mat
hbb{R}^2$.

**Regularity requirements.** For the Poisson formula to yield a classical ($C^2$
) solution it suffices that
- $\varphi\in C^3(\mathbb{R}^2)$ and $\psi\in C^2(\mathbb{R}^2)$.

Under these hypotheses the function given by the Poisson formula is $C^2$ in $(x
,y,t)$ for $t>0$, satisfies the wave equation, and attains the initial data:

$$\lim_{t\to 0^+}u(x,y,t)=\varphi(x,y),\qquad \lim_{t\to 0^+}u_t(x,y,t)=\psi(x,y
).$$

**Uniqueness** follows from the energy method: define

$$E(t)=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^2}\!\big(u_t^2+a^2(u_x^2+u_y^2)\big)\,dx\,dy
.$$

For a solution with compactly supported (or sufficiently decaying) data, $E'(t)=
0$, so $E(t)=E(0)$. If two solutions share the same initial data, their differen
ce has $E(0)=0$, hence is identically zero — proving uniqueness. (For non-decayi
ng data, uniqueness holds locally by applying the energy argument on truncated d
omains with the finite-propagation property.)

**Summary of conditions.** A unique solution is determined by:
- the PDE $a^2(u_{xx}+u_{yy})=u_{tt}$ on the domain,
- the initial displacement $\varphi(x,y)=u(x,y,0)$,
- the initial velocity $\psi(x,y)=u_t(x,y,0)$,

with $\varphi\in C^3$, $\psi\in C^2$ (and appropriate growth/decay conditions at
 infinity if one wants the energy argument to close globally). The solution is t
hen given explicitly by the Poisson formula above.

If the problem is posed on a **bounded domain** $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^2$ rath
er than the whole plane, then in addition to the two initial conditions one must
 prescribe a **boundary condition** on $\partial\Omega$ for all $t>0$ — most com
monly:
- Dirichlet: $u\big|_{\partial\Omega}=0$ (clamped membrane), or
- Neumann: $\partial u/\partial n\big|_{\partial\Omega}=0$ (free boundary),

and uniqueness is again obtained by the energy method on $\Omega$.
