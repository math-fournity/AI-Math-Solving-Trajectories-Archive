## Proof

**Claim:** The statement is **true**.

**Setup.** Fix an arbitrary $n \in \mathbb{N}$ (with $n \geq 1$). Define
$$\theta_n := \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\arctan\!\left(\frac{k}{n}\right).$$
We must show there exists $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^+$ with $\arctan(\alpha) = \the
ta_n$, i.e. $n\arctan(\alpha) = \sum_{k=1}^n \arctan(k/n)$.

**Step 1: $\theta_n \in (0,\,\pi/2)$.**

For every $k \in \{1,\dots,n\}$, the argument $k/n$ is a strictly positive real
number, so
$$0 < \arctan(k/n) < \frac{\pi}{2}.$$
The quantity $\theta_n$ is the arithmetic mean of $n$ numbers, each lying strict
ly inside $(0,\pi/2)$. The mean of finitely many values from an open interval ag
ain lies in that interval (it is bounded below by the smallest value, which is $
>0$, and above by the largest, which is $<\pi/2$). Hence
$$0 < \theta_n < \frac{\pi}{2}.$$

**Step 2: $\arctan$ is a bijection from $\mathbb{R}^+$ onto $(0,\pi/2)$.**

The function $\arctan : \mathbb{R} \to (-\pi/2,\pi/2)$ is strictly increasing an
d continuous, hence a bijection onto its image. Restricted to $\mathbb{R}^+ = (0
,\infty)$, its image is exactly $(0,\pi/2)$:
- $\arctan(0) = 0$ and $\lim_{x\to 0^+}\arctan(x) = 0$, so values approach $0$ b
ut never reach it for $x>0$;
- $\lim_{x\to+\infty}\arctan(x) = \pi/2$, approached but never attained.

Thus $\arctan\big|_{\mathbb{R}^+} : \mathbb{R}^+ \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;} (0,\pi/2
)$ is a bijection.

**Step 3: Existence (and uniqueness) of $\alpha$.**

Since $\theta_n \in (0,\pi/2)$ and $\arctan\big|_{\mathbb{R}^+}$ maps $\mathbb{R
}^+$ bijectively onto $(0,\pi/2)$, there exists a **unique** $\alpha \in \mathbb
{R}^+$ such that
$$\arctan(\alpha) = \theta_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\arctan\!\left(\frac{k}{
n}\right).$$
Multiplying both sides by $n$ yields
$$n\arctan(\alpha) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\arctan\!\left(\frac{k}{n}\right).$$

**Conclusion.** The argument holds for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$, so
$$(\forall\, n\in\mathbb{N})\;(\exists\,\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^+):\; n\arctan(\alph
a)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\arctan\!\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)$$
is **true**. (In fact the witnessing $\alpha$ is unique for each $n$.) $\blacksq
