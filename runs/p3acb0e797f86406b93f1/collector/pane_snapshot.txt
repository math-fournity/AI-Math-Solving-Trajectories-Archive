No. A bijective $\mathbb{Q}$-linear map $T:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ need **not**
 satisfy $T(x)=T(1)x$ for all $x$.

## Proof

View $\mathbb{R}$ as a vector space over $\mathbb{Q}$. Since $\mathbb{R}$ is unc
ountable-dimensional over $\mathbb{Q}$, it admits a **Hamel basis** $B\subset\ma
thbb{R}$ (a $\mathbb{Q}$-linearly independent set with $\mathbb{Q}$-span equal t
o $\mathbb{R}$). We may choose $B$ so that $1\in B$ and $\sqrt{2}\in B$: startin
g from the linearly independent set $\{1,\sqrt{2}\}$, extend it to a Hamel basis
 by Zorn's lemma.

Define $T$ on the basis $B$ by swapping two basis elements and fixing the rest:
$$
T(1)=\sqrt{2},\qquad T(\sqrt{2})=1,\qquad T(b)=b\ \text{for all }b\in B\setminus
\{1,\sqrt{2}\},
$$
and extend $T$ $\mathbb{Q}$-linearly to all of $\mathbb{R}$. Explicitly, for $x=
\sum_{b\in B}q_b\, b$ (finite sum, $q_b\in\mathbb{Q}$),
$$
T(x)=q_1\sqrt{2}+q_{\sqrt{2}}\cdot 1+\sum_{b\neq 1,\sqrt{2}}q_b\,b.
$$

**$T$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-linear.** This is immediate from the definition: $T$ is de
fined by linear extension from a basis.

**$T$ is bijective.** The map on $B$ induced by $T$ is the transposition $(1\;\s
qrt{2})$, a permutation of $B$. Hence $T$ sends the basis $B$ bijectively onto i
tself, so $T$ is a $\mathbb{Q}$-linear automorphism of $\mathbb{R}$ (its inverse
 is itself, since a transposition is an involution).

**$T$ does not satisfy $T(x)=T(1)x$ for all $x$.** We have $T(1)=\sqrt{2}$. If t
he identity $T(x)=T(1)x=\sqrt{2}\,x$ held for all $x$, then in particular for $x
=\sqrt{2}$:
$$
T(\sqrt{2})\stackrel{?}{=}\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{2}=2.
$$
But by construction $T(\sqrt{2})=1\neq 2$. Contradiction.

Therefore $T$ is a bijective $\mathbb{Q}$-linear map that fails to satisfy $T(x)
=T(1)x$, so the answer to the question is **no**: not every bijective $\mathbb{Q
}$-linear map $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is of the form $x\mapsto T(1)x$.
