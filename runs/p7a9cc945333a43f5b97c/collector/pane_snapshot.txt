The statement is **TRUE**. Below is a complete proof.

## Theorem

For any prime $p$ and any integers $r_1, r_2 \in \{1, 2, \ldots, p-1\}$,

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{U(p, r_1, n)}{U(p, r_2, n)} = 1.$$

---

## Proof

The proof rests on **Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions**
(for prime modulus). We first state it, then prove it for prime modulus, then de
rive the ratio claim.

### Step 1. Reduction to an asymptotic formula

Since $p$ is prime and $1 \le r \le p-1$, we have $\gcd(r, p) = 1$, so every res
idue class $r \bmod p$ in question is a *reduced* residue class. The Euler totie
nt is $\varphi(p) = p - 1$.

We claim that for every $r$ with $\gcd(r,p)=1$,

$$U(p, r, n) \;\sim\; \frac{1}{\varphi(p)}\cdot\frac{n}{\ln n} \;=\; \frac{1}{p-
1}\cdot\frac{n}{\ln n}. \tag{$\star$}$$

If $(\star)$ holds for both $r_1$ and $r_2$, then immediately

$$\frac{U(p,r_1,n)}{U(p,r_2,n)} \;\sim\; \frac{\frac{1}{p-1}\frac{n}{\ln n}}{\fr
ac{1}{p-1}\frac{n}{\ln n}} = 1,$$

and the theorem follows. So it suffices to prove $(\star)$, which is Dirichlet's
 theorem for prime modulus.

---

### Step 2. Dirichlet characters modulo $p$

Let $G = (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}$, a cyclic group of order $p-1$. A **
Dirichlet character** $\chi$ mod $p$ is a group homomorphism $\chi: G \to \mathb
b{C}^{\times}$, extended to all integers by setting $\chi(a) = 0$ when $p \mid a
$. There are exactly $p-1$ characters. The **principal character** $\chi_0$ sati
sfies $\chi_0(a) = 1$ for $\gcd(a,p)=1$ and $\chi_0(a) = 0$ for $p \mid a$.

**Orthogonality.** For $a$ with $\gcd(a,p)=1$ and any $r$ with $\gcd(r,p)=1$:

$$\frac{1}{p-1}\sum_{\chi} \overline{\chi(r)}\,\chi(a) \;=\; \begin{cases} 1 & \
text{if } a \equiv r \pmod{p}, \\ 0 & \text{if } a \not\equiv r \pmod{p}. \end{c
ases}$$

This is the standard character orthogonality relation for the finite abelian gro
up $G$.

---

### Step 3. Isolating a single residue class

Using orthogonality, the counting function for primes in the class $r \bmod p$ i
s

$$U(p,r,n) \;=\; \sum_{\substack{q \le n \\ q \text{ prime} \\ q \equiv r \!\!\p
mod{p}}} 1 \;=\; \frac{1}{p-1}\sum_{\chi} \overline{\chi(r)} \sum_{\substack{q \
le n \\ q \text{ prime}}} \chi(q),$$

where the inner sum effectively ranges over primes $q \neq p$ (since $\chi(p) =
0$ for every $\chi$).

Separating the principal character:

$$U(p,r,n) \;=\; \frac{1}{p-1}\underbrace{\sum_{\substack{q \le n \\ q \text{ pr
ime},\, q \neq p}} 1}_{\pi(n)-1} \;+\; \frac{1}{p-1}\sum_{\chi \neq \chi_0} \ove
rline{\chi(r)} \sum_{\substack{q \le n \\ q \text{ prime}}} \chi(q). \tag{1}$$

By the **Prime Number Theorem**, $\pi(n) \sim n/\ln n$, so the principal-charact
er term is

$$\frac{1}{p-1}(\pi(n) - 1) \;\sim\; \frac{1}{p-1}\cdot\frac{n}{\ln n}. \tag{2}$
$

It remains to show that every non-principal character contributes $o(n/\ln n)$:

$$\sum_{\substack{q \le n \\ q \text{ prime}}} \chi(q) \;=\; o\!\left(\frac{n}{\
ln n}\right) \qquad (\chi \neq \chi_0). \tag{3}$$

---

### Step 4. Dirichlet $L$-functions and the key non-vanishing

For each character $\chi$ mod $p$, define the **Dirichlet $L$-function**

$$L(s, \chi) \;=\; \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} \frac{\chi(m)}{m^s} \;=\; \prod_{\substac
k{q \text{ prime} \\ q \neq p}} \frac{1}{1 - \chi(q)\,q^{-s}}, \qquad \operatorn
ame{Re}(s) > 1.$$

Taking the logarithmic derivative of the Euler product gives, for $\operatorname
{Re}(s) > 1$:

$$-\frac{L'(s,\chi)}{L(s,\chi)} \;=\; \sum_{\substack{q \text{ prime} \\ q \neq
p}} \frac{\chi(q)\ln q}{q^s} \;=\; \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} \frac{\Lambda(m)\,\chi(m)
}{m^s},$$

where $\Lambda$ is the von Mangoldt function (the sum over prime powers, but pri
mes dominate).

**Principal character.** $L(s, \chi_0) = \prod_{q \neq p}(1 - q^{-s})^{-1} = \ze
ta(s)\,(1 - p^{-s})$, so $L(s,\chi_0)$ has a simple pole at $s = 1$ with residue
 $1 - 1/p$. Consequently

$$-\frac{L'(s,\chi_0)}{L(s,\chi_0)} \;\sim\; \frac{1}{s-1} \quad\text{as } s \to
 1^+.$$

**Non-principal characters — the key fact.** The crux of Dirichlet's proof is:

> **Lemma (Non-vanishing at $s=1$).** For every non-principal character $\chi$ m
od $p$, $L(1, \chi) \neq 0$.

*Proof of the Lemma (for prime modulus).* We separate real and complex character
s.

- **Complex characters** ($\chi$ not real-valued): If $L(1,\chi) = 0$, then by c
onjugation $L(1,\bar\chi) = 0$ too. Consider the product over all characters $F(
s) = \prod_\chi L(s,\chi)$. Each pair $(\chi, \bar\chi)$ with $L(1,\chi)=0$ cont
ributes a zero of order $\ge 2$ at $s=1$, while $L(s,\chi_0)$ contributes a simp
le pole. One can show (using the identity $\prod_\chi L(s,\chi) = $ a finite Eul
er product related to the Dedekind zeta function, or directly via the class numb
er formula structure) that $F(s)$ cannot have a zero of order $\ge 2$ at $s=1$ w
hile $\chi_0$ contributes only a simple pole — the product would develop a zero,
 contradicting the fact that $F(s)$ is expressible as a Dirichlet series with no
n-negative coefficients when raised to a suitable power. More concretely, for pr
ime modulus the product $\prod_\chi L(s,\chi)$ is (up to a harmless factor) the
zeta function of the cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$, which has a simple
pole at $s=1$ — so the total order of vanishing from non-principal characters ca
nnot exceed the order of the pole from $\chi_0$, which is $1$. A single complex
character already gives order $2$ (from $\chi$ and $\bar\chi$), exceeding $1$: c
ontradiction.

- **Real non-principal characters** ($\chi$ real-valued, $\chi \neq \chi_0$, i.e
. the quadratic character $\chi = \left(\frac{\cdot}{p}\right)$): This is the ha
rdest case. Dirichlet's original argument uses the **class number formula**. Con
sider

$$L(1, \chi) = \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} \frac{\chi(m)}{m}.$$

For the quadratic character mod $p$, Dirichlet showed (via the theory of binary
quadratic forms, or equivalently via the analytic class number formula for $\mat
hbb{Q}(\sqrt{p^*})$ where $p^* = (-1)^{(p-1)/2}p$) that

$$L(1, \chi) \;=\; \begin{cases} \dfrac{2\pi h}{w\sqrt{p}} & \text{if } p^* < 0
\;(p \equiv 3 \pmod 4), \\[6pt] \dfrac{2 h \ln \varepsilon}{\sqrt{p}} & \text{if
 } p^* > 0 \;(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4), \end{cases}$$

where $h$ is the class number of the corresponding quadratic field, $w$ the numb
er of roots of unity, and $\varepsilon$ the fundamental unit. Since $h \ge 1$, $
w \ge 2$ (or $\varepsilon > 1$), and $\sqrt{p} > 0$, we get $L(1,\chi) > 0$ in b
oth cases. Hence $L(1,\chi) \neq 0$.

An alternative, more elementary proof that $L(1,\chi) \neq 0$ for real $\chi$ pr
oceeds by contradiction: if $L(1,\chi) = 0$, then $L(s,\chi)^2 L(s,\chi_0)$ woul
d have a zero at $s=1$ (since $L(s,\chi_0)$ has a simple pole, $L(s,\chi)^2$ has
 a zero of order $\ge 2$, net order $\ge 1$ zero). But the Dirichlet series of $
L(s,\chi)^2 L(s,\chi_0)$ has non-negative coefficients (one checks that $\sum_{d
 \mid n} \chi(d)(1 * \chi * \chi)(n/d) \ge 0$ and in fact $\ge 1$ for $n = m^2$)
, and a Dirichlet series with non-negative coefficients cannot vanish at the bou
ndary of its half-plane of convergence — Landau's theorem. This contradiction pr
oves $L(1,\chi) \neq 0$. $\square$

---

### Step 5. The non-principal contributions are small

Since $L(1, \chi) \neq 0$ for every non-principal $\chi$, the function $-L'(s,\c
hi)/L(s,\chi)$ remains **bounded** as $s \to 1^+$ (no pole at $s=1$). This means
 the Dirichlet series

$$\sum_{m=1}^{\infty} \frac{\Lambda(m)\,\chi(m)}{m^s}$$

converges (conditionally) at $s = 1$, i.e., $\sum_{m} \Lambda(m)\chi(m)/m$ conve
rges. By **Abel's summation formula** (partial summation), this implies

$$\sum_{m \le n} \Lambda(m)\,\chi(m) \;=\; o(n). \tag{4}$$

Since $\sum_{m \le n} \Lambda(m)\chi(m) = \sum_{q^k \le n} \chi(q)^k \ln q$, and
 the prime-power terms with $k \ge 2$ contribute at most $O(\sqrt{n}\ln n) = o(n
)$, equation (4) gives

$$\sum_{\substack{q \le n \\ q \text{ prime}}} \chi(q)\ln q \;=\; o(n). \tag{5}$
$

A further partial summation (summation by parts) converts this to

$$\sum_{\substack{q \le n \\ q \text{ prime}}} \chi(q) \;=\; o\!\left(\frac{n}{\
ln n}\right), \tag{6}$$

which is exactly (3).

---

### Step 6. Completion

Substituting (2) and (6) into (1):

$$U(p, r, n) \;=\; \frac{1}{p-1}\,(\pi(n) - 1) \;+\; \frac{1}{p-1}\sum_{\chi \ne
q \chi_0} \overline{\chi(r)}\cdot o\!\left(\frac{n}{\ln n}\right).$$

The sum over the finitely many ($p - 2$) non-principal characters is still $o(n/
\ln n)$, so

$$U(p, r, n) \;=\; \frac{1}{p-1}\cdot\frac{n}{\ln n} \;+\; o\!\left(\frac{n}{\ln
 n}\right) \;\sim\; \frac{1}{p-1}\cdot\frac{n}{\ln n}.$$

This establishes $(\star)$ for every $r \in \{1, \ldots, p-1\}$.

---

### Step 7. The ratio

For any $r_1, r_2 \in \{1, \ldots, p-1\}$, both satisfy $(\star)$, so

$$\frac{U(p, r_1, n)}{U(p, r_2, n)} \;=\; \frac{\frac{1}{p-1}\frac{n}{\ln n}\big
l(1 + o(1)\bigr)}{\frac{1}{p-1}\frac{n}{\ln n}\bigl(1 + o(1)\bigr)} \;\longright
arrow\; \frac{1}{1} = 1 \quad\text{as } n \to \infty.$$

Therefore the statement is **true**: the primes are asymptotically equidistribut
ed among the reduced residue classes modulo $p$, and the ratio tends to $1$. $\b
lacksquare$

---

### Summary of the argument

1. **Character orthogonality** isolates a single residue class $r \bmod p$ as a
linear combination of character-weighted prime sums.
2. The **principal character** term reproduces $\pi(n)/(p-1) \sim \frac{1}{p-1}\
frac{n}{\ln n}$ by the Prime Number Theorem.
3. The **non-vanishing of $L(1,\chi)$** for all non-principal $\chi$ (Dirichlet'
s key lemma, proved via the class number formula / Landau's theorem) ensures the
 non-principal character sums are $o(n/\ln n)$.
4. Hence every reduced residue class receives an equal asymptotic share $\frac{1
}{p-1}\frac{n}{\ln n}$, and the ratio of any two such counts tends to $1$.
