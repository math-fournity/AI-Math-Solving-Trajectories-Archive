## Proof

We show that $f$ must be a (non-constant) polynomial.

**Step 1: $f$ is non-constant.** If $f \equiv c$ were constant, then $f^{-1}(c)
= \mathbb{C}$, which is unbounded, contradicting the hypothesis. Hence $f$ is no
n-constant.

**Step 2: Apply the Casorati–Weierstrass / Great Picard dichotomy at $\infty$.**
 Since $f$ is entire, it has an isolated singularity at $\infty$. There are two
cases:

- *Removable singularity at $\infty$*: then $f$ extends to a holomorphic functio
n on the Riemann sphere, hence $f$ is constant — already excluded.
- *Pole at $\infty$*: then $f$ is a polynomial.
- *Essential singularity at $\infty$*: then $f$ is a transcendental entire funct
ion.

So it remains only to rule out the essential case.

**Step 3: The essential case is impossible.** Suppose $f$ has an essential singu
larity at $\infty$. By the **Great Picard Theorem**, in every neighborhood of $\
infty$ (equivalently, outside every compact subset of $\mathbb{C}$), $f$ takes e
very complex value, with **at most one exception**, infinitely many times.

Consequently, for every $a \in \mathbb{C}$ with at most one exceptional value, t
he set $f^{-1}(a)$ is infinite and unbounded (its elements accumulate only at $\
infty$, so they escape every compact set). In particular, there exists at least
one value $a$ — in fact all values save possibly one — for which $f^{-1}(a)$ is
unbounded.

This directly contradicts the hypothesis that $f^{-1}(a)$ is bounded for **every
** $a \in \mathbb{C}$.

**Conclusion.** The singularity at $\infty$ is neither removable (which would fo
rce $f$ constant) nor essential (ruled out by Picard). Therefore it is a pole, w
hich means $f$ is a polynomial. Combined with Step 1, $f$ is a non-constant poly
nomial.

Conversely, every non-constant polynomial $p$ satisfies the hypothesis: for each
 $a$, the equation $p(z) = a$ has at most $\deg p$ solutions (by the fundamental
 theorem of algebra applied to $p(z) - a$), so $p^{-1}(a)$ is finite and hence b
ounded.

Thus an entire function with all fibers bounded is **exactly** a non-constant po
lynomial. In particular, the answer to the question is **yes**: such an $f$ must
 necessarily be a polynomial.
