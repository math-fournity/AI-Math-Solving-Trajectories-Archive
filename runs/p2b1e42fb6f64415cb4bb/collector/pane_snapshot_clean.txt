**Claim:** No. Not every bounded connected subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$ has a bounda
ry that can be parametrized by a continuous mapping.

We interpret "parametrized by a continuous mapping" in the standard sense: there
 exists a continuous surjection $f:[0,1]\to\partial S$ (equivalently from $\math
bb{R}$ or $S^1$ — all connected domains). We give two counterexamples of increas
ing strength.

---

**Counterexample 1 (Disconnected boundary).** Let

$$A=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2:1<x^2+y^2<4\}$$

be the open annulus. Then:

- $A$ is **bounded** (contained in the disk of radius $2$).
- $A$ is **connected** (indeed path-connected: any two points are joined by a ci
rcular arc followed by a radial segment, all staying inside the annulus).
- $\partial A=\{x^2+y^2=1\}\cup\{x^2+y^2=4\}$, the union of two disjoint circles
, which is **disconnected**.

Since $[0,1]$ is connected and the continuous image of a connected space is conn
ected, no continuous surjection $f:[0,1]\to\partial A$ can exist. $\square$

---

**Counterexample 2 (Connected but non-locally-connected boundary).** Even if one
 restricts attention to sets whose boundary *is* connected, the claim still fail
s. Define $g(x)=\sin(1/x)$ for $x\in(0,1]$ and set

$$\Omega=\{(x,y):0<x<1,\; g(x)<y<g(x)+1\}.$$

Then:

- $\Omega$ is **bounded**: $x\in(0,1)$ and $y\in(g(x),\,g(x)+1)\subset(-1,2)$.
- $\Omega$ is **connected** (indeed path-connected, being a "wavy strip").
- The boundary $\partial\Omega$ consists of:
  - the lower curve $L=\{(x,\sin(1/x)):0<x\le 1\}$,
  - the upper curve $U=\{(x,\sin(1/x)+1):0<x\le 1\}$,
  - the left accumulation segment $\{0\}\times[-1,2]$,
  - the right edge $\{1\}\times[\sin 1,\,\sin 1+1]$.

The set $L\cup(\{0\}\times[-1,1])$ is the **closed topologist's sine curve**, wh
ich is connected. Likewise $U\cup(\{0\}\times[0,2])$ is connected. These two sha
re the segment $\{0\}\times[0,1]$, and the right edge connects $L$ (at $(1,\sin
1)$) to $U$ (at $(1,\sin 1+1)$). Hence $\partial\Omega$ is **connected** (and co
mpact, being closed and bounded).

However, $\partial\Omega$ is **not locally connected**. Consider any point $(0,y
_0)$ with $y_0\in(-1,1)$. For sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$, the ball $B((0
,y_0),\varepsilon)$ intersects $\partial\Omega$ in the vertical segment $\{0\}\t
imes(y_0-\varepsilon,\,y_0+\varepsilon)$ together with infinitely many disjoint
small arcs of the oscillating curve $L$ that accumulate on this segment but do n
ot touch it. The connected component of $B((0,y_0),\varepsilon)\cap\partial\Omeg
a$ containing $(0,y_0)$ is exactly the vertical segment piece, which is **not**
a neighborhood of $(0,y_0)$ in $\partial\Omega$ (since every neighborhood also c
ontains those detached arcs). Therefore $\partial\Omega$ fails to be locally con
nected at $(0,y_0)$.

By the **Hahn–Mazurkiewicz theorem**, a compact metric space is a continuous ima
ge of $[0,1]$ if and only if it is compact, connected, and **locally connected**
. Since $\partial\Omega$ is compact and connected but not locally connected, it
is **not** a continuous image of $[0,1]$. $\square$

---

Both counterexamples establish that the answer is **no**: not every bounded conn
ected subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$ has a boundary that can be parametrized by a cont
inuous mapping. The first shows the boundary need not even be connected; the sec
ond shows that even connectivity of the boundary does not suffice, as the bounda
ry can fail to be locally connected — the necessary and sufficient condition (to
gether with compactness and connectedness) for being a continuous image of $[0,1
]$.
