Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the condition that all metr
ic circles on $M$ are Euclidean circles forces $M$ to have constant nonnegative
Gaussian curvature.

**Answer: Yes.** Here is the proof.

---

## Proof

Let $f: M \to \mathbb{R}^n$ be the isometric embedding. A *metric circle* of rad
ius $r$ centered at $p$ is the set $C_r(p) = \{q \in M : d_M(p,q) = r\}$, and th
e hypothesis is that $f(C_r(p))$ is a round circle in $\mathbb{R}^n$ (lying in s
ome 2-plane) for every $p$ and every $r$.

### Step 1: Small geodesic circles force every point to be umbilic

Fix $p \in M$ and let $e_1, e_2$ be an orthonormal basis of $T_p M$. Let $v(\the
ta) = \cos\theta\, e_1 + \sin\theta\, e_2$. The geodesic $\gamma_\theta(t)$ from
 $p$ with $\gamma_\theta'(0) = v(\theta)$ has the Taylor expansion in the embedd
ing:

$$f(\gamma_\theta(t)) = f(p) + t\, v(\theta) + \frac{t^2}{2}\, \mathrm{II}(v(\th
eta), v(\theta)) + \frac{t^3}{6}\, (\nabla_{v(\theta)} \mathrm{II})(v(\theta), v
(\theta)) + O(t^4),$$

where $\mathrm{II}$ is the second fundamental form (normal-vector-valued). Writi
ng $h_{ij} = \mathrm{II}(e_i, e_j)$, we compute:

$$\mathrm{II}(v(\theta), v(\theta)) = \frac{h_{11}+h_{22}}{2} + \frac{h_{11}-h_{
22}}{2}\cos 2\theta + h_{12}\sin 2\theta.$$

Setting $H = \frac{h_{11}+h_{22}}{2}$ (mean curvature vector) and $S(\theta) = \
frac{h_{11}-h_{22}}{2}\cos 2\theta + h_{12}\sin 2\theta$ (traceless part), the g
eodesic circle of radius $r$ is:

$$X_r(\theta) = \underbrace{\left(f(p) + \frac{r^2}{2}H + O(r^3)\right)}_{\text{
center } c_r} + \; r\, v(\theta) + \frac{r^2}{2}\, S(\theta) + O(r^3).$$

Relative to the center, the curve is $Y_r(\theta) = r\, v(\theta) + \frac{r^2}{2
}\, S(\theta) + O(r^3)$, where $r\, v(\theta)$ lies in the **tangent plane** $T_
p M$ and $S(\theta)$ is **normal** to $T_p M$.

**Planarity analysis.** By hypothesis, $Y_r(\theta)$ lies in some 2-plane $P_r$.
 The tangent projection of $Y_r$ is $r\, v(\theta) + O(r^3)$, which spans the fu
ll 2-dimensional tangent plane. Therefore $P_r$ must be a graph over $T_p M$: it
s normal part must be a *linear* function of the tangent coordinates $(r\cos\the
ta, r\sin\theta)$, i.e., of the form $(a_r \cos\theta + b_r \sin\theta)\,\nu_r$
for some normal direction $\nu_r$ and scalars $a_r, b_r$.

Equating normal parts:
$$\frac{r^2}{2}\, S(\theta) + O(r^3) = r(a_r \cos\theta + b_r \sin\theta)\,\nu_r
.$$

Dividing by $r$ and letting $r \to 0$ (with $a_r = O(r)$, $b_r = O(r)$ since the
 left side is $O(r^2)$):

$$\frac{1}{2}\, S(\theta) = (\alpha \cos\theta + \beta \sin\theta)\,\nu_0$$

for some constants $\alpha, \beta$ and normal direction $\nu_0$. But $S(\theta)
= \frac{h_{11}-h_{22}}{2}\cos 2\theta + h_{12}\sin 2\theta$ is a linear combinat
ion of $\cos 2\theta$ and $\sin 2\theta$, while the right side is a combination
of $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$. Since $\{\cos\theta, \sin\theta, \cos 2\theta,
 \sin 2\theta\}$ are linearly independent functions, both sides must vanish:

$$S(\theta) = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad h_{11} = h_{22}, \quad h_{12} = 0.$$

This means $\mathrm{II} = H \cdot g$ at $p$; i.e., **$p$ is an umbilic point**.
Since $p$ was arbitrary, every point of $M$ is umbilic.

### Step 2: Codazzi equation forces $H$ to be parallel

With $\mathrm{II} = H \cdot g$ everywhere, the Codazzi equation $(\nabla_X \math
rm{II})(Y,Z) = (\nabla_Y \mathrm{II})(X,Z)$ gives (using metric compatibility $\
nabla g = 0$):

$$(\nabla^\perp_X H)\, g(Y,Z) = (\nabla^\perp_Y H)\, g(X,Z).$$

Setting $(X,Y,Z) = (e_1, e_2, e_2)$: $\nabla^\perp_{e_1} H = 0$. Setting $(X,Y,Z
) = (e_2, e_1, e_1)$: $\nabla^\perp_{e_2} H = 0$. Therefore:

$$\nabla^\perp H = 0,$$

so $H$ is **parallel in the normal bundle**, and $|H|^2$ is constant.

### Step 3: Gauss equation gives $K = |H|^2 = \text{const} \geq 0$

The Gauss equation for a submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^n$ reads:

$$K = \langle \mathrm{II}(e_1, e_1), \mathrm{II}(e_2, e_2) \rangle - \langle \ma
thrm{II}(e_1, e_2), \mathrm{II}(e_2, e_1) \rangle.$$

With $\mathrm{II}(e_1,e_1) = H$, $\mathrm{II}(e_2,e_2) = H$, $\mathrm{II}(e_1,e_
2) = 0$:

$$K = \langle H, H \rangle = |H|^2.$$

Since $|H|^2$ is constant (Step 2), $K$ is **constant**, and since $K = |H|^2 \g
eq 0$, the curvature is **nonnegative**. $\blacksquare$

### Remark on sharpness

The conclusion is sharp: spheres ($K > 0$) and planes ($K = 0$) both satisfy the
 hypothesis — geodesic circles on a sphere are intersections with parallel plane
s (hence round circles), and geodesic circles on a plane are ordinary circles. C
onversely, surfaces like the cylinder (flat but not umbilic in its standard embe
dding) fail the condition: their geodesic circles are not planar curves in $\mat
hbb{R}^3$ for generic radii.
