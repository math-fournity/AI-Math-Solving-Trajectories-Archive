## Proof

Let $A = [0,1]^2$ be the unit square. We claim the largest area of a triangle wi
th vertices on the perimeter of $A$ is $\boxed{\tfrac{1}{2}}$.

**Upper bound via the "slide to a corner" argument.**

Let $T$ be any triangle with vertices $P_1, P_2, P_3$ on the perimeter of $A$. T
he signed area of $T$ is

$$
\mathcal{A}(P_1,P_2,P_3) = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl((x_2-x_1)(y_3-y_1) - (y_2-y_1)(x_3-
x_1)\bigr).
$$

Expanding and collecting terms in $P_1 = (x_1,y_1)$ (with $P_2, P_3$ held fixed)
:

$$
(x_2-x_1)(y_3-y_1) - (y_2-y_1)(x_3-x_1)
= (x_2 y_3 - y_2 x_3) + x_1(y_2 - y_3) + y_1(x_3 - x_2),
$$

which is an **affine (linear) function** of $(x_1, y_1)$. The actual area is $\t
frac{1}{2}|\text{(this linear function)}|$.

Now, each $P_i$ lies on the perimeter, hence on some edge (side) of the square —
 unless it is already a corner, in which case there is nothing to do. A linear f
unction restricted to a line segment is monotone, so its absolute value attains
its maximum at one of the **two endpoints** of that segment. Therefore we may sl
ide $P_1$ to a corner of the square **without decreasing** the area. Repeating t
his for $P_2$ (with the new $P_1$ and the old $P_3$ fixed) and then for $P_3$, w
e obtain a triangle $T'$ whose three vertices are all corners of $A$ and whose a
rea is **at least** that of $T$.

Thus the maximum-area inscribed triangle is achieved with vertices among the fou
r corners of the square.

**Evaluating the corner triangles.**

Any three corners of the unit square form a right triangle with legs of length $
1$ (e.g. $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)$), giving area

$$
\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = \tfrac{1}{2}.
$$

All four choices of three corners give the same area $\tfrac{1}{2}$.

**Achievability.** The triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, $(0,1)$ — all on
 the perimeter — has area $\tfrac{1}{2}$, so the bound is tight.

**Conclusion.** The largest area of a triangle whose vertices lie on the perimet
er of the unit square is $\dfrac{1}{2}$.
