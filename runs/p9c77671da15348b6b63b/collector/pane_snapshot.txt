Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the condition that $f(x, mx
)$ has a local minimum at the origin for every $m$ forces $f$ itself to have a l
ocal minimum at the origin, given that the origin is a non-degenerate critical p
oint.

**Answer: Yes, $f$ has a local minimum at the origin.**

**Proof.**

Since the origin is a critical point, $\nabla f(0,0) = 0$. Since it is non-degen
erate, the Hessian
$$H = \begin{pmatrix} f_{xx} & f_{xy} \\ f_{xy} & f_{yy} \end{pmatrix}\bigg|_{(0
,0)}$$
satisfies $\det(H) = f_{xx}f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2 \neq 0$.

For each $m \in \mathbb{R}$, define $g_m(x) = f(x, mx)$. Then $g_m(0) = 0$ and
$$g_m'(0) = f_x(0,0) + m\,f_y(0,0) = 0,$$
so $x = 0$ is a critical point of $g_m$. The second derivative is
$$g_m''(0) = f_{xx}(0,0) + 2m\,f_{xy}(0,0) + m^2\,f_{yy}(0,0).$$

Since $g_m$ has a local minimum at $x = 0$, we must have $g_m''(0) \geq 0$. This
 holds for **every** $m \in \mathbb{R}$, so the quadratic in $m$
$$q(m) = f_{yy}\,m^2 + 2f_{xy}\,m + f_{xx}$$
is non-negative for all $m \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Case 1: $f_{yy} < 0$.** Then $q(m) \to -\infty$ as $m \to \pm\infty$, so $q(m)
 \geq 0$ for all $m$ is impossible. Contradiction.

**Case 2: $f_{yy} = 0$.** Then $q(m) = 2f_{xy}\,m + f_{xx}$ is linear. For this
to be $\geq 0$ for all $m$, we need $f_{xy} = 0$ and $f_{xx} \geq 0$. But then $
\det(H) = f_{xx} \cdot 0 - 0 = 0$, contradicting non-degeneracy. Contradiction.

**Case 3: $f_{yy} > 0$.** The quadratic $q(m)$ opens upward. For $q(m) \geq 0$ f
or all $m$, the discriminant must be non-positive:
$$\Delta = 4f_{xy}^2 - 4f_{yy}\,f_{xx} \leq 0 \implies f_{xy}^2 \leq f_{xx}\,f_{
yy} \implies \det(H) = f_{xx}f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2 \geq 0.$$
Combined with non-degeneracy ($\det(H) \neq 0$), we get $\det(H) > 0$.

Since $f_{yy} > 0$ and $\det(H) > 0$, we also have $f_{xx} > 0$ (because $f_{xx}
 \geq f_{xy}^2/f_{yy} > 0$ when $\det > 0$). Therefore the Hessian $H$ is **posi
tive definite**.

By the second derivative test for non-degenerate critical points (equivalently,
by the Morse lemma, which gives a local coordinate change under which $f$ become
s a positive definite quadratic form), $f$ has a **strict local minimum** at the
 origin. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The non-degeneracy hypothesis is essential. Without it, one could ha
ve $\det(H) = 0$ with $f_{yy} > 0$ and $f_{xy}^2 = f_{xx}f_{yy}$, making $q(m) \
geq 0$ for all $m$ with equality at one value of $m$; at that value the second d
erivative test is inconclusive and $f$ need not have a local minimum along that
line, so the argument would break down.
