**Answer: No.**

The convergence of each term $s_n(x) \to s_n$ pointwise, together with the conve
rgence of $\sum s_n$, does **not** imply $S(x) \to S$. The obstruction is that t
he convergence of $s_n(x) \to s_n$ need not be uniform in $n$, so the "mass" of
the series can escape to infinity.

**Counterexample.**

Let $x$ range over $[1,\infty)$. Define
$$
s_n(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } n \le x < n+1, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \e
nd{cases}
$$

**Step 1: Pointwise limit of each term.** Fix $n$. For all $x \ge n+1$ we have $
s_n(x) = 0$. Hence
$$
\lim_{x\to\infty} s_n(x) = 0 =: s_n \quad \text{for every } n.
$$

**Step 2: The limit series converges.** Since $s_n = 0$ for all $n$,
$$
S = \sum_{n=1}^\infty s_n = 0,
$$
which converges.

**Step 3: $S(x)$ does not converge to $S$.** For every $x \ge 1$, exactly one in
teger $n$ satisfies $n \le x < n+1$ (namely $n = \lfloor x \rfloor$), so
$$
S(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty s_n(x) = 1 \quad \text{for all } x \ge 1.
$$
Therefore
$$
\lim_{x\to\infty} S(x) = 1 \ne 0 = S.
$$

**Why this happens.** The "unit mass" sits in a single term $s_n(x)$, but *which
* term carries the mass slides off to $n=\infty$ as $x\to\infty$. Each fixed ter
m eventually settles to $0$, yet the sum never does. Uniform convergence of $s_n
(x)\to s_n$ (uniform in $n$) plus convergence of $\sum s_n$ would be sufficient
to force $S(x)\to S$; without it, the limit and the sum cannot be interchanged.
