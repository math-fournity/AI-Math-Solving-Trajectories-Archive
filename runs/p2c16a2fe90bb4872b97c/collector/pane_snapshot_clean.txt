## Solution

**The answer is No.** It need not be true that $m^k = 0$.

### Why the "obvious" argument fails

If $A$ were Noetherian, then $m$ would be finitely generated, hence $m^k$ would
be a finitely generated $A$-module. The hypothesis $m^k = m^{k+1} = m \cdot m^k$
 together with Nakayama's lemma (applicable since $m$ is the Jacobson radical of
 the local ring) would force $m^k = 0$.

The problem does **not** assume $A$ is Noetherian, so Nakayama's lemma need not
apply. We give an explicit counterexample.

### Counterexample: a valuation domain with value group $\mathbb{Q}$

Let $k$ be a field and consider the field
$$K = \bigcup_{n \geq 1} k((t^{1/n})),$$
equipped with the valuation $v: K^\times \to \mathbb{Q}$ defined by
$$v\!\left(\sum_{i} c_i\, t^{a_i}\right) = \min\{a_i : c_i \neq 0\},$$
extended by $v(0) = \infty$. This is a well-defined Krull valuation with value g
roup $\mathbb{Q}$.

Set $V = \{x \in K : v(x) \geq 0\}$, the valuation ring of $v$. Then $V$ is a lo
cal domain with maximal ideal
$$m = \{x \in V : v(x) > 0\}.$$

**Claim.** $m^2 = m$ and $m \neq 0$.

*Proof that $m \neq 0$.* The element $t \in K$ has $v(t) = 1 > 0$, so $t \in m$.
 Hence $m \neq 0$.

*Proof that $m^2 = m$.* The inclusion $m^2 \subseteq m$ is immediate since $m$ i
s an ideal. For the reverse inclusion, let $x \in m$, so $v(x) = q$ for some $q
\in \mathbb{Q}_{>0}$. Since $q/2 \in \mathbb{Q}_{>0}$, there exists $y \in K$ wi
th $v(y) = q/2$ (for instance, $y = t^{q/2}$, which lies in $K$ because $K$ cont
ains $t^{r}$ for every $r \in \mathbb{Q}$). Then $y \in m$ (as $v(y) = q/2 > 0$)
 and $z := x/y \in K$ satisfies $v(z) = q - q/2 = q/2 > 0$, so $z \in m$ as well
. Therefore
$$x = y \cdot z \in m \cdot m = m^2.$$
This shows $m \subseteq m^2$, and we conclude $m^2 = m$. $\square$

### Conclusion

Taking $k = 1$, we have $m^k = m^{k+1}$ (i.e., $m = m^2$), yet $m^k = m \neq 0$.
 Thus the statement "$m^k = 0$" does **not** follow from the hypothesis $m^k = m
^{k+1}$ for a general local ring.

**Remark.** The statement *is* true when $A$ is Noetherian (by Nakayama's lemma,
 as noted above). The counterexample exploits the failure of finite generation:
the maximal ideal $m$ of this valuation domain is not finitely generated, so $m^
k$ is not a finitely generated $A$-module, and Nakayama's lemma cannot be applie
d.
