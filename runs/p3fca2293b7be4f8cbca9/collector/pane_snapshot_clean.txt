## Proof

Since $S$ is finite, among all triangles $\triangle XYZ$ with $X,Y,Z \in S$ ther
e is one of maximum area; call it $\triangle ABC$. By hypothesis, $\operatorname
{area}(\triangle ABC)\le 1$.

**Edge case.** If $\operatorname{area}(\triangle ABC)=0$, then all points of $S$
 are collinear (every triple has area $0$). The set $S$ is then contained in a l
ine segment, which is easily covered by a triangle of area $4$ (take any triangl
e of area $4$ whose interior meets the line of $S$ in a segment long enough to c
ontain $S$). Henceforth assume $\operatorname{area}(\triangle ABC)>0$, so $\tria
ngle ABC$ is non-degenerate.

**The anticomplementary triangle.** Let $\ell_A$ be the line through $A$ paralle
l to $BC$, $\ell_B$ the line through $B$ parallel to $CA$, and $\ell_C$ the line
 through $C$ parallel to $AB$. These three lines bound a triangle $\triangle A'B
'C'$ (the *anticomplementary triangle* of $\triangle ABC$) that contains $\trian
gle ABC$ in its interior. Since $\triangle ABC$ is the medial triangle of $\tria
ngle A'B'C'$,

$$\operatorname{area}(\triangle A'B'C')=4\cdot\operatorname{area}(\triangle ABC)
\le 4.$$

**Claim.** $S\subseteq \triangle A'B'C'$ (together with its interior).

Suppose for contradiction that some $D\in S$ lies outside $\triangle A'B'C'$. Th
e triangle $\triangle A'B'C'$ is the intersection of three closed half-planes, s
o $D$ must lie strictly beyond at least one of the three bounding lines. Without
 loss of generality, suppose $D$ lies on the side of $\ell_A$ opposite to $BC$.

Let $h_A=\operatorname{dist}(A,BC)$. The line $\ell_A$ is parallel to $BC$ and p
asses through $A$, so every point on the $BC$-side of $\ell_A$ is at distance $\
le h_A$ from line $BC$, while every point on the far side is at distance $>h_A$
from line $BC$. Since $D$ is on the far side,

$$\operatorname{dist}(D,BC)>h_A=\operatorname{dist}(A,BC).$$

Therefore

$$\operatorname{area}(\triangle DBC)=\tfrac12|BC|\cdot\operatorname{dist}(D,BC)>
\tfrac12|BC|\cdot h_A=\operatorname{area}(\triangle ABC).$$

But $D,B,C\in S$, so $\triangle DBC$ is one of the triangles among points of $S$
, and its area exceeds that of $\triangle ABC$—contradicting the maximality of $
\operatorname{area}(\triangle ABC)$. The same argument applies (by symmetry) if
$D$ lies beyond $\ell_B$ or $\ell_C$. Hence no such $D$ exists, and $S\subseteq\
triangle A'B'C'$.

**Adjusting to area exactly $4$.** We have $S\subseteq\triangle A'B'C'$ and $\op
eratorname{area}(\triangle A'B'C')\le 4$. If the area is already $4$, we are don
e. If $\operatorname{area}(\triangle A'B'C')<4$, scale $\triangle A'B'C'$ about
its centroid by the factor $t=\sqrt{4/\operatorname{area}(\triangle A'B'C')}>1$.
 The centroid is an interior point, so scaling by $t>1$ produces a larger triang
le that still contains $\triangle A'B'C'$, hence still contains $S$, and has are
a exactly $t^2\cdot\operatorname{area}(\triangle A'B'C')=4$.

In both cases, there exists a triangle of area $4$ that, together with its inter
ior, covers $S$. $\blacksquare$
