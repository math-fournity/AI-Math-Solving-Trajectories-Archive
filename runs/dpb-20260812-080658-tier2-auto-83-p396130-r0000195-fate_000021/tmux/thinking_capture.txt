Theorem. Every algebraically closed field is infinite.

Proof.

Let $F$ be an algebraically closed field. Suppose, for contradiction, that $F$
is finite, say $F = {a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n}$ with $n$ elements.

Since $F$ is a field, it has a multiplicative identity $1$. Consider the
polynomial

$$f(x) = 1 + \prod_{i=1}^{n}(x - a_i) \in F[x].$$

This is a well-defined polynomial in $F[x]$ (each factor $x - a_i$ lies in
$F[x]$, and the product and the addition of $1$ keep it in $F[x]$). Its degree
is $n \geq 1$ (every field has at least two elements, $0$ and $1$, so $n \geq
2$; in particular $\deg f = n \geq 1$).

Now evaluate $f$ at every element $a_j \in F$:

$$f(a_j) = 1 + \prod_{i=1}^{n}(a_j - a_i).$$

The product $\prod_{i=1}^{n}(a_j - a_i)$ contains the factor with $i = j$,
namely $(a_j - a_j) = 0$. Therefore the entire product is $0$, and so

$$f(a_j) = 1 + 0 = 1 \neq 0$$

for every $j = 1, \dots, n$.

Thus $f$ has no root in $F$. But $f$ is a non-constant polynomial of degree $n
\geq 1$ over the algebraically closed field $F$, so by definition of algebraic
closure, $f$ must have a root in $F$. This is a contradiction.

Therefore our assumption that $F$ is finite must be false. Hence $F$ is
infinite. $\blacksquare$
