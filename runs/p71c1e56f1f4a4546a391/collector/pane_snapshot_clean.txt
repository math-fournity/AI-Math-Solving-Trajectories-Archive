## Proof

Let $X$ denote the set of all $f\colon\mathbb Q\to\mathbb Q$ satisfying the "Cau
chy at infinity" condition: for every $\epsilon\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$ there is $\del
ta\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$ such that for all $x,y\in\mathbb Q$ with $x,y>\delta$, $|f(
x)-f(y)|<\epsilon$. Define $f\sim g$ iff $\lim_{x\to\infty}(f(x)-g(x))=0$. We sh
ow that $X/{\sim}$ is isomorphic (in fact, as a $\mathbb Q$-vector space) to $\m
athbb R$.

### Step 1. Every $f\in X$ has a well-defined limit in $\mathbb R$.

The condition on $f$ is exactly the Cauchy criterion for the limit $\lim_{x\to\i
nfty,\,x\in\mathbb Q} f(x)$. Since $\mathbb R$ is the (order/metric) completion
of $\mathbb Q$, every Cauchy net/sequence in $\mathbb Q$ converges in $\mathbb R
$. Concretely, pick any sequence $x_n\in\mathbb Q$ with $x_n\to+\infty$; then $(
f(x_n))$ is a Cauchy sequence in $\mathbb Q$ (given $\epsilon>0$, choose $\delta
$ from the hypothesis, then $N$ with $x_n>\delta$ for $n\ge N$, giving $|f(x_n)-
f(x_m)|<\epsilon$), hence converges to some $L_f\in\mathbb R$. A standard argume
nt shows the limit is independent of the chosen sequence $x_n\to\infty$: if $x_n
,y_n\to\infty$, interleave them and use the Cauchy condition. Thus
$$L_f:=\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x)\in\mathbb R$$
is well defined.

### Step 2. Equivalence classes are exactly determined by $L_f$.

For $f,g\in X$,
$$f\sim g \iff \lim_{x\to\infty}(f(x)-g(x))=0 \iff L_f=L_g.$$
So the map
$$\Phi\colon X/{\sim}\;\longrightarrow\;\mathbb R,\qquad [f]\longmapsto L_f$$
is well defined and injective.

### Step 3. $\Phi$ is surjective.

Given any $r\in\mathbb R$, we construct $f\in X$ with $L_f=r$.

Choose a sequence of rationals $(r_n)_{n\ge 0}$ with $r_n\to r$ in $\mathbb R$.
Define $f\colon\mathbb Q\to\mathbb Q$ by
$$f(x)=\begin{cases} r_n & \text{if } n\le x<n+1,\; n\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0},\\ 0 &
 \text{if } x<0.\end{cases}$$
(This is well defined since each rational $x\ge 0$ lies in a unique interval $[n
,n+1)$.)

*Cauchy at infinity:* Let $\epsilon\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$. Since $r_n\to r$, there i
s $N$ with $|r_n-r|<\epsilon/2$ for all $n\ge N$. Take $\delta:=N$. For $x,y\in\
mathbb Q$ with $x,y>\delta=N$, say $x\in[n,n+1)$, $y\in[m,m+1)$ with $n,m\ge N$,
 we have
$$|f(x)-f(y)|=|r_n-r_m|\le |r_n-r|+|r_m-r|<\epsilon.$$
So $f\in X$.

*Limit:* For $x\in[n,n+1)$ with $n\to\infty$, $f(x)=r_n\to r$, hence $L_f=r$.

Thus every $r\in\mathbb R$ is hit, and $\Phi$ is surjective.

### Step 4. $\Phi$ is an isomorphism of $\mathbb Q$-vector spaces.

$X$ carries a natural $\mathbb Q$-vector space structure by pointwise operations
, and $\sim$ is compatible with it (if $f\sim f'$ and $g\sim g'$, then $f+g\sim
f'+g'$ and $cf\sim cf'$ for $c\in\mathbb Q$), so $X/{\sim}$ is a $\mathbb Q$-vec
tor space. For $f,g\in X$ and $c\in\mathbb Q$,
$$L_{f+g}=\lim(f+g)=L_f+L_g,\qquad L_{cf}=\lim(cf)=c\,L_f.$$
Hence $\Phi([f+g])=\Phi([f])+\Phi([g])$ and $\Phi([cf])=c\,\Phi([f])$: $\Phi$ is
 $\mathbb Q$-linear. Combined with bijectivity from Steps 2–3, $\Phi$ is a $\mat
hbb Q$-linear isomorphism.

### Conclusion

The map $[f]\mapsto\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)$ is a well-defined bijection (indeed a
$\mathbb Q$-vector-space isomorphism) between $X/{\sim}$ and $\mathbb R$. Theref
ore **yes**, the set of equivalence classes of $X$ under $\sim$ is isomorphic to
 the set of real numbers.
