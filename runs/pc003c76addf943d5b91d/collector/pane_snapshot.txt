## Solution

**Claim:** Yes, $I(z) = 0$ for every complex $z$ with $|z|<1$.

**Proof.** Make the substitution $w = e^{it}$, so $dw = ie^{it}\,dt = iw\,dt$, i
.e. $dt = \frac{dw}{iw}$. As $t$ runs from $0$ to $2\pi$, $w$ traverses the unit
 circle $|w|=1$ once counterclockwise. Also $e^{-it} = 1/w$. Hence

$$
I(z) = \oint_{|w|=1} \frac{1/w}{w-z}\cdot\frac{dw}{iw} = \frac{1}{i}\oint_{|w|=1
}\frac{dw}{w^{2}(w-z)}.
$$

The integrand $f(w)=\dfrac{1}{w^{2}(w-z)}$ has poles at $w=0$ (order $2$) and $w
=z$ (simple). Since $|z|<1$, both lie inside the unit circle.

**Residue at $w=z$** (simple pole):
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{w=z} f = \lim_{w\to z}(w-z)f(w) = \frac{1}{z^{2}}.
$$

**Residue at $w=0$** (pole of order $2$): write $f(w)=\frac{1}{w^{2}}\cdot g(w)$
 with $g(w)=\frac{1}{w-z}$. The residue is $g'(0)$:
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{w=0} f = g'(0) = -\frac{1}{(w-z)^{2}}\Big|_{w=0} = -\frac{1}
{z^{2}}.
$$

Sum of residues inside $|w|=1$:
$$
\frac{1}{z^{2}} + \left(-\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right) = 0.
$$

By the residue theorem,
$$
I(z) = \frac{1}{i}\cdot 2\pi i \cdot 0 = 0.
$$

**Sanity check at $z=0$:** $I(0)=\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{e^{-it}}{e^{it}}dt=\int_0^{2
\pi}e^{-2it}dt=0$, consistent. $\blacksquare$
