## Proof

**Claim.** In the decomposition of a semisimple Lie algebra \(L\) into simple Li
e algebras
\[
L = L_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus L_n,
\]
each component \(L_i\) is necessarily an ideal of \(L\).

---

### Step 1. What the direct-sum decomposition means

The symbol \(\oplus\) in the decomposition of a Lie algebra is the **direct sum
of Lie algebras**. Concretely, it means:

1. **(Vector-space direct sum)** \(L = L_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus L_n\) as vector
spaces, so every \(x \in L\) admits a unique expression \(x = x_1 + \cdots + x_n
\) with \(x_i \in L_i\).

2. **(Each \(L_i\) is a subalgebra)** For all \(x_i, y_i \in L_i\), \([x_i, y_i]
 \in L_i\).

3. **(Cross-terms vanish)** For \(i \neq j\), \([L_i, L_j] = 0\), i.e. \([x_i, x
_j] = 0\) for every \(x_i \in L_i\), \(x_j \in L_j\).

Condition (3) is the defining feature that distinguishes a *direct sum of Lie al
gebras* from a mere vector-space direct sum of subspaces: the distinct summands
commute with each other.

---

### Step 2. Each \(L_i\) is an ideal

Fix an index \(i\). To show \(L_i \trianglelefteq L\), we must verify
\[
[L_i,\, L] \subseteq L_i.
\]

Take arbitrary \(x \in L_i\) and \(y \in L\). By the vector-space decomposition
(condition 1), write
\[
y = y_1 + \cdots + y_n, \qquad y_j \in L_j.
\]
Using bilinearity of the bracket,
\[
[x, y] = [x,\, y_1 + \cdots + y_n] = \sum_{j=1}^{n} [x, y_j].
\]

Now split the sum:

- **For \(j \neq i\):** by condition (3), \([L_i, L_j] = 0\), so \([x, y_j] = 0\
).
- **For \(j = i\):** by condition (2), \(L_i\) is a subalgebra, so \([x, y_i] \i
n L_i\).

Hence
\[
[x, y] = [x, y_i] \in L_i.
\]

Since \(x \in L_i\) and \(y \in L\) were arbitrary,
\[
[L_i, L] \subseteq L_i,
\]
which is precisely the statement that \(L_i\) is an ideal of \(L\). ∎

---

### Step 3. Why such a decomposition exists (context)

The existence of this decomposition for a semisimple Lie algebra is a standard t
heorem, and its proof *also* produces ideals directly:

- A Lie algebra \(L\) is **semisimple** iff its Killing form \(\kappa\) is non-d
egenerate (Cartan's semisimplicity criterion).
- For any ideal \(I \trianglelefteq L\), its orthogonal complement \(I^\perp = \
{x \in L : \kappa(x, I) = 0\}\) is again an ideal of \(L\), and non-degeneracy o
f \(\kappa\) together with the fact that an ideal of a semisimple algebra is its
elf semisimple gives
  \[
  L = I \oplus I^\perp.
  \]
- Iterating this splitting on each non-simple summand until no further decomposi
tion is possible yields
  \[
  L = L_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus L_n,
  \]
  where each \(L_i\) is a **simple ideal** of \(L\).

Thus the components produced by the canonical decomposition theorem are ideals b
y construction, and — as shown in Step 2 — *any* direct-sum decomposition into s
imple Lie algebras necessarily has ideal summands, because the cross-vanishing c
ondition \([L_i, L_j] = 0\) forces \([L_i, L] \subseteq L_i\).

---

### Step 4. Strengthening: the \(L_i\) are precisely the minimal nonzero ideals

In fact the components are not just ideals but the **minimal** nonzero ideals of
 \(L\):

- Each \(L_i\) is simple, so it has no proper nonzero ideals *as a Lie algebra*.
 Any ideal \(J \trianglelefteq L\) with \(J \subseteq L_i\) satisfies \([J, L_i]
 \subseteq J\) (since \(J\) is an ideal of \(L\) and \(L_i \subseteq L\)), makin
g \(J\) an ideal of \(L_i\); simplicity forces \(J = 0\) or \(J = L_i\).
- Conversely, any minimal nonzero ideal of \(L\) must equal some \(L_i\): if \(I
 \trianglelefteq L\) is nonzero, pick \(0 \neq x \in I\) with \(x = x_1 + \cdots
 + x_n\); since \(I\) is an ideal and the \(L_j\) commute pairwise, projecting o
nto each summand shows each nonzero \(x_j\) lies in \(I\), so \(I\) contains som
e \(L_j\), and minimality gives \(I = L_j\).

This shows the decomposition is **unique up to permutation** of the summands, an
d the simple components are intrinsically characterized as the minimal nonzero i
deals of \(L\).

---

### Conclusion

**Yes.** In the decomposition \(L = L_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus L_n\) of a semisimp
le Lie algebra into simple Lie algebras, each component \(L_i\) is necessarily a
n ideal of \(L\). This follows immediately from the defining property of a direc
t sum of Lie algebras, namely \([L_i, L_j] = 0\) for \(i \neq j\), which gives \
([L_i, L] = [L_i, L_i] \subseteq L_i\). Moreover, these components are precisely
 the minimal nonzero ideals of \(L\), so the decomposition is canonical up to pe
rmutation.
