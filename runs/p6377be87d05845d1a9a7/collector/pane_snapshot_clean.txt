**Proof.**

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space, and let $T: V \to V$ be a linear t
ransformation that is both nilpotent and diagonalizable (i.e., admits a basis of
 eigenvectors). We show that $T = 0$.

**Step 1: Nilpotency forces every eigenvalue to be $0$.**

Suppose $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $T$ with eigenvector $v \neq 0$, so $Tv =
\lambda v$. Since $T$ is nilpotent, there exists a positive integer $k$ with $T^
k = 0$. Then

$$0 = T^k v = \lambda^k v.$$

Since $v \neq 0$, we get $\lambda^k = 0$, hence $\lambda = 0$. Thus the only eig
envalue of $T$ is $0$.

**Step 2: Diagonalizability forces $T$ to equal $0$.**

Since $T$ has a basis of eigenvectors, $V$ admits a basis $\mathcal{B} = \{v_1,
\dots, v_n\}$ with $T v_i = \lambda_i v_i$ for each $i$. By Step 1, every $\lamb
da_i = 0$, so $T v_i = 0$ for every basis vector $v_i$. A linear transformation
that vanishes on a basis vanishes on all of $V$:

$$T\!\left(\sum_i c_i v_i\right) = \sum_i c_i\, T v_i = 0.$$

Hence $T = 0$.

**Step 3: Verification of the converse.**

The zero transformation $T = 0$ is nilpotent ($T^1 = 0$) and every vector is an
eigenvector (with eigenvalue $0$), so any basis of $V$ is a basis of eigenvector
s.

**Conclusion.** The only linear transformation $T: V \to V$ that is both nilpote
nt and has a basis of eigenvectors is the zero transformation $T = 0$.
