## Evaluation of $\displaystyle I = \int_{0}^{\infty}\!\left(\frac{\sin x}{x}\ri
ght)^{2}dx$ via the Residue Theorem

### 1. Reformulation

Using $\sin^{2}x = \dfrac{1-\cos 2x}{2}$,

$$I = \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos 2x}{2x^{2}}\,dx.$$

The integrand is even, so equivalently

$$I = \frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos 2x}{2x^{2}}\,dx = \frac{1}{
2}\,\Re\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1-e^{2ix}}{2x^{2}}\,dx.$$

### 2. Choice of meromorphic function and contour

Set

$$f(z)=\frac{1-e^{2iz}}{2z^{2}}.$$

The only singularity is at $z=0$. Expanding $e^{2iz}=1+2iz-2z^{2}-\tfrac{4i}{3}z
^{3}+\cdots$,

$$1-e^{2iz}=-2iz+2z^{2}+\tfrac{4i}{3}z^{3}-\cdots,$$

$$f(z)=\frac{-2iz+2z^{2}+\cdots}{2z^{2}}=\frac{-i}{z}+1+\tfrac{2i}{3}z-\cdots.$$

Thus $z=0$ is a **pole of order 2** (the $1/z^{2}$ coefficient cancels, leaving
a simple pole term $-i/z$ plus a regular part). The **residue** at $z=0$ is

$$\operatorname{Res}_{z=0} f(z) = -i.$$

**Contour.** Since the pole lies on the real axis, we indent around it. Take the
 closed positively-oriented contour $\Gamma$ consisting of:

- $\gamma_{1}$: real segment from $-R$ to $-\varepsilon$,
- $C_{\varepsilon}$: small semicircle $z=\varepsilon e^{i\theta}$, $\theta:\pi\t
o 0$ (upper half-plane, **excluding** $0$ from the interior),
- $\gamma_{2}$: real segment from $\varepsilon$ to $R$,
- $C_{R}$: large semicircle $z=Re^{i\theta}$, $\theta:0\to\pi$ (upper half-plane
).

The interior of $\Gamma$ contains **no poles** (the only pole $z=0$ is excluded
by the indentation), so by Cauchy's theorem

$$\oint_{\Gamma} f(z)\,dz = 0.$$

### 3. The large semicircle $C_{R}$

For $z=Re^{i\theta}$ with $0\le\theta\le\pi$, we have $\Im z = R\sin\theta\ge 0$
, hence

$$|e^{2iz}|=e^{-2\Im z}=e^{-2R\sin\theta}\le 1,$$

and in fact $e^{2iz}\to 0$ for $\theta\in(0,\pi)$. Therefore $f(z)\sim \dfrac{1}
{2z^{2}}$ on $C_{R}$, and

$$\left|\int_{C_{R}} f(z)\,dz\right|\le \int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{1+|e^{2iz}|}{2R^{2}}
\,R\,d\theta \le \frac{\pi}{R}\xrightarrow[R\to\infty]{}0.$$

### 4. The small semicircle $C_{\varepsilon}$ (the second-order pole contributio
n)

Using the Laurent expansion $f(z)=\dfrac{-i}{z}+1+O(z)$:

$$\int_{C_{\varepsilon}} f(z)\,dz = \int_{C_{\varepsilon}}\frac{-i}{z}\,dz + \in
t_{C_{\varepsilon}}1\,dz + \int_{C_{\varepsilon}}O(z)\,dz.$$

With $z=\varepsilon e^{i\theta}$, $dz=i\varepsilon e^{i\theta}d\theta$, $\theta:
\pi\to 0$:

$$\int_{C_{\varepsilon}}\frac{-i}{z}\,dz = -i\int_{\pi}^{0}\frac{i\varepsilon e^
{i\theta}}{\varepsilon e^{i\theta}}\,d\theta = -i\cdot i\int_{\pi}^{0}d\theta =
(1)(-\pi)=-\pi.$$

$$\int_{C_{\varepsilon}}1\,dz = \int_{\pi}^{0}i\varepsilon e^{i\theta}d\theta =
\varepsilon\bigl(e^{0}-e^{i\pi}\bigr)=2\varepsilon\to 0,$$

and $|O(z)|=O(\varepsilon)$ on $C_{\varepsilon}$ gives a contribution $O(\vareps
ilon^{2})\to 0$. Hence

$$\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{C_{\varepsilon}} f(z)\,dz = -\pi.$$

### 5. Assembling the pieces

Letting $R\to\infty$ and $\varepsilon\to 0$ in $\displaystyle\oint_{\Gamma}f(z)\
,dz=0$:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx \;+\; (-\pi) \;+\; 0 \;=\; 0,$$

so

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1-e^{2ix}}{2x^{2}}\,dx = \pi.$$

Taking real parts (the imaginary part is a principal-value integral that vanishe
s by symmetry):

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos 2x}{2x^{2}}\,dx = \pi.$$

The integrand is even, so

$$\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos 2x}{2x^{2}}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{2}.$$

### 6. Conclusion

Since $\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sin x}{x}\right)^{2}=\frac{1-\cos 2x}{2x^{2}}$,

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\sin x}{x}\right)^{2}dx = \fr
ac{\pi}{2}.}$$

The key residue-theorem ingredient was the **second-order pole at $z=0$** of $f(
z)=\dfrac{1-e^{2iz}}{2z^{2}}$: although the $1/z^{2}$ term cancels in the Lauren
t expansion, the pole is genuinely of order $2$ (the numerator vanishes only to
first order at $z=0$, making $1-e^{2iz}$ have a simple zero, so the quotient by
$z^{2}$ has a pole of order $2$). Indenting the contour above this real-axis pol
e produced the contribution $-\pi$ from the small semicircle, which is precisely
 what yields the final value $\pi/2$.
