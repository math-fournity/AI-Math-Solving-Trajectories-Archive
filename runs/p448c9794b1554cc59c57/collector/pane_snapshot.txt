Yes. At any fixed point $q \in M$, one can choose coordinates so that $(g_{ij})$
 is the identity at $q$.

## Proof

Fix any coordinate system $(u^1,\dots,u^k)$ on a neighborhood of $q$ with $q$ co
rresponding to $u=0$. The coordinate vector fields
$$e_i := \frac{\partial \vec{x}}{\partial u^i}\bigg|_q \in \mathbb{R}^n$$
are $k$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Since $(u^1,\dots,u^k)$ is a genuine coordina
te system on the $k$-dimensional submanifold $M$, the vectors $e_1,\dots,e_k$ ar
e linearly independent (they form a basis of $T_qM$). Consequently the matrix
$$G := (g_{ij}(q)) = (e_i \cdot e_j)$$
is symmetric and **positive definite**.

We seek new coordinates $(v^1,\dots,v^k)$, related to the old ones by a constant
 invertible linear change
$$v^i = \sum_{j=1}^k A^i_{\;j}\, u^j, \qquad A \in \mathrm{GL}(k,\mathbb{R}),$$
so that the induced metric at $q$ is the identity.

By the chain rule,
$$\frac{\partial \vec{x}}{\partial v^i}\bigg|_q = \sum_{j=1}^k \frac{\partial u^
j}{\partial v^i}\, \frac{\partial \vec{x}}{\partial u^j}\bigg|_q = \sum_{j=1}^k
(A^{-1})^{j}_{\;i}\, e_j.$$

Hence the new metric at $q$ is
$$\tilde g_{ij}(q) = \frac{\partial \vec{x}}{\partial v^i}\cdot \frac{\partial \
vec{x}}{\partial v^j}\bigg|_q = \sum_{m,n} (A^{-1})^{m}_{\;i}\, (A^{-1})^{n}_{\;
j}\, g_{mn}(q) = \bigl[(A^{-1})^{\mathsf T}\, G\, A^{-1}\bigr]_{ij}.$$

We want $\tilde g_{ij}(q) = \delta_{ij}$, i.e.
$$(A^{-1})^{\mathsf T}\, G\, A^{-1} = I \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G = A^{\ma
thsf T} A.$$

Since $G$ is symmetric positive definite, such an $A$ always exists. For instanc
e:

- **Spectral form.** Write $G = Q\,\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_k)\, Q^
{\mathsf T}$ with $Q$ orthogonal and $\lambda_i > 0$. Set $A = Q\,\mathrm{diag}(
\sqrt{\lambda_1},\dots,\sqrt{\lambda_k})\, Q^{\mathsf T}$, the unique symmetric
positive definite square root $G^{1/2}$. Then $A^{\mathsf T}A = G^{1/2}G^{1/2} =
 G$.
- **Cholesky form.** Equally, take $A$ to be the Cholesky factor $G = L^{\mathsf
 T}L$ with $L$ upper triangular.

With either choice, $A$ is invertible, so $v = Au$ is a valid coordinate change,
 and at $q$
$$\tilde g_{ij}(q) = \delta_{ij}.$$

Thus the new coordinates $(v^1,\dots,v^k)$ make the first fundamental form the i
dentity matrix at the prescribed point $q$. $\blacksquare$
