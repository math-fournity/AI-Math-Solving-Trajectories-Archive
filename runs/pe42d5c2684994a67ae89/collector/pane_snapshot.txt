## Solution

We evaluate the improper integral

$$I = \int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{s_2}\left(\frac{1}{s_2-s_1}\right)^{3/2} ds_1\,
ds_2 = \int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{s_2}(s_2-s_1)^{-3/2}\,ds_1\,ds_2.$$

### Step 1: Analyze the inner integral

Fix any $s_2 > 0$. The inner integral is

$$J(s_2) = \int_{0}^{s_2}(s_2-s_1)^{-3/2}\,ds_1.$$

The integrand has a singularity at $s_1 = s_2$ (where $s_2 - s_1 \to 0^+$). This
 is an improper integral of the second kind, so we define it as

$$J(s_2) = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0^+}\int_{0}^{s_2-\varepsilon}(s_2-s_1)^{-3/2}\
,ds_1.$$

### Step 2: Compute via substitution

Let $u = s_2 - s_1$, so $du = -ds_1$. When $s_1 = 0$, $u = s_2$; when $s_1 = s_2
 - \varepsilon$, $u = \varepsilon$. Thus

$$\int_{0}^{s_2-\varepsilon}(s_2-s_1)^{-3/2}\,ds_1 = \int_{\varepsilon}^{s_2}u^{
-3/2}\,du = \left[-2u^{-1/2}\right]_{\varepsilon}^{s_2} = -2\,s_2^{-1/2}+2\,\var
epsilon^{-1/2}.$$

### Step 3: Take the limit

$$J(s_2) = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0^+}\left(-2\,s_2^{-1/2}+2\,\varepsilon^{-1/2}\
right) = +\infty.$$

The term $2\,\varepsilon^{-1/2} \to +\infty$ as $\varepsilon \to 0^+$, since $-\
tfrac{3}{2} < -1$ (equivalently, the exponent $p = \tfrac{3}{2} > 1$ in the sing
ularity $u^{-p}$), which is precisely the threshold for divergence of $\int_0^{a
} u^{-p}\,du$.

### Step 4: Conclude

For **every** $s_2 > 0$, the inner integral $J(s_2)$ diverges to $+\infty$. Sinc
e the integrand $(s_2-s_1)^{-3/2}$ is strictly positive on the domain $0 \le s_1
 < s_2$, the double integral (which is the integral of a nonnegative function) s
atisfies

$$I = \int_{0}^{\infty} J(s_2)\,ds_2 = \int_{0}^{\infty}(+\infty)\,ds_2 = +\inft

The nonnegativity of the integrand rules out any cancellation that could rescue
convergence (by Tonelli's theorem, the iterated integral equals the double integ
ral over the region, and either both are finite and equal, or both are $+\infty$

### Conclusion

The improper integral **diverges**; its value is $+\infty$.
