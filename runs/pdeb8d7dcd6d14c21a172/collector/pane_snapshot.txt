## Proof

**Interpretation.** A *complex structure* on the real plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ is th
e structure of a one-dimensional complex manifold — equivalently, a Riemann surf
ace structure — on the topological space $\mathbb{R}^2$. Two such structures are
 *the same* if they are biholomorphic (via a homeomorphism of the underlying spa
ce). We count the number of distinct structures up to biholomorphic equivalence.

---

**Step 1. $\mathbb{R}^2$ is simply connected and non-compact.**

The real plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ is contractible, hence simply connected. It is non
-compact.

**Step 2. Apply the Uniformization Theorem.**

The Uniformization Theorem states that every simply connected Riemann surface is
 biholomorphic to exactly one of:

1. the Riemann sphere $\widehat{\mathbb{C}} = \mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}$ (topolog
ically $S^2$),
2. the complex plane $\mathbb{C}$,
3. the open unit disk $\mathbb{D} = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z|<1\}$ (equivalently,
 the upper half-plane $\mathbb{H}$).

**Step 3. Exclude the compact case.**

The Riemann sphere $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ is compact (homeomorphic to $S^2$), wh
ile $\mathbb{R}^2$ is non-compact. A biholomorphism is in particular a homeomorp
hism, and compactness is a topological invariant. Therefore $\widehat{\mathbb{C}
}$ cannot carry the topology of $\mathbb{R}^2$, and this case is excluded.

**Step 4. The two remaining cases are distinct.**

It remains to show that $\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbb{D}$ are **not** biholomorphic.

Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists a biholomorphism $f:\mathbb{C}\to\
mathbb{D}$. Then $f$ is an entire function (holomorphic on all of $\mathbb{C}$)
and $|f(z)|<1$ for all $z\in\mathbb{C}$, so $f$ is bounded. By **Liouville's The
orem**, every bounded entire function is constant. But a constant function canno
t be a bijection — contradiction.

Hence $\mathbb{C}\not\cong\mathbb{D}$ biholomorphically.

**Step 5. Both structures are realizable on $\mathbb{R}^2$.**

- $\mathbb{C}$ is itself $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the standard complex structure $z =
 x+iy$.
- $\mathbb{D}$ is the open unit disk in $\mathbb{C}$, which is homeomorphic to $
\mathbb{R}^2$ (via, e.g., $z \mapsto \frac{z}{1-|z|}$ in polar form, or any diff
eomorphism $\mathbb{D}\cong\mathbb{R}^2$). Pulling back the complex structure of
 $\mathbb{D}$ along this homeomorphism gives a complex structure on the topologi
cal space $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Conclusion.**

By the Uniformization Theorem, every complex structure on the simply connected,
non-compact surface $\mathbb{R}^2$ is biholomorphic to either $\mathbb{C}$ or $\
mathbb{D}$, and these two are not biholomorphic by Liouville's Theorem. Both are
 realizable. Therefore the number of different complex structures on the real pl
ane is:

$$\boxed{2}$$
