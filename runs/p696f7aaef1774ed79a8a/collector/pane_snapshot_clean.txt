**Proof.**

We claim that $f$ must be identically zero on $[a,b]$. We argue by contradiction
.

Suppose, to the contrary, that $f$ is not identically zero. Then there exists a
point $c \in [a,b]$ with $f(c) \neq 0$.

**Case 1: $c \in (a,b)$.** Without loss of generality, assume $f(c) > 0$ (otherw
ise replace $f$ by $-f$). Since $f$ is continuous, there exists $\delta > 0$ suc
h that $[c-\delta, c+\delta] \subset (a,b)$ and
$$f(x) > \frac{f(c)}{2} > 0 \quad \text{for all } x \in [c-\delta, c+\delta].$$

Choose $\delta$ small enough that $[c-2\delta, c+2\delta] \subset (a,b)$. Define
 a continuous "bump" function $g:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ by
$$
g(x) = \begin{cases}
\left(\delta^2 - (x-c)^2\right)^2, & |x - c| \le \delta, \\
0, & |x - c| \ge \delta.
\end{cases}
$$
(One may also use any standard non-negative bump supported in $[c-\delta,c+\delt
a]$.) Then $g$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, $g(a) = g(b) = 0$ (since the support li
es strictly inside $(a,b)$), $g(x) \ge 0$ everywhere, and $g(x) > 0$ on $(c-\del
ta, c+\delta)$.

By hypothesis,
$$0 = \int_a^b f(x)g(x)\,dx = \int_{c-\delta}^{c+\delta} f(x)g(x)\,dx.$$
However, the integrand $f(x)g(x)$ is strictly positive on $(c-\delta, c+\delta)$
 (since both $f(x) > f(c)/2 > 0$ and $g(x) > 0$ there) and non-negative on $[c-\
delta, c+\delta]$. A continuous non-negative function that is strictly positive
on a set of positive measure has a strictly positive integral. Therefore
$$\int_{c-\delta}^{c+\delta} f(x)g(x)\,dx > 0,$$
contradicting $\int_a^b f(x)g(x)\,dx = 0$.

**Case 2: $c = a$ (or $c = b$).** Suppose $f(a) \neq 0$; WLOG $f(a) > 0$. By con
tinuity, there exists $\delta > 0$ with $[a, a+\delta] \subset [a,b]$ and $f(x)
> f(a)/2 > 0$ on $[a, a+\delta]$. Choose $\delta$ small enough that $a + 2\delta
 < b$. Define
$$
g(x) = \begin{cases}
\left(\delta^2 - (x - (a+\delta))^2\right)^2, & |x - (a+\delta)| \le \delta, \\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
$$
i.e., a bump centered at $a+\delta$ supported on $[a, a+2\delta]$. This satisfie
s $g(a) = g(b) = 0$, $g \ge 0$, and $g > 0$ on $(a, a+2\delta)$, where $f > 0$.
The same argument yields $\int_a^b f(x)g(x)\,dx > 0$, a contradiction. The case
$c = b$ is symmetric.

**Conclusion.** In all cases the assumption $f(c) \neq 0$ leads to a contradicti
on. Hence $f(x) = 0$ for every $x \in [a,b]$; that is, $f$ is identically zero.
$\blacksquare$
