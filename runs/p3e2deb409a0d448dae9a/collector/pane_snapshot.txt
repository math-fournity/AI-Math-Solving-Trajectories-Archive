## Proof

**Key factorization.** We claim:
$$Q(x) = \bigl(P(x) + x\,P'(x)\bigr)\bigl(x\,P(x) + P'(x)\bigr).$$

Indeed, expanding the right-hand side:
$$(P + xP')(xP + P') = xP^2 + PP' + x^2 PP' + x(P')^2 = xP^2 + (1+x^2)PP' + x(P'
)^2 = Q(x). \quad\checkmark$$

Set $A(x) = P(x) + x\,P'(x)$ and $B(x) = x\,P(x) + P'(x)$, so $Q = A \cdot B$.

**Derivative interpretations.** Note:
- $A(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x\,P(x)\bigr]$. Let $F(x) = x\,P(x)$.
- $B(x) = e^{-x^2/2}\,\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x^2/2}\,P(x)\bigr]$. Let $G(x) = e^{x
^2/2}\,P(x)$, so $G'(x) = e^{x^2/2}\,B(x)$.

**Roots of $A$ via $F$.** Let $r_1 < r_2 < \cdots < r_n$ be the roots of $P$, al
l greater than $1$. Then $F(x) = x\,P(x)$ has $n+1$ distinct real roots:
$$0,\; r_1,\; r_2,\; \ldots,\; r_n$$
(since $r_i > 1 > 0$). By Rolle's theorem, $A = F'$ has at least one root in eac
h of the $n$ open intervals
$$(0,\,r_1),\;(r_1,\,r_2),\;\ldots,\;(r_{n-1},\,r_n).$$
So $A$ has **at least $n$** distinct real roots.

**Roots of $B$ via $G$.** Since $e^{x^2/2} > 0$ for all $x$, the function $G(x)
= e^{x^2/2}P(x)$ has exactly the same roots as $P$: $r_1, \ldots, r_n$. By Rolle
's theorem, $G'$ (and hence $B$, since $G' = e^{x^2/2}B$) has at least one root
in each of the $n-1$ open intervals
$$(r_1,\,r_2),\;\ldots,\;(r_{n-1},\,r_n).$$
So $B$ has **at least $n-1$** distinct real roots.

**No common roots in $(r_i, r_{i+1})$.** Suppose $c \in (r_i, r_{i+1})$ satisfie
s both $A(c) = 0$ and $B(c) = 0$, i.e.,
$$P(c) + c\,P'(c) = 0, \qquad c\,P(c) + P'(c) = 0.$$
From the first equation, $P(c) = -c\,P'(c)$. Substituting into the second:
$$c\bigl(-c\,P'(c)\bigr) + P'(c) = P'(c)\bigl(1 - c^2\bigr) = 0.$$
Since $c > r_i > 1$, we have $c^2 \neq 1$, so $P'(c) = 0$. Then $P(c) = -c \cdot
 0 = 0$, meaning $c$ is a root of $P$—contradicting $c \in (r_i, r_{i+1})$ (an i
nterval with no roots of $P$). Therefore, **in each interval $(r_i, r_{i+1})$, e
very root of $A$ is distinct from every root of $B$**.

**Counting distinct roots of $Q$.** In each of the $n - 1$ intervals $(r_1, r_2)
, \ldots, (r_{n-1}, r_n)$:
- $A$ contributes at least $1$ root,
- $B$ contributes at least $1$ root,
- these roots are distinct (by the argument above).

This gives at least $2(n-1)$ distinct roots of $Q$ from these intervals.

Additionally, $A$ has at least one root in $(0, r_1)$ (from Rolle's theorem on $
F$). This root lies in a **different interval** from all the roots counted above
, so it is distinct from all of them, contributing at least $1$ more distinct ro
ot of $Q$.

**Total:** at least $2(n-1) + 1 = 2n - 1$ distinct real roots.

**Conclusion.** $Q$ must have at least $2n - 1$ distinct real roots. $\blacksqua
re$
