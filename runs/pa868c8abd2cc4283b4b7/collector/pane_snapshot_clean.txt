## Proof

We seek positive integers $x, y$ minimizing $x^2 + y^2$ subject to:
- $x(x+1) \mid y(y+1)$,
- $x \nmid y,\; x \nmid (y+1),\; (x+1) \nmid y,\; (x+1) \nmid (y+1)$.

**Step 1: $x \geq 2$.** If $x=1$, then $x\mid y$ for every $y$, violating the co
nstraint.

**Step 2: Neither $x$ nor $x+1$ can be a prime power.** Suppose $x = p^a$ (prime
 $p$, $a\ge1$). Since $x(x+1)\mid y(y+1)$ and $\gcd(y,y+1)=1$, the full power $p
^a$ must divide $y$ or $y+1$, i.e. $x\mid y$ or $x\mid(y+1)$—contradiction. The
same argument applies to $x+1$. Hence both $x$ and $x+1$ must have **at least tw
o distinct prime factors**.

**Step 3: $x \geq 14$.** Checking $x = 2,\dots,13$: in every case at least one o
f $\{x, x+1\}$ is a prime power (including primes themselves). The first $x$ whe
re both have ≥2 distinct prime factors is $x=14$:
$$14 = 2\cdot 7,\qquad 15 = 3\cdot 5.$$

**Step 4: Find the minimum valid $y$ for $x=14$.** We have $x(x+1)=210=2\cdot3\c
dot5\cdot7$. Since $\gcd(y,y+1)=1$, each prime in $\{2,3,5,7\}$ divides exactly
one of $y, y+1$. The non-overlap constraints force:
- **Split 14:** primes 2 and 7 must go to *different* sides (one divides $y$, th
e other $y+1$).
- **Split 15:** primes 3 and 5 must go to *different* sides.

This yields 4 CRT systems. The one giving the smallest $y$: assign $2,5\mid y$ a
nd $3,7\mid(y+1)$, i.e.
$$y \equiv 0 \pmod{10},\qquad y \equiv -1 \pmod{21}.$$
Solving: $y=10k$, $10k\equiv 20\pmod{21}$, $k\equiv 2\pmod{21}$, so $y\equiv 20\
pmod{210}$. The smallest positive solution is $y=20$.

**Verification of $(x,y)=(14,20)$:**
- $x(x+1)=210$; $y(y+1)=20\cdot21=420=2\cdot210$. ✓
- $14\nmid 20$ and $14\nmid 21$. ✓
- $15\nmid 20$ and $15\nmid 21$. ✓

**Step 5: Optimality.** We have $x^2+y^2 = 196+400 = 596$. For any $x>14$, the n
ext valid value (passing Step 2) is $x=20$ (since $15,\dots,19$ all fail). Then
$x^2=400$ and $y(y+1)\ge x(x+1)=420$ forces $y\ge 20$, giving $x^2+y^2\ge 800>59
6$.

Therefore the minimum possible value is

$$\boxed{596}$$
