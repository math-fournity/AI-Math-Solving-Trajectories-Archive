## Proof

**Claim.** The only bounded harmonic function $h$ on the upper half-plane $H = \
{(x,y): y>0\}$ satisfying $h(x,0)=1$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$ is the constant fu
nction $h\equiv 1$.

**Step 1. Poisson representation of bounded harmonic functions on $H$.**

A standard result in harmonic analysis states: if $u$ is bounded and harmonic on
 $H$, then there exists a unique $f\in L^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ such that

$$
u(x,y) \;=\; \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{y}{(x-t)^2+y^2}\,f(t)\,d
t, \qquad (x,y)\in H.
$$

Moreover, $u$ has non-tangential boundary limit $f(x)$ at almost every $x\in\mat
hbb{R}$.

**Step 2. Identify the boundary data.**

The hypothesis $h(x,0)=1$ for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$ means the boundary values o
f $h$ exist everywhere and equal $1$. In particular the non-tangential boundary
limit is $1$ a.e., so the representing function is $f\equiv 1$ (a.e.).

**Step 3. Evaluate the Poisson integral with $f\equiv 1$.**

For every $(x,y)\in H$,

$$
h(x,y) \;=\; \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{y}{(x-t)^2+y^2}\,dt.
$$

Substituting $s=(t-x)/y$, so $dt = y\,ds$:

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{y}{(x-t)^2+y^2}\,dt
\;=\; \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{y}{y^2 s^2+y^2}\,y\,ds
\;=\; \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{s^2+1}\,ds
\;=\; \pi.
$$

Hence $h(x,y)=\frac{1}{\pi}\cdot\pi = 1$ for every $(x,y)\in H$.

**Step 4. Verification.**

The constant function $h\equiv 1$ is harmonic (its Laplacian vanishes), bounded,
 and satisfies $h(x,0)=1$ for all $x$. Thus it is indeed a solution, and by Step
s 1–3 it is the only one.

**Conclusion.** The unique bounded harmonic function on $H$ with boundary value
$1$ everywhere is

$$
\boxed{h\equiv 1}.
$$
