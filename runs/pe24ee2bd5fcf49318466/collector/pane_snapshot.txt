## Proof

Omega$ itself is uncountable.

We consider $\Omega \times [0,1)$ equipped with the **lexicographic order**:
$$(\alpha, x) < (\beta, y) \quad\iff\quad \alpha < \beta,\ \text{or}\ \alpha = \
beta\ \text{and}\ x < y.$$

**Claim.** $\Omega \times [0,1)$ has no greatest element.

**Key fact: $\Omega$ has no greatest element.** For any $\alpha \in \Omega$, the
 initial segment below $\alpha$ is countable, so $\alpha$ is a countable ordinal
. Its successor $\alpha + 1$ is also countable, hence $\alpha + 1 \in \Omega$, a
nd $\alpha < \alpha + 1$. Therefore no element of $\Omega$ is maximal.

**Proof of the claim.** Suppose for contradiction that $(\alpha^\*, x^\*) \in \O
mega \times [0,1)$ is a greatest element. Then for every $(\beta, y) \in \Omega
\times [0,1)$ we have $(\beta, y) \leq (\alpha^\*, x^\*)$.

By the key fact, $\alpha^\*$ is not maximal in $\Omega$, so there exists $\gamma
 \in \Omega$ with $\alpha^\* < \gamma$ (for instance $\gamma = \alpha^\* + 1$).
Now consider the element $(\gamma, 0) \in \Omega \times [0,1)$. Since $\alpha^\*
 < \gamma$, the lexicographic order gives
$$(\alpha^\*, x^\*) < (\gamma, 0).$$
This contradicts the assumption that $(\alpha^\*, x^\*)$ is greatest.

Since $\alpha^\*$ was arbitrary, no element of $\Omega \times [0,1)$ is greatest
. $\blacksquare$
