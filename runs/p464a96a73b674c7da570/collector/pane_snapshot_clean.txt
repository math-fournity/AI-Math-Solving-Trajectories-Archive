## Proof

**Setup.** Since $f$ is analytic on $\mathbb{D}$, the radius of convergence $R$
satisfies $R \geq 1$. The question is whether the additional hypothesis (uniform
 convergence of $f_n$ to $f$ on $\overline{\mathbb{D}}$) forces $R > 1$.

**Claim.** The radius of convergence can be exactly $1$. That is, $R > 1$ is **n
ot** forced by the hypothesis.

**Counterexample.** Define
$$f(z) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{z^n}{n^2}, \qquad a_0 = 0,\; a_n = \frac{1}{n
^2}\;(n \geq 1).$$

**Step 1: $f$ is analytic on $\mathbb{D}$.** The radius of convergence is
$$R = \frac{1}{\limsup_{n\to\infty} |a_n|^{1/n}} = \frac{1}{\limsup_{n\to\infty}
 n^{-2/n}} = \frac{1}{1} = 1,$$
since $n^{-2/n} = e^{-2\ln n/n} \to e^0 = 1$. So $f$ is analytic on $\mathbb{D}$
 and $R = 1$.

**Step 2: $f_n \to f$ uniformly on $\overline{\mathbb{D}}$.** For every $|z| \le
q 1$ and every $n \geq 1$,
$$\left|\frac{z^n}{n^2}\right| \leq \frac{1}{n^2}.$$
Since $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} < \infty$, the **Weierstrass M-test** g
uarantees that the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} z^n/n^2$ converges uniformly on $
\overline{\mathbb{D}}$. Consequently, the partial sums $f_n(z) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}
z^k/k^2$ converge uniformly to $f$ on $\overline{\mathbb{D}}$.

**Step 3: $R = 1$ exactly (not $R > 1$).** It remains to verify that $R$ is not
strictly greater than $1$. Suppose for contradiction that $R > 1$. Then $f$ woul
d extend to an analytic function on a disk $\{|z| < R\}$ with $R > 1$, and in pa
rticular $f$ would be analytic at $z = 1$.

However, for $z \in \mathbb{D}$ we may differentiate term-by-term:
$$f'(z) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{z^{n-1}}{n} = \frac{1}{z}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
 \frac{z^n}{n} = -\frac{\log(1-z)}{z}, \qquad z \in \mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}.$$
As $z \to 1^{-}$ along the real axis, $\log(1-z) \to -\infty$, so $f'(z) \to +\i
nfty$. Thus $f$ is not analytic at $z = 1$, contradicting $R > 1$. Therefore $R
= 1$.

**Conclusion.** The example $f(z) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} z^n/n^2$ satisfies all t
he hypotheses:
- $f$ is analytic on $\mathbb{D}$,
- the Taylor partial sums $f_n$ converge uniformly to $f$ on $\overline{\mathbb{
D}}$,
- yet the radius of convergence is exactly $R = 1$.

Therefore, **the radius of convergence can be exactly $1$**; it is not necessari
ly strictly greater than $1$.
