## Statement

Let $N$ be a positive integer and $k \geq 2$ an integer. Write $\mathcal{M}_k(\G
amma_0(N))$ for the space of holomorphic modular forms of weight $k$ on $\Gamma_
0(N)$, and let

$$\mathcal{M}_k(\Gamma_0(N))^0 \;\subset\; \mathcal{M}_k(\Gamma_0(N))$$

denote the subspace of **newforms together with the forms obtained from lower le
vels by the degeneracy maps** — more precisely, the orthogonal complement (with
respect to the Petersson inner product) of the space of oldforms. Equivalently,
$\mathcal{M}_k(\Gamma_0(N))^0$ is the subspace spanned by the newforms of level
dividing $N$ that are eigenforms for the full Hecke algebra, together with their
 images under the degeneracy maps $\alpha_d : f(z)\mapsto f(dz)$. A form $f\in\m
athcal{M}_k(\Gamma_0(N))^0$ is characterized by the property that it is determin
ed by its Fourier coefficients $a_n$ with $\gcd(n,N)=1$ (the "primitive" coeffic
ients), and the $L$-function attached to $f$ admits a functional equation of con
ductor exactly $N$.

Let

$$L(s) \;=\; \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{a_n}{n^s}$$

be a Dirichlet series with coefficients $a_n$ satisfying a polynomial growth con
dition $a_n = O(n^{C})$ for some $C>0$, so that $L(s)$ converges in a right half
-plane. For every primitive Dirichlet character $\chi$ modulo $m$ with $\gcd(m,N
)=1$, form the **twisted Dirichlet series**

$$L(s,\chi) \;=\; \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{a_n\,\chi(n)}{n^s}.$$

For each such $\chi$, let $\tau(\chi)=\sum_{a\bmod m}\chi(a)\,e^{2\pi i a/m}$ be
 the Gauss sum, and define the **completed twisted $L$-function**

$$\Lambda(s,\chi) \;=\; \left(\frac{Nm^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{s/2}\,(2\pi)^{-s}\,\Ga
mma(s)\,L(s,\chi) \;=\; \left(\frac{\sqrt{N}\,m}{2\pi}\right)^{s}\,\Gamma(s)\,L(
s,\chi).$$

(The exact normalizing constant depends on the parity convention; the essential
point is that the conductor entering the functional equation is $N\cdot m^2$.)

> **Weil's Converse Theorem (1953).** *Suppose the following conditions hold:*
>
> **(i) Analytic continuation.** *For every primitive Dirichlet character $\chi$
 modulo $m$ with $\gcd(m,N)=1$ (including the trivial character $\chi=\chi_0$),
the completed function $\Lambda(s,\chi)$ extends to a holomorphic function on al
l of $\mathbb{C}$, and is bounded in every vertical strip.*
>
> **(ii) Functional equation.** *For every such $\chi$, there exists a complex n
umber $\lambda_\chi$ with $|\lambda_\chi|=1$ such that*
> $$\Lambda(s,\chi) \;=\; \lambda_\chi\,\Lambda(k-s,\overline{\chi}).$$
>
> **(iii) Boundedness / growth.** *The coefficients satisfy $a_n = O(n^{k/2+\var
epsilon})$ for every $\varepsilon>0$ (or equivalently, $L(s)$ has abscissa of ab
solute convergence $\leq (k+1)/2$).*
>
> *Then there exists a modular form*
> $$f(z) \;=\; \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n\, q^n, \qquad q = e^{2\pi i z},$$
> *of weight $k$ on $\Gamma_0(N)$, lying in the subspace $\mathcal{M}_k(\Gamma_0
(N))^0$, whose Fourier coefficients are exactly the $a_n$ (with $a_0$ determined
 by the residue of $L(s)$ at $s=k$, or $a_0=0$ if $L(s)$ is regular at $s=k$). I
n particular $L(s) = L(s,f)$ is the $L$-function of $f$.*

## Explanation

### 1. What the theorem says, conceptually

The **forward direction** is classical: if $f\in\mathcal{M}_k(\Gamma_0(N))$ is a
 cusp form (or more generally a modular form), then its $L$-function $L(s,f)=\su
m a_n n^{-s}$ and all its twists $L(s,f\otimes\chi)$ enjoy analytic continuation
, boundedness in vertical strips, and a functional equation of the standard shap
e relating $s\leftrightarrow k-s$. This follows from the modularity of $f$ via M
ellin transform of $f$ against additive twists, i.e., from the fact that $f$ sat
isfies the transformation law under $\Gamma_0(N)$.

**Weil's Converse Theorem** is the **reverse implication**: the analytic propert
ies (continuation + functional equation) of $L(s)$ **and all its twists by primi
tive characters of conductor coprime to $N$** are *sufficient* to force the exis
tence of a modular form $f$ with those coefficients. In other words, modularity
is *equivalent* to the family of functional equations of the twisted $L$-functio
ns.

### 2. Why twists are necessary

A single functional equation for $L(s)$ alone (the untwisted case $\chi=\chi_0$)
 is **not sufficient** to conclude modularity. Hecke's original converse theorem
 (for $N=1$) required only the untwisted functional equation, but that works bec
ause for $N=1$ the group $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ is generated by $T$ and $S$
, and the single functional equation encodes both transformations. For general $
\Gamma_0(N)$, the group is generated by $T$ together with matrices $\begin{pmatr
ix}a & b \\ c & d\end{pmatrix}$ with $N\mid c$, and the transformation laws for
$f$ under these additional generators are encoded by the **twisted** functional
equations. Specifically, the twist by a character $\chi$ modulo $m$ probes the b
ehavior of $f$ under matrices whose lower-left entry is divisible by $Nm$ (rough
ly), so requiring functional equations for *all* primitive $\chi$ of conductor c
oprime to $N$ gives enough transformation laws to pin down $f$ on all of $\Gamma
_0(N)$.

### 3. The role of the condition $\gcd(m,N)=1$

The restriction to characters of conductor $m$ with $\gcd(m,N)=1$ is natural: th
e conductor of the twisted $L$-function $L(s,f\otimes\chi)$ is $N\cdot m^2$ when
 $\gcd(m,N)=1$ (the "primitive" twisting regime). If $\gcd(m,N)>1$, the conducto
r is smaller (there is ramification interaction), and the functional equation ta
kes a different, more complicated form. Weil's theorem uses only the coprime twi
sts, and this turns out to be exactly the right amount of data: the coprime twis
ts suffice to recover the full $\Gamma_0(N)$ structure.

### 4. The subspace $\mathcal{M}_k(\Gamma_0(N))^0$

The theorem produces a form in $\mathcal{M}_k(\Gamma_0(N))^0$, not merely in $\m
athcal{M}_k(\Gamma_0(N))$. This is because the functional equation of conductor
$N$ (for the trivial twist) selects the "level-$N$ part" of the form — it rules
out oldforms coming from lower levels, which would have functional equations of
smaller conductor. More precisely:

- An oldform $f(dz)$ with $d\mid N$, $d>1$, has $L$-function $\sum a_n n^{-s}$ w
ith $a_n$ supported on the sublattice, and its functional equation has conductor
 $N/d$ (or $N/d^2$ depending on convention), not $N$. So the requirement that th
e **trivial twist** has functional equation of conductor exactly $N$ forces $f$
to be in the "new at level $N$" part, i.e., in $\mathcal{M}_k(\Gamma_0(N))^0$.

- The coprime twists then further constrain $f$ to satisfy the full $\Gamma_0(N)
$ transformation law, completing the identification.

### 5. Sketch of the proof strategy

The proof proceeds by constructing, from the coefficients $a_n$ and the twisted
$L$-functions, a function

$$f(z) = \sum_{n\geq 0} a_n\, q^n$$

and then **verifying the modular transformation law** $f\bigl(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\
bigr) = (cz+d)^k\, f(z)$ for all $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_
0(N)$.

The key tool is the **Mellin transform / inverse Mellin transform**:

$$f(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\sigma)} L(s)\,(2\pi z)^{-s}\,\Gamma(s)\,ds,$$

and similarly for the twists. The functional equation of $\Lambda(s,\chi)$ is eq
uivalent (via Mellin inversion) to a transformation law for the "twisted" theta-
like series

$$f_\chi(z) = \sum_{n\geq 1} a_n\,\chi(n)\, e^{2\pi i n z}.$$

Specifically, the functional equation $\Lambda(s,\chi)=\lambda_\chi\,\Lambda(k-s
,\bar\chi)$ translates, via the Gauss-sum relation between $\chi$ and its conjug
ate, into a relation of the form

$$f_\chi\!\left(-\frac{1}{N m^2 z}\right) = \lambda_\chi\cdot(\text{Gauss sum fa
ctor})\cdot (Nm^2 z)^{k}\, f_{\bar\chi}(z),$$

which is precisely the modular transformation law for $f$ under the matrix $\beg
in{pmatrix}0 & -1 \\ Nm^2 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ (up to the character twist). By vary
ing $\chi$ over all primitive characters of conductor $m$ coprime to $N$, and us
ing the orthogonality of characters to "untwist," one recovers the transformatio
n law of $f$ under a generating set of $\Gamma_0(N)$. The boundedness-in-vertica
l-strips condition ensures the Mellin integrals converge and that $f$ is holomor
phic (including at the cusps), ruling out poles that would correspond to Eisenst
ein series contributions or worse.

The holomorphy at the cusp $\infty$ (i.e., $f$ holomorphic on the upper half-pla
ne and at the cusp) follows from the holomorphy of $\Lambda(s,\chi)$; the behavi
or at the other cusps of $\Gamma_0(N)$ is controlled by the twisted functional e
quations. The constant term $a_0$ is determined by the possible pole of $L(s)$ a
t $s=k$: if $L(s)$ is regular at $s=k$, then $a_0=0$ and $f$ is a cusp form; if
$L(s)$ has a simple pole at $s=k$, then $a_0\neq 0$ and $f$ is a non-cuspidal mo
dular form (an Eisenstein-type contribution).

### 6. Significance and context

- **Hecke (1936)** proved the converse theorem for $N=1$ (level 1): analytic con
tinuation + functional equation of $L(s)$ alone $\Rightarrow$ $f\in\mathcal{M}_k
(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z}))$.

- **Weil (1953)** generalized this to arbitrary level $N$ by introducing the **t
wists**. The crucial insight is that for $\Gamma_0(N)$ with $N>1$, a single func
tional equation is insufficient, but the *family* of functional equations for al
l coprime twists is exactly the right substitute.

- Weil's Converse Theorem is a cornerstone of the **Langlands philosophy**: it s
hows that the analytic properties of $L$-functions (continuation, functional equ
ation) are *equivalent* to automorphy, at least for $\mathrm{GL}_2$ over $\mathb
b{Q}$. This paradigm extends (conjecturally) to $\mathrm{GL}_n$ and beyond, wher
e the role of Dirichlet character twists is played by twists by automorphic repr
esentations of auxiliary groups.

- The theorem was instrumental in the proof of **modularity of elliptic curves o
ver $\mathbb{Q}$** (the Shimura–Taniyama–Weil conjecture, proved by Wiles and Ta
ylor–Wiles for semistable curves, and in full generality by Breuil–Conrad–Diamon
d–Taylor): one shows that the Hasse–Weil $L$-function $L(E,s)$ and its twists sa
tisfy the requisite analytic properties, then invokes the converse theorem to co
nclude $E$ is modular.

- Modern refinements (e.g., by **Li**, **Cogdell–Piatetski-Shapiro**, **Bump–Fri
edberg–Hoffstein**) extend converse theorems to $\mathrm{GL}_n$ and relax the tw
isting requirements, but Weil's original $\mathrm{GL}_2$ theorem remains the fou
ndational result.

## Summary

**Weil's Converse Theorem** states that a Dirichlet series $L(s)=\sum a_n n^{-s}
$ with coefficients of polynomial growth is the $L$-function of a modular form $
f\in\mathcal{M}_k(\Gamma_0(N))^0$ of weight $k$ and level $N$ **if and only if**
 $L(s)$ and **all** its twists $L(s,\chi)$ by primitive Dirichlet characters $\c
hi$ of conductor coprime to $N$ admit analytic continuation to $\mathbb{C}$, are
 bounded in vertical strips, and satisfy the standard functional equation of con
ductor $N\cdot m^2$ relating $s$ and $k-s$. The twists are essential because the
y encode the transformation laws of $f$ under the full group $\Gamma_0(N)$, not
just under $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$. The theorem establishes that modularity
is equivalent to the analytic properties of the family of twisted $L$-functions,
 and is a foundational result in the Langlands program and in the proof of modul
arity of elliptic curves.
