## Proof

We determine the number of ordered pairs $(X, Y)$ of $n \times n$ matrices (over
 a field) satisfying $XY - YX = I_n$.

**Key observation: the trace of a commutator is always zero.**

For any two $n \times n$ matrices $X$ and $Y$, using the cyclic property of the
trace:
$$\operatorname{tr}(XY) = \operatorname{tr}(YX).$$

Therefore:
$$\operatorname{tr}(XY - YX) = \operatorname{tr}(XY) - \operatorname{tr}(YX) = 0
.$$

**On the other hand**, the trace of the identity matrix is:
$$\operatorname{tr}(I_n) = n.$$

**Applying the trace to both sides** of the equation $XY - YX = I_n$:
$$\operatorname{tr}(XY - YX) = \operatorname{tr}(I_n),$$
$$0 = n.$$

This is a contradiction whenever $n \geq 1$ (and in particular for every positiv
e integer $n$, which is the meaningful case for $n \times n$ matrices).

**Conclusion.** Since the trace identity forces $0 = n$, no pair $(X, Y)$ can sa
tisfy $XY - YX = I_n$. The set of ordered pairs is empty, so the number of such
pairs is:

$$\boxed{0}.$$

This is the classical **Wielandt–Jacobson trace argument**: the identity matrix
cannot be expressed as a commutator of finite-dimensional matrices, precisely be
cause the trace annihilates every commutator while the identity carries trace $n
 \neq 0$.
