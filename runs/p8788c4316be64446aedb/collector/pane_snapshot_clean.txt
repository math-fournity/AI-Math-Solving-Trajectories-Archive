## Proof

**Yes.** The solution $u = u(r)$ must be bounded on $0 \le r \le R$; it cannot t
ake an infinite value at $r = 0$. The boundedness follows from the **hypotheses*
* of Theorem 1, and the radial-decreasing **conclusion** then identifies the (fi
nite) maximum as occurring at the origin.

### 1. The hypotheses already force $u(0) < \infty$

Theorem 1 of Gidas–Ni–Nirenberg concerns a *classical* positive solution of
$$
\Delta u = f(u) \quad \text{in } B_R, \qquad u = 0 \quad \text{on } \partial B_R
, \qquad u > 0 \quad \text{in } B_R,
$$
with the standing regularity hypothesis
$$
u \in C^{2}(B_R) \cap C(\overline{B_R}).
$$

The origin $r = 0$ is an **interior point** of $B_R$. Since $u \in C^{2}(B_R)$,
in particular $u$ is continuous (indeed twice continuously differentiable) at $r
 = 0$. A continuous function takes a finite value at every point of its domain,
so
$$
u(0) < \infty.
$$

Equivalently, $\overline{B_R}$ is compact and $u \in C(\overline{B_R})$, so $u$
is bounded on $\overline{B_R}$ by the extreme-value theorem. Either way, $u$ can
not blow up at the origin.

### 2. The radial-decreasing conclusion gives the global bound

Theorem 1 asserts that $u$ depends only on $r = |x|$ and that
$$
u'(r) < 0 \quad \text{for } 0 < r < R.
$$
Hence $u$ is strictly decreasing in $r$, and its supremum over $[0,R]$ is attain
ed at $r = 0$:
$$
u(r) \le u(0) \quad \text{for all } r \in [0,R].
$$
Combining with step 1,
$$
u(r) \le u(0) < \infty \quad \text{for all } r \in [0,R],
$$
and since $u \ge 0$ (positivity hypothesis, with $u(R) = 0$),
$$
0 \le u(r) \le u(0) < \infty \quad \text{for all } r \in [0,R].
$$
Thus $u$ is bounded on $[0,R]$.

### 3. The conclusion alone would not suffice — the regularity hypothesis is ess
ential

It is worth noting that the radial-decreasing property **by itself** does *not*
imply boundedness. For example, $v(r) = 1/r$ is radial and strictly decreasing o
n $(0,R]$, yet $v(0) = +\infty$. What rules out such behavior is not the symmetr
y conclusion but the hypothesis $u \in C^{2}(B_R)$: a function with a singularit
y at the origin is not $C^{2}$ (not even defined) there, so it is excluded from
the class of solutions to which the theorem applies.

Moreover, the PDE itself is required to hold classically in all of $B_R$, includ
ing at $x = 0$. If $u$ blew up at $0$, the Laplacian $\Delta u$ could not be a f
inite, continuous function of $u$ there, contradicting $\Delta u = f(u)$ with $f
$ locally Lipschitz (hence finite on bounded sets). So the equation is inconsist
ent with an interior singularity.

### 4. Conclusion

Theorem 1 applies only to classical solutions $u \in C^{2}(B_R) \cap C(\overline
{B_R})$, which are necessarily finite (in fact smooth) at the interior point $r
= 0$. The theorem's conclusion that $u$ is radial and strictly decreasing then p
laces the global maximum at $r = 0$ and shows it is finite. Therefore:

> The solution $u = u(r)$ **must be bounded** on $0 \le r \le R$; it **cannot**
take an infinite value at $r = 0$.
