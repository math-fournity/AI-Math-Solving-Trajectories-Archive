## Proof

We construct a non-atomic probability measure $\mu$ on $[0,1]$ that is singular
with respect to Lebesgue measure $m$ and has **full support** ($\mu(U)>0$ for ev
ery open $U\subset[0,1]$). Then $f(x)=\mu([0,x])$ is the desired function.

### The Cantor measure

Let $C\subset[0,1]$ be the standard middle-thirds Cantor set and $\nu$ the Canto
r measure: the unique non-atomic probability measure supported on $C$. Key prope
rties:
- $\nu$ is non-atomic,
- $\nu\perp m$ (supported on $C$, which has $m(C)=0$),
- $\nu$ has full support on $C$: every nonempty relatively open subset of $C$ ha
s positive $\nu$-measure.

The complement $[0,1]\setminus C=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}G_j^{(0)}$ is a countable
 union of disjoint open intervals (the "gaps" of $C$), with all gap lengths $\le
 1/3$.

### Recursive construction

We build measures $\mu_0,\mu_1,\mu_2,\ldots$ and supports $S_0,S_1,S_2,\ldots$

**Stage 0.** Set $S_0=C$ and $\mu_0=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\nu$. Then $\mu_0$ has mass $1
/2$, is non-atomic and singular, supported on $S_0$ (measure $0$). The gaps of $
S^{(0)}:=S_0$ have maximum length $1/3$.

**Stage $k$ ($k\ge 1$).** The set $S^{(k-1)}=S_0\cup\cdots\cup S_{k-1}$ is close
d with $m(S^{(k-1)})=0$. Its complement $[0,1]\setminus S^{(k-1)}=\bigcup_j G_j^
{(k)}$ is a countable union of disjoint open intervals. For each gap $G_j^{(k)}=
(a,b)$, let $\psi_j^{(k)}:[0,1]\to[a,b]$ be the affine bijection and $C_j^{(k)}=
\psi_j^{(k)}(C)$ the Cantor set inside $[a,b]$. Define

$$\mu_k = \frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\sum_j w_j^{(k)}\cdot(\psi_j^{(k)})_{\#}\nu, \qquad w
_j^{(k)}>0,\ \sum_j w_j^{(k)}=1,$$

and $S_k=\bigcup_j C_j^{(k)}$.

Then $\mu_k$ has mass $1/2^{k+1}$, is non-atomic and singular (supported on $S_k
$, a countable union of Cantor sets). Since each $C_j^{(k)}$ lies in an interval
 of length $\le(1/3)^k$, its gaps have length $\le(1/3)^{k+1}$. Thus **all gaps
of $S^{(k)}$ have length $\le(1/3)^{k+1}$**.

**The measure.** Define $\mu=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mu_k$. Total mass: $\sum_{k=0}^
{\infty}1/2^{k+1}=1$, so $\mu$ is a probability measure.

### Verification of properties

**$\mu$ is non-atomic.** Each $\mu_k$ is a countable sum of pushforwards of the
non-atomic measure $\nu$, hence non-atomic. A countable sum of non-atomic measur
es is non-atomic. $\checkmark$

**$\mu$ is singular.** $\mu$ is supported on $S=\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}S_k$. Each
 $S_k$ is a countable union of Cantor sets (measure $0$), so $m(S)=0$. Thus $\mu
\perp m$. $\checkmark$

**$\mu$ has full support.** Let $(a,b)\subset[0,1]$ be open with $b-a>0$. Choose
 $k$ with $(1/3)^{k+1}<b-a$. Every gap of $S^{(k)}$ has length $\le(1/3)^{k+1}<b
-a$, so $(a,b)$ cannot lie inside any single gap. Hence $(a,b)\cap S^{(k)}\neq\e
mptyset$.

- If $(a,b)\cap S_0\neq\emptyset$: then $(a,b)\cap C\neq\emptyset$, and since $\
nu$ has full support on $C$, $\nu((a,b))>0$, giving $\mu((a,b))\ge\tfrac{1}{2}\n
u((a,b))>0$.
- If $(a,b)\cap S_j\neq\emptyset$ for some $j\ge 1$: then $(a,b)$ intersects som
e Cantor set $C'$ placed at stage $j$. The measure $(\psi)_{\#}\nu$ has full sup
port on $C'$, and $(a,b)\cap C'$ is a nonempty relatively open subset of $C'$, s
o $\mu_j((a,b))>0$, hence $\mu((a,b))>0$.

In all cases $\mu((a,b))>0$. $\checkmark$

### The function and its properties

Define $f:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ by $f(x)=\mu([0,x])$.

**Continuity.** $\mu$ is non-atomic, so its distribution function $f$ is continu
ous: for any $x$, $|f(x+h)-f(x)|=\mu((x,x+h])\to 0$ as $h\to 0$ since $\mu(\{x\}
)=0$. $\checkmark$

**Strict monotonicity.** For $0\le x<y\le 1$,
$$f(y)-f(x)=\mu((x,y])\ge\mu((x,y))>0,$$
since $(x,y)$ is open and $\mu$ has full support. $\checkmark$

**Singularity.** Since $\mu\perp m$, by the Lebesgue–Radon–Nikodym theorem the a
bsolutely continuous part of $\mu$ is zero. The derivative of the distribution f
unction satisfies $f'(x)=\frac{d\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}}{dm}(x)=0$ for $m$-a.e. $x\in[
0,1]$. Hence $f'=0$ a.e. and $f$ is singular. $\checkmark$

### Conclusion

The function $f(x)=\mu([0,x])$, where $\mu$ is the measure constructed above, is
 strictly increasing, continuous, and singular on $[0,1]$. $\blacksquare$
