## Proof

**Step 1: Trigonometric reformulation.**

Using the identity $\sin^3 x = \frac{3\sin x - \sin 3x}{4}$, we write

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^3 x}{x^3}\,dx = \frac{1}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{\
infty}\frac{3\sin x - \sin 3x}{x^3}\,dx = \frac{1}{4}\,\operatorname{Im}\!\int_{
-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{3e^{ix}-e^{3ix}}{x^3}\,dx.$$

Note that $\frac{\sin^3 x}{x^3}\to 1$ as $x\to 0$ (removable singularity) and $|
\frac{\sin^3 x}{x^3}|\le \frac{1}{|x|^3}$ for large $|x|$, so the integral conve
rges absolutely.

**Step 2: Laurent expansion at the origin.**

Set $f(z)=\frac{3e^{iz}-e^{3iz}}{z^3}$. Expanding the exponentials:

$$3e^{iz}=3+3iz-\tfrac{3}{2}z^2-\tfrac{i}{2}z^3+\cdots,\qquad e^{3iz}=1+3iz-\tfr
ac{9}{2}z^2-\tfrac{9i}{2}z^3+\cdots$$

Subtracting:

$$3e^{iz}-e^{3iz}=2+0\cdot z+3z^2+4iz^3+\cdots$$

Hence

$$f(z)=\frac{2}{z^3}+\frac{0}{z^2}+\frac{3}{z}+4i+\cdots$$

The residue at $z=0$ is $\operatorname{Res}(f,0)=3$ (the coefficient of $1/z$).

**Step 3: Contour selection.**

Integrate $f(z)$ over the standard indented semicircular contour in the **upper*
* half-plane: along the real axis from $-R$ to $-\varepsilon$, a small clockwise
 semicircle $C_\varepsilon$ of radius $\varepsilon$ (upper half-plane) from $-\v
arepsilon$ to $+\varepsilon$, along the real axis from $\varepsilon$ to $R$, and
 a large counterclockwise semicircle $C_R$ of radius $R$ from $R$ back to $-R$.

Since $f$ is analytic inside this contour (the pole at $0$ is excluded by the in
dentation), Cauchy's theorem gives:

$$\operatorname{P.V.}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx+\int_{C_\varepsilon}f(z)\
,dz+\int_{C_R}f(z)\,dz=0.$$

**Step 4: The large arc $C_R$ vanishes.**

For $z=Re^{i\theta}$ with $0\le\theta\le\pi$ (upper half-plane, $\operatorname{I
m}z\ge 0$):

$$|e^{iz}|=e^{-\operatorname{Im}z}\le 1,\qquad |e^{3iz}|=e^{-3\operatorname{Im}z
}\le 1.$$

Therefore $|f(z)|\le\frac{3+1}{R^3}=\frac{4}{R^3}$, and

$$\left|\int_{C_R}f(z)\,dz\right|\le \frac{4}{R^3}\cdot\pi R=\frac{4\pi}{R^2}\xr
ightarrow{R\to\infty}0.$$

**Step 5: The small arc $C_\varepsilon$.**

Parametrize $C_\varepsilon$: $z=\varepsilon e^{i\theta}$, $\theta$ from $\pi$ to
 $0$ (clockwise). Using the Laurent expansion $f(z)=\frac{2}{z^3}+\frac{3}{z}+g(
z)$ with $g$ analytic (hence bounded near $0$):

- **$1/z^3$ term:** $\displaystyle\int_{C_\varepsilon}\frac{2}{z^3}\,dz=\frac{2i
}{\varepsilon^2}\int_\pi^0 e^{-2i\theta}\,d\theta=\frac{2i}{\varepsilon^2}\cdot\
frac{e^{-2i\theta}}{-2i}\bigg|_\pi^0=\frac{2i}{\varepsilon^2}\cdot\frac{1-1}{-2i
}=0.$

- **$1/z$ term:** $\displaystyle\int_{C_\varepsilon}\frac{3}{z}\,dz=3i\int_\pi^0
 d\theta=3i(0-\pi)=-3\pi i.$

- **Analytic part:** $\displaystyle\int_{C_\varepsilon}g(z)\,dz\to 0$ as $\varep
silon\to 0$ since $g$ is bounded and the arc length $\to 0$.

Thus $\displaystyle\int_{C_\varepsilon}f(z)\,dz\to -3\pi i$ as $\varepsilon\to 0
$.

**Step 6: Assemble the result.**

$$\operatorname{P.V.}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{3e^{ix}-e^{3ix}}{x^3}\,dx-3\
pi i+0=0 \implies \operatorname{P.V.}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{3e^{ix}-e^{3
ix}}{x^3}\,dx=3\pi i.$$

Taking imaginary parts:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{3\sin x-\sin 3x}{x^3}\,dx=3\pi.$$

This integral converges in the ordinary (non-PV) sense because $\frac{3\sin x-\s
in 3x}{x^3}=\frac{4\sin^3 x}{x^3}$ is continuous at $x=0$ (limit $=4$) and $O(1/
x^3)$ at infinity. (The real part $\frac{3\cos x-\cos 3x}{x^3}$ has an odd $2/x^
3$ singularity whose PV is $0$, consistent with $\operatorname{Re}(3\pi i)=0$.)

**Step 7: Final answer.**

$$\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^3 x}{x^3}\,dx=\frac{1}{4}\cdot 3\pi=\
frac{3\pi}{4}.}$$
