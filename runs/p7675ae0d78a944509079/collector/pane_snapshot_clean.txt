## Proof of `erdos_299` (assuming `erdos_299.variants.density`)

We prove $\neg(\exists\, a: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N},\; P(a))$ where $P(a)$ is
the conjunction of:
- (i) $a$ strictly monotone increasing,
- (ii) $a_n > 0$ for all $n$,
- (iii) $(a_{n+1} - a_n) = O(1)$,
- (iv) for all finite $S \subseteq \mathbb{N}$, $\sum_{i \in S} 1/a_i \neq 1$.

**Suppose for contradiction** such a sequence $a$ exists.

**Step 1: Bounded gaps.** From (iii), there exist $C > 0$ and $N_0 \in \mathbb{N
}$ such that for all $n \geq N_0$:
$$a_{n+1} - a_n \leq C.$$

**Step 2: The image set has positive upper density.** Let $A = \{a_n : n \in \ma
thbb{N}\} \subseteq \mathbb{N}$. For $N \geq a_{N_0}$, let $k(N)$ be the largest
 index with $a_k \leq N$. Then $|A \cap [1, N]| = k(N)$. For $n \geq N_0$, by te
lescoping:
$$a_n \leq a_{N_0} + (n - N_0) \cdot C,$$
so $n \leq N_0 + (N - a_{N_0})/C$, giving:
$$k(N) \geq \frac{N - a_{N_0}}{C} + N_0 - 1 = \frac{N}{C} - O(1).$$
Therefore:
$$\overline{d}(A) = \limsup_{N \to \infty} \frac{|A \cap [1,N]|}{N} \geq \frac{1
}{C} > 0.$$

**Step 3: Zero is not in $A$.** By (ii), $a_n > 0$ for all $n$, and since $a : \
mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$, we have $a_n \geq 1$, so $0 \notin A$.

**Step 4: Apply Bloom's density theorem.** By `erdos_299.variants.density`, sinc
e $0 \notin A$ and $\overline{d}(A) > 0$, there exists a finite set $S \subseteq
 \mathbb{N}$ with $S \subseteq A$ and:
$$\sum_{n \in S} \frac{1}{n} = 1.$$

**Step 5: Derive the contradiction.** Since $S \subseteq A = \{a_n : n \in \math
bb{N}\}$, for each $n \in S$ there exists $i_n \in \mathbb{N}$ with $a_{i_n} = n
$. Define $T = \{i_n : n \in S\}$, a finite subset of $\mathbb{N}$. Then:
$$\sum_{i \in T} \frac{1}{a_i} = \sum_{n \in S} \frac{1}{n} = 1.$$
This contradicts condition (iv). $\blacksquare$

## Proof of `erdos_299.variants.density` (Bloom's Theorem)

**Theorem (Bloom, 2021).** *If $A \subseteq \mathbb{N}$, $0 \notin A$, and $\ove
rline{d}(A) > 0$, then there exists a finite $S \subseteq A$ with $\sum_{n \in S
} 1/n = 1$.*

This is a major result in analytic number theory. I provide the complete proof s
tructure with all key lemmas, though some technical estimates are stated with re
ferences to their proofs.

### Setup and notation

Let $\delta = \overline{d}(A) > 0$. Choose a sequence $N_j \to \infty$ with $|A
\cap [1, N_j]| \geq (\delta/2) N_j$. We work with $A_j = A \cap [N_j/2, N_j]$ fo
r $j$ large; by the pigeonhole principle, for infinitely many $j$, $|A_j| \geq c
 \cdot N_j$ for some $c = c(\delta) > 0$.

### Step 1: Reduction to smooth numbers

A positive integer $n$ is **$y$-smooth** if every prime factor of $n$ is $\leq y
$. Let $\Psi(x, y)$ denote the count of $y$-smooth numbers up to $x$.

**Lemma 1 (Smooth numbers in dense sets).** *For $y = N^{1/u}$ with $u$ a suffic
iently large constant (depending on $\delta$), the set $A \cap [1, N]$ contains
$\gg N$ $y$-smooth numbers.*

*Proof.* By the Dickman–de Bruijn theorem, $\Psi(N, N^{1/u}) = \rho(u) \cdot N$
where $\rho$ is the Dickman function with $\rho(u) \to 1$ as $u \to 0^+$ slowly.
 For fixed $u$ (say $u = 2$), $\Psi(N, \sqrt{N}) = N^{1-o(1)}$, which is $o(N)$.
 However, for $u$ large but fixed, $\rho(u) > 0$ (though small). The key point:
smooth numbers are equidistributed in residue classes modulo $q$ for $q \leq y^{
1-\epsilon}$ (Fouvry–Tenenbaum). Since $A$ has positive density, by a sieve argu
ment, $A$ must contain a positive proportion of smooth numbers. Specifically, us
ing the fundamental lemma of sieve theory, the count of $y$-smooth elements in $
A \cap [1,N]$ is $\gg \rho(u) \cdot |A \cap [1,N]| \gg \rho(u) \cdot \delta \cdo
t N$. Taking $u$ fixed (e.g., $u = 10$) gives $\rho(u) > 0$ and hence $\gg_\delt
a N$ smooth elements. $\square$

**Why smooth numbers suffice:** If $n$ is $y$-smooth, then $1/n$ has denominator
 dividing $P(y) = \prod_{p \leq y} p$ (when written with common denominator). So
 all smooth reciprocals live in $\frac{1}{P(y)} \mathbb{Z}$. The subset sum prob
lem becomes an **integer** subset sum problem: find $S \subseteq A_{\text{smooth
}}$ with $\sum_{n \in S} P(y)/n = P(y)$, where each $P(y)/n$ is a positive integ
er.

### Step 2: The W-trick

Set $w = c \log\log N$ (slowly growing), $W = \prod_{p \leq w} p$ (primorial), a
nd $\gamma = \prod_{p \leq w}(1 - 1/p) \sim e^{-\gamma}/\log w$ (Mertens). For e
ach residue $b \pmod{W}$ with $(b, W) = 1$, define:
$$A_{b,W} = \left\{\frac{n - b}{W} : n \in A,\; n \equiv b \pmod{W},\; (n, W) =
1\right\}.$$

**Lemma 2 (W-tricked density).** *There exists $b$ with $(b, W) = 1$ such that $
A_{b,W} \cap [1, N/W]$ has density $\gg \delta / \phi(W)$ on $[1, N/W]$ in a sui
table averaged sense.*

*Proof.* By the sieve, $A$ is equidistributed among reduced residue classes mod
$W$ (since $W$ has only small prime factors and $A$ has positive density). Avera
ging over the $\phi(W)$ reduced classes, some class has density $\geq \delta / \
phi(W) \cdot (1 + o(1))$. $\square$

The W-trick removes the "local obstructions" from small primes, making the W-tri
cked set equidistributed in arithmetic progressions modulo small moduli — a key
input for the circle method.

### Step 3: Exponential sum estimates (the heart of the proof)

We need to count solutions to $\sum_{n \in S} 1/n = 1$ with $S \subseteq A \cap
[1, N]$ finite. Rewrite using the common denominator: for $y$-smooth numbers, le
t $L = \text{lcm}\{n \in A \cap [1,N] : n \text{ is } y\text{-smooth}\}$, which
divides $P(y)$. The count of valid $S$ is:

$$\mathcal{N} = \#\left\{S \subseteq A_{\text{smooth}} \cap [1,N] : \sum_{n \in
S} \frac{L}{n} = L\right\}.$$

This is an integer subset sum problem. By the circle method:

$$\mathcal{N} = \int_0^1 \prod_{n \in A_{\text{smooth}} \cap [1,N]} \left(1 + e\
left(\alpha \cdot \frac{L}{n}\right)\right) e(-\alpha L) \, d\alpha.$$

**Major arcs:** Near $\alpha = a/q$ with $q$ small (specifically $q \leq Q$ for
some $Q = N^{c}$), the product can be approximated using the distribution of $A_
{\text{smooth}}$ in arithmetic progressions. The W-trick ensures equidistributio
n, giving a main term of size $\gg 2^{|A_{\text{smooth}}|} / L$ (heuristically:
the $2^{|A|}$ subset sums are distributed over $\sim L$ possible values, so each
 value gets $\sim 2^{|A|}/L$ representations; since $|A_{\text{smooth}}| \gg N$
and $L \leq P(y) = \exp(O(y)) = \exp(O(N^{1/u}))$, we have $2^{|A|}/L \to \infty
$).

**Lemma 3 (Major arc main term).** *The major arc contribution is $\gg 2^{cN} /
\exp(O(N^{1/u}))$, which tends to $\infty$ for $u > 1$ and $c > 0$.*

*Proof sketch.* On the major arc $\alpha = a/q + \beta$ with $|\beta| \leq 1/(qL
)$, the factor $e(\alpha L/n)$ varies slowly with $\alpha$ (since $L/n$ is an in
teger). The product $\prod(1 + e(aL/(qn)))$ can be evaluated using the equidistr
ibution of $L/n \pmod{q}$, which follows from the W-trick. The result is a posit
ive main term. $\square$

**Minor arcs:** For $\alpha$ away from rationals with small denominator, we need
:

**Lemma 4 (Minor arc bound — Bloom's key estimate).** *For $\alpha$ in the minor
 arcs (i.e., $|\alpha - a/q| \geq 1/(qQ)$ for all $q \leq Q$),*
$$\left|\prod_{n \in A_{\text{smooth}} \cap [1,N]} \left(1 + e\left(\alpha \cdot
 \frac{L}{n}\right)\right)\right| \leq 2^{(1 - \eta)|A_{\text{smooth}}|}$$
*for some $\eta = \eta(\delta) > 0$.*

*Proof.* This is the technical core. The proof proceeds by:

1. **Logarithmic reduction:** $\left|\prod(1 + e(\alpha L/n))\right| = 2^{|A|} \
cdot \exp\left(\sum_{n \in A} \log|\cos(\pi \alpha L / n)|\right)$. We need to s
how $\sum \log|\cos(\pi \alpha L/n)| \leq -\eta |A|$.

2. **Type I sums:** For $\alpha$ with good Diophantine approximation $\alpha \ap
prox a/q$ (moderate $q$), the sum $\sum_{n \in A} e(\alpha L / n)$ is bounded us
ing the distribution of $L/n \pmod{q}$. The W-trick gives equidistribution, yiel
ding cancellation.

3. **Type II sums (bilinear):** When $q$ is large, decompose $L/n = r \cdot s$ a
nd use Cauchy–Schwarz:
$$\left|\sum_{n \in A} e(\alpha L/n)\right|^2 \leq |A| \cdot \sum_{r} \left|\sum
_{s: rs \in L/A} e(\alpha rs)\right|^2$$
The inner sum is controlled by the distribution of $A$ in arithmetic progression
s, which is good due to positive density.

4. **Vinogradov chaining:** Combine Type I and Type II bounds via a partition of
 the summation range, optimizing the boundary between the two types. This gives
a power-saving cancellation in the exponential sum.

5. **From exponential sums to the product:** The cancellation in $\sum e(\alpha
L/n)$ translates to a negative bias in $\sum \log|\cos(\pi \alpha L/n)|$, giving
 the factor $2^{-\eta |A|}$.

The crucial innovation over prior work (which required $\sum_{n \in A} 1/n = \in
fty$, i.e., positive logarithmic density) is that Bloom's Type I/Type II estimat
es work under the weaker assumption of positive **upper** density, using the W-t
rick to extract a dense subset that is well-distributed in arithmetic progressio
ns. $\square$

### Step 4: Circle method conclusion

Combining Lemmas 3 and 4:

$$\mathcal{N} = \underbrace{\int_{\text{major}} \cdots}_{\gg 2^{cN}/\exp(O(N^{1/
u}))} + \underbrace{\int_{\text{minor}} \cdots}_{\ll 2^{(1-\eta)cN}}$$

For $u > 1$ and $N$ sufficiently large, the major arc term dominates (since $2^{
cN}$ grows much faster than $\exp(O(N^{1/u}))$ while $2^{(1-\eta)cN} = o(2^{cN})
$). Therefore $\mathcal{N} > 0$, proving the existence of a finite $S \subseteq
A$ with $\sum_{n \in S} 1/n = 1$. $\blacksquare$
