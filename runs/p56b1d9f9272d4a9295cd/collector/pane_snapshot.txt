# The Relationship Between Compactness and Continuity for Linear Operators

## Main Theorem

**Theorem.** Let $X$ and $Y$ be normed linear spaces over $\mathbb{R}$ (or $\mat
hbb{C}$), and let $T: X \to Y$ be a **linear** operator. If $T$ is compact, then
 $T$ is continuous (equivalently, bounded).

Recall that $T$ is *compact* if it maps bounded subsets of $X$ to relatively com
pact subsets of $Y$; equivalently, for every bounded sequence $(x_n)$ in $X$, th
e sequence $(Tx_n)$ has a convergent subsequence in $Y$.

## Proof

We prove the contrapositive: if $T$ is not bounded, then $T$ is not compact.

Suppose $T$ is not bounded. Then
$$\sup_{\|x\| \le 1} \|Tx\| = +\infty,$$
so there exists a sequence $(x_n)$ in $X$ with $\|x_n\| \le 1$ for all $n$ and $
\|Tx_n\| \to \infty$.

Since each $x_n$ lies in the closed unit ball $B = \{x \in X : \|x\| \le 1\}$, t
he images $Tx_n$ all lie in $T(B)$. If $T$ were compact, then $\overline{T(B)}$
would be compact in $Y$, and every sequence in $T(B)$ would admit a convergent (
hence bounded) subsequence.

However, $\|Tx_n\| \to \infty$ implies that **no** subsequence of $(Tx_n)$ is bo
unded: for any subsequence $(Tx_{n_k})$ we still have $\|Tx_{n_k}\| \to \infty$.
 A convergent sequence in a normed space must be bounded, so no subsequence of $
(Tx_n)$ can converge.

This contradicts the compactness of $T$. Therefore $T$ must be bounded, and a bo
unded linear operator between normed spaces is continuous. $\blacksquare$

## The Converse Is False in General

Continuity does **not** imply compactness in infinite-dimensional settings.

**Counterexample.** Let $X = Y = \ell^2$ and let $T = I$ be the identity operato
r. Then $I$ is clearly continuous (it is bounded with $\|I\| = 1$). However, $I$
 is not compact: the sequence $e_n = (0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots)$ (with $1$ in the $n$
-th position) satisfies $\|e_n\| = 1$, yet $\|e_n - e_m\| = \sqrt{2}$ for $n \ne
 m$, so $(e_n)$ has no convergent subsequence. Thus the closed unit ball is not
compact, and $I$ is not compact.

## When the Converse Holds

The converse *does* hold under finite-dimensionality conditions:

**Proposition.** Let $T: X \to Y$ be a bounded linear operator between normed sp
aces.

1. If $Y$ is finite-dimensional, then $T$ is compact.
2. If $X$ is finite-dimensional, then $T$ is continuous (every linear map out of
 a finite-dimensional normed space is bounded), and $T$ is compact if and only i
f $Y$ is finite-dimensional (or more generally, if $T$ has finite rank and $Y$ s
upports relative compactness of bounded sets—automatic when $\dim Y < \infty$).

*Proof of (1).* A bounded linear operator maps bounded sets to bounded sets. In
a finite-dimensional normed space, the Heine–Borel property holds: closed and bo
unded sets are compact. Hence $T$ maps bounded sets to relatively compact sets,
i.e., $T$ is compact. $\blacksquare$

*Proof of (2) (continuity).* On a finite-dimensional normed space, all norms are
 equivalent, so we may use any convenient norm. Writing $x = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha
_i e_i$ in a basis, linearity gives $Tx = \sum \alpha_i Te_i$, and $\|Tx\| \le (
\max_i \|Te_i\|) \sum |\alpha_i| \le C\|x\|$. Hence $T$ is bounded. $\blacksquar
e$

## Summary

For linear operators $T: X \to Y$ between normed spaces:

| Property | Implication |
|---|---|
| $T$ compact | $\Longrightarrow$ $T$ continuous (always) |
| $T$ continuous | $\Longrightarrow$ $T$ compact (only if $\dim Y < \infty$, or
$T$ finite-rank with appropriate codomain) |
| $\dim X, \dim Y < \infty$ | compact $\iff$ continuous (all linear maps are bot
h) |

The essential reason compactness is stronger than continuity is that compactness
 demands **sequential precompactness of the image of the unit ball**, whereas co
ntinuity only demands that the image of the unit ball be **bounded**. In infinit
e dimensions, boundedness is strictly weaker than precompactness (Riesz's lemma)
, which is exactly the gap between the two notions.
