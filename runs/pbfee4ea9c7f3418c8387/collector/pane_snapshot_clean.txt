## Proof

We prove by contradiction. Recall that $-\infty$ is an accumulation point of $(a
_n)$ if and only if: for every $M \in \mathbb{R}$ and every $N \in \mathbb{N}$,
there exists $n \geq N$ with $a_n < M$.

**Assume** that $-\infty$ is **not** an accumulation point of $(a_n)$. Then ther
e exist $M_0 \in \mathbb{R}$ and $N_0 \in \mathbb{N}$ such that

$$a_n \geq M_0 \quad \text{for all } n \geq N_0. \tag{$\star$}$$

**Claim.** Every accumulation point $b \in \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}$ of $(a_n
)$ satisfies $b \geq M_0$.

*Proof of Claim.* Since accumulation points are invariant under changing finitel
y many terms, the accumulation points of $(a_n)$ coincide with those of the tail
 $(a_n)_{n \geq N_0}$, which by $(\star)$ is bounded below by $M_0$.

- $b = -\infty$ is impossible: by $(\star)$, no term of the tail satisfies $a_n
< M_0$, so taking $M = M_0$ in the definition shows $-\infty$ is not an accumula
tion point of the tail.

- $b \in \mathbb{R}$ with $b < M_0$ is impossible: set $\varepsilon = \frac{M_0
- b}{2} > 0$. If $|a_n - b| < \varepsilon$ for some $n \geq N_0$, then $a_n < b
+ \varepsilon = \frac{b + M_0}{2} < M_0$, contradicting $(\star)$. Hence $b$ can
not be an accumulation point.

Therefore every accumulation point $b \in \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}$ satisfies
 $b \geq M_0$. $\square$

**Applying the hypothesis.** Set $r = M_0$. By the hypothesis of the problem, th
ere exists an accumulation point $b \in \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}$ of $(a_n)$
with $b < M_0$. But the Claim forces $b \geq M_0$. This is a contradiction.

The contradiction arose from assuming that $-\infty$ is not an accumulation poin
t. Hence $-\infty$ **is** an accumulation point of $(a_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$. $
\blacksquare$
