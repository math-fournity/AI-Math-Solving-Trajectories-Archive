# Verification of the Spectral Projection Formula

## Setup and Statement

Let $A$ be a bounded linear operator on a complex Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$, w
ith spectrum $\sigma(A) \subset \mathbb{C}$. Let $\Delta \subseteq \sigma(A)$ be
 a clopen (relatively open and closed) subset of $\sigma(A)$, and let $\gamma$ b
e a positively-oriented, piecewise-smooth Jordan curve (or finite union of such)
 in the resolvent set $\rho(A) = \mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(A)$ that encloses $\D
elta$ and excludes $\sigma(A)\setminus\Delta$. Define

$$P \;=\; \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}(z\mathbb{1}-A)^{-1}\,dz.$$

We verify that $P$ is a well-defined bounded operator satisfying:

1. **$P$ is a projection**: $P^2 = P$ and $P^* = P$ (when $A$ is normal).
2. **$P$ commutes with $A$**: $AP = PA$.
3. **$P$ is the spectral projection onto the invariant subspace associated with
$\Delta$**: $\sigma(A|_{P\mathcal{H}}) = \Delta$ and $\sigma(A|_{(\mathbb{1}-P)\
mathcal{H}}) = \sigma(A)\setminus\Delta$.

---

## Step 1. Well-definedness and basic properties

The **resolvent** $R(z) := (z\mathbb{1}-A)^{-1}$ is an analytic $\mathcal{B}(\ma
thcal{H})$-valued function on $\rho(A)$. Since $\gamma \subset \rho(A)$ is compa
ct and $R$ is continuous (hence bounded) on a neighborhood of $\gamma$, the inte
gral converges in operator norm, defining $P \in \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$.

**$P$ is independent of the contour.** If $\gamma_1, \gamma_2$ are two admissibl
e contours enclosing the same $\Delta$, by Cauchy's theorem applied to the analy
tic $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$-valued function $R(z)$ on the annular region betw
een them (where $R$ is holomorphic),

$$\int_{\gamma_1} R(z)\,dz = \int_{\gamma_2} R(z)\,dz.$$

Hence $P$ depends only on $\Delta$, not on the particular contour.

---

## Step 2. The resolvent identity

The fundamental **resolvent identity** is:

$$R(z) - R(w) = (z-w)\,R(z)\,R(w), \qquad z,w \in \rho(A). \tag{1}$$

*Proof.* $(z\mathbb{1}-A) - (w\mathbb{1}-A) = (z-w)\mathbb{1}$. Multiplying $(z\
mathbb{1}-A)^{-1}$ on the left and $(w\mathbb{1}-A)^{-1}$ on the right:

$$R(w) - R(z) = (z-w)\,R(z)\,R(w),$$

which is (1) up to sign rearrangement. ∎

---

## Step 3. Idempotence: $P^2 = P$

This is the central computation. Choose two contours:
- $\gamma_1$ enclosing $\Delta$ (used to define $P$),

utside $\gamma_1$), still within $\rho(A)$ and excluding $\sigma(A)\setminus\Del
ta$.

Then

$$P^2 = \left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^2 \int_{\gamma_2}\int_{\gamma_1} R(z)\,R(w

By the resolvent identity (1), $R(z)R(w) = \frac{R(z)-R(w)}{z-w}$ (for $z\neq w$
). Substituting:

$$P^2 = \left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^2 \int_{\gamma_2}\int_{\gamma_1} \frac{R(z
)-R(w)}{z-w}\,dw\,dz.$$

$$P^2 = \left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^2 \left[\int_{\gamma_2} R(z)\left(\int_{\g
amma_1}\frac{dw}{z-w}\right)dz \;-\; \int_{\gamma_1} R(w)\left(\int_{\gamma_2}\f
rac{dz}{z-w}\right)dw\right]. \tag{2}$$

$$\int_{\gamma_1}\frac{dw}{z-w} = -\oint_{\gamma_1}\frac{dw}{w-z} = -2\pi i \cdo
t n(\gamma_1, z) = 0, \qquad z \in \gamma_2, \tag{3}$$

## Step 4. $P$ commutes with $A$

Since $A(z\mathbb{1}-A) = (z\mathbb{1}-A)A$, multiplying by $R(z)$ on both sides
 gives $AR(z) = R(z)A$ for all $z\in\rho(A)$. Integrating:

$$AP = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_\gamma A\,R(z)\,dz = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_\gamma R(z
)\,A\,dz = PA. \quad\checkmark$$

---

## Step 5. Self-adjointness when $A$ is normal

If $A$ is normal ($AA^*=A^*A$), then $R(z)^* = (\bar z\,\mathbb{1}-A^*)^{-1}$. T
aking $\gamma$ symmetric under conjugation (enclosing $\Delta$ and $\bar\Delta$
appropriately, or $\Delta$ conjugation-invariant), one shows $P^* = P$. In the g
eneral (non-normal) case, $P$ is idempotent but not necessarily self-adjoint; it
 is an **oblique** projection (Riesz projection).

---

## Step 6. $P$ is the spectral projection onto the $\Delta$-invariant subspace

Since $P^2=P$ and $AP=PA$, the range $P\mathcal{H}$ and kernel $(\mathbb{1}-P)\m
athcal{H}$ are **$A$-invariant** subspaces, and $\mathcal{H} = P\mathcal{H}\oplu
s(\mathbb{1}-P)\mathcal{H}$.

**Claim.** $\sigma(A|_{P\mathcal{H}}) = \Delta$ and $\sigma(A|_{(\mathbb{1}-P)\m
athcal{H}}) = \sigma(A)\setminus\Delta$.

*Proof sketch.* For $\lambda \in \rho(A)$, the resolvent $R(\lambda)$ commutes w
ith $P$, so $R(\lambda)$ restricts to both $P\mathcal{H}$ and $(\mathbb{1}-P)\ma
thcal{H}$. One shows:

- For $\lambda \notin \Delta$: the function $z\mapsto R(z)$ is analytic on a nei
ghborhood of $\overline{\Delta}$, and the contour $\gamma$ can be shrunk to a po
int inside the resolvent set near $\Delta$... More precisely, for $\lambda\notin
\Delta$ but $\lambda\in\sigma(A)\setminus\Delta$, the resolvent $R(z)$ has its s
ingularity at $\lambda$ **outside** $\gamma$, so $(\lambda\mathbb{1}-A)|_{P\math
cal{H}}$ is invertible with inverse $\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_\gamma \frac{R(z)}{\la
mbda - z}dz$ (using the resolvent identity and the fact that the singularity of
$R(z)$ at points of $\Delta$ is enclosed while $\lambda$ is not). Hence $\lambda
 \in \rho(A|_{P\mathcal{H}})$.

- For $\lambda \in \Delta$: $(\lambda\mathbb{1}-A)|_{P\mathcal{H}}$ is **not** i
nvertible. If it were, say with inverse $S$, then one could construct a bounded
inverse of $(\lambda\mathbb{1}-A)$ on all of $\mathcal{H}$ by $SP \oplus R(\lamb
da)(\mathbb{1}-P)$, contradicting $\lambda\in\sigma(A)$. (Here $R(\lambda)$ exis
ts on $(\mathbb{1}-P)\mathcal{H}$ because $\lambda\in\Delta$ is separated from $
\sigma(A)\setminus\Delta$ by $\gamma$.)

Therefore $\sigma(A|_{P\mathcal{H}}) = \Delta$. The complementary statement foll
ows analogously. ∎

---

## Step 7. Consistency with the spectral theorem (normal case)

When $A$ is normal, by the spectral theorem $A = \int_{\sigma(A)} \lambda\, dE(\
lambda)$ for a projection-valued measure $E$. The resolvent is $(z\mathbb{1}-A)^
{-1} = \int_{\sigma(A)}\frac{dE(\lambda)}{z-\lambda}$. Then

$$P = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_\gamma\int_{\sigma(A)}\frac{dE(\lambda)}{z-\lambda}\,
dz = \int_{\sigma(A)}\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\gamma\frac{dz}{z-\
lambda}\right)}_{=\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\lambda\in\Delta\}}}dE(\lambda) = E(\Delta),$$

where we used Fubini (justified by boundedness) and the winding-number identity
$\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\gamma\frac{dz}{z-\lambda} = \mathbf{1}_\Delta(\lambda)$.
 This confirms $P = E(\Delta)$, the spectral-measure projection, exactly as the
spectral theorem prescribes. ∎

---

## Conclusion

We have verified that

$$P = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_\gamma (z\mathbb{1}-A)^{-1}\,dz$$

is a well-defined bounded operator that:

1. **Is a projection** ($P^2 = P$), via the resolvent identity and the winding-n
umber argument (Step 3).
2. **Commutes with $A$** ($AP=PA$), since the resolvent commutes with $A$ (Step
4).
3. **Is self-adjoint when $A$ is normal**, and an oblique (Riesz) projection in
general (Steps 5, 7).
4. **Splits the spectrum**: $\sigma(A|_{P\mathcal{H}})=\Delta$ and $\sigma(A|_{(
\mathbb{1}-P)\mathcal{H}})=\sigma(A)\setminus\Delta$ (Step 6).
5. **Agrees with the spectral-measure projection** $E(\Delta)$ in the normal cas
e (Step 7).

The formula is therefore the correct and consistent definition of the **Riesz sp
ectral projection** associated with the spectral subset $\Delta$. $\blacksquare$
