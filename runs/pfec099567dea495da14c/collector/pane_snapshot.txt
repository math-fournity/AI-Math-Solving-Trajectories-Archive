Yes — every finite group embeds into the group of invertible matrices over some
(in fact, any) field.

## Proof

Let $G$ be a finite group of order $n$. We construct an explicit injective homom
orphism from $G$ into $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$, where $F$ is **any** field (so in part
icular we may take $F = \mathbb{Q}$, or $\mathbb{R}$, or $\mathbb{F}_p$, etc.).

### The (left) regular representation

Let $F$ be an arbitrary field and let $V = F^n$ be the $n$-dimensional vector sp
ace over $F$, with basis $\{e_g : g \in G\}$ indexed by the elements of $G$.

For each $h \in G$, define a linear map $\rho(h) : V \to V$ by
$$\rho(h)(e_g) = e_{hg}, \qquad \text{for all } g \in G.$$

Since left multiplication by $h$ is a bijection on $G$ (with inverse given by le
ft multiplication by $h^{-1}$), the map $\rho(h)$ permutes the basis vectors and
 is therefore an invertible linear transformation. Hence $\rho(h) \in \mathrm{GL
}_n(F)$.

### $\rho$ is a homomorphism

For $h_1, h_2 \in G$ and any basis vector $e_g$:
$$\rho(h_1 h_2)(e_g) = e_{h_1 h_2 g} = \rho(h_1)(e_{h_2 g}) = \rho(h_1)\big(\rho
(h_2)(e_g)\big) = (\rho(h_1) \circ \rho(h_2))(e_g).$$

Since the two linear maps agree on a basis, $\rho(h_1 h_2) = \rho(h_1)\rho(h_2)$
. Thus
$$\rho : G \to \mathrm{GL}_n(F)$$
is a group homomorphism.

### $\rho$ is injective

Suppose $\rho(h) = \mathrm{id}_V$ for some $h \in G$. Then in particular $\rho(h
)(e_e) = e_e$, where $e$ is the identity of $G$. But $\rho(h)(e_e) = e_{he} = e_
h$, so $e_h = e_e$, which forces $h = e$.

Therefore $\ker \rho = \{e\}$, and $\rho$ is injective.

### Conclusion

$\rho$ is an injective homomorphism, i.e., an embedding of $G$ into $\mathrm{GL}
_n(F)$. Since $F$ was arbitrary, every finite group $G$ of order $n$ embeds into
 $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ for **any** field $F$.

**Remark.** Equivalently, this follows by combining two classical facts: (1) Cay
ley's theorem embeds $G$ into the symmetric group $S_n$, and (2) $S_n$ embeds in
to $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ via permutation matrices (each permutation $\sigma$ maps t
o the matrix with 1's in position $(i, \sigma(i))$ and 0's elsewhere). The regul
ar representation above is exactly the composition of these two embeddings.
