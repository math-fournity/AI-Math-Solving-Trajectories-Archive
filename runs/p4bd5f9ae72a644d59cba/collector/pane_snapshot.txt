## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. If $X \subset \mathbb{R}$ is Lebesgue measurable and $|X| < 2^{\
aleph_0}$, then $X$ is a null set (has Lebesgue measure zero). This is a theorem
 of ZFC — no hypothesis on the continuum is needed.

---

**Proof.** Suppose, for contradiction, that $X$ is Lebesgue measurable with $m(X
) > 0$. We show $|X| = 2^{\aleph_0}$, contradicting $|X| < 2^{\aleph_0}$.

**Step 1: $X$ contains a compact subset of positive measure.**

By inner regularity of Lebesgue measure, for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exist
s a compact $K \subseteq X$ with $m(K) > m(X) - \varepsilon$. Taking $\varepsilo
n = m(X)/2 > 0$, we obtain a compact $K \subseteq X$ with $m(K) \ge m(X)/2 > 0$.
 In particular $K$ is uncountable (a countable set has measure zero).

**Step 2: Every uncountable closed (hence compact) subset of $\mathbb{R}$ contai
ns a nonempty perfect subset.**

By the Cantor–Bendixson theorem, every closed set $F \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ decom
poses as $F = P \cup C$ where $P$ is perfect (closed with no isolated points) an
d $C$ is countable (the scattered part). Since $K$ is uncountable and $C$ is cou
ntable, $P = K \setminus C$ is nonempty (in fact uncountable). Being a closed su
bset of $K$, $P$ is compact, and $P \subseteq X$.

**Step 3: Every nonempty perfect subset of $\mathbb{R}$ has cardinality $2^{\ale
ph_0}$.**

Let $P$ be a nonempty perfect set. Pick $x_0 \in P$. Since $P$ is perfect, $x_0$
 is a limit point of $P$, so we can find two distinct points $x_0^0, x_0^1 \in P
$ arbitrarily close to $x_0$. Each of these is again a limit point of $P$, so we
 may inductively choose, for each finite binary sequence $s \in 2^{<\omega}$, a
point $x_s \in P$ and pairwise disjoint closed intervals $I_s$ such that:
- $I_s \cap P \neq \emptyset$ and $x_s \in I_s \cap P$,
- $I_{s^\frown 0}, I_{s^\frown 1} \subseteq I_s$ are disjoint subintervals of le
ngth $< 2^{-|s|}$,
- each $I_{s^\frown i}$ contains a point of $P$.

For each infinite binary sequence $\alpha \in 2^{\omega}$, the nested intervals
$I_{\alpha|n}$ shrink to a point $x_\alpha = \bigcap_n I_{\alpha|n}$, which lies
 in $P$ (since $P$ is closed and each $I_{\alpha|n} \cap P \neq \emptyset$). Dis
tinct $\alpha, \beta \in 2^\omega$ yield distinct $x_\alpha, x_\beta$ (they even
tually lie in disjoint intervals). Thus $P$ contains a copy of $2^\omega$, so $|
P| \ge 2^{\aleph_0}$. Since $P \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, $|P| = 2^{\aleph_0}$.

**Step 4: Conclusion.**

From Steps 1–3, $X \supseteq P$ with $|P| = 2^{\aleph_0}$, so $|X| \ge 2^{\aleph
_0}$. But $X \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ gives $|X| \le 2^{\aleph_0}$. Hence $|X| = 2^
{\aleph_0}$, contradicting the hypothesis $|X| < 2^{\aleph_0}$.

Therefore $m(X) = 0$; i.e., **$X$ must be a null set.** $\blacksquare$

---

**Remark on necessity of measurability.** The measurability hypothesis is essent
ial. It is consistent with ZFC + $\neg$CH that there exists a *Sierpiński set*:
a set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ with $|S| = \aleph_1 < 2^{\aleph_0}$ that interse
cts every null set in only countably many points. Such an $S$ is not null (it ca
nnot be contained in any null set, else it would intersect itself countably, for
cing $|S| \le \aleph_0$), yet $|S| < 2^{\aleph_0}$. Of course $S$ is necessarily
 non-measurable — consistent with our theorem. Thus the result fails for arbitra
ry (non-measurable) sets, but holds for all Lebesgue measurable sets in ZFC alon
e.
