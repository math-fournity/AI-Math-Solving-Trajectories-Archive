## Proof

Consider the Sturm–Liouville equation
$$Ly \equiv -\frac{1}{w}\bigl[p(r)\,y'\bigr]' + q(r)\,y = \lambda\,y, \qquad r\i
n[0,R],$$
and let $y_1, y_2$ be a fundamental pair of linearly independent solutions (for
a fixed $\lambda$). The general solution is
$$y(r) = c_1\,y_1(r) + c_2\,y_2(r).$$

**The regular case (for contrast).** If $r=0$ were a regular point, both $y_1$ a
nd $y_2$ would be finite with finite derivatives at $r=0$. Then $y(0)$ and $y'(0
)$ are well-defined linear functions of $(c_1,c_2)$, and a single homogeneous co
ndition $a\,y(0)+b\,y'(0)=0$ selects a one-dimensional subspace of the two-dimen
sional solution space — exactly what is needed to fix a boundary condition.

**The singular case.** By hypothesis $r=0$ is a singular point, so at least one
of $y_1, y_2$ diverges as $r\to 0$. Relabel so that $y_2(r)\to\infty$ (or $y_2'(
r)\to\infty$) as $r\to 0$, while $y_1$ remains bounded. Then for a generic linea
r combination,
$$y(0) = c_1\,y_1(0) + c_2\,y_2(0), \qquad y'(0) = c_1\,y_1'(0) + c_2\,y_2'(0),$
$
and at least one of the quantities $y_2(0)$, $y_2'(0)$ is **not finite**. Conseq
uently the expression $a\,y(0)+b\,y'(0)$ is not a well-defined finite number for
 arbitrary $(c_1,c_2)$; it is meaningless whenever $c_2\neq 0$. The boundary con
dition $a\,y(0)+b\,y'(0)=0$ therefore **cannot be imposed on the full two-dimens
ional solution space**, because its left-hand side is undefined on the part of t
hat space spanned by the divergent solution $y_2$.

There are two ways to try to rescue the condition, and both fail:

1. **Restrict to bounded solutions first.** The physical/functional requirement
that $y$ be finite (or, more generally, square-integrable with weight $w$ on $[0
,R]$) already forces $c_2=0$, eliminating the divergent solution. After this res
triction the solution space is one-dimensional, $y = c_1 y_1$. Imposing $a\,y(0)
+b\,y'(0)=0$ on this one-dimensional space gives a *single scalar equation in a
single constant* $c_1$: either it is satisfied identically (giving no informatio
n — the condition is vacuous), or it forces $c_1=0$, leaving only the trivial so
lution. In neither case does it function as a boundary condition that selects a
nontrivial one-dimensional subspace.

2. **Try to interpret the condition as a limit.** One might attempt to write $a\
lim_{r\to 0^+}y(r)+b\lim_{r\to 0^+}y'(r)=0$. But for the divergent solution thes
e limits are infinite, so the condition reduces to "$c_2\cdot\infty = 0$," i.e.
it again just forces $c_2=0$ — which is precisely the boundedness/square-integra
bility requirement, not an independent boundary condition of the regular type. I
t carries no information beyond "discard the divergent solution," and it does so
 in a way that depends on the divergent solution's behavior rather than on a fre
e choice of $(a,b)$.

**Conclusion.** A boundary condition of the form $a\,y(0)+b\,y'(0)=0$ presuppose
s that $y(0)$ and $y'(0)$ are finite, well-defined quantities for every solution
 — equivalently, that $r=0$ is a *regular* point at which the solution space is
fully evaluable. At a singular point this presupposition fails: the values $y(0)
$, $y'(0)$ do not exist for the divergent branch of the solution space, so the l
inear functional $a\,y(0)+b\,y'(0)$ is not defined on the whole space and cannot
 serve as a boundary condition. The role of a boundary condition at a singular e
ndpoint is instead played by a **qualitative** requirement — boundedness, or $w$
-square-integrability (the Friedrichs/Weyl criterion) — which selects the physic
ally admissible solution. This requirement is not of the regular form $a\,y(0)+b
\,y'(0)=0$; it is a global integrability condition, and it is precisely the subs
titute needed because the local pointwise condition is meaningless at a singular
ity.
