## Construction

We build a "fat Cantor set" $C\subseteq[0,1]$ by iteratively removing open middl
e intervals, removing *less* total length than the classical Cantor set so that
measure $\tfrac12$ remains.

**Base case.** Set $F_0=[0,1]$.

**Inductive step.** Suppose after stage $n-1$ we have $F_{n-1}$, a union of $2^{
n-1}$ disjoint closed intervals. From the *middle* of each such interval remove
an open interval of length
$$a_n=\left(\tfrac14\right)^n=\frac{1}{4^n}.$$
Call the resulting closed set $F_n$. Thus $F_n$ is a union of $2^n$ disjoint clo
sed intervals and $F_n\subseteq F_{n-1}$.

**Feasibility.** After stage $n$ the total remaining length is
$$|F_n|=1-\sum_{k=1}^{n}2^{k-1}a_k=1-\sum_{k=1}^{n}2^{k-1}\cdot 4^{-k}=1-\sum_{k
=1}^{n}2^{-(k+1)}=1-\tfrac12\bigl(1-2^{-n}\bigr)=\tfrac12+2^{-(n+1)}.$$
Each of the $2^n$ intervals comprising $F_n$ has length
$$\ell_n=\frac{|F_n|}{2^n}=\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}+\frac{1}{2^{2n+1}}.$$
The next removal requires $a_{n+1}=4^{-(n+1)}=2^{-(2n+2)}\le \ell_n$, which hold
s since $2^{-(2n+2)}<2^{-(2n+1)}<\ell_n$. So every stage is well defined.

**Definition.** Set
$$C=\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}F_n.$$

## Verification of the required properties

### 1. Lebesgue measure $\tfrac12$

The sets $F_n$ are nested and measurable, so by continuity from above (each $F_n
$ has finite measure),
$$|C|=\lim_{n\to\infty}|F_n|=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Bigl(\tfrac12+2^{-(n+1)}\Bigr)=\t
frac12.$$

### 2. Closed

Each $F_n$ is a finite union of closed intervals, hence closed. An arbitrary int
ersection of closed sets is closed, so $C$ is closed.

### 3. Nowhere dense

$C$ is closed, so it suffices to show $C$ has empty interior, i.e. contains no n
on-degenerate interval. Every interval comprising $F_n$ has length $\ell_n\to 0$
 as $n\to\infty$. Hence no interval of positive length can be contained in $C=\b
igcap_n F_n$. Therefore $\operatorname{int}C=\varnothing$, and since $C$ is clos
ed, $C$ is nowhere dense.

### 4. Perfect (closed with no isolated points)

Let $x\in C$. For each $n$, $x$ lies in one of the $2^n$ closed intervals $I_n$
of $F_n$, and $I_n\supseteq I_{n+1}$ with $\operatorname{diam} I_n=\ell_n\to0$,
so $\bigcap_n I_n=\{x\}$.

We exhibit points of $C\setminus\{x\}$ arbitrarily close to $x$. The interval $I
_n$ has two children in $F_{n+1}$: a left child $I_n^{L}$ and a right child $I_n
^{R}$, separated by the gap removed at stage $n+1$. Since $x\in C\subseteq F_{n+
1}$, $x$ lies in exactly one child; say (without loss) $x\in I_n^{L}$. Then $I_n
^{R}$ is a non-degenerate closed interval with $I_n^{R}\subseteq F_{n+1}$, and t
he further construction inside $I_n^{R}$ is a scaled copy of the whole process,
so $C\cap I_n^{R}$ is nonempty (indeed uncountable). Pick $y_n\in C\cap I_n^{R}$
. Then $y_n\neq x$ (they lie in disjoint children), and $y_n\in I_n$, so $|y_n-x
|\le\ell_n\to0$.

Thus $x$ is a limit point of $C$. Since $x$ was arbitrary, $C$ has no isolated p
oints; being closed, $C$ is perfect.

### 5. Uncountable

A nonempty perfect subset of $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable: if it were countable,
list it as $\{p_1,p_2,\dots\}$; since it is perfect, $p_1$ is a limit point, so
pick $q_1\in C\setminus\{p_1\}$, and inductively pick $q_n\in C\setminus\{p_1,\d
ots,p_n,q_1,\dots,q_{n-1}\}$ (always possible since each $p_i$ is not isolated).
 The sequence $(q_n)$ in the compact set $C$ has a convergent subsequence with l
imit $q\in C$; then $q\neq p_i$ for every $i$ (each $p_i$ was excluded from a ta
il), contradicting that the list exhausted $C$.

(Equivalently, $C$ is uncountable because the standard left/right coding gives a
 surjection $\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\twoheadrightarrow C$.)

## Summary

The set
$$C=\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}F_n,\qquad F_n=\text{result of removing, at stage }k,\
text{ an open middle interval of length }4^{-k}\text{ from each of the }2^{k-1}\
text{ intervals of }F_{k-1},$$
is a closed, uncountable, perfect, nowhere dense subset of $[0,1]$ with Lebesgue
 measure exactly $\tfrac12$. The total removed length is $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{
k-1}4^{-k}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-(k+1)}=\tfrac12$, leaving $|C|=\tfrac12$.
