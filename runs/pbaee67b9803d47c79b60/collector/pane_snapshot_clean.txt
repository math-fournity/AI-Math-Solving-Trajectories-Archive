# Solution

## Problem Setup

We solve Laplace's equation $u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0$ in the strip $D = \{(x,y) \mid
 -\infty < x < \infty,\; 0 < y < 1\}$ with boundary conditions:
- $u(x,0) = f(x) = x\,e^{-x^2/4}$
- $u(x,1) = 0$
- $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_0^1 |u|\,dy\,dx < \infty$

**Answer-leak check:** The problem statement contains no solutions, answer value
s, or proof sketches. Proceeding.

---

## Step 1: Fourier Transform in $x$

Define the Fourier transform $\hat{u}(k,y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} u(x,y)\,e^{
-ikx}\,dx$. Laplace's equation transforms to:

$$\hat{u}_{yy} - k^2 \hat{u} = 0$$

with boundary conditions $\hat{u}(k,0) = \hat{f}(k)$ and $\hat{u}(k,1) = 0$.

The general solution satisfying $\hat{u}(k,1)=0$ is:

$$\hat{u}(k,y) = \hat{f}(k)\,\frac{\sinh\bigl(k(1-y)\bigr)}{\sinh(k)}$$

(with the limiting value $1-y$ at $k=0$).

---

## Step 2: Compute $\hat{f}(k)$

Since $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/4}e^{-ikx}\,dx = 2\sqrt{\pi}\,e^{-k^2}$,
and using $\mathcal{F}\{x\,g(x)\} = i\,\frac{d}{dk}\hat{g}(k)$:

$$\hat{f}(k) = i\,\frac{d}{dk}\Bigl[2\sqrt{\pi}\,e^{-k^2}\Bigr] = i\cdot 2\sqrt{
\pi}\cdot(-2k)\,e^{-k^2} = -4i\sqrt{\pi}\,k\,e^{-k^2}$$

---

## Step 3: Fourier Integral Representation

By inverse transform $u(x,y) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \hat{u}(k,y
)\,e^{ikx}\,dk$:

$$u(x,y) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \bigl(-4i\sqrt{\pi}\,k\,e^{-k^2
}\bigr)\,\frac{\sinh(k(1-y))}{\sinh(k)}\,e^{ikx}\,dk = \frac{-2i}{\sqrt{\pi}}\in
t_{-\infty}^{\infty} k\,e^{-k^2}\,\frac{\sinh(k(1-y))}{\sinh(k)}\,e^{ikx}\,dk$$

Observe that $g(k) = k\,e^{-k^2}\,\frac{\sinh(k(1-y))}{\sinh(k)}$ is **odd** in
$k$ (since $k$ is odd, $e^{-k^2}$ is even, and $\sinh(k(1-y))/\sinh(k)$ is even
— ratio of two odd functions). Therefore:

- $g(k)\cos(kx)$ is odd → integrates to $0$,
- $g(k)\sin(kx)$ is even → $2\int_0^\infty g(k)\sin(kx)\,dk$.

Hence:

$$\boxed{u(x,y) = \frac{4}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_0^{\infty} k\,e^{-k^2}\,\frac{\sinh\b
igl(k(1-y)\bigr)}{\sinh(k)}\,\sin(kx)\,dk}$$

### Verification of boundary conditions

**At $y=0$:** $\sinh(k)/\sinh(k)=1$, so $u(x,0)=\frac{4}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_0^\inft
y k\,e^{-k^2}\sin(kx)\,dk$. Using $\int_0^\infty e^{-k^2}\cos(kx)\,dk = \frac{\s
qrt{\pi}}{2}e^{-x^2/4}$ and differentiating w.r.t. $x$:

$$\int_0^\infty k\,e^{-k^2}\sin(kx)\,dk = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,x}{4}\,e^{-x^2/4}$$

so $u(x,0) = \frac{4}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,x}{4}\,e^{-x^2/4} = x\,e
^{-x^2/4}$. ✓

**At $y=1$:** $\sinh(0)/\sinh(k)=0$, so $u(x,1)=0$. ✓

**Integrability:** $f\in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ and the Poisson kernel for the strip i
s integrable, so $\int\!\!\int |u|\,dy\,dx<\infty$. ✓

---

## Step 4: Evaluation by the Convolution (Poisson Kernel) Method

The solution can be written as a convolution $u(x,y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f
(s)\,P(x-s,y)\,ds$ where the Poisson kernel for the strip is:

$$P(x,y) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\sinh(k(1-y))}{\sinh(k)}\
,e^{ikx}\,dk = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\infty \frac{\sinh(k(1-y))}{\sinh(k)}\cos(kx)
\,dk$$

### Evaluating the kernel integral

Using the expansion (valid for $k>0$):

$$\frac{\sinh(k(1-y))}{\sinh(k)} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Bigl[e^{-k(2n+y)} - e^{-k
(2n+2-y)}\Bigr]$$

and $\int_0^\infty e^{-ak}\cos(kx)\,dk = \frac{a}{a^2+x^2}$, the kernel becomes:

$$\int_0^\infty \frac{\sinh(k(1-y))}{\sinh(k)}\cos(kx)\,dk = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\left[\frac{2n+y}{(2n+y)^2+x^2} - \frac{2n+2-y}{(2n+2-y)^2+x^2}\right]$$

The second sum re-indexes (set $m=-(n+1)$) to show this equals $\sum_{n=-\infty}
^{\infty}\frac{2n+y}{(2n+y)^2+x^2}$. Writing $\frac{2n+y}{(2n+y)^2+x^2} = \opera
torname{Re}\frac{1}{2n+y-ix}$ and using the partial-fraction identity $\sum_{n=-
\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n+a}=\pi\cot(\pi a)$:

$$\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2n+y-ix} = \frac{\pi}{2}\cot\!\left(\frac{\p
i(y-ix)}{2}\right)$$

Setting $\alpha=\frac{\pi y}{2},\;\beta=\frac{\pi x}{2}$ and computing $\operato
rname{Re}\cot(\alpha-i\beta)$:

$$\cot(\alpha-i\beta) = \frac{\sin(\pi y)+i\sinh(\pi x)}{\cosh(\pi x)-\cos(\pi y
)}$$

Taking the real part:

$$\int_0^\infty \frac{\sinh(k(1-y))}{\sinh(k)}\cos(kx)\,dk = \frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\
frac{\sin(\pi y)}{\cosh(\pi x)-\cos(\pi y)}$$

Therefore the **closed-form Poisson kernel** for the strip is:

$$\boxed{P(x,y) = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\sin(\pi y)}{\cosh(\pi x)-\cos(\pi y)}}$$

### Convolution form of the solution

$$\boxed{u(x,y) = \frac{\sin(\pi y)}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{s\,e^{-s^2/
4}}{\cosh\!\bigl(\pi(x-s)\bigr)-\cos(\pi y)}\,ds}$$

This is the evaluated Fourier integral: the oscillatory integral over $k$ has be
en reduced to a real-space convolution with the explicit closed-form kernel $P(x
,y)$.

---

## Step 5: Properties and 3D Surface Description

**Symmetry:** Since $f(x)=x\,e^{-x^2/4}$ is odd and $P(x,y)$ is even in $x$, we
have $u(-x,y)=-u(x,y)$: the solution is **odd in $x$**.

**Behavior:** The boundary profile $f(x)=x\,e^{-x^2/4}$ has a maximum at $x=\sqr
t{2}$ with value $\sqrt{2}\,e^{-1/2}\approx 0.858$ and a minimum at $x=-\sqrt{2}
$ with value $-\sqrt{2}\,e^{-1/2}\approx -0.858$. As $y$ increases from $0$ to $
1$, the potential decays smoothly to zero, with the peak/trough broadening and f
lattening due to the kernel's smoothing.

**Equipotential lines** $u(x,y)=c$:
- For $c>0$: closed contour loops in the right half-plane ($x>0$), nested around
 the peak region, shrinking as $c$ increases toward the maximum, and collapsing
toward $y=0$ as $c\to 0^+$.
- For $c<0$: mirror-image loops in the left half-plane ($x<0$).
- For $c=0$: the line $y=1$ (top boundary) and the line $x=0$ (by odd symmetry).
- The equipotential curves are smooth, symmetric about $x=0$ with opposite signs
, and confined to the strip $0\le y\le 1$.

**3D surface:** The surface $z=u(x,y)$ over a portion of the domain (e.g., $-4\l
e x\le 4$, $0\le y\le 1$) would show a smooth ridge rising from the boundary cur
ve $z=x\,e^{-x^2/4}$ at $y=0$ toward a single positive peak near $(x,y)\approx(\
sqrt{2},0)$ and a symmetric valley near $(-\sqrt{2},0)$, both decaying to the pl
ane $z=0$ as $y\to 1$. The surface is antisymmetric: $z(-x,y)=-z(x,y)$.

To generate the plot numerically, one evaluates either the Fourier sine integral
 or the convolution form above on a grid $(x_i, y_j)$ and renders the surface $z
=u(x_i,y_j)$ with contour projections for the equipotential lines.
