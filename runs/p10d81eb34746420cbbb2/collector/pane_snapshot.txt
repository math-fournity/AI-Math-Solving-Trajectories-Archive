Since the language has $\omega_1$ propositional variables, for **every** formula

### The algebra is atomless

Let $[\varphi]\in A$ be any nonzero element, i.e., $\varphi$ is satisfiable. Let
 $S=\operatorname{supp}(\varphi)$ (countable) and pick $p\notin S$.

Since $\varphi$ is satisfiable, there is a valuation $v$ with $v\models\varphi$.

- $v_1$: agrees with $v$ on $S$, and $v_1(p)=1$;
- $v_2$: agrees with $v$ on $S$, and $v_2(p)=0$.

Both $v_1$ and $v_2$ agree with $v$ on $S$, so $v_1\models\varphi$ and $v_2\mode

Therefore $[\varphi]$ is **not** an atom: it has a nonzero element strictly belo

### Why no formula can be an atom (structural reason)

An atom would correspond to a formula satisfied by a *single* valuation (up to e
quivalence). Pinning down a single valuation among all $2^{\omega_1}$ valuations
 requires specifying the truth value of every one of the $\omega_1$ variables—i.
e., a conjunction of $\omega_1$ literals. But $L_{\omega_1}$ only permits conjun
ctions of length $<\omega_1$ (countable), so any formula leaves $\omega_1\setmin
us S$ (still of cardinality $\omega_1$) variables unspecified, yielding $2^{\ome
ga_1}$ many satisfying valuations. There is always "room" to split.

### Conclusion

Since $[\varphi]$ was an arbitrary nonzero element of $A$ and we exhibited a non
zero element strictly below it, **$A$ has no atoms**.

$$\boxed{A \text{ is atomless; it contains no atoms.}}$$
