**Step 1: $\lambda'$ is periodic with period $T$.**

Since $\lambda(t+T) = \lambda(t)$ for all $t$, differentiating both sides yields
 $\lambda'(t+T) = \lambda'(t)$ for all $t$. Hence $g(t) := [\lambda'(t)]^2$ is a
lso periodic with period $T$ and satisfies $g(t) \geq 0$.

**Step 2: $\lambda$ is absolutely continuous.**

Since $\lambda'$ is bounded, say $|\lambda'(t)| \le M$, the function $\lambda$ i

mbda$ is absolutely continuous.

**Step 3: The integral over one period is strictly positive.**

Since $\lambda$ is non-constant, $\lambda'$ is not identically zero. Moreover, s
ince $\lambda$ is absolutely continuous, if $\lambda' = 0$ a.e. on $[0,T]$, then
 $\lambda$ would be constant on $[0,T]$, and by periodicity constant everywhere
— contradicting the hypothesis. Therefore the set
$$E = \{t \in [0,T] : \lambda'(t) \neq 0\}$$
has positive Lebesgue measure. Since $g(t) = [\lambda'(t)]^2 \ge 0$ and $g > 0$
on $E$ (which has positive measure),
$$C := \int_0^T [\lambda'(t)]^2\,dt > 0.$$

**Step 4: $f$ grows linearly along multiples of $T$.**

For any positive integer $n$, using the $T$-periodicity of $g$:
$$f(nT) = \int_0^{nT} [\lambda'(t)]^2\,dt = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\int_{kT}^{(k+1)T} [
\lambda'(t)]^2\,dt = n\int_0^T [\lambda'(t)]^2\,dt = nC.$$

As $n \to \infty$, $f(nT) = nC \to +\infty$.

**Step 5: $f$ is non-decreasing, hence diverges to $+\infty$.**

Since $g(t) = [\lambda'(t)]^2 \ge 0$, the function $f(x) = \int_0^x g(t)\,dt$ is
 non-decreasing. Therefore, for any $x \ge nT$:
$$f(x) \ge f(nT) = nC.$$

Given any $M > 0$, choose an integer $n > M/C$. Then for all $x \ge nT$, we have
 $f(x) \ge nC > M$. This proves
$$\lim_{x \to +\infty} f(x) = +\infty. \qquad \blacksquare$$
