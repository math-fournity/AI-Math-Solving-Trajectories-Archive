### Lemma

*Let $Y$ be a compact topological vector space over $\mathbb{R}$ (the same argum
ent works over $\mathbb{C}$). Then $Y$ carries the indiscrete topology: the only
 open sets are $\varnothing$ and $Y$.*

**Proof of Lemma.** Suppose, for contradiction, that the topology is not indiscr
ete. Then there exists a **proper** open neighborhood $U$ of $0$ with $U\subsetn
eq Y$.

By continuity of scalar multiplication $m:\mathbb{R}\times Y\to Y$ at $(0,0)$ an
d the fact that $m(0,0)=0\in U$, there exist $\varepsilon>0$ and an open neighbo
rhood $V$ of $0$ in $Y$ such that
$$tV\subseteq U\qquad\text{for all }|t|<\varepsilon.$$
Set
$$B=\bigcup_{|t|<\varepsilon}tV.$$
Then $B$ is open (a union of open sets, since multiplication by a nonzero scalar
 is a homeomorphism), contains $0$, and satisfies $B\subseteq U\subsetneq Y$, so
 $B\subsetneq Y$. Moreover $B$ is **balanced**: for $|s|\le 1$,
$$sB=\bigcup_{|t|<\varepsilon}st\,V=\bigcup_{|u|<\varepsilon|s|}uV\subseteq\bigc
up_{|u|<\varepsilon}uV=B.$$

Now consider the family $\{2^{n}B\}_{n\ge 0}$.

- **It is increasing.** Since $B$ is balanced, $\tfrac{1}{2}B\subseteq B$, hence
 $B\subseteq 2B$, and inductively $2^{n}B\subseteq 2^{n+1}B$.
- **Each $2^{n}B$ is open**, multiplication by $2^{n}$ being a homeomorphism.
- **It covers $Y$.** For any $y\in Y$, continuity of scalar multiplication gives
 $\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}y\to 0$ in $Y$ (because $\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}\to 0$ in $\mathbb{R}
$). Since $B$ is a neighborhood of $0$, there exists $N$ with $\tfrac{1}{2^{N}}y
\in B$, i.e. $y\in 2^{N}B$.

Thus $\{2^{n}B\}_{n\ge 0}$ is an increasing open cover of the compact space $Y$.
 Compactness yields a finite subcover; because the family is increasing, that fi
nite subcover is just $\{2^{N}B\}$ for some $N$. Hence $2^{N}B=Y$, and applying
the homeomorphism $y\mapsto \tfrac{1}{2^{N}}y$ gives $B=Y$ — contradicting $B\su
bsetneq Y$.

Therefore no proper open neighborhood of $0$ exists. Consequently every nonempty
 open set equals $Y$: if $O\neq\varnothing$ is open and $x\in O$, then $O-x$ is
an open neighborhood of $0$, so $O-x=Y$, i.e. $O=Y$. Hence the topology is indis
crete. $\blacksquare$

### Applying the Lemma

Since $Y$ is a compact topological vector space, the Lemma forces $Y$ to be indi
screte. The closed subsets of an indiscrete space are exactly $\varnothing$ and
$Y$.

Let $W\subseteq Y$ be closed.

- If $W=\varnothing$, then $T^{-1}(W)=\varnothing$, which is closed in $X$.
- If $W=Y$, then $T^{-1}(W)=X$, which is closed in $X$.

In either case $T^{-1}(W)$ is closed in $X$. (Note that neither continuity nor s
urjectivity of $T$ is even needed once the topology of $Y$ is forced to be indis
crete; the conclusion holds for *any* linear map $T:X\to Y$.)

### Conclusion

The statement is **true**: for every closed $W\subseteq Y$, the inverse image $T
^{-1}(W)$ is closed in $X$.
