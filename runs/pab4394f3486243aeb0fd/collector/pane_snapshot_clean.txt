# Derivation of the Riemann–Liouville and Caputo Fractional Derivatives via Kern
el Convolutions

## Setup and Definitions

**The kernel family $\{\Phi_p\}_{p \in \mathbb{R}}$.** Define the causal kernel

$$
\Phi_p(t) = \frac{t_+^{\,p-1}}{\Gamma(p)}, \qquad t_+ = \max(t,0),
$$

for $p > 0$, and extend to non-positive $p$ distributionally:

- $\Phi_0(t) = \delta(t)$ (the Dirac delta),
- $\Phi_{-n}(t) = \delta^{(n)}(t)$ for $n \in \mathbb{N}$ (the $n$-th distributi
onal derivative of $\delta$).

**Two key properties.**

1. **Convolution (semigroup) property:**
$$
\Phi_p * \Phi_q = \Phi_{p+q}, \qquad \forall\, p,q \in \mathbb{R},
$$
where convolution is $(\Phi_p * \Phi_q)(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \Phi_p(t-\t
au)\,\Phi_q(\tau)\,d\tau$.

2. **Differentiation property:**
$$
D\,\Phi_p = \Phi_{p-1}, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad D^n\,\Phi_p = \Phi_{p-n}, \quad
 n \in \mathbb{N}.
$$

**Fractional integral and ordinary derivative as convolutions.** For a causal fu
nction $f$ (i.e., $f(t)=0$ for $t<0$):

- The **Riemann–Liouville fractional integral** of order $\beta > 0$ is
$$
I^\beta f(t) = \Phi_\beta * f = \frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta)}\int_0^t (t-\tau)^{\beta-
1}\,f(\tau)\,d\tau.
$$

- The **ordinary $n$-fold derivative** is, in the distributional sense,
$$
D^n f = \Phi_{-n} * f.
$$

## Decomposition of the Fractional Derivative

Let $m-1 < \alpha \leq m$ with $m = \lceil \alpha \rceil \in \mathbb{N}$. The fr
actional derivative of order $\alpha$ is decomposed into an **integer-order deri
vative** $D^m$ (kernel $\Phi_{-m}$) and a **fractional integral** $I^{m-\alpha}$
 (kernel $\Phi_{m-\alpha}$, with $m - \alpha \geq 0$). The two kernels $\Phi_{-m
}$ and $\Phi_{m-\alpha}$ satisfy

$$
\Phi_{-m} * \Phi_{m-\alpha} = \Phi_{-\alpha},
$$

so that formally $D^\alpha f = \Phi_{-\alpha} * f$. The **two alternative defini
tions** arise from the **two possible orders** in which these kernels are convol
ved with $f$.

---

## Definition 1 — Riemann–Liouville: Integrate First, Then Differentiate

**Convolution form:**
$$
\boxed{(D^\alpha_{\mathrm{RL}} f)(t) = \bigl[\Phi_{-m} * (\Phi_{m-\alpha} * f)\b
igr](t)}
$$

**Interpretation.** The inner convolution $\Phi_{m-\alpha} * f = I^{m-\alpha} f$
 is the fractional integral of order $m - \alpha$:

$$
I^{m-\alpha} f(t) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(m-\alpha)}\int_0^t (t-\tau)^{m-\alpha-1}\,f(
\tau)\,d\tau.
$$

The outer convolution $\Phi_{-m} * (\cdot) = D^m(\cdot)$ applies $m$ ordinary de
rivatives. Hence:

$$
\boxed{(D^\alpha_{\mathrm{RL}} f)(t) = \frac{d^m}{dt^m}\left[\frac{1}{\Gamma(m-\
alpha)}\int_0^t (t-\tau)^{m-\alpha-1}\,f(\tau)\,d\tau\right]}
$$

**Requirements.** $f$ need only be integrable (e.g., $f \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}$)
; no classical differentiability of $f$ is required. The singularity at $\tau =
t$ is integrable since $m - \alpha - 1 > -1$.

---

## Definition 2 — Caputo: Differentiate First, Then Integrate

**Convolution form:**
$$
\boxed{(D^\alpha_{\mathrm{C}} f)(t) = \bigl[\Phi_{m-\alpha} * (\Phi_{-m} * f)\bi
gr](t)}
$$

**Interpretation.** The inner convolution $\Phi_{-m} * f = D^m f = f^{(m)}$ is t
he classical $m$-th derivative of $f$. The outer convolution $\Phi_{m-\alpha} *
(\cdot) = I^{m-\alpha}(\cdot)$ is the fractional integral of order $m - \alpha$
applied to $f^{(m)}$:

$$
\boxed{(D^\alpha_{\mathrm{C}} f)(t) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(m-\alpha)}\int_0^t (t-\tau
)^{m-\alpha-1}\,f^{(m)}(\tau)\,d\tau}
$$

**Requirements.** $f$ must be $m$-times classically differentiable with $f^{(m)}
 \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}$, and the initial values $f(0), f'(0), \ldots, f^{(m-1)}
(0)$ must exist.

---

## Why the Two Orders Differ: Distributional Boundary Terms

If convolution with distributions were freely associative and commutative, we wo
uld have

$$
\Phi_{-m} * (\Phi_{m-\alpha} * f) \;\stackrel{?}{=}\; (\Phi_{-m} * \Phi_{m-\alph
a}) * f \;\stackrel{?}{=}\; (\Phi_{m-\alpha} * \Phi_{-m}) * f \;\stackrel{?}{=}\
; \Phi_{m-\alpha} * (\Phi_{-m} * f),
$$

giving identical results. **They are not identical** because $f$ is a *causal* f
unction (extended by zero for $t < 0$), and the distributional derivative $\Phi_
{-m} * f = D^m[f_+]$ generates **boundary terms** (delta distributions at the or
igin) from the jumps in $f$ and its derivatives at $t = 0$:

$$
D^m[f_+] = f^{(m)}_+ + \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} f^{(k)}(0^+)\,\delta^{(m-1-k)} = f^{(m)}
_+ + \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} f^{(k)}(0^+)\,\Phi_{k+1-m}.
$$

**Caputo path** — differentiate first:
$$
\Phi_{m-\alpha} * (\Phi_{-m} * f) = \Phi_{m-\alpha} * \left[f^{(m)}_+ + \sum_{k=
0}^{m-1} f^{(k)}(0^+)\,\Phi_{k+1-m}\right]
$$
$$
= \underbrace{\Phi_{m-\alpha} * f^{(m)}_+}_{= \; D^\alpha_{\mathrm{C}} f} \;+\;
\sum_{k=0}^{m-1} f^{(k)}(0^+)\,\underbrace{\Phi_{m-\alpha} * \Phi_{k+1-m}}_{= \;
\Phi_{k+1-\alpha}}.
$$

**Riemann–Liouville path** — integrate first:
$$
\Phi_{-m} * (\Phi_{m-\alpha} * f) = \Phi_{-\alpha} * f = D^\alpha_{\mathrm{RL}}
f.
$$

Equating the two expressions (both formally equal to $\Phi_{-\alpha} * f$ when a
ll terms are retained):

$$
D^\alpha_{\mathrm{RL}} f(t) = D^\alpha_{\mathrm{C}} f(t) + \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} \fra
c{f^{(k)}(0^+)}{\Gamma(k+1-\alpha)}\,t^{k-\alpha}, \qquad t > 0.
$$

The **sum of initial-condition terms** $\sum_{k=0}^{m-1} f^{(k)}(0^+)\,t^{k-\alp
ha}/\Gamma(k+1-\alpha)$ is precisely the discrepancy between the two definitions
. These terms are **singular at $t = 0$** (since $k - \alpha < 0$ for $k \leq m-
1$ and $\alpha > m-1$), reflecting the fact that:

- **Riemann–Liouville** assigns **singular initial conditions** $\bigl[D^{\alpha
-k-1}_{\mathrm{RL}} f\bigr](0^+)$, which are generally non-physical.
- **Caputo** uses the **physical initial conditions** $f^{(k)}(0^+)$, which is w
hy it is preferred in applied problems.

The two definitions are the two **non-commuting convolution factorizations** of
