## Proof

Let $E$ be the elliptic curve (a smooth, irreducible, projective curve over $\ma
thbb{k}$) with distinguished point $O$, and let $x, y \in \mathbb{k}(E)$ be the
functions with poles of order $2$ and $3$ at $O$ respectively (and regular elsew
here, since $E$ is complete). Let $C \subset \mathbb{P}^2$ be the plane curve cu
t out by the Weierstrass relation satisfied by $x$ and $y$, and let

$$\Phi : E \longrightarrow C$$

be the morphism defined by the pair $(x, y)$ (i.e. $\Phi = (x : y : 1)$ on the a
ffine part, extended to all of $E$). We show $\Phi$ is birational.

---

**Step 1. $\Phi$ is dominant.**

The functions $x$ and $y$ are non-constant (each has a pole at $O$), so $\Phi$ i
s a non-constant morphism of irreducible curves. A non-constant morphism of irre
ducible curves has image a dense subset of the target. Hence $\Phi(E)$ is dense
in $C$, i.e. $\Phi$ is dominant.

Dominance guarantees that the pullback on function fields

$$\Phi^* : \mathbb{k}(C) \hookrightarrow \mathbb{k}(E)$$

is well-defined: a rational function on $C$ pulls back to a rational function on
 $E$ because the generic point of $E$ maps to the generic point of $C$.

---

**Step 2. $\Phi^*$ is injective.**

For a dominant morphism of irreducible varieties, the pullback on function field
s is always injective. Concretely: if $f \in \mathbb{k}(C)^*$ is a nonzero ratio
nal function, then $f$ is nonzero on a nonempty open subset $U \subset C$. Since
 $\Phi$ is dominant, $\Phi^{-1}(U)$ is a nonempty open subset of $E$, and on it
$\Phi^*(f) = f \circ \Phi \neq 0$. Hence $\Phi^*(f) \neq 0$ in $\mathbb{k}(E)$.
So $\Phi^*$ is injective.

---

**Step 3. $\Phi^*$ is surjective.**

The function field $\mathbb{k}(C)$ is generated as a field over $\mathbb{k}$ by
the coordinate functions $X, Y$ on $C$ (these are the restrictions of the ambien
t affine coordinates). By definition of $\Phi$,

$$\Phi^*(X) = x, \qquad \Phi^*(Y) = y.$$

Therefore the image of $\Phi^*$ is the subfield $\mathbb{k}(x, y) \subseteq \mat
hbb{k}(E)$. By the given hypothesis,

$$\mathbb{k}(E) = \mathbb{k}(x, y),$$

so $\Phi^*(\mathbb{k}(C)) = \mathbb{k}(x, y) = \mathbb{k}(E)$. Hence $\Phi^*$ is
 surjective.

---

**Step 4. Conclusion.**

Steps 2 and 3 show that $\Phi^* : \mathbb{k}(C) \to \mathbb{k}(E)$ is an isomorp
hism of function fields. A morphism of (irreducible) varieties that induces an i
somorphism on function fields is, by definition, **birational**. Therefore $\Phi
 : E \to C$ is birational.

---

**Remark on the hypothesis.** The key input $\mathbb{k}(E) = \mathbb{k}(x,y)$ is
 what makes the argument work: it says precisely that the two functions defining
 $\Phi$ already generate the entire function field of $E$, so no information is
lost in passing from $E$ to $C$. Geometrically, the fibers of $\Phi$ are generic
ally a single point (the map is generically one-to-one), which is exactly birati
onality. The pole-order data ($2$ and $3$ at $O$) is what guarantees that $x$ an
d $y$ are algebraically independent enough to generate a plane curve and that th
e Weierstrass relation has the right form, but the logical engine of the proof i
s the field-generation hypothesis $\mathbb{k}(E) = \mathbb{k}(x,y)$.
