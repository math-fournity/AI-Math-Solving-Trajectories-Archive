The j-invariant is the fundamental invariant that classifies elliptic curves ove
r an algebraically closed field up to isomorphism. Its significance in the modul
i space of elliptic curves is as follows.

**Theorem.** Let $k$ be an algebraically closed field. Two elliptic curves $E_1,
 E_2$ over $k$ are isomorphic (over $k$) if and only if $j(E_1) = j(E_2)$.

**Proof.** Every elliptic curve over $k$ can be put into Weierstrass form. When
$\operatorname{char}(k) \neq 2, 3$, a short Weierstrass form is
$$E: y^2 = x^3 + ax + b, \quad a, b \in k, \quad \Delta = -16(4a^3 + 27b^2) \neq
 0.$$
The j-invariant is defined as
$$j(E) = -1728 \frac{4a^3}{4a^3 + 27b^2} = 1728 \frac{4a^3}{\Delta'}$$
where $\Delta' = -(4a^3 + 27b^2)$. A change of variables $x = u^2 x',\ y = u^3 y
'$ with $u \in k^\times$ sends $(a,b) \mapsto (u^4 a,\ u^6 b)$, which leaves $j$
 unchanged. Hence $j$ is an isomorphism invariant.

Conversely, suppose $j(E_1) = j(E_2) = j_0$.

- If $j_0 \neq 0, 1728$: each curve is isomorphic to one of the form $y^2 = x^3
+ Ax + 1$ (after scaling $b$ to $1$, possible since $b \neq 0$ when $j \neq 0$).
 The value of $j$ determines $A^3$ uniquely, and over an algebraically closed fi
eld every element has a cube root, so $A$ is determined up to a cube root of uni
ty, which is absorbed by a further change of variables. Thus the curves are isom
orphic.

- If $j_0 = 1728$: each curve is isomorphic to $y^2 = x^3 + Ax$ with $A \neq 0$.
 Scaling sends $A \mapsto u^4 A$, and since $k$ is algebraically closed, any two
 nonzero values of $A$ are related by such a $u$. So all such curves are isomorp
hic.

- If $j_0 = 0$: each curve is isomorphic to $y^2 = x^3 + B$ with $B \neq 0$. Sca
ling sends $B \mapsto u^6 B$, and again any two nonzero values are related. So a
ll such curves are isomorphic.

The cases $\operatorname{char}(k) = 2$ and $\operatorname{char}(k) = 3$ are hand
led by the appropriate Weierstrass normal forms and the corresponding j-invarian
t formulas; the same conclusion holds — the j-invariant is a complete isomorphis
m invariant over an algebraically closed field. $\square$

**Significance for the moduli space.** The (coarse) moduli space of elliptic cur
ves $\mathcal{M}_{1,1}$ parametrizes isomorphism classes of elliptic curves. The
 theorem above shows that the map
$$j: \mathcal{M}_{1,1} \longrightarrow \mathbb{A}^1_k, \qquad [E] \longmapsto j(
E)$$
is a bijection on $k$-points (since $k$ is algebraically closed, every value of
$j$ is attained — e.g., $j = 1728 \cdot \frac{4t}{4t+27}$ for $y^2 = x^3 + tx +
1$ sweeps all values, and $j=0, 1728$ are covered by the special forms). In fact
, as an algebraic stack/scheme, $\mathcal{M}_{1,1}$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{A}
^1_k$, and the j-invariant provides this isomorphism.

Therefore the significance of the j-invariant is:

1. **It is a complete invariant.** Over an algebraically closed field, $j(E)$ de
termines $E$ up to isomorphism — no two non-isomorphic elliptic curves share the
 same j-value.

2. **It identifies the moduli space with the affine line.** The coarse moduli sp
ace $\mathcal{M}_{1,1}$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{A}^1_k$ via the j-map, so the
moduli space is one-dimensional and parametrized by a single number.

3. **It is surjective.** Every element of $k$ arises as $j(E)$ for some elliptic
 curve, so the moduli space has no "gaps."

4. **It is a modular function.** The j-invariant is the prototypical modular fun
ction on the upper half-plane (in characteristic $0$): $j(\tau) = j(E_\tau)$ whe
re $E_\tau = \mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z} + \mathbb{Z}\tau)$, and it generates the fun
ction field of the modular curve $X(1) \cong \mathbb{P}^1$. This connects the al
gebraic moduli problem to the classical analytic theory of modular forms.

In summary, the j-invariant is the coordinate on the moduli space of elliptic cu
rves: it provides a faithful, complete, and surjective parametrization of isomor
phism classes, realizing $\mathcal{M}_{1,1}$ as the affine line over $k$.
