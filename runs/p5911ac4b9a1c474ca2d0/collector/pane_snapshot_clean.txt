# The 1D Anderson Model: Spectral Analysis

## Problem Setup

We consider the operator $L_\omega$ on $\ell^2(\mathbb{Z})$ defined by
$$(L_\omega u)(n) = u(n+1) + u(n-1) + V_\omega(n)\,u(n),$$
where $V_\omega(n) \in \{0,1\}$ are IID random variables with distribution $\mu
= p\,\delta_0 + (1-p)\,\delta_1$, $p \in (0,1)$. We write $L_\omega = \Delta + V
_\omega$, where $\Delta$ is the free discrete Laplacian.

---

## Step 1: Ergodicity and Deterministic Spectrum

The probability space is $\Omega = \{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}$ equipped with the produ
ct measure $\mathbb{P} = \mu^{\mathbb{Z}}$. Define the shift $T:\Omega\to\Omega$
 by $(T\omega)(n) = \omega(n+1)$. Since $\mu$ is a product of identical marginal
 distributions, $T$ is **ergodic** with respect to $\mathbb{P}$.

Let $U$ be the unitary shift on $\ell^2(\mathbb{Z})$, $(Uu)(n) = u(n+1)$. Then o
ne checks directly:
$$L_{T\omega} = U^{-1}\, L_\omega\, U.$$

This covariance relation makes $(L_\omega)$ an **ergodic family** of self-adjoin
t operators. By the Pastur–Shubin theorem:

- The spectrum $\sigma(L_\omega)$ is **deterministic almost surely**: there exis
ts a fixed closed set $\Sigma$ such that $\sigma(L_\omega) = \Sigma$ for $\mathb
b{P}$-a.e. $\omega$.
- The spectral components (pure point, absolutely continuous, singular continuou
s parts) are also deterministic a.s. in the sense that the corresponding spectra
l projections have a.s. constant rank.

**Computation of $\Sigma$:** For the free Laplacian, $\sigma(\Delta) = [-2,2]$.
The almost-sure spectrum of the Anderson model is given by the Minkowski sum:
$$\Sigma = \sigma(\Delta) + \operatorname{supp}(\mu) = [-2,2] + \{0,1\} = [-2,2]
\cup[-1,3] = [-2,3].$$

This can be justified as follows. The upper bound $\Sigma \subseteq [-2,3]$ foll
ows from the fact that $\|L_\omega\| \leq \|\Delta\| + \|V_\omega\|_\infty = 2 +
 1 = 3$ and $L_\omega \geq -2 + 0 = -2$. For the reverse inclusion, one uses the
 Wegner estimate (which shows that the expected number of eigenvalues in any int
erval $I \subseteq [-2,3]$ for finite-volume restrictions is positive) combined
with a covering argument, establishing that every $E \in [-2,3]$ belongs to $\Si
gma$.

---

## Step 2: Transfer Matrices and the Lyapunov Exponent

The eigenvalue equation $L_\omega u = E u$ reads:
$$u(n+1) + u(n-1) + V_\omega(n)\,u(n) = E\,u(n),$$
or equivalently:
$$u(n+1) = (E - V_\omega(n))\,u(n) - u(n-1).$$

This defines the **transfer matrix**:
$$A_E^\omega(n) = \begin{pmatrix} E - V_\omega(n) & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \i
n \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R}),$$
so that $\binom{u(n+1)}{u(n)} = A_E^\omega(n)\,\binom{u(n)}{u(n-1)}$.

The **Lyapunov exponent** is defined as:
$$\gamma(E) = \lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{N}\, \mathbb{E}\!\left[\log\left\|A_E^\
omega(N)\cdots A_E^\omega(1)\right\|\right],$$
where the limit exists by subadditivity (Kingman's ergodic theorem). By ergodici
ty, the time-average also converges a.s. to $\gamma(E)$.

---

## Step 3: Positivity of the Lyapunov Exponent (Fürstenberg's Theorem)

**Theorem (Fürstenberg, 1963):** Let $\nu$ be a probability measure on $\mathrm{
SL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ whose support generates a **non-compact** subgroup $G_\nu \su
bseteq \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})$, and which is **non-degenerate** (not supporte
d on a single one-parameter subgroup). Then the Lyapunov exponent associated to
products of IID matrices with law $\nu$ is strictly positive.

**Application to our model:** The distribution of $A_E^\omega(n)$ is supported o
n the two matrices
$$A_0 = \begin{pmatrix} E & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A_1 = \begin{pmatr
ix} E-1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
each taken with positive probability (since $p \in (0,1)$).

- **Non-degeneracy:** $A_0 \neq A_1$ (since $E \neq E-1$), and they do not commu
te in general (one verifies $[A_0, A_1] \neq 0$ for all $E$), so the support is
not contained in a single one-parameter subgroup.

- **Non-compactness of $G_\nu$:** The group generated by $\{A_0, A_1\}$ is non-c
ompact. This can be seen from the fact that $A_0$ has eigenvalues $\frac{E \pm \
sqrt{E^2-4}}{2}$, which are real and distinct for $|E| > 2$ (so $A_0$ is hyperbo
lic with $|\lambda| \neq 1$), and the group generated by a hyperbolic element to
gether with a non-commuting element is non-compact. For $|E| \leq 2$, one uses t
he fact that the group generated by $A_0$ and $A_1$ (which differ by a translati
on in the $(1,1)$-entry) cannot be contained in the compact group $\mathrm{SO}(2
)$; a more detailed argument (cf. Furstenberg's original paper or the treatment
in Cycon–Froese–Kirsch–Simon) shows non-compactness for all $E$.

Therefore, by Fürstenberg's theorem:
$$\boxed{\gamma(E) > 0 \quad \text{for all } E \in \mathbb{R}.}$$

---

## Step 4: Absence of Absolutely Continuous Spectrum

**Theorem (Ishii–Pastur, Kotani):** For an ergodic 1D Schrödinger operator, the
absolutely continuous spectrum satisfies:
$$\sigma_{\mathrm{ac}}(L_\omega) = \overline{\{E \in \mathbb{R} : \gamma(E) = 0\
}}^{\mathrm{ess}} \quad \text{a.s.}$$

Since $\gamma(E) > 0$ for all $E$ (Step 3), we conclude:
$$\sigma_{\mathrm{ac}}(L_\omega) = \emptyset \quad \text{a.s.}$$

---

## Step 5: Anderson Localization — Pure Point Spectrum with Exponential Decay

The remaining task is to show that the spectrum is **pure point** (not singular
continuous) and that eigenfunctions decay exponentially. This is the content of
**Anderson localization** in one dimension.

**Theorem (Anderson localization in 1D):** For the 1D Anderson model with any no
n-degenerate IID potential (including Bernoulli), the spectrum is almost surely
**pure point** with **exponentially decaying eigenfunctions**:
$$\sigma_{\mathrm{pp}}(L_\omega) = \Sigma = [-2,3], \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{sc}}(
L_\omega) = \emptyset, \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{ac}}(L_\omega) = \emptyset \quad \
text{a.s.},$$
and the eigenfunctions $\{\phi_j\}$ satisfy $|\phi_j(n)| \leq C_j\, e^{-\gamma(E
_j)|n - n_j|}$ for some localization centers $n_j$ and constants $C_j > 0$.

**Proof sketch (for the Bernoulli case):** Several methods establish this:

1. **Transfer matrix / Lyapunov exponent method (Kunz–Souillard 1980, Carmona–Kl
ein–Martinelli 1985):** The positivity of $\gamma(E)$ implies that generalized e
igenfunctions grow exponentially. Using large-deviation estimates for the produc
ts of transfer matrices (which hold even for the Bernoulli case, as shown by Car
mona–Klein–Martinelli), one proves that spectral measures are pure point. The ke
y steps are:
   - Large deviations: $\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|\frac{1}{N}\log\|A_E(N)\cdots A_
E(1)\| - \gamma(E)\right| > \epsilon\right) \leq e^{-cN}$.
   - This, combined with a Wegner-type estimate (for Bernoulli, one uses the **M
inami estimate** or the approach of Carmona–Klein–Martinelli that bypasses the n
eed for a density), gives exponential decay of eigenfunction correlators and hen
ce localization.

2. **Fractional moment method (Aizenman–Molchanov 1993, Aizenman et al.):** One
shows that the **fractional moment** of the Green's function decays exponentiall
y:
   $$\mathbb{E}\!\left[|G_\omega(E; m, n)|^s\right] \leq C\, e^{-\mu |m-n|}, \qu
ad s \in (0,1),$$
   for some $\mu > 0$. This directly implies localization (pure point spectrum +
 exponential decay) via the relation between the Green's function and spectral m
easures. This method works in any dimension but in 1D it applies for all energie
s and all non-degenerate IID distributions, including Bernoulli.

3. **Multi-scale analysis (Fröhlich–Spencer 1983, and extensions):** Starting fr
om the positivity of the Lyapunov exponent (which provides the initial length sc
ale estimate), one inductively proves probabilistic estimates on the Green's fun
ction at increasing length scales. This also yields localization, though for the
 Bernoulli case the Wegner estimate requires the Minami estimate or modified arg
uments.

All three approaches confirm: **the spectrum is pure point with exponentially lo
calized eigenfunctions.**

## Summary of Results

For the 1D Anderson model $L_\omega = \Delta + V_\omega$ on $\ell^2(\mathbb{Z})$
 with IID Bernoulli potential $V_\omega(n) \in \{0,1\}$, $p \in (0,1)$:

| Property | Result |
|---|---|
| **Almost sure spectrum** | $\Sigma = [-2, 3]$ (deterministic, independent of $
\omega$ a.s.) |
| **Spectral type** | **Pure point** almost surely (Anderson localization) |
| **Absolutely continuous spectrum** | $\sigma_{\mathrm{ac}} = \emptyset$ a.s. |
| **Singular continuous spectrum** | $\sigma_{\mathrm{sc}} = \emptyset$ a.s. |
| **Eigenfunction decay** | Exponential: $|\phi_j(n)| \leq C_j\, e^{-\gamma(E_j)
|n-n_j|}$ |
| **Lyapunov exponent** | $\gamma(E) > 0$ for all $E \in \mathbb{R}$ (Fürstenber
g) |

The physical interpretation: in one dimension, **any amount of disorder** (here,
 the random potential taking values 0 or 1) causes complete localization of all
quantum states. There are no extended (delocalized) states — every eigenstate is
 exponentially localized around some site $n_j$. This is the phenomenon of **And
erson localization**, and it is rigorous in 1D for all energies, in contrast to
higher dimensions where localization typically holds only for large disorder or
near spectral edges, and the existence of a mobility edge (transition between lo
calized and extended states) remains an open problem.
