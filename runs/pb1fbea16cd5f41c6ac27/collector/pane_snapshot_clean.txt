**Claim:** Yes, $R(x)$ is necessarily a polynomial.

**Proof.**

Write $R(x) = f(x)/g(x)$ where $f, g \in \mathbb{C}[x]$, $g \not\equiv 0$, and $
\gcd(f,g)=1$. Since $R(n) \in \mathbb{Z}$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, the value
$R(n)$ is finite, so $g(n) \neq 0$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

Perform polynomial division of $f$ by $g$:
$$f(x) = q(x)\,g(x) + r(x), \qquad \deg r < \deg g,$$
so that
$$R(x) = q(x) + \frac{r(x)}{g(x)},$$
where $q \in \mathbb{C}[x]$ and $r/g$ is a **proper** rational function (i.e., $
r(x)/g(x) \to 0$ as $|x| \to \infty$).

If $g$ is constant, then $r \equiv 0$ and $R = q$ is already a polynomial. So su
ppose $g$ is non-constant and, for contradiction, that $r \not\equiv 0$. Let $d
= \deg q$ (set $d = -1$ if $q \equiv 0$).

**Key tool — forward differences.** Define $\Delta h(x) = h(x+1) - h(x)$ and $\D
elta^{k+1} = \Delta \circ \Delta^k$. Two facts:

1. **Integer-valuedness is preserved:** Since $R(n) \in \mathbb{Z}$ for all $n \
in \mathbb{Z}$, we have $\Delta^k R(n) \in \mathbb{Z}$ for every $k \geq 0$ and
every $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

2. **Differences kill polynomials:** $\Delta^{d+1} q(x) \equiv 0$ since $q$ has
degree $d$.

Combining these:
$$\Delta^{d+1} R(n) = \underbrace{\Delta^{d+1} q(n)}_{=\,0} + \Delta^{d+1}\!\lef
t(\frac{r}{g}\right)(n) = \Delta^{d+1}\!\left(\frac{r}{g}\right)(n).$$

**The proper part vanishes under high differences.** Since $r(x)/g(x) \to 0$ as
$|x|\to\infty$ and the forward difference of a function tending to $0$ also tend
s to $0$ (as $h(x+1)\to 0$ and $h(x)\to 0$), by induction:
$$\Delta^{d+1}\!\left(\frac{r}{g}\right)(n) \longrightarrow 0 \quad \text{as } n
 \to \infty.$$

But $\Delta^{d+1}(r/g)(n) = \Delta^{d+1} R(n) \in \mathbb{Z}$ for all $n$. An in
teger sequence tending to $0$ is eventually $0$:
$$\Delta^{d+1}\!\left(\frac{r}{g}\right)(n) = 0 \quad \text{for all sufficiently
 large } n \in \mathbb{Z}.$$

**Conclusion.** The function $\Delta^{d+1}(r/g)$ is a rational function of $x$ (
differences of rational functions are rational: $\Delta(r/g) = \frac{r(x+1)g(x)
- r(x)g(x+1)}{g(x)\,g(x+1)}$). A rational function that vanishes at infinitely m
any points is identically zero (its numerator polynomial has infinitely many roo
ts). Therefore:
$$\Delta^{d+1}\!\left(\frac{r}{g}\right) \equiv 0.$$

This means $r(x)/g(x)$ is a polynomial of degree at most $d$. But $r/g$ is a pro
per rational function with $r(x)/g(x) \to 0$ as $|x| \to \infty$. The only polyn
omial tending to $0$ at infinity is the zero polynomial. Hence:
$$\frac{r(x)}{g(x)} \equiv 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad r(x) \equiv 0.$$

Therefore $R(x) = q(x)$, a polynomial. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The converse is false: not every polynomial maps $\mathbb{Z}$ to $\m
athbb{Z}$ (e.g., $x/2$). The polynomials that do are exactly the integer linear
combinations of binomial coefficients $\binom{x}{k}$. But the result above shows
 that **no non-polynomial rational function** can be integer-valued on all of $\
mathbb{Z}$—the proper fractional part is forced to vanish by the forward-differe
nce argument.
