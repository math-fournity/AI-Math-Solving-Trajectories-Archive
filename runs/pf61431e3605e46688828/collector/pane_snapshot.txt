## Solution

**The statement is FALSE.**

### Counterexample

Let $X$ and $Y$ be independent, identically distributed random variables with $X
, Y \sim N(0,1)$. Define the sequence $X_n = Y$ for all $n \geq 1$, and let the
limit be $X$.

**Verification of the hypotheses:**

1. **$X_n \to X$ in distribution.** Since $X_n = Y$ for every $n$, and $Y$ has t
he same distribution as $X$ (both $N(0,1)$), the sequence $\{X_n\}$ is constant
in law and equals the law of $X$. Hence $X_n \Rightarrow X$ trivially.

2. **$\{X_n^2\}$ is uniformly integrable.** The family $\{X_n^2\} = \{Y^2\}$ is
a singleton. A single integrable random variable is always uniformly integrable:
 since $E[Y^2] = 1 < \infty$, for any $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $K$ large e
nough that $E[Y^2 \mathbf{1}_{Y^2 > K}] < \varepsilon$ (by the absolute continui
ty of the integral), and this bound holds uniformly over the singleton. ✓

**Failure of the conclusion:**

We check $L^2$-convergence. We have
$$
E\bigl[(X_n - X)^2\bigr] = E\bigl[(Y - X)^2\bigr].
$$
Using independence of $X$ and $Y$ (so $E[XY] = E[X]\,E[Y] = 0$):
$$
E\bigl[(Y-X)^2\bigr] = E[Y^2] - 2\,E[XY] + E[X^2] = 1 - 0 + 1 = 2.
$$
Thus $E[(X_n - X)^2] = 2$ for every $n$, which does **not** tend to $0$. Hence $
X_n \not\to X$ in $L^2$.

### Why the implication fails (intuition)

The standard theorem states: $X_n \to X$ in $L^p$ **if and only if** $X_n \to X$
 **in probability** and $\{|X_n|^p\}$ is uniformly integrable. Convergence in di
stribution is strictly weaker than convergence in probability (the latter requir
es control over the *joint* law of $(X_n, X)$, whereas the former only concerns
the *marginal* laws). Uniform integrability of $\{X_n^2\}$ guarantees that momen
ts converge to the moment of the distributional limit, i.e., $E[X_n^2] \to E[X^2
]$, but it says nothing about the coupling between $X_n$ and $X$. In the counter
example above, $X_n$ and $X$ are coupled to be *independent*, so even though eac
h has the correct marginal, their difference never vanishes.
