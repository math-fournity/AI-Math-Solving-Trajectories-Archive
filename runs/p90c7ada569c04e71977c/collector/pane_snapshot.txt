## Solution

We evaluate $I = \displaystyle\int_{|z|=8}\frac{1+z}{1-\sin z}\,dz$ via the resi
due theorem.

### Step 1: Locate the poles

The poles occur where $1-\sin z = 0$, i.e. $\sin z = 1$, giving
$$z_k = \frac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi k, \quad k \in \mathbb{Z}.$$

Checking $|z_k|<8$ (using $\pi\approx 3.14159$):

| $k$ | $z_k$ | $\|z_k\|$ | inside? |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $\pi/2 \approx 1.571$ | $1.571$ | ✓ |
| $1$ | $5\pi/2 \approx 7.854$ | $7.854$ | ✓ |
| $-1$ | $-3\pi/2 \approx -4.712$ | $4.712$ | ✓ |
| $2$ | $9\pi/2 \approx 14.137$ | $14.137$ | ✗ |
| $-2$ | $-7\pi/2 \approx -10.996$ | $10.996$ | ✗ |

So there are **three** poles inside $|z|=8$: $z_0=\pi/2$, $z_1=5\pi/2$, $z_{-1}=
-3\pi/2$.

### Step 2: Determine the order of each pole

Set $w = z - z_k$. Since $\sin z_k = 1$ and $\cos z_k = 0$,
$$\sin(z_k+w) = \sin z_k\cos w + \cos z_k\sin w = \cos w.$$
Hence
$$1-\sin z = 1-\cos w = \frac{w^2}{2}-\frac{w^4}{24}+\cdots = \frac{w^2}{2}\Bigl
(1-\frac{w^2}{12}+\cdots\Bigr).$$
The leading term is $w^2/2$, so **each pole is of order 2**.

### Step 3: Compute the residue at each pole

Expand:
$$\frac{1}{1-\sin z}=\frac{1}{1-\cos w}=\frac{2}{w^2}\Bigl(1-\frac{w^2}{12}+\cdo
ts\Bigr)^{-1}=\frac{2}{w^2}+\frac{1}{6}+O(w^2).$$

Also $1+z = 1+z_k+w$. Multiplying:
$$\frac{1+z}{1-\sin z}=(1+z_k+w)\Bigl(\frac{2}{w^2}+\frac{1}{6}+O(w^2)\Bigr)=\fr
ac{2(1+z_k)}{w^2}+\frac{2}{w}+\frac{1+z_k}{6}+O(w).$$

The coefficient of $1/w$ — the residue — is
$$\operatorname{Res}_{z=z_k}\frac{1+z}{1-\sin z} = 2,$$
**independent of $k$**. (The $1/w$ term arises solely from $w\cdot\frac{2}{w^2}$
, which carries no $z_k$ dependence.)

### Step 4: Apply the residue theorem

$$I = 2\pi i\sum_{\text{poles}}\operatorname{Res} = 2\pi i\,(2+2+2)=2\pi i\cdot
6=12\pi i.$$

### Answer

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{|z|=8}\frac{1+z}{1-\sin z}\,dz = 12\pi i}$$
