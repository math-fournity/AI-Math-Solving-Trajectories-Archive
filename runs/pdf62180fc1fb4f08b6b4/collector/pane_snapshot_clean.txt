Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the product of two function
s—each having every point as a Lebesgue point—can fail to have all Lebesgue poin
ts.

**Answer: Yes.**

## Proof

**Setup.** Define $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ by
$$g(x) = \begin{cases} |x|^{-1/2} & x \neq 0, \\ 0 & x = 0. \end{cases}$$
Set $h = g$. Both are Borel measurable. We show every point of $\mathbb{R}$ is a
 Lebesgue point of $g$ (and $h$), yet $0$ is not a Lebesgue point of $gh$.

**Step 1: $g$ is locally integrable.** For any bounded interval $[a,b]$, the onl
y concern is near $0$. We have $\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon} |x|^{-1/2}\,dx = 2\i
nt_0^\epsilon x^{-1/2}\,dx = 4\sqrt{\epsilon} < \infty$. Away from $0$, $g$ is c
ontinuous hence locally integrable. So $g \in L^1_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})$.

**Step 2: Every point is a Lebesgue point of $g$.**

- **At $x = 0$:** With $g(0) = 0$,
$$\frac{1}{2r}\int_{-r}^{r} |g(y) - g(0)|\,dy = \frac{1}{2r}\int_{-r}^{r} |y|^{-
1/2}\,dy = \frac{1}{r}\int_0^r y^{-1/2}\,dy = \frac{2\sqrt{r}}{r} = \frac{2}{\sq
rt{r}} \xrightarrow{r\to 0} 0.$$

- **At $x \neq 0$:** The function $|x|^{-1/2}$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}\setm
inus\{0\}$, so every $x \neq 0$ is a Lebesgue point (continuity implies the Lebe
sgue point condition: $|g(y)-g(x)| \to 0$ pointwise, and local boundedness gives
 the dominated convergence needed for the average to tend to $0$).

Thus every $x \in \mathbb{R}$ is a Lebesgue point of $g$, and by symmetry of $h
= g$, the same holds for $h$.

**Step 3: $0$ is not a Lebesgue point of $gh$.**

The product is $gh(x) = |x|^{-1}$ for $x \neq 0$ and $(gh)(0) = g(0)\cdot h(0) =
 0$. We check local integrability near $0$:
$$\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon} |gh(y)|\,dy = 2\int_0^{\epsilon} \frac{1}{y}\,dy =
 2\bigl[\ln y\bigr]_0^{\epsilon} = +\infty.$$

Since $gh$ is **not locally integrable** at $0$, the Lebesgue point condition ca
nnot even be formulated there (the integral $\frac{1}{2r}\int_{-r}^{r}|gh(y) - (
gh)(0)|\,dy$ diverges for every $r > 0$). Therefore $0$ is not a Lebesgue point
of $gh$.

**Conclusion.** We have exhibited measurable, locally integrable functions $g$ a
nd $h$, each having every point of $\mathbb{R}$ as a Lebesgue point, whose produ
ct $gh$ is not locally integrable at $0$ and hence has $0$ as a non-Lebesgue poi
nt. The key mechanism is that $g \in L^1_{\text{loc}}$ with $g(0) = 0$ and $\fra
c{1}{r}\int_0^r |g| \to 0$ (so $0$ is a Lebesgue point), yet $g \notin L^2_{\tex
t{loc}}$ (so $g^2 = gh$ is not locally integrable). The Lebesgue point property
is preserved under multiplication by a **locally bounded** function, but not in
general.
