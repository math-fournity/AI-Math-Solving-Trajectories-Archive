## Proof: The Weyl Tensor in Two Dimensions

**Claim:** In two dimensions, the Weyl tensor is identically zero. (It is not "u
ndefined"; it is well-defined and equal to zero.)

### 1. Setup and definitions

Let $(M, g)$ be a 2-dimensional pseudo-Riemannian manifold with Riemann curvatur
e tensor $R_{abcd}$, Ricci tensor $R_{ab} = g^{cd}R_{acbd}$, and Ricci scalar $R
 = g^{ab}R_{ab}$.

The Weyl tensor is defined as the **totally trace-free part** of the Riemann ten
sor, possessing the same algebraic symmetries (antisymmetry in first and last pa
irs, pair symmetry, first Bianchi identity). In $n \geq 3$ dimensions it admits
the explicit formula

$$C_{abcd} = R_{abcd} - \frac{2}{n-2}\bigl(g_{a[c}R_{d]b} - g_{b[c}R_{d]a}\bigr)
 + \frac{2}{(n-1)(n-2)}R\, g_{a[c}g_{d]b}.$$

This formula has denominators $(n-2)$ and $(n-1)(n-2)$, which vanish at $n=2$ —
suggesting at first glance that the Weyl tensor is "undefined" in 2D. We show th
is is a **artifact of the formula**, not of the tensor itself: the Weyl tensor,
properly defined as the trace-free projection of Riemann, is well-defined and eq
uals zero.

### 2. The Riemann tensor in 2D

In 2 dimensions, the space of algebraic curvature tensors (tensors with the symm
etries of Riemann) is **1-dimensional**. Concretely, every such tensor is a scal
ar multiple of

$$G_{abcd} := g_{ac}g_{bd} - g_{ad}g_{bc}.$$

Indeed, the number of independent components of a Riemann-like tensor in $n$ dim
ensions is $\frac{1}{12}n^2(n^2-1)$, which equals $1$ when $n=2$. Hence

$$R_{abcd} = \frac{R}{2}\bigl(g_{ac}g_{bd} - g_{ad}g_{bc}\bigr) = \frac{R}{2}\,G
_{abcd}.$$

This identity can also be verified directly: contracting $R_{abcd} = K\,G_{abcd}
$ on $ac$ and $bd$ gives $R = 2K$, so $K = R/2$.

### 3. The Ricci tensor in 2D

From the expression above,

$$R_{ab} = g^{cd}R_{acbd} = \frac{R}{2}\bigl(g^{cd}g_{ac}g_{bd} - g^{cd}g_{ad}g_
{bc}\bigr) = \frac{R}{2}\bigl(\delta^c_c g_{ab} - g_{ab}\bigr) = \frac{R}{2}(2g_
{ab} - g_{ab}) = \frac{R}{2}g_{ab}.$$

So in 2D the Ricci tensor is **entirely determined by the scalar curvature**: $R
_{ab} = \tfrac{R}{2}g_{ab}$.

### 4. The Weyl tensor is the trace-free part — and it vanishes

The Weyl tensor is characterized (independently of the explicit $n\geq 3$ formul
a) as the unique tensor $C_{abcd}$ such that:
- $C$ has the algebraic symmetries of the Riemann tensor,
- $C$ is totally trace-free: $g^{ac}C_{abcd} = 0$ (and hence all traces vanish),
- $C = R - (\text{terms built from } g, R_{ab}, R)$.

In 2D, every tensor with the symmetries of Riemann is a multiple of $G_{abcd} =
g_{ac}g_{bd} - g_{ad}g_{bc}$. The trace of $G$ is

$$g^{ac}G_{abcd} = g^{ac}(g_{ac}g_{bd} - g_{ad}g_{bc}) = 2\,g_{bd} - g_{bd} = g_
{bd} \neq 0.$$

So $G_{abcd}$ is **not** trace-free. The only trace-free multiple of $G$ is $0 \
cdot G = 0$. Therefore the space of trace-free tensors with Riemann symmetries i
n 2D is **zero-dimensional**, and

$$\boxed{C_{abcd} = 0 \quad \text{in 2 dimensions.}}$$

### 5. Reconciling with the singular formula

The singularity of the explicit formula at $n=2$ reflects the fact that in 2D th
e "trace part" (the terms involving $R_{ab}$ and $R$) **exhausts the entire Riem
ann tensor**. Substituting $R_{ab} = \tfrac{R}{2}g_{ab}$ into the trace part and
 taking the limit, one finds the trace part equals exactly $R_{abcd} = \tfrac{R}
{2}G_{abcd}$, leaving $C_{abcd} = 0$. The $1/(n-2)$ divergence is cancelled by t
he numerator also vanishing — a removable singularity. Thus the formula's singul
arity is an artifact, not a genuine undefinedness.

### 6. Conclusion

The Weyl tensor in two dimensions is **well-defined and identically zero**. The
Riemann curvature is entirely captured by the Ricci scalar (equivalently, the Ga
ussian curvature), leaving no trace-free remainder. The apparent singularity in
the standard $n\geq 3$ formula is removable and does not indicate undefinedness.
