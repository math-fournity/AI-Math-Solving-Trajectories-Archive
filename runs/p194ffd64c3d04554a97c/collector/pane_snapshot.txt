## Solution

**Yes, $f$ is necessarily of bounded variation.**

**Proof.** Since $g$ is increasing on $[0,1]$, it is bounded, hence integrable.
Let $G(x)=\int_0^x g(t)\,dt$; then $G$ is absolutely continuous on $[0,1]$, and
$f(x)=G(x)/x$ for $x>0$.

**Step 1: $f$ is increasing on $(0,1]$.**

For any $\varepsilon>0$, $f=G/x$ is absolutely continuous on $[\varepsilon,1]$ (
quotient of an AC function by a smooth nonvanishing function). Hence $f$ is diff
erentiable a.e. on $[\varepsilon,1]$ with
$$f'(x)=\frac{xg(x)-G(x)}{x^2}.$$
Since $g$ is increasing, for every $t\in[0,x]$ we have $g(t)\le g(x)$, so
$$G(x)=\int_0^x g(t)\,dt\le \int_0^x g(x)\,dt = x\,g(x).$$
Thus $xg(x)-G(x)\ge 0$, giving $f'(x)\ge 0$ a.e. on $[\varepsilon,1]$. An absolu
tely continuous function with nonnegative derivative a.e. is nondecreasing, so $
f$ is nondecreasing on $[\varepsilon,1]$. As this holds for every $\varepsilon>0
$, $f$ is nondecreasing on $(0,1]$.

**Step 2: Behavior at $0$.**

Since $g$ is increasing and bounded, the right limit $g(0^+)=\lim_{t\to 0^+}g(t)
$ exists. For $t\in[0,x]$, $g(0)\le g(t)\le g(x)$, and $g(x)\to g(0^+)$ as $x\to
 0^+$, so by squeezing
$$\lim_{x\to 0^+} f(x)=\lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{1}{x}\int_0^x g(t)\,dt = g(0^+).$$
By definition $f(0)=0$, so $f$ has at most a single jump discontinuity at $0$, o
f size $|g(0^+)|$.

**Step 3: Bounded variation.**

Take any partition $0=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_n=1$. The variation sum splits into the j
ump at $0$ plus the variation on $(0,1]$:
$$\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f(x_i)-f(x_{i-1})| = |f(x_1)-f(0)|+\sum_{i=2}^{n}|f(x_i)-f(x_{i
-1})|.$$
Since $f$ is nondecreasing on $(0,1]$, the second sum equals $f(1)-f(x_1)\le f(1
)-g(0^+)$. The first term is bounded by $|f(x_1)|+|f(0)|\to |g(0^+)|+0$ as $x_1\
to 0^+$. Hence the total variation is bounded by
$$|g(0^+)| + \bigl(f(1)-g(0^+)\bigr),$$
which is finite (using $f(1)\ge g(0^+)$, since $f$ is nondecreasing on $(0,1]$ a
nd $f(x)\to g(0^+)$ as $x\to 0^+$). Therefore $f$ is of bounded variation on $[0
,1]$. $\blacksquare$
