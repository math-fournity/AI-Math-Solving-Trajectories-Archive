## Proof

We analyze the singularities of

$$F(z) = \frac{\pi\,\cot(\pi z)\,\coth(\pi z)}{z^{3}}.$$

Recall the basic facts:

- $\cot(\pi z) = \dfrac{\cos(\pi z)}{\sin(\pi z)}$ has **simple poles** at every
 integer $z = n$, $n\in\mathbb{Z}$, since $\sin(\pi z)$ has simple zeros there a
nd $\cos(\pi n)=(-1)^n\neq 0$. Near $z=n$,
  $$\cot(\pi z) = \frac{1}{\pi(z-n)} + O(z-n), \qquad \operatorname{Res}_{z=n}\c
ot(\pi z)=\frac{1}{\pi}.$$

- $\coth(\pi z) = \dfrac{\cosh(\pi z)}{\sinh(\pi z)}$ has **simple poles** where
 $\sinh(\pi z)=0$, i.e. $\pi z = i\pi k$, so $z = ik$, $k\in\mathbb{Z}$. Since $
\cosh(\pi\cdot ik)=\cos(\pi k)=(-1)^k\neq 0$, these are simple. Near $z=ik$,
  $$\coth(\pi z) = \frac{1}{\pi(z-ik)} + O(z-ik), \qquad \operatorname{Res}_{z=i
k}\coth(\pi z)=\frac{1}{\pi}.$$

The factor $z^{3}$ contributes a pole of order $3$ at $z=0$ and is analytic and
nonzero elsewhere.

The set of candidate singularities is therefore
$$\{0\}\;\cup\;\{n:n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\}\;\cup\;\{ik:k\in\mathbb{Z}\set
minus\{0\}\}.$$
These three sets are disjoint (a nonzero integer is real, a nonzero $ik$ is pure
ly imaginary; only $0$ belongs to both families).

---

### Pole at $z=0$

Use the Laurent expansions about $0$:
$$\cot(\pi z) = \frac{1}{\pi z} - \frac{\pi z}{3} - \frac{\pi^{3}z^{3}}{45} - \c
dots,$$
$$\coth(\pi z) = \frac{1}{\pi z} + \frac{\pi z}{3} - \frac{\pi^{3}z^{3}}{45} + \
cdots.$$

Multiplying,
$$\cot(\pi z)\coth(\pi z) = \frac{1}{\pi^{2}z^{2}} + \Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\pi z}\cdot
\tfrac{\pi z}{3}\Bigr) + \Bigl(-\tfrac{\pi z}{3}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\pi z}\Bigr) + \c
dots.$$

The two $O(z^{0})$ cross-terms cancel: $\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{3}=0$. The next ter
m is $O(z^{2})$. Hence
$$\cot(\pi z)\coth(\pi z) = \frac{1}{\pi^{2}z^{2}} + O(z^{2}),$$
with **no $z^{0}$ and no $z^{1}$ term** in between (the $z^{0}$ term vanishes by
 cancellation, and the $z^{1}$ term vanishes by parity: $\cot$ and $\coth$ are b
oth odd, so their product is even).

Therefore
$$F(z) = \frac{\pi}{z^{3}}\left(\frac{1}{\pi^{2}z^{2}} + O(z^{2})\right) = \frac
{1}{\pi z^{5}} + O(z^{-1}).$$

The most singular term is $\dfrac{1}{\pi z^{5}}$, so $z=0$ is a **pole of order
$5$**. ✓

(Its principal part is $\dfrac{1}{\pi z^{5}} + \dfrac{c}{z}$ for some constant $
c$; the even-parity of $\cot\cdot\coth$ kills the $z^{-4},z^{-3},z^{-2}$ terms,
leaving only $z^{-5}$ and $z^{-1}$.)

### Poles at $z = \pm n$, $n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}$ (nonzero integers)

Fix $n\neq 0$. At $z=n$:

- $\cot(\pi z)$ has a simple pole: $\cot(\pi z)\sim\dfrac{1}{\pi(z-n)}$.
- $\coth(\pi z)$ is analytic at $z=n$ (since $n$ is real and nonzero, $\sinh(\pi
 n)\neq 0$), and $\coth(\pi n)\neq 0$.
- $z^{3}$ is analytic and nonzero at $z=n$.

A simple pole times a nonzero analytic function times a nonzero analytic functio
n is a **simple pole**. Explicitly,
$$F(z) = \frac{\pi\cdot\coth(\pi n)}{n^{3}}\cdot\frac{1}{\pi(z-n)} + O(1) = \fra
c{\coth(\pi n)}{n^{3}}\cdot\frac{1}{z-n} + O(1),$$
so
$$\operatorname{Res}_{z=n}F(z) = \frac{\coth(\pi n)}{n^{3}}\neq 0.$$
Thus each $z=\pm n$ is a **simple pole**. ✓

---

### Poles at $z = \pm ni$, $n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}$ (nonzero imaginary integers)

Fix $n\neq 0$. At $z=in$:

- $\coth(\pi z)$ has a simple pole: $\coth(\pi z)\sim\dfrac{1}{\pi(z-in)}$.
- $\cot(\pi z)$ is analytic at $z=in$: $\sin(\pi\cdot in)=i\sinh(\pi n)\neq 0$,
$\cos(\pi\cdot in)=\cosh(\pi n)\neq 0$, so $\cot(\pi in) = \dfrac{\cosh(\pi n)}{
i\sinh(\pi n)} = -i\coth(\pi n)\neq 0$.
- $z^{3}=(in)^{3}=-in^{3}\neq 0$ is analytic and nonzero.

Again a simple pole times a nonzero analytic function times a nonzero analytic f
unction is a **simple pole**. Explicitly,
$$F(z) = \frac{\pi\cdot\cot(\pi in)}{(in)^{3}}\cdot\frac{1}{\pi(z-in)} + O(1) =
\frac{-i\coth(\pi n)}{-in^{3}}\cdot\frac{1}{z-in} + O(1) = \frac{\coth(\pi n)}{n
^{3}}\cdot\frac{1}{z-in} + O(1),$$
so
$$\operatorname{Res}_{z=in}F(z) = \frac{\coth(\pi n)}{n^{3}}\neq 0.$$
Thus each $z=\pm ni$ is a **simple pole**. ✓

### Summary

| Location | Source of singularity | Order |
|---|---|---|
| $z = 0$ | triple zero of $z^{3}$ **and** simple poles of both $\cot(\pi z)$, $
\coth(\pi z)$ coincide here | **5** |
| $z = \pm n$, $n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}$ | simple pole of $\cot(\pi z)$ alone | **1**
 (simple) |
| $z = \pm ni$, $n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}$ | simple pole of $\coth(\pi z)$ alone | **1
** (simple) |

The order $5$ at the origin is accounted for arithmetically: $z^{3}$ contributes
 order $3$, $\cot(\pi z)$ contributes order $1$, $\coth(\pi z)$ contributes orde
r $1$, and $3+1+1=5$. The reason the three singularities "stack" cleanly at $0$
(rather than canceling) is that both $\cot(\pi z)$ and $\coth(\pi z)$ have **pos
itive** (pole) order there, so their orders add. At every other candidate point
only **one** of the two trigonometric/hyperbolic factors is singular, so only a
simple pole results.

No other singularities exist, since $\cot(\pi z)$, $\coth(\pi z)$, and $1/z^{3}$
 are meromorphic with the pole sets listed above, and the union of those sets is
 exactly $\{0\}\cup\{\pm n:n\ge 1\}\cup\{\pm ni:n\ge 1\}$.
