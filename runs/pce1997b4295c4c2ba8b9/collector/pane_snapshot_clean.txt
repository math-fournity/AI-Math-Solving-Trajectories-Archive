## Solution

We analyze the integral
$$I(x) = \int_{x}^{x+x^{\alpha}} \frac{\sin t}{t^{1/3}}\,dt$$
as $x \to \infty$, where $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Key observation.** Define $F(x) = \int_{0}^{x} \frac{\sin t}{t^{1/3}}\,dt$. Th
en
$$I(x) = F(x + x^{\alpha}) - F(x).$$

**Step 1: $F(x)$ converges as $x \to \infty$.**

We show that the improper integral $\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{\sin t}{t^{1/3}}\,dt
$ converges.

*Near $t = 0$:* Since $\sin t \sim t$ as $t \to 0$, we have $\frac{\sin t}{t^{1/
3}} \sim t^{2/3}$, which is integrable on $[0, 1]$.

*Near $t = \infty$:* Apply **Dirichlet's test**. The function $g(t) = t^{-1/3}$
is monotonically decreasing and $g(t) \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$. The partial inte
grals of $\sin t$ are bounded: $\left|\int_{1}^{T} \sin t\,dt\right| = |-\cos T
+ \cos 1| \le 2$. Therefore $\int_{1}^{\infty} t^{-1/3}\sin t\,dt$ converges.

Combining both parts, $F(x) \to L := \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{\sin t}{t^{1/3}}\,d
t$ (a finite real number) as $x \to \infty$.

**Step 2: The upper limit $x + x^{\alpha} \to \infty$ for every $\alpha \in \mat
hbb{R}$.**

- If $\alpha > 0$: $x^{\alpha} \to \infty$, so $x + x^{\alpha} \to \infty$.
- If $\alpha = 0$: $x^{\alpha} = 1$, so $x + 1 \to \infty$.
- If $\alpha < 0$: $x^{\alpha} \to 0$, so $x + x^{\alpha} \to \infty$ (the $x$ t
erm dominates).

In all cases, $x + x^{\alpha} \to \infty$.

**Step 3: The limit of $I(x)$.**

Since both $x \to \infty$ and $x + x^{\alpha} \to \infty$, and $F$ converges to
$L$:
$$\lim_{x \to \infty} I(x) = \lim_{x \to \infty} F(x + x^{\alpha}) - \lim_{x \to
 \infty} F(x) = L - L = 0.$$

**Conclusion.** The integral $\int_{x}^{x+x^{\alpha}} \frac{\sin t}{t^{1/3}}\,dt
$ converges for every $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$, and its limit as $x \to \infty$ i
s

$$\boxed{0}.$$

### PROOF COMPLETE
