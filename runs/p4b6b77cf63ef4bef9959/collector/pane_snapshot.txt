**Yes**, it necessarily follows that $g(x)=0$ for all $x\in[a,b]$.

**Proof.** Since $g$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, the function $g^{2}$ is also cont
inuous on $[a,b]$. By the Weierstrass Approximation Theorem, for every $\varepsi
lon>0$ there exists a polynomial $p_\varepsilon(x)$ such that

$$
\sup_{x\in[a,b]}\bigl|g(x)-p_\varepsilon(x)\bigr|<\varepsilon .
$$

By hypothesis, $\int_a^b g(x)\,p_\varepsilon(x)\,dx=0$ for every polynomial $p_\
varepsilon$. Hence

$$
\int_a^b g(x)^{2}\,dx
= \int_a^b g(x)\bigl(g(x)-p_\varepsilon(x)\bigr)\,dx
\quad\text{(since }\int_a^b g\,p_\varepsilon=0\text{)}.
$$

Therefore, using $|g|\le M$ on $[a,b]$ for some constant $M$,

$$
0\le \int_a^b g(x)^{2}\,dx
= \left|\int_a^b g(x)\bigl(g(x)-p_\varepsilon(x)\bigr)\,dx\right|
\le \int_a^b |g(x)|\cdot|g(x)-p_\varepsilon(x)|\,dx
\le M\varepsilon(b-a).
$$

Since $\varepsilon>0$ is arbitrary, we conclude

$$
\int_a^b g(x)^{2}\,dx=0.
$$

The integrand $g^{2}$ is continuous and non-negative. A continuous non-negative
function with zero integral must vanish identically: indeed, if $g(c)\neq 0$ for
 some $c\in[a,b]$, then by continuity $g^{2}>\delta>0$ on some neighborhood of $
c$, forcing the integral to be strictly positive—a contradiction.

Hence $g(x)^{2}=0$ for all $x\in[a,b]$, and therefore

$$
g(x)=0\quad\text{for all }x\in[a,b].
$$
