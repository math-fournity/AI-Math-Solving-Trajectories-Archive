**Claim.** The only polynomial satisfying $P(x^2+1)=(P(x))^2+1$ and $P(0)=0$ is
$P(x)=x$.

**Verification that $P(x)=x$ works.** $P(x^2+1)=x^2+1=(P(x))^2+1=(x^2)+1$. ✓

**Uniqueness.** Define the sequence $\{x_n\}_{n\ge 0}$ by
$$x_0=0,\qquad x_{n+1}=x_n^2+1.$$
The first few terms are $0,1,2,5,26,677,\dots$ Since $x_n\ge 0$ for all $n$ and
$x_{n+1}=x_n^2+1>x_n$ whenever $x_n\ge 1$ (and $x_1=1>x_0=0$), the sequence is *
*strictly increasing**, hence all the $x_n$ are **distinct**.

We prove by induction that $P(x_n)=x_n$ for every $n\ge 0$.

- **Base case:** $P(x_0)=P(0)=0=x_0$. ✓
- **Inductive step:** Suppose $P(x_n)=x_n$. Then
$$P(x_{n+1})=P(x_n^2+1)=(P(x_n))^2+1=x_n^2+1=x_{n+1}. \checkmark$$

Thus $P(x_n)=x_n$ for all $n\ge 0$.

Now consider the polynomial $Q(x)=P(x)-x$. We have shown $Q(x_n)=0$ for every $n
\ge 0$. Since the $x_n$ are infinitely many **distinct** real numbers and $Q$ is
 a polynomial, a nonzero polynomial of degree $d$ can have at most $d$ roots. Ha
ving infinitely many roots forces
$$Q(x)\equiv 0,$$
that is, $P(x)=x$.

Therefore $P(x)=x$ is the unique polynomial satisfying both $P(x^2+1)=(P(x))^2+1
$ and $P(0)=0$. $\blacksquare$
