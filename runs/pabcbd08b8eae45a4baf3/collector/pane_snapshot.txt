Let $f(x+iy) = u(x,y) + i\,u(x,y) = (1+i)\,u(x,y)$, where $u\colon\mathbb{R}^2\t
o\mathbb{R}$ is $C^{1}$. Write $f = U + iV$ with
$$U(x,y) = u(x,y), \qquad V(x,y) = u(x,y).$$

Since $u\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$, both $U$ and $V$ are $C^{1}$, so $f$ is holo
morphic on $\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}$ if and only if the **Cauchy–Riemann e
quations** hold everywhere:
$$U_{x} = V_{y}, \qquad U_{y} = -V_{x}.$$

Substituting $U=V=u$:
$$u_{x} = u_{y}, \qquad u_{y} = -u_{x}.$$

From the first equation $u_{y}=u_{x}$; inserting this into the second gives
$$u_{x} = -u_{x} \;\Longrightarrow\; 2u_{x}=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; u_{x}\equiv 0.
$$
Then $u_{y}=u_{x}\equiv 0$ as well. Thus both partial derivatives of $u$ vanish
identically on $\mathbb{R}^{2}$.

Since $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ is connected and $u$ is $C^{1}$ with $\nabla u \equiv 0$,
 $u$ is **constant**: there exists $c\in\mathbb{R}$ with $u(x,y)=c$ for all $(x,
y)$.

**Conversely**, if $u\equiv c$ is constant, then $f(z) = (1+i)c$ is a constant (
hence entire, in particular holomorphic) function. So every constant $u$ is a so
lution.

**Conclusion.** The $C^{1}$ functions $u\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}$ for w
hich $f(x+iy)=u(x,y)+i\,u(x,y)$ is holomorphic are exactly the **constant functi
ons**. In particular, yes — the only such functions are the constants.
