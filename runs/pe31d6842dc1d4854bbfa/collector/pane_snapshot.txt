**Answer: No.** Here is a counterexample in Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}$.

Let
$$A = [0,1]\cup[2,\infty), \qquad B = [0,1]\cup\{2\}.$$

**Both are closed.** $[0,1]$ and $[2,\infty)$ are closed, so their union $A$ is
closed; $[0,1]$ and $\{2\}$ are closed, so their union $B$ is closed.

**Their boundaries coincide.**
$$\operatorname{int}(A) = (0,1)\cup(2,\infty), \qquad \operatorname{int}(B) = (0
,1).$$
Since both sets are closed, $\partial A = A\setminus\operatorname{int}(A)$ and $
\partial B = B\setminus\operatorname{int}(B)$:
$$\partial A = \bigl([0,1]\cup[2,\infty)\bigr)\setminus\bigl((0,1)\cup(2,\infty)
\bigr)=\{0,1,2\},$$
$$\partial B = \bigl([0,1]\cup\{2\}\bigr)\setminus(0,1)=\{0,1,2\}.$$
Hence $\partial A = \partial B = \{0,1,2\}$.

**Their interiors intersect non-trivially.**
$$\operatorname{int}(A)\cap\operatorname{int}(B)=\bigl((0,1)\cup(2,\infty)\bigr)
\cap(0,1)=(0,1)\neq\varnothing,$$
and $(0,1)$ is itself a nonempty open set, so the intersection is non-trivial in
 the strongest sense.

**Yet $A\neq B$.** For instance, $3\in A$ but $3\notin B$ (equivalently, $[2,\in
fty)\subset A$ contributes an entire ray that $B$ lacks, retaining only the sing
le point $2$).

Thus all three hypotheses are satisfied — $A$ and $B$ are closed, share the same
 boundary $\{0,1,2\}$, and have interiors meeting in the nonempty open set $(0,1
)$ — but $A\neq B$.

**Why this works.** For a closed set $X$, we have $X=\operatorname{int}(X)\sqcup
\partial X$, so $X$ is determined by the pair $(\operatorname{int}(X),\partial X
)$. Sharing the boundary $\partial X$ only forces the *complement of the interio
r* to agree; it does not force the interiors to agree. The point $2\in\partial A
=\partial B$ is a one-sided boundary point of both sets: it is approached by int
erior from the right in $A$ (via $(2,\infty)$) but not in $B$, while the conditi
on of being a boundary point only requires that *some* neighborhood portion lie
outside the set — which is supplied by the left side $(1,2)$ being absent from b
oth interiors. This freedom lets the two closed sets attach different "interior
material" on the same side of a shared boundary point while keeping that point o
n the boundary of each. Consequently the stated conditions do not force $A=B$.
