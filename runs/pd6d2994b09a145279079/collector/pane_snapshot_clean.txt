## Solution

We find the limit $\displaystyle L(y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{y}{2n}\right)
^n H_n\!\left(\frac{n}{y}\right)$ for real $y\neq 0$ (the result extends continu
ously to $y=0$).

**Integral representation of $H_n$.** From the Rodrigues formula $H_n(x)=(-1)^n
e^{x^2}\frac{d^n}{dx^n}e^{-x^2}$ and the Fourier transform of a Gaussian,
$$e^{-x^2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-t^2}e^{2ixt}\,dt,$$
we differentiate under the integral $n$ times:
$$\frac{d^n}{dx^n}e^{-x^2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-t^2}(2it
)^n e^{2ixt}\,dt.$$
Therefore
$$H_n(x)=\frac{(-2i)^n e^{x^2}}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^n e^{-t^2+2ixt
}\,dt=\frac{(-2i)^n}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^n e^{-(t-ix)^2}\,dt,$$
since $-t^2+2ixt+x^2=-(t-ix)^2$. Shifting the contour back to the real axis (jus
tified by Cauchy's theorem and the decay of $e^{-u^2}$) and substituting $u=t-ix
$:
$$H_n(x)=\frac{(-2i)^n}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(u+ix)^n e^{-u^2}\,du.$$
Writing $(u+ix)^n=i^n(x-iu)^n$ and absorbing $(-2i)^n i^n=2^n$, then using the s
ubstitution $u\mapsto -t$ (the Gaussian is even):
$$\boxed{H_n(x)=\frac{2^n}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(x+it)^n e^{-t^2}\,dt
.}\tag{1}$$
(One verifies directly: $n=0$ gives $1$; $n=1$ gives $2x$; $n=2$ gives $4x^2-2$.
)

**Applying the representation.** Setting $x=n/y$ in (1):
$$\left(\frac{y}{2n}\right)^n H_n\!\left(\frac{n}{y}\right)=\frac{y^n}{(2n)^n}\c
dot\frac{2^n}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\left(\frac{n}{y}+it\right)^n e^
{-t^2}\,dt=\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{iyt}{n}\right)
^n e^{-t^2}\,dt,\tag{2}$$
since $\dfrac{y^n}{n^n}\!\left(\dfrac{n}{y}+it\right)^n=\left(1+\dfrac{iyt}{n}\r
ight)^n$.

**Passing to the limit.** For each fixed $t$,
$$\left(1+\frac{iyt}{n}\right)^n \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} e^{iyt}.$$
To justify exchanging limit and integral via dominated convergence, bound the mo
dulus:
$$\left|\left(1+\frac{iyt}{n}\right)^n\right|=\left(1+\frac{y^2t^2}{n^2}\right)^
{n/2}\le \exp\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\cdot\frac{y^2t^2}{n^2}\right)=\exp\!\left(\frac
{y^2t^2}{2n}\right).$$
For all $n\ge N:=\lceil y^2\rceil$ (and any $N\ge 1$ when $y=0$), $\frac{y^2}{2n
}\le\frac12$, hence
$$\left|\left(1+\frac{iyt}{n}\right)^n e^{-t^2}\right|\le e^{t^2/2}\,e^{-t^2}=e^
{-t^2/2}\in L^1(\mathbb R),$$
with the bound independent of $n$. Dominated convergence applies to (2):
$$L(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{iyt}\,e^{-t^2}\,dt.$$

**Evaluating the Gaussian integral.** Completing the square, $-t^2+iyt=-(t-iy/2)
^2-y^2/4$, so
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-t^2+iyt}\,dt=e^{-y^2/4}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-(
t-iy/2)^2}\,dt=\sqrt\pi\,e^{-y^2/4}.$$
(The contour shift back to $\mathbb R$ is again justified by Cauchy's theorem.)

Therefore
$$\boxed{L(y)=e^{-y^2/4}.}$$

This holds for every real $y\neq 0$, and extends continuously to $y=0$ (where th
e limit equals $1=e^0$).
