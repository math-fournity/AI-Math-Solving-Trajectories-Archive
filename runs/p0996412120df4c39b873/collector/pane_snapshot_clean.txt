## Answer

**No.** Theorem 1 of Gidas–Ni–Nirenberg (*Symmetry and related properties via th
e maximum principle*, Comm. Pure Appl. Math., 1979) does **not** ensure boundedn
ess of the radial solution $u(r)$ on $[0,R]$ as a *conclusion*. Boundedness ente
rs the theorem as a **hypothesis** (via the assumed regularity of the solution),
 not as something the theorem produces.

### What Theorem 1 actually states

Theorem 1 concerns a classical positive solution of a semilinear elliptic equati
on on a ball. In its standard form it reads roughly:

> Let $u \in C^{2}(B_{R})\cap C(\overline{B_{R}})$ be a **positive** solution of
> $$\Delta u + f(u) = 0 \quad\text{in } B_{R}=\{x:|x|<R\}, \qquad u=0 \quad\text
{on }\partial B_{R},$$
> where $f$ is locally Lipschitz. Then $u$ is radially symmetric ($u(x)=u(r)$, $
r=|x|$) and strictly decreasing in $r$, i.e. $\frac{du}{dr}<0$ for $0<r<R$.

The **conclusion** of the theorem is **radial symmetry and monotonicity** — not
boundedness.

### Why boundedness is a hypothesis, not a conclusion

The regularity assumption $u\in C^{2}(B_{R})\cap C(\overline{B_{R}})$ already en
tails:

1. **Continuity on the closed ball.** Since $u\in C(\overline{B_{R}})$ and $\ove
rline{B_{R}}$ is compact, $u$ is bounded on $\overline{B_{R}}$, hence on $[0,R]$
 after the symmetry reduction.
2. **Boundary condition.** The requirement $u=0$ on $\partial B_{R}$ is meaningf
ul only because $u$ extends continuously to the boundary, which again presuppose
s boundedness up to $r=R$.

Thus the boundedness of $u$ on $0\le r\le R$ is **built into the hypotheses** of
 Theorem 1 through the assumed classical regularity and the boundary condition.
The theorem takes boundedness for granted and uses it (together with the maximum
 principle and the method of moving planes) to deduce symmetry.

### The logical direction

The dependence is:

$$\underbrace{u\in C^{2}(B_{R})\cap C(\overline{B_{R}}),\; u>0,\; u|_{\partial B
_{R}}=0}_{\text{hypotheses (which already imply boundedness)}} \;\Longrightarrow
\; \underbrace{u \text{ is radial and } u_{r}<0}_{\text{conclusion of Theorem 1}
}.$$

Boundedness sits on the **left** side of the implication. Theorem 1 does not der
ive it; it **consumes** it. If one were handed only a weak (e.g., unbounded or n
on-continuous-up-to-boundary) solution, Theorem 1 would not even apply, and it c
ertainly could not be invoked to *prove* that such a solution is bounded.

### Conclusion

Theorem 1 of Gidas–Ni–Nirenberg guarantees **radial symmetry and radial monotoni
city** of a positive classical solution under stated regularity hypotheses. The
boundedness of $u(r)$ on $[0,R]$ is a **prerequisite** supplied by the hypothesi
s $u\in C(\overline{B_{R}})$ (compactness + continuity), not a consequence that
the theorem ensures. Therefore the answer to the question is **no**: Theorem 1 d
oes not ensure boundedness; it presupposes it.
