**Answer: Yes.** The condition implies $f(z) \to 0$ as $z \to z_0$, and $z_0$ is
 a removable singularity.

---

## Proof

**Setup.** Let $f$ be analytic on the punctured disk $\{z : 0 < |z - z_0| < r\}$
. The hypothesis is:

> (H) For every sequence $\{z_n\}$ with $z_n \to z_0$ and $z_n \neq z_0$, there
exists a subsequence $\{z_{r_n}\}$ such that $f(z_{r_n}) \to 0$.

**Step 1: The subsequence condition forces the full limit.**

We prove $f(z) \to 0$ as $z \to z_0$ by contradiction. Suppose $f(z) \not\to 0$
as $z \to z_0$. Then there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that for every $\delta
> 0$, we can find a point $z$ with $0 < |z - z_0| < \delta$ and $|f(z)| \geq \va
repsilon$.

Taking $\delta = 1/n$ for $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$, we obtain a sequence $\{z_n\}$
with $z_n \to z_0$, $z_n \neq z_0$, and
$$|f(z_n)| \geq \varepsilon \quad \text{for all } n.$$

By hypothesis (H), this sequence has a subsequence $\{z_{r_n}\}$ with $f(z_{r_n}
) \to 0$. But every term of this subsequence still satisfies $|f(z_{r_n})| \geq
\varepsilon > 0$, so $f(z_{r_n})$ cannot converge to $0$. **Contradiction.**

Therefore $f(z) \to 0$ as $z \to z_0$. $\square$

**Step 2: $z_0$ is a removable singularity.**

Since $f$ is analytic on the punctured disk and $\lim_{z \to z_0} f(z) = 0$ exis
ts (and is finite), Riemann's theorem on removable singularities applies directl
y: $z_0$ is a removable singularity of $f$. Defining
$$\tilde{f}(z) = \begin{cases} f(z) & \text{if } 0 < |z - z_0| < r, \\ 0 & \text
{if } z = z_0, \end{cases}$$
yields a function analytic on the full disk $D_r(z_0)$.

---

## Remark on the key idea

The argument in Step 1 is a general metric-space fact: the condition "every sequ
ence converging to $z_0$ has a subsequence whose image converges to $L$" is **eq
uivalent** to "$f(z) \to L$ as $z \to z_0$." The forward direction (subsequence
$\Rightarrow$ full limit) is proved by the contrapositive above: if the full lim
it fails, one extracts a sequence staying uniformly far from $L$, which then adm
its no subsequence converging to $L$. The analyticity of $f$ is not needed for t
his implication—it is used only in Step 2 to invoke Riemann's theorem.
