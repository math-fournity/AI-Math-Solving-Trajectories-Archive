## Step 1: The condition forces T to vanish on compactly supported functions

Let $D \subset L^2(\mathbb{R})$ be the subspace of compactly supported $L^2$ fun
ctions. Fix any $f \in D$. The hypothesis gives
$$\langle Tf, g\rangle = 0 \quad \text{for all } g \in D.$$
Since $T: L^2 \to L^2$, we have $Tf \in L^2$. The map $g \mapsto \langle Tf, g\r
angle$ is a **continuous** linear functional on $L^2$ (by Cauchy–Schwarz, $|\lan
gle Tf,g\rangle| \le \|Tf\|\,\|g\|$). Since $D$ is dense in $L^2$ and this funct
ional vanishes on $D$, it vanishes on all of $L^2$. Hence $\langle Tf, g\rangle
= 0$ for every $g \in L^2$, which forces
$$Tf = 0 \quad \text{for every } f \in D.$$

Note: this step used only that $Tf \in L^2$ (given) and the continuity of the in
ner product — **not** any continuity of $T$ itself.

## Step 2: Why this does NOT imply $T = 0$ in general

The conclusion $Tf = 0$ on $D$ extends to $T = 0$ on all of $L^2$ **only if $T$
is continuous (bounded)**, because then $T$ vanishing on a dense subspace forces
 $T$ to vanish everywhere by continuity.

But the problem only assumes $T$ is **linear** (defined on all of $L^2$, mapping
 into $L^2$), with no continuity hypothesis. For a merely linear — possibly disc
ontinuous — operator, vanishing on a dense subspace does **not** force vanishing
 everywhere.

## Step 3: Explicit counterexample (Hamel basis construction)

$D$ is a proper dense subspace of $L^2(\mathbb{R})$: for instance, the Gaussian
$h(x) = e^{-x^2}$ belongs to $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ but is **not** compactly supporte
d, so $h \in L^2 \setminus D$.

Choose a Hamel basis $B_D$ of the vector space $D$, and extend it (using the Axi
om of Choice) to a Hamel basis $B = B_D \cup B'$ of $L^2(\mathbb{R})$, with $h \
in B'$. Define a linear map $T: L^2 \to L^2$ by specifying it on $B$:
$$T(b) = 0 \;\text{for all } b \in B_D, \qquad T(h) = h, \qquad T(b) = 0 \;\text
{for all other } b \in B',$$
and extend $T$ linearly to all of $L^2$ via the Hamel-basis representation. Then
:

- $T$ is a well-defined linear operator $L^2(\mathbb{R}) \to L^2(\mathbb{R})$ (e
very $L^2$ vector is a *finite* linear combination of Hamel-basis elements, so $
T$ of it is a finite linear combination of $L^2$ vectors, hence in $L^2$).
- For every compactly supported $f \in D$: $f$ is a finite linear combination of
 elements of $B_D$, so $Tf = 0$. Therefore $\langle Tf, g\rangle = 0$ for **all*
* $g$ (hence all compactly supported $g$). ✓
- Yet $T \neq 0$, since $Th = h \neq 0$. ✓

This $T$ satisfies the hypothesis $\langle Tf, g\rangle = 0$ for all compactly s
upported $f, g$, but is **not** the zero operator.

## Step 4: The bounded case (for completeness)

If one additionally assumes $T$ is **bounded**, then the answer becomes **yes**:
 $T = 0$ on the dense subspace $D$ implies $T = 0$ on $L^2$ by continuity, since
 for any $f \in L^2$ and any sequence $f_n \in D$ with $f_n \to f$, we have $Tf
= \lim Tf_n = 0$.

## Conclusion

Under the problem's stated hypotheses — $T$ merely linear, with no continuity/bo
undedness assumption — **$T$ is not necessarily the zero operator**. The natural
 "density argument" that would force $T = 0$ requires $T$ to be continuous, and
discontinuous linear operators on $L^2$ (constructed via a Hamel basis) can vani
sh on the dense subspace of compactly supported functions while being nonzero el
