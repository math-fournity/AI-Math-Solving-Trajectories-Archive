**Answer: Yes, $\check{H}^1(Y;\mathbb{Z})$ must also be trivial.**

## Proof

We use the classical characterization of tree-like continua via Čech cohomology.

**Key Theorem (Shape Theory / Continuum Theory).** *For a 1-dimensional continuu
m $X$, the following are equivalent:*
1. *$X$ is tree-like (i.e., for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a finite t
ree $T$ and a continuous surjection $f: X \to T$ with $\operatorname{diam}(f^{-1
}(t)) < \varepsilon$ for all $t \in T$).*
2. *$\check{H}^1(X;\mathbb{Z}) = 0$.*
3. *$X$ has trivial shape (the shape of a point).*

*Justification of the theorem.* For a continuum, $\check{H}^0(X;\mathbb{Z}) \con
g \mathbb{Z}$. Since $\dim X \leq 1$, we have $\check{H}^n(X;\mathbb{Z}) = 0$ fo
r all $n \geq 2$. By the Universal Coefficient Theorem for Čech cohomology (whic
h holds for compact spaces), $\check{H}^1(X;G) \cong \check{H}^1(X;\mathbb{Z}) \
otimes G$ for any abelian group $G$ (the Tor term involves $\check{H}^2$, which
vanishes). Thus $\check{H}^1(X;\mathbb{Z}) = 0$ implies $\check{H}^1(X;G) = 0$ f
or **all** coefficient groups $G$.

For 1-dimensional compacta, the shape is completely determined by the first Čech
 cohomology group (this is the shape-theoretic Whitehead theorem in low dimensio
ns: all higher homotopy/cohomology invariants vanish for dimension reasons, and
$\check{H}^0 \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is fixed for continua). Therefore $\check{H}^1(X;
\mathbb{Z}) = 0$ forces $X$ to have trivial shape. A 1-dimensional continuum wit
h trivial shape is tree-like, since the only 1-dimensional polyhedron (ANR) with
 trivial shape is a point, and tree-like continua are exactly the inverse limits
 of trees — equivalently, the 1-dimensional continua with the shape of a point.
Conversely, tree-like continua are inverse limits of trees, each having $\check{
H}^1 = 0$, and Čech cohomology is continuous (preserves inverse limits of contin
ua with surjective bonding maps), giving $\check{H}^1(X;\mathbb{Z}) = 0$. $\squa
re$

**Main Argument.** Let $X$ be a 1-dimensional continuum with $\check{H}^1(X;\mat
hbb{Z}) = 0$, and let $Y$ be a subcontinuum of $X$.

**Step 1: $X$ is tree-like.** By the Key Theorem (direction $(2) \Rightarrow (1)
$), $X$ is tree-like.

**Step 2: $Y$ is tree-like.** Since $X$ is tree-like, for every $\varepsilon > 0
$ there exists a finite tree $T$ and a continuous surjection $f: X \to T$ with $
\operatorname{diam}(f^{-1}(t)) < \varepsilon$ for all $t \in T$. Consider the re
striction $f|_Y : Y \to f(Y)$. Since $Y$ is a continuum and $f$ is continuous, $
f(Y)$ is a subcontinuum of the tree $T$. A subcontinuum of a tree is itself a tr
ee (or a point, which is a degenerate tree). For each $s \in f(Y)$:
$$(f|_Y)^{-1}(s) = f^{-1}(s) \cap Y \subseteq f^{-1}(s),$$
so $\operatorname{diam}((f|_Y)^{-1}(s)) \leq \operatorname{diam}(f^{-1}(s)) < \v
arepsilon$.

Thus $f|_Y : Y \to f(Y)$ is an $\varepsilon$-map onto a tree, for every $\vareps
ilon > 0$. Therefore $Y$ is tree-like.

**Step 3: $Y$ is 1-dimensional (if non-degenerate).** Since $Y \subseteq X$ and
$\dim X = 1$, the subspace theorem for covering dimension gives $\dim Y \leq 1$.
 If $Y$ is non-degenerate (more than one point), then $Y$ is a non-degenerate co
ntinuum, hence connected and not totally disconnected, so $\dim Y \geq 1$. There
fore $\dim Y = 1$. (If $Y$ is a single point, $\check{H}^1(Y;\mathbb{Z}) = 0$ tr
ivially.)

**Step 4: $\check{H}^1(Y;\mathbb{Z}) = 0$.** Since $Y$ is a 1-dimensional tree-l
ike continuum, the Key Theorem (direction $(1) \Rightarrow (2)$) gives $\check{H
}^1(Y;\mathbb{Z}) = 0$.

**Conclusion.** For any subcontinuum $Y$ of a 1-dimensional continuum $X$ with $
\check{H}^1(X;\mathbb{Z}) = 0$, we have $\check{H}^1(Y;\mathbb{Z}) = 0$. The tri
viality of the first Čech cohomology group is inherited by subcontinua in dimens
ion one. $\blacksquare$
