## Solution

**Claim:** The integral $\displaystyle\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1+x^5}}$ cannot be ex
pressed in terms of elementary functions.

**Proof.**

We apply **Chebyshev's theorem on binomial differentials**:

> **Theorem (Chebyshev, 1853).** The integral
> $$\int x^p\,(a+bx^n)^q\,dx,$$
> where $a,b\neq 0$ are constants and $p,q,n$ are rational numbers (with $n\neq
0$), is expressible in terms of elementary functions **if and only if** at least
 one of the following three quantities is an integer:
> $$\frac{p+1}{n}, \qquad q, \qquad \frac{p+1}{n}+q.$$

*Sketch of the theorem's proof (for completeness).* By Liouville's theorem, if $
f(x)=x^p(a+bx^n)^q$ has an elementary antiderivative, then it must take the form
$$\int f\,dx = R(x)\,(a+bx^n)^{q+1} \;+\; \sum_i c_i \ln\phi_i(x),$$
where $R$ is a rational function. Substituting $t=x^n$ (after clearing denominat
ors of $p,q$ via $x=t^d$) reduces the problem to analyzing when a certain ration
al-function identity can hold. A careful coefficient comparison (differentiating
 the ansatz and matching powers of $x$ and $(a+bx^n)$) shows that the rational f
unction $R$ exists precisely when one of the three conditions above is satisfied
. When $\frac{p+1}{n}\in\mathbb{Z}$, the substitution $u=(a+bx^n)^{1/n}$ rationa
lizes the integrand; when $q\in\mathbb{Z}$, the integrand is already a rational
function of $x$ (after clearing denominators); when $\frac{p+1}{n}+q\in\mathbb{Z
}$, the substitution $u=\left(\frac{a+bx^n}{x^n}\right)^{1/n}$ rationalizes it.
In all other cases, no elementary antiderivative exists. $\square$

**Application to our integral.** We rewrite:
$$\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1+x^5}} = \int x^0\,(1+x^5)^{-1/2}\,dx.$$

This is a binomial differential with:
$$p = 0, \quad a = 1, \quad b = 1, \quad n = 5, \quad q = -\tfrac{1}{2}.$$

We check the three conditions of Chebyshev's theorem:

1. $\displaystyle\frac{p+1}{n} = \frac{0+1}{5} = \frac{1}{5}.$ This is **not** a
n integer.

2. $\displaystyle q = -\frac{1}{2}.$ This is **not** an integer.

3. $\displaystyle\frac{p+1}{n}+q = \frac{1}{5}+\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac
{2-5}{10} = -\frac{3}{10}.$ This is **not** an integer.

None of the three quantities is an integer. Therefore, by Chebyshev's theorem, t
he integral

$$\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1+x^5}}$$

**is not expressible in terms of elementary functions.** $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The algebraic curve $y^2 = 1+x^5$ has genus $g = \left\lfloor\frac{5
-1}{2}\right\rfloor = 2$, so this is a hyperelliptic integral of genus 2. Such i
ntegrals are, in general, not reducible to elementary functions or even to ellip
tic (genus-1) integrals. The antiderivative can be expressed in terms of hyperge
ometric functions or Lauricella functions, but not in closed elementary form.
