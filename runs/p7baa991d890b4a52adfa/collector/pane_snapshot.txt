**Answer: No, in general $|f_n|$ does not converge weakly to $|f|$.**

## Counterexample

Work in $L^p(\mathbb{R})$ (so $d=1$; this suffices to disprove the claim). Let
$$f_n(x) = \sin(nx)\,\chi_{[0,2\pi]}(x), \qquad f \equiv 0.$$

### Step 1. $f_n \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^p(\mathbb{R})$.

Let $q$ be the conjugate exponent ($1/p+1/q=1$) and let $g\in L^q(\mathbb{R})$ b
e arbitrary. Then
$$\langle f_n, g\rangle = \int_0^{2\pi} g(x)\sin(nx)\,dx.$$
Since $[0,2\pi]$ has finite measure, $g\,\chi_{[0,2\pi]}\in L^1([0,2\pi])$. By t
he Riemann–Lebesgue lemma (the Fourier coefficients of any $L^1$ function vanish
 at infinity),
$$\int_0^{2\pi} g(x)\sin(nx)\,dx \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 0.$$
As $g\in L^q(\mathbb{R})$ was arbitrary, $f_n\rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^p(\mathbb{
R})$.

### Step 2. $|f_n|\not\rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^p(\mathbb{R})$.

We have $|f_n|(x)=|\sin(nx)|\,\chi_{[0,2\pi]}(x)$. Take the test function $g=\ch
i_{[0,2\pi]}\in L^q(\mathbb{R})$. Then
$$\langle |f_n|, g\rangle = \int_0^{2\pi}|\sin(nx)|\,dx.$$
Substituting $u=nx$,
$$\int_0^{2\pi}|\sin(nx)|\,dx = \frac{1}{n}\int_0^{2\pi n}|\sin u|\,du = \frac{1
}{n}\cdot n\int_0^{2\pi}|\sin u|\,du = \int_0^{2\pi}|\sin u|\,du = 4,$$
using that $|\sin|$ has period $\pi$ and $\int_0^{\pi}\sin u\,du=2$. Hence
$$\langle |f_n|, g\rangle \equiv 4 \not\to 0 = \langle |f|, g\rangle.$$

So $|f_n|$ does **not** converge weakly to $|f|=0$.

## Conclusion

The map $f\mapsto |f|$ is **not** weakly continuous on $L^p(\mathbb{R}^d)$ for $
1<p<\infty$. Weak convergence $f_n\rightharpoonup f$ does not imply $|f_n|\right
harpoonup |f|$.

(Intuitively: weak convergence only "sees" averages against $L^q$ test functions
, and oscillations can cancel in those averages while the *amplitude* $|f_n|$ ha
s nothing left to cancel—so its averages against a fixed positive test function
stay bounded away from zero.)
