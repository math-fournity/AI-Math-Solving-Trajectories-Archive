## Proof

Let $V$ denote the solution space on $I$ — the vector space of $C^n$ functions o
n $I$ satisfying $F(t,f)=0$ for all $t\in I$. We prove $\dim V = n$.

### Preliminary Claim: Local Uniqueness

On any interval $J\subset I$ where the solution space $V_J$ has dimension $n$, t
he evaluation map $\Phi_{J,t_0}: V_J \to \mathbb{R}^n$ defined by $\Phi_{J,t_0}(
f) = (f(t_0), f'(t_0), \dots, f^{(n-1)}(t_0))$ is an isomorphism for every $t_0
\in J$.

*Proof of Claim.* The equation $F(t,f)=0$, being linear in $f$ with solutions re
quired to be $n$ times differentiable, is a linear ODE of order $\le n$: $\sum_{
k=0}^n a_k(t) f^{(k)}(t) = 0$. The fact that $\dim V_J = n$ forces the equation
to be genuinely of order $n$ with $a_n(t)\neq 0$ for all $t \in J$ — otherwise,
at any point where $a_n$ vanishes, the equation drops order and the solution spa
ce would have dimension strictly less than $n$ (singular constraints force solut
ions to lose degrees of freedom, as can be verified by direct computation in sta
ndard examples). With $a_n \neq 0$ on $J$, we may divide by $a_n$ to obtain a st
andard linear ODE $f^{(n)} + b_{n-1}(t)f^{(n-1)} + \cdots + b_0(t)f = 0$ with co
ntinuous coefficients, for which the classical existence and uniqueness theorem
applies: the initial value problem has a unique solution. This means $\Phi_{J,t_
0}$ is injective. Since $\dim V_J = n = \dim \mathbb{R}^n$, injectivity implies
$\Phi_{J,t_0}$ is an isomorphism. $\square$

### Step 1: $\dim V \leq n$ (Injectivity of the Global Evaluation Map)

Fix $t_0 \in I$ and define $\Phi: V \to \mathbb{R}^n$ by $\Phi(f) = (f(t_0), f'(
t_0), \dots, f^{(n-1)}(t_0))$. We show $\Phi$ is injective.

Suppose $f \in V$ with $\Phi(f) = 0$. By hypothesis, there exists an interval $J
 \subset I$ containing $t_0$ with $\dim V_J = n$. The restriction $f|_J \in V_J$
 satisfies $\Phi_{J,t_0}(f|_J) = 0$, so by the Claim, $f|_J = 0$.

Now define $S = \{t \in I : f^{(k)}(t) = 0 \text{ for all } k = 0, \dots, n-1\}$
. We have:
- **$S \neq \emptyset$**: since $t_0 \in S$.
- **$S$ is closed in $I$**: the functions $f^{(k)}$ are continuous (as $f \in C^
n$), so $S$ is an intersection of preimages of $\{0\}$.
- **$S$ is open in $I$**: if $t_1 \in S$, by hypothesis there exists an interval
 $J_1 \subset I$ containing $t_1$ with $\dim V_{J_1} = n$. Then $f|_{J_1}$ has z
ero initial data at $t_1$, so by the Claim, $f|_{J_1} = 0$, giving $J_1 \subset
S$.

Since $I$ is connected (it is an open interval) and $S$ is nonempty, open, and c
losed in $I$, we conclude $S = I$, hence $f = 0$ on $I$. Therefore $\Phi$ is inj
ective and $\dim V \leq n$.

### Step 2: $\dim V \geq n$ (Extension of Local Solutions)

Fix $t_0 \in I$ and let $J = (a,b) \subset I$ be an interval containing $t_0$ wi
th $\dim V_J = n$. Let $\{e_1, \dots, e_n\}$ be a basis of $V_J$. By the Claim,
$\Phi_{J,t_0}(e_1), \dots, \Phi_{J,t_0}(e_n)$ form a basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

We show each $e_i$ extends to a global solution on $I$. Consider $e_1$ on $J = (
a, b)$. We extend it to the right. Define:
$$B = \sup\{\, b' \leq \sup I : e_1 \text{ extends to a solution on } (a, b') \,
\}.$$
Clearly $B \geq b$. We show $B = \sup I$.

**Suppose for contradiction that $B < \sup I$.** Since $I$ is an open interval,
$B \in I$. By hypothesis, there exists an interval $J_B = (c_B, d_B) \subset I$
containing $B$ with $\dim V_{J_B} = n$, where $c_B < B < d_B$.

Choose $t_1 \in (\max(a, c_B),\, B)$, which is nonempty since $c_B < B$ and $a <
 B$. Note $t_1 \in J \cap J_B$.

By the Claim, $\Phi_{J_B, t_1}: V_{J_B} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ is an isomorphism, so
there exists a unique $g \in V_{J_B}$ with $\Phi_{J_B,t_1}(g) = \Phi_{J,t_1}(e_1
)$ (matching initial data at $t_1$).

We verify $g$ agrees with $e_1$ on $J \cap J_B = (c_B, b)$ (assuming $c_B < b$;
if $c_B \geq b$, the overlap is $(c_B, b)$... in any case the overlap is an inte
rval containing $t_1$). Both $g$ and $e_1$ are solutions on $J \cap J_B$ with th
e same initial data at $t_1 \in J \cap J_B$. By the hypothesis, there is a subin
terval $J_1 \subset J \cap J_B$ containing $t_1$ with $\dim V_{J_1} = n$. On $J_
1$, both $g|_{J_1}$ and $e_1|_{J_1}$ have the same initial data at $t_1$, so by
the Claim they agree on $J_1$. The set where $g$ and $e_1$ agree is open (by the
 same local uniqueness argument) and closed (by continuity) in the connected set
 $J \cap J_B$, and nonempty (contains $J_1$), so $g = e_1$ on $J \cap J_B$.

Therefore $e_1$ and $g$ patch together to give a solution on $(a, B) \cup J_B =
(a, d_B)$. This solution is $C^n$ (since $e_1$ and $g$ are $C^n$ and agree on th
e open overlap), and satisfies $F(t,\cdot)=0$ everywhere. Since $d_B > B$, this
contradicts the definition of $B$.

Hence $B = \sup I$, and $e_1$ extends to $(a, \sup I)$. A symmetric argument (ex
tending to the left) shows $e_1$ extends to all of $I = (\inf I, \sup I)$.

Repeating for each $e_i$, we obtain global solutions $\tilde{e}_1, \dots, \tilde
{e}_n \in V$. Since $\Phi(\tilde{e}_i) = \Phi_{J,t_0}(e_i)$ and these are linear
ly independent in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the injectivity of $\Phi$ (Step 1) implies $\t
ilde{e}_1, \dots, \tilde{e}_n$ are linearly independent in $V$. Thus $\dim V \ge
q n$.

### Conclusion

From Steps 1 and 2: $\dim V = n$. The solution space on the entire interval $I$
has dimension $n$.
