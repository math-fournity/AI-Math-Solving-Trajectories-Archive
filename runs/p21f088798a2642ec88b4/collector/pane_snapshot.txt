Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether contraction of general fibe
rs of $g$ by $f$ forces contraction of all fibers.

**Answer: No, it is not true in general.** Here is a counterexample.

---

**Setup.** Let $C$ be a nodal projective curve (e.g., a nodal plane cubic), and
let $h:\mathbb{P}^1 \to C$ be its normalization. Denote the node by $c_0 \in C$,
 so $h^{-1}(c_0) = \{p_1, p_2\}$ with $p_1 \neq p_2 \in \mathbb{P}^1$. Over ever
y other point $c \in C \setminus \{c_0\}$, the fiber $h^{-1}(c)$ is a single poi
nt.

**The varieties and maps.** Set:
- $X = \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ (a smooth, hence normal, projective sur
face),
- $Y = \mathbb{P}^1$ (smooth, hence normal, projective),
- $S = X$ (a surface in $X$),
- $f = \mathrm{pr}_1 : X \to Y$, the first projection, so $f(p,q) = p$,
- $g' = \mathrm{pr}_1 : S \to \mathbb{P}^1$, also the first projection,
- $g = h \circ g' : S \to C$, so $g(p,q) = h(p)$.

**Verification of the hypotheses.**

1. **$f:X\to Y$ is a morphism of normal projective varieties.** Both $X$ and $Y$
 are smooth projective varieties. ✓

2. **$S \subseteq X$ is a surface admitting $g:S\to C$.** $S=X$ is a surface, an
d $g = h\circ g'$ is a composition of morphisms (projection and finite normaliza
tion map), hence a morphism. ✓

3. **Every fiber of $g$ is a curve.** For any $c \in C$:
   $$g^{-1}(c) = h^{-1}(c) \times \mathbb{P}^1.$$
   For $c \neq c_0$: $h^{-1}(c) = \{p\}$, so $g^{-1}(c) = \{p\}\times\mathbb{P}^
1 \cong \mathbb{P}^1$, which is a curve. ✓
   For $c = c_0$: $h^{-1}(c_0) = \{p_1,p_2\}$, so $g^{-1}(c_0) = (\{p_1\}\times\
mathbb{P}^1)\cup(\{p_2\}\times\mathbb{P}^1)$, a union of two curves, hence 1-dim
ensional. ✓

4. **The general fiber of $g$ is contracted to a point by $f$.** For general $c
\in C$, the fiber is $g^{-1}(c) = \{p\}\times\mathbb{P}^1$ where $h(p)=c$. Then
$f(\{p\}\times\mathbb{P}^1) = \{p\}$, a single point. ✓

**The special fiber is NOT contracted.** The fiber over the node is:
$$g^{-1}(c_0) = (\{p_1\}\times\mathbb{P}^1)\cup(\{p_2\}\times\mathbb{P}^1).$$
Applying $f$:
$$f(g^{-1}(c_0)) = f(\{p_1\}\times\mathbb{P}^1) \cup f(\{p_2\}\times\mathbb{P}^1
) = \{p_1\}\cup\{p_2\} = \{p_1, p_2\}.$$
Since $p_1 \neq p_2$, this is **two distinct points**, not a single point. There
fore $g^{-1}(c_0)$ is **not** contracted by $f$.

---

**Remark on why this works.** The key mechanism is that $C$ is non-normal at the
 node. The normalization $h:\mathbb{P}^1\to C$ separates the node into two disti
nct points $p_1, p_2$ in the normalization. The map $f = g' = \mathrm{pr}_1$ fac
tors through the normalization $\mathbb{P}^1$ (not through $C$ itself), so it di
stinguishes $p_1$ from $p_2$. The fibration $g = h\circ g'$, however, identifies
 $p_1$ and $p_2$ (both mapping to the node $c_0$), so $g^{-1}(c_0)$ has two conn
ected components that $f$ sends to different points.

If $C$ were assumed smooth (hence normal), the answer would be **yes**: the imag
e of $(g, f|_S): S \to C\times Y$ would be a curve $Z$ with $Z\to C$ finite bira
tional, hence an isomorphism by normality of $C$, forcing $f|_S$ to factor throu
gh $g$ and contract every fiber. The counterexample exploits precisely the non-n
ormality of $C$.
