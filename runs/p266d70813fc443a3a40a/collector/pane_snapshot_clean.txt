## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. An inner product on $\mathbb{Q}^n$ involving only rational coeff
icients and equivalent to the standard inner product exists; in fact, the standa
rd Euclidean inner product itself, restricted to $\mathbb{Q}^n$, is such an inne
r product.

**Construction.** On the $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space $\mathbb{Q}^n$, define
$$
\langle x, y \rangle \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i\, y_i, \qquad x=(x_1,\dots,x_n),\;
 y=(y_1,\dots,y_n)\in\mathbb{Q}^n.
$$

**Verification of the inner-product axioms.**

1. **Rational-valued with rational coefficients.** For $x,y\in\mathbb{Q}^n$, eac
h product $x_i y_i\in\mathbb{Q}$, so $\langle x,y\rangle\in\mathbb{Q}$. The coef
ficient matrix is the identity $I_n$, all of whose entries are rational. Hence t
he inner product "involves only rational coefficients."

2. **Bilinearity over $\mathbb{Q}$.** For $a,b\in\mathbb{Q}$ and $x,y,z\in\mathb
b{Q}^n$,
   $$\langle ax+by,\, z\rangle = \sum_i (ax_i+by_i)z_i = a\sum_i x_i z_i + b\sum
_i y_i z_i = a\langle x,z\rangle + b\langle y,z\rangle,$$
   and similarly in the second argument. So $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is $\mat
hbb{Q}$-bilinear.

3. **Symmetry.** $\langle x,y\rangle = \sum x_i y_i = \sum y_i x_i = \langle y,x
\rangle$.

4. **Positive definiteness.** $\langle x,x\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2$. Each $x
_i\in\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ satisfies $x_i^2\geq 0$, with $x_i^2=0\iff x
_i=0$. Hence $\langle x,x\rangle\geq 0$, and $\langle x,x\rangle=0\iff x_i=0\;\f
orall i\iff x=0$.

Thus $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is a genuine inner product on $\mathbb{Q}^n$ wi
th only rational coefficients.

**Equivalence to the standard inner product.** This inner product *is* the stand
ard Euclidean inner product (restricted from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{Q}^n$).
It is therefore trivially equivalent to itself. In particular, the norm it induc
es, $\|x\|=\sqrt{\sum x_i^2}$, is the standard Euclidean norm, so it generates t
he standard Euclidean topology on $\mathbb{Q}^n$ (the subspace topology inherite
d from $\mathbb{R}^n$), and its completion is $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the standard i
nner product.

**Remark (non-triviality of the question).** One might worry that defining an in
ner product "equivalent to the standard one" requires irrational coefficients (e
.g., rescaling by $\sqrt{2}$). The above shows this is unnecessary: the standard
 inner product already lives entirely over $\mathbb{Q}$. More generally, any sym
metric positive-definite matrix $A\in\mathrm{Mat}_n(\mathbb{Q})$ defines a $\mat
hbb{Q}$-valued inner product $\langle x,y\rangle_A = x^{\mathsf T}\!A y$ on $\ma
thbb{Q}^n$ that is topologically equivalent to the standard one (since all norms
 on a finite-dimensional space are equivalent). However, such an inner product i
s *isometrically* equivalent (rationally congruent) to the standard one only whe
n $A$ is congruent to $I_n$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, which by Hasse–Minkowski requires
 $\det A\in(\mathbb{Q}^*)^2$ and matching local Hasse invariants. The identity m
atrix $A=I_n$ satisfies these conditions trivially.

**Conclusion.** The standard inner product $\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_i x_i y_i$ o
n $\mathbb{Q}^n$ involves only rational coefficients (the identity matrix) and i
s equivalent to the standard inner product (being itself the standard inner prod
uct). Therefore, **yes**, such an inner product can be defined.
