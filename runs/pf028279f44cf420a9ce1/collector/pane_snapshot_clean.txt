**Theorem.** For $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $\varphi(n) \mid n$ if and only if $n \le 1
$ or $n = 2^a \cdot 3^b$ for some $a > 0$ and $b \ge 0$.

**Proof.**

**(⟸) We verify each case.**

- **$n = 0$:** $\varphi(0) = 0$ and $0 \mid 0$.
- **$n = 1$:** $\varphi(1) = 1$ and $1 \mid 1$.
- **$n = 2^a \cdot 3^b$ with $a > 0$, $b \ge 0$:**
  - If $b = 0$: $n = 2^a$, $\varphi(n) = 2^{a-1}$, and $2^{a-1} \mid 2^a$. ✓
  - If $b \ge 1$: By multiplicativity of $\varphi$ (since $\gcd(2^a, 3^b) = 1$),
    $$\varphi(n) = \varphi(2^a)\,\varphi(3^b) = 2^{a-1} \cdot 2 \cdot 3^{b-1} =
2^a \cdot 3^{b-1},$$
    and $2^a \cdot 3^{b-1} \mid 2^a \cdot 3^b$. ✓

**(⟹) Suppose $\varphi(n) \mid n$ and $n > 1$. We show $n = 2^a \cdot 3^b$ with
$a > 0$.**

Write $n = p_1^{e_1} \cdots p_k^{e_k}$ where $p_1 < \cdots < p_k$ are distinct p
rimes and $e_i \ge 1$. Then
$$\varphi(n) = \prod_{i=1}^{k} p_i^{e_i - 1}(p_i - 1).$$

The condition $\varphi(n) \mid n$ gives
$$\prod_{i=1}^{k} p_i^{e_i-1}(p_i - 1) \;\Big|\; \prod_{i=1}^{k} p_i^{e_i},$$
which simplifies to
$$\prod_{i=1}^{k} (p_i - 1) \;\Big|\; \prod_{i=1}^{k} p_i. \qquad (\ast)$$

The right-hand side is **squarefree** (a product of distinct primes, each to the
 first power). Therefore:

- **(A)** The product $\prod (p_i - 1)$ is squarefree: each $(p_i - 1)$ is squar
efree, and the $(p_i - 1)$ are **pairwise coprime** (no prime divides two of the
m).
- **(B)** Every prime factor of every $(p_i - 1)$ belongs to $\{p_1, \dots, p_k\
}$.

**Step 1: $2 \mid n$ (i.e., $a \ge 1$).**

If $n = 2^a$ we are done (take $b = 0$). Otherwise $n$ has an odd prime factor $
p_i$. Then $p_i - 1$ is even, so $2 \mid (p_i - 1)$. By (B), $2 \in \{p_1, \dots
, p_k\}$, so $2 \mid n$.

**Step 2: No prime $p \ge 5$ divides $n$.**

Suppose for contradiction that some $p \ge 5$ divides $n$. Since $p$ is odd, $2
\mid (p-1)$.

By **pairwise coprimality** (A), no other $(q - 1)$ can be divisible by $2$. But
 every odd prime $q$ satisfies $2 \mid (q - 1)$. Therefore:
- No odd prime other than $p$ can divide $n$.
- In particular, $3 \nmid n$ (since $3 - 1 = 2$ and $2 \mid (p-1)$ would give $2
 \mid \gcd(p-1,\, 3-1)$, violating pairwise coprimality).

So the only primes dividing $n$ are $\{2, p\}$. By (B), every prime factor of $(
p - 1)$ lies in $\{2, p\}$. Since $p \nmid (p - 1)$, the only prime factor of $(
p-1)$ is $2$, so $p - 1 = 2^j$ for some $j \ge 1$. By squarefreeness (A), $j = 1
$, giving $p - 1 = 2$, i.e., $p = 3$. This contradicts $p \ge 5$.

**Step 3: $n = 2^a \cdot 3^b$ with $a > 0$.**

From Steps 1–2, the only primes dividing $n$ are $2$ and $3$, so $n = 2^a \cdot
3^b$. We must show $a > 0$. If $a = 0$, then $n = 3^b$ with $b \ge 1$ (since $n
> 1$), and $\varphi(n) = 2 \cdot 3^{b-1}$. The divisibility $\varphi(n) \mid n$
would require $2 \mid 3^b$, which is impossible. Hence $a \ge 1$, i.e., $a > 0$.
 $\blacksquare$
