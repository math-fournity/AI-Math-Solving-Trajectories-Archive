## Proof

**Key Observation.** Every term in the sum is strictly positive (since $n/k^2 >
0$ for all $1 \leq k \leq n$). Therefore the partial sum is bounded below by any
 single term. I will show that the $k=1$ term alone diverges to $+\infty$.

**The $k=1$ term.** Setting $k=1$:

$$\left(\frac{n}{1^2}\right)^{\frac{n}{1^2}+1} = n^{\,n+1}.$$

As $n \to \infty$, $n^{n+1} = n \cdot n^n \to +\infty$ (both factors $n$ and $n^
n$ tend to $+\infty$).

**Lower bound.** Since all summands are non-negative:

$$\sum_{k=1}^n \left(\frac{n}{k^2}\right)^{\frac{n}{k^2}+1} \;\geq\; \left(\frac
{n}{1^2}\right)^{\frac{n}{1^2}+1} = n^{n+1} \;\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\; +\infty
.$$

**Stronger observation (optional).** In fact, for *every* fixed $k$, setting $a_
n = n/k^2 \to \infty$, the $k$-th term is $a_n^{a_n+1} = a_n \cdot a_n^{a_n} \to
 +\infty$. So even any finite collection of terms (say $k=1,\dots,K$) drives the
 sum to $+\infty$.

**Conclusion.** The sum is bounded below by a sequence tending to $+\infty$, hen
ce:

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{k=1}^n \left(\frac{n}{k^2}\right)^{\frac{n}{k^2}+1} =
+\infty.$$
