Proof

Let $n > 1$ be odd, so $n \geq 3$, and let $c_1, \dots, c_n \in \mathbb{Z}$. For
each permutation $a = (a_1, \dots, a_n)$ of ${1, \dots, n}$, set $S(a) =
\sum_{i=1}^n c_i a_i$. We prove there exist distinct permutations $a, b$ with
$n! \mid S(a) - S(b)$.

Key idea. There are exactly $n!$ permutations. If all the values $S(a)$ were
pairwise incongruent modulo $n!$, they would form a complete residue system
modulo $n!$. We compute $\sum_a S(a)$ (sum over all permutations) in two ways
and derive a contradiction.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Step 1. The sum if all residues were distinct.

If the $n!$ values $S(a)$ were all distinct mod $n!$, they would be congruent to
$0, 1, 2, \dots, n!-1$ in some order, so

$$ \sum_a S(a) \equiv \sum_{k=0}^{n!-1} k = \frac{(n!-1),n!}{2} \pmod{n!}. $$

Since $n \geq 3$, $n!$ is even, so $\frac{n!}{2}$ is an integer. Then

$$ \frac{(n!-1),n!}{2} = (n!-1)\cdot\frac{n!}{2} \equiv (-1)\cdot\frac{n!}{2}
\equiv \frac{n!}{2} \pmod{n!}. $$

Thus, under the distinctness hypothesis,

$$ \sum_a S(a) \equiv \frac{n!}{2} \pmod{n!}. \tag{$\star$} $$

Note $\frac{n!}{2} \not\equiv 0 \pmod{n!}$, since $n! \nmid \frac{n!}{2}$
(equivalently $2 \nmid 1$).

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Step 2. The actual sum.

Compute directly:

$$ \sum_a S(a) = \sum_a \sum_{i=1}^n c_i a_i = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \left(\sum_a
a_i\right). $$

For a fixed index $i$, as $a$ ranges over all $n!$ permutations, the value $a_i$
takes each element of ${1,\dots,n}$ exactly $(n-1)!$ times. Hence

$$ \sum_a a_i = (n-1)!\cdot \frac{n(n+1)}{2}. $$

Therefore, letting $C = c_1 + \cdots + c_n$,

$$ \sum_a S(a) = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \cdot (n-1)!\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2} = (n-1)!
\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\cdot C = \frac{n!,(n+1)}{2},C. $$

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Step 3. The contradiction.

Since $n$ is odd, $n+1$ is even, so $(n+1)C$ is even and $\frac{(n+1)C}{2}$ is
an integer. Thus

$$ \sum_a S(a) = n!\cdot \frac{(n+1)C}{2} \equiv 0 \pmod{n!}. $$

But under the distinctness hypothesis, $(\star)$ gives $\sum_a S(a) \equiv
\frac{n!}{2} \pmod{n!}$, and $\frac{n!}{2} \not\equiv 0 \pmod{n!}$. This is a
contradiction.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Conclusion.

The assumption that all $S(a)$ are distinct modulo $n!$ is false. Hence there
exist two distinct permutations $a \neq b$ with $S(a) \equiv S(b) \pmod{n!}$,
i.e., $n! \mid S(a) - S(b)$. $\blacksquare$
