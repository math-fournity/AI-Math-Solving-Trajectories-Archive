## Proof

**Step 1: Decompose the integral by unit intervals.**

Since $|\sin(\pi x)|$ has period 1, substituting $x = k + t$ with $t \in [0,1]$
and using $|\sin(\pi(k+t))| = |\sin(\pi t)|$:

$$I(n) = \int_0^n |\sin(\pi x)|^x\,dx = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \int_0^1 |\sin(\pi t)|^
{k+t}\,dt = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} J_k,$$

where $J_k := \int_0^1 |\sin(\pi t)|^{k+t}\,dt$.

**Step 2: Asymptotic of $J_k$ via Laplace's method.**

Write $J_k = \int_0^1 e^{(k+t)\ln|\sin(\pi t)|}\,dt$. Set $g(t) = \ln|\sin(\pi t
)|$ and $h(t) = |\sin(\pi t)|^t = e^{t\,g(t)}$. Then $J_k = \int_0^1 e^{k\,g(t)}
 h(t)\,dt$.

The function $g$ has a unique maximum at $t_0 = \tfrac{1}{2}$:
- $g(t_0) = \ln 1 = 0$,
- $g'(t_0) = \pi\cot(\pi/2) = 0$,
- $g''(t_0) = -\pi^2\csc^2(\pi/2) = -\pi^2$,
- $h(t_0) = 1^{1/2} = 1$.

By Laplace's method, as $k \to \infty$:

$$J_k \sim h(t_0)\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{k\,|g''(t_0)|}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{k\pi^2}}
= \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi k}}.$$

More precisely, $J_k = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi k}}\bigl(1 + O(1/k)\bigr)$, since the
next-order corrections come from the quartic term in $g$ (contributing $O(1/k)$)
 and the variation of $h$ (also $O(1/k)$).

**Step 3: Asymptotic of $I(n)$.**

$$I(n) = J_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} J_k = J_0 + \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}
\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} + O\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k^{3/2}}\right).$$

Using $\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} = 2\sqrt{n} + O(1)$ and $\sum_{k=1}^{
\infty} \frac{1}{k^{3/2}} < \infty$:

$$\boxed{I(n) = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,\sqrt{n} + O(1).}$$

**Step 4: Determine convergence of $\sum a_n$.**

Since $a_n = I(n)/n^p$ and $I(n) > 0$ for all $n \geq 1$, all terms are positive
. We have:

$$a_n = \frac{1}{n^p}\left(\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,\sqrt{n} + O(1)\right)
= \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,n^{1/2 - p} + O(n^{-p}).$$

The leading term is $C\, n^{1/2-p}$ with $C = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}} > 0$.

- **If $p > \frac{3}{2}$:** Then $\frac{1}{2} - p < -1$, so $\sum n^{1/2-p}$ con
verges (convergent $p$-series). Since $a_n \sim C\,n^{1/2-p}$ and the remainder
$O(n^{-p})$ also gives a convergent series (as $p > 3/2 > 1$), $\sum a_n$ **conv
erges**.

- **If $p = \frac{3}{2}$:** Then $a_n \sim \frac{C}{n}$, so $\sum a_n$ **diverge
s** (harmonic series).

- **If $p < \frac{3}{2}$:** Then $\frac{1}{2} - p > -1$, so $\sum n^{1/2-p}$ div
erges. Since $a_n \sim C\,n^{1/2-p} > 0$ and the leading term dominates, $\sum a
_n$ **diverges** by limit comparison with $\sum n^{1/2-p}$.

**Conclusion:** The series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ converges if and only if $p
 > \dfrac{3}{2}$.

Final Answer: $\boxed{p > \dfrac{3}{2}}$
