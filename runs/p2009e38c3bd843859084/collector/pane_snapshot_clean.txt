# Solution

**Claim:** Yes, such sequences exist.

**Construction.** Define
$$a_n = \frac{3}{2} + \frac{\sin n}{2}, \qquad b_n = \frac{1}{a_n} = \frac{1}{\f
rac{3}{2} + \frac{\sin n}{2}}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

We verify all required properties.

---

**1. Boundedness.** Since $-1 \le \sin n \le 1$, we have $1 \le a_n \le 2$, so $
a_n \in [1,2]$. Moreover, $\sin n = -1$ would require $n = -\frac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi
 k$ for some integer $k$, which is impossible for integer $n$ (since $\pi$ is ir
rational). Hence $a_n > 1$ strictly, giving $a_n \in (1, 2]$ and $b_n = 1/a_n \i
n [1/2, 1)$. Both sequences are bounded.

**2. Each consists of unique numbers.** Suppose $\sin n = \sin m$ for positive i
ntegers $n \ne m$. Then either $n - m = 2\pi k$ or $n + m = \pi(2k+1)$ for some
integer $k$. In the first case, $n - m = 2\pi k$ with $n - m \ne 0$ an integer,
contradicting the irrationality of $\pi$. In the second case, $n + m = \pi(2k+1)
$ with $n + m$ a positive integer, again contradicting the irrationality of $\pi
$. Therefore all $\sin n$ are distinct, hence all $a_n$ are distinct. Since $x \
mapsto 1/x$ is injective on $(1, 2]$, all $b_n$ are distinct as well.

**3. The sequences are different from each other.** We have $a_n \in (1, 2]$ and
 $b_n \in [1/2, 1)$, so the ranges are disjoint. In particular $a_n \ne b_n$ for
 every $n$, so the sequences are different.

**4. Non-convergent.** Since $\frac{1}{2\pi}$ is irrational, by **Weyl's equidis
tribution theorem**, the sequence $\{n \bmod 2\pi : n \ge 1\}$ is equidistribute
d (hence dense) in $[0, 2\pi)$. Therefore $\{\sin n\}$ is dense in $[-1, 1]$, an
d $\{a_n\}$ is dense in $[1, 2]$. A sequence dense in a non-degenerate interval
cannot converge, so $(a_n)$ is non-convergent. Since $x \mapsto 1/x$ is continuo
us and injective on $(1, 2]$, the sequence $(b_n)$ is also non-convergent (a con
tinuous function of a non-convergent sequence with dense image remains non-conve
rgent: if $b_n$ converged, then $a_n = 1/b_n$ would converge, contradiction).

**5. The product converges.** By construction,
$$a_n \cdot b_n = a_n \cdot \frac{1}{a_n} = 1 \quad \text{for all } n.$$
Thus $a_n b_n \to 1$, a finite limit.

---

All five conditions are satisfied: both sequences are bounded, each has all dist
inct terms, they differ from each other, neither converges, and their product co
nverges to the finite limit $1$.
