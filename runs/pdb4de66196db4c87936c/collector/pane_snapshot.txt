## Proof

**Yes**, $f \in C^0(\mathbb{R})$. In fact, this follows immediately from the hyp
othesis of absolute continuity; the $L^2$ assumptions are not needed.

Recall the definition: a function $g$ is *absolutely continuous* on a compact in
terval $[a,b]$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such tha
t for every finite collection of pairwise-disjoint subintervals $(a_k, b_k) \sub
set [a,b]$,

$$\sum_k (b_k - a_k) < \delta \implies \sum_k |g(b_k) - g(a_k)| < \varepsilon.$$

A standard and immediate consequence is:

> **If $g$ is absolutely continuous on $[a,b]$, then $g$ is (uniformly) continuo
us on $[a,b]$.**

Indeed, taking a *single* subinterval $(x,y) \subset [a,b]$ in the definition, t
he condition $|x - y| < \delta$ gives $|g(x) - g(y)| < \varepsilon$. This is pre
cisely uniform continuity on $[a,b]$.

Now apply this to our hypotheses. We are given that $f$ is absolutely continuous
 on $[-c, c]$ for every $c > 0$. By the observation above, $f$ is continuous on
$[-c,c]$ for every $c > 0$.

Let $x \in \mathbb{R}$ be arbitrary. Choose any $c > |x|$ (for instance $c = |x|
 + 1$). Then $x \in (-c, c) \subset [-c, c]$, and $f$ is continuous on $[-c,c]$,
 hence $f$ is continuous at $x$. Since $x$ was arbitrary,

$$f \in C^0(\mathbb{R}).$$

The assumptions $f \in L^2(\mathbb{R})$ and $f' \in L^2(\mathbb{R})$ play no rol
e in establishing continuity; they are additional regularity/integrability condi
tions that are compatible with — but not required for — the conclusion. $\blacks
quare$
