Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether two self-adjoint projection
s with equal centralizers must satisfy $p = \pm q$.

**Answer: No, it is not true.** Here is a counterexample and the analysis.

## Counterexample

Take $H = \mathbb{C}^2$ and let
$$p = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad q = \begin{pmatrix} 0
 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = I - p.$$
Both are self-adjoint projections ($p^2 = p = p^*$, $q^2 = q = q^*$).

**Compute $C(p)$.** A matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$
commutes with $p$ iff
$$Ap = \begin{pmatrix} a & 0 \\ c & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0
 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = pA,$$
which forces $b = 0$ and $c = 0$. Hence $C(p) = \{\text{diagonal } 2\times 2 \te
xt{ matrices}\}$.

**Compute $C(q)$.** By the identical calculation (or noting $q = I - p$, so $Aq
= qA \iff A(I-p) = (I-p)A \iff Ap = pA$), we get $C(q) = \{\text{diagonal } 2\ti
mes 2 \text{ matrices}\}$.

Thus $C(p) = C(q)$.

**But $p \neq \pm q$:**
- $p \neq q$ since $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \neq \begin{pma
trix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
- $p \neq -q$ since $-q = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \neq \be
gin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = p$.

So $C(p) = C(q)$ does **not** imply $p = \pm q$. $\square$

## What the correct conclusion is

By the double commutant theorem, $C(p) = C(q)$ implies $\{p\}'' = \{q\}''$, i.e.
, $W^*(p) = W^*(q)$: the two projections generate the same von Neumann algebra.

For a projection $p$, the algebra $W^*(p) = \{\alpha p + \beta(I-p) : \alpha, \b
eta \in \mathbb{C}\}$ consists of all operators that are scalar on $pH$ and scal
ar on $(I-p)H$. The condition $W^*(p) = W^*(q)$ means these two decompositions o
f $H$ into "scalar blocks" coincide.

In the generic (abelian, multiplicity-free) case, this forces $q$ to be a projec
tion valued in $\{0,1\}$ on each character of $W^*(p) \cong \mathbb{C}^2$, and t
o generate the same algebra $q$ must separate the two characters — so $q = p$ or
 $q = I - p$. The second option $q = I - p$ is **not** $-p$ (since $I - p = -p$
would give $I = 0$), which is exactly the obstruction in the counterexample abov
e.

(With multiplicities or in degenerate cases $p \in \{0, I\}$, the situation is e
ven coarser: e.g. $p = 0, q = I$ gives $C(p) = C(q) = B(H)$ but $p \neq \pm q$ o
n any nonzero $H$.)

**Conclusion:** The statement "$p = \pm q$" is **false**. The correct conclusion
 from $C(p) = C(q)$ is that $p$ and $q$ generate the same von Neumann algebra, w
hich for projections yields $q = p$ or $q = I - p$ (in the nondegenerate abelian
 case) — and $I - p$ is generally not $\pm p$.
