# Equivalence of Analyticity and Complex-Differentiability at a Point

## Definitions

**Complex-differentiable at a point.** Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{C}$ be an open s
et and $f : U \to \mathbb{C}$ a function. We say $f$ is **complex-differentiable
** at a point $z_0 \in U$ if the limit

$$
f'(z_0) \;=\; \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(z_0 + h) - f(z_0)}{h}, \qquad h \in \mathbb
{C},
$$

exists. Here the limit is taken over **all complex** $h \to 0$, i.e. $h$ may app
roach $0$ from any direction in the complex plane, and the limiting value must b
e the same regardless of the path. This is a strictly stronger requirement than
real differentiability of the underlying map $\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$, be
cause the difference quotient must have a single limit independent of the direct
ion of approach.

**Analytic at a point.** A function $f$ is **analytic** (or *holomorphic*) at a
point $z_0 \in \mathbb{C}$ if there exists an open neighborhood $V$ of $z_0$ (wi
th $V \subseteq U$) such that $f$ admits a convergent power-series expansion on
$V$:

$$
f(z) \;=\; \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n (z - z_0)^n, \qquad z \in V,
$$

for some coefficients $a_n \in \mathbb{C}$. Equivalently, $f$ agrees with its Ta
ylor series about $z_0$ in some open disk around $z_0$.

## The Equivalence (Theorem)

> **Theorem.** *Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{C}$ be open and $f : U \to \mathbb{C}$.
 For a point $z_0 \in U$, the following are equivalent:*
>
> *(i) $f$ is analytic at $z_0$.*
>
> *(ii) $f$ is complex-differentiable at every point of some open neighborhood o
f $z_0$.*
>
> *(iii) $f$ is complex-differentiable at $z_0$ and the derivative $f'$ is conti
nuous in a neighborhood of $z_0$.*

A subtle but crucial point: a function can be complex-differentiable **at a sing
le isolated point** without being analytic there. Analyticity at $z_0$ requires
complex-differentiability on a whole **neighborhood** of $z_0$, not merely at $z
_0$ itself. The deep content of the theorem is that *once* complex-differentiabi
lity holds on an open set, the full rigidity of complex analysis forces the powe
r-series expansion — analyticity comes "for free."

## Proof

### (i) $\Rightarrow$ (ii): Analytic implies complex-differentiable on a neighbo
rhood.

Suppose $f$ is analytic at $z_0$, so on some open disk $D(z_0, r)$ we have

$$
f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n (z - z_0)^n.
$$

A power series converges absolutely and uniformly on every compact subdisk $\ove
rline{D(z_0, \rho)}$ with $\rho < r$. Within its disk of convergence, a power se
ries may be **term-wise differentiated**: for any $z \in D(z_0, r)$,

$$
f'(z) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n\, a_n\, (z - z_0)^{n-1},
$$

and this differentiated series has the **same radius of convergence** $r$ (since
 $\limsup |n\,a_n|^{1/(n-1)} = \limsup |a_n|^{1/n}$). Hence $f'$ exists at every
 point of $D(z_0, r)$, an open neighborhood of $z_0$. In particular $f$ is compl
ex-differentiable on a neighborhood of $z_0$, giving (ii). $\checkmark$

### (ii) $\Rightarrow$ (i): Complex-differentiable on a neighborhood implies ana
lytic.

This is the deep direction. Suppose $f$ is complex-differentiable at every point
 of an open disk $D(z_0, R)$. We show $f$ admits a power-series expansion on $D(
z_0, R)$.

**Step 1 — Cauchy's integral formula.** Because $f$ is complex-differentiable on
 $D(z_0, R)$, one first establishes (via Goursat's theorem on triangles and a su
bdivision argument) that $f$ satisfies **Cauchy's theorem**: the integral of $f$
 over any closed piecewise-$C^1$ curve in $D(z_0, R)$ that bounds a triangle van
ishes. From this, $f$ has a primitive locally, and one derives **Cauchy's integr
al formula**: for any $z \in D(z_0, R)$ and any circle $C = \partial D(z_0, \rho
)$ with $|z - z_0| < \rho < R$,

$$
f(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_C \frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta - z}\, d\zeta.
$$

**Step 2 — Expand the kernel.** For $|z - z_0| < |\zeta - z_0| = \rho$,

$$
\frac{1}{\zeta - z} = \frac{1}{(\zeta - z_0) - (z - z_0)} = \frac{1}{\zeta - z_0
} \cdot \frac{1}{1 - \frac{z - z_0}{\zeta - z_0}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(z
 - z_0)^n}{(\zeta - z_0)^{n+1}}.
$$

This geometric series converges uniformly in $\zeta$ on $C$ (since $|z - z_0|/\r
ho < 1$), so we may interchange summation and integration:

$$
f(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_C f(\zeta) \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(z - z_0)^n}{
(\zeta - z_0)^{n+1}}\, d\zeta = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{2
\pi i}\oint_C \frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta - z_0)^{n+1}}\, d\zeta\right)}_{a_n} (z - z
_0)^n.
$$

Setting $a_n = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C \frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta - z_0)^{n+1}}\,d\z
eta$, we obtain

$$
f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n (z - z_0)^n, \qquad |z - z_0| < \rho.
$$

Since $\rho < R$ was arbitrary (with $\rho > |z - z_0|$), this series converges
on all of $D(z_0, R)$. Thus $f$ is analytic at $z_0$. $\checkmark$

**Remark on Goursat.** The key subtlety is that Goursat's theorem requires **onl
y** complex-differentiability (no continuity of $f'$ is assumed). Goursat's inge
nious subdivision argument shows that $\oint_{\partial T} f = 0$ for every trian
gle $T$ using only the existence of $f'$ at each point, by subdividing $T$ into
$4$ smaller triangles and using the local linear approximation $f(z) = f(z_0) +
f'(z_0)(z - z_0) + o(|z - z_0|)$. This is what makes the implication (ii) $\Righ
tarrow$ (i) work **without** any a priori continuity assumption on $f'$.

### (iii) $\Leftrightarrow$ (ii): The role of continuity of $f'$.

- **(ii) $\Rightarrow$ (iii):** Once (ii) holds, the Cauchy integral formula giv
es $f'(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C \frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta-z)^2}\,d\zeta$, which
 is continuous in $z$ (differentiate under the integral sign). So complex-differ
entiability on an open set **automatically** yields continuity of $f'$ — the con
tinuity is a *consequence*, not an additional hypothesis. This is the content of
 **Goursat's theorem**: the hypothesis "$f'$ continuous" in the older Cauchy–Gou
rsat theory is redundant.

- **(iii) $\Rightarrow$ (ii):** Trivial, since (iii) includes complex-differenti
ability on a neighborhood by hypothesis.

Thus (iii) is equivalent to (ii), but the equivalence is **asymmetric in spirit*
*: Goursat's contribution was showing that the continuity of $f'$ in (iii) is *a
utomatically satisfied* whenever (ii) holds, so (ii) alone suffices.

## Summary of the Equivalence

| Direction | Content |
|---|---|
| Analytic $\Rightarrow$ complex-diff. on a neighborhood | Term-by-term differen
tiation of a power series (elementary) |
| Complex-diff. on a neighborhood $\Rightarrow$ analytic | Cauchy integral formu
la + geometric-series expansion of the kernel (deep; relies on Goursat) |
| $f'$ continuous $\Leftrightarrow$ redundant | Goursat's theorem: continuity of
 $f'$ is automatic |

## Important Caveat: Pointwise vs. Neighborhood

The equivalence is between **analytic at $z_0$** and **complex-differentiable on
 a neighborhood of $z_0$** — *not* between analytic at $z_0$ and complex-differe
ntiable *at $z_0$ alone*. A function can be complex-differentiable at a single p
oint without being analytic there. The standard example is

$$
f(z) = |z|^2 = z\,\overline{z}.
$$

At $z_0 = 0$:

$$
\frac{f(h) - f(0)}{h} = \frac{|h|^2}{h} = \overline{h} \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \qu
ad \text{as } h \to 0,
$$

so $f'(0) = 0$ exists. But for $z \neq 0$, writing $z = x + iy$,

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x, \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2y,
$$

and the Cauchy–Riemann equations ($\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partia
l v}{\partial y}$, $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial v}{\partial
x}$, with $u = x^2+y^2$, $v = 0$) give $2x = 0$ and $2y = 0$, satisfied only at
the origin. Hence $f$ is complex-differentiable **only** at $z = 0$ and is **not
** analytic there (no neighborhood of $0$ on which $f$ is complex-differentiable
). This confirms that the neighborhood condition in the equivalence is essential
 and cannot be relaxed to pointwise complex-differentiability.

## Conclusion

A function $f$ is **analytic at a point $z_0$** if and only if it is **complex-d
ifferentiable at every point of some open neighborhood of $z_0$**. The forward d
irection is elementary (power series are term-wise differentiable); the reverse
direction is the deep theorem of Cauchy and Goursat, showing that complex-differ
entiability on an open set forces a convergent power-series representation via t
he Cauchy integral formula. The continuity of the derivative, once a separate hy
pothesis, is now known to be automatic (Goursat's theorem). Complex-differentiab
ility at a single isolated point, however, does **not** imply analyticity — a ne
ighborhood of differentiability is required.
