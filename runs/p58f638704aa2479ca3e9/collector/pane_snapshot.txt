## Proof

We work in the polynomial ring $\mathbb{F}_p[x]$, where $\mathbb{F}_p$ is the fi
eld with $p$ elements.

**Key fact (Freshman's dream).** For any $a, b$ in a commutative ring of charact
eristic $p$ (with $p$ prime),
$$(a+b)^p = a^p + b^p.$$
This holds because by the binomial theorem,
$$(a+b)^p = \sum_{k=0}^{p} \binom{p}{k} a^{p-k} b^{k},$$
and every middle binomial coefficient $\binom{p}{k}$ for $1 \le k \le p-1$ is di
visible by $p$ (since $p$ is prime and $\binom{p}{k} = \frac{p}{k}\binom{p-1}{k-
1}$, with $k$ coprime to $p$). Hence $\binom{p}{k} \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$ for $1 \le
 k \le p-1$, leaving only $a^p + b^p$.

By induction, this extends to any number of terms:
$$\left(\sum_i a_i\right)^{p} = \sum_i a_i^{p}, \qquad\text{and more generally}\
qquad \left(\sum_i a_i\right)^{p^r} = \sum_i a_i^{p^r}.$$

---

**Applying it to the proof.** Consider the polynomial $(1+x)^m \in \mathbb{Z}[x]
$. Raise both sides to the $p$-th power and reduce coefficients modulo $p$; equi
valently, work in $\mathbb{F}_p[x]$:

$$(1+x)^{pm} = \bigl((1+x)^{m}\bigr)^{p}.$$

By the Freshman's dream applied to the polynomial $(1+x)^m = \sum_{j=0}^{m} \bin
om{m}{j} x^j$ viewed as a sum of monomials in $\mathbb{F}_p[x]$,

$$\bigl((1+x)^{m}\bigr)^{p} = \sum_{j=0}^{m} \binom{m}{j}^{p}\, x^{jp}.$$

Since $\mathbb{F}_p$ has characteristic $p$, Fermat's little theorem gives $\bin
om{m}{j}^{p} \equiv \binom{m}{j} \pmod{p}$ for every integer $\binom{m}{j}$. The
refore, in $\mathbb{F}_p[x]$,

$$(1+x)^{pm} = \sum_{j=0}^{m} \binom{m}{j}\, x^{jp}.$$

---

**Comparing coefficients.** On the other hand, expanding directly,

$$(1+x)^{pm} = \sum_{i=0}^{pm} \binom{pm}{i}\, x^{i}.$$

Equating coefficients of $x^{pn}$ on both sides (valid since we are in $\mathbb{
F}_p[x]$, where coefficient comparison is legitimate):

- From the direct expansion, the coefficient of $x^{pn}$ is $\binom{pm}{pn}$.
- From the Freshman's-dream form $\sum_{j=0}^{m} \binom{m}{j}\, x^{jp}$, the coe
fficient of $x^{pn}$ is $\binom{m}{n}$ (the unique term with $j = n$; all other
terms have exponent $jp \neq pn$ since $0 \le j \le m$ and $j \neq n$).

Therefore,
$$\binom{pm}{pn} \equiv \binom{m}{n} \pmod{p}. \qquad \blacksquare$$

### PROOF COMPLETE
