## Solution

**The converse of Schur's lemma is false.** I provide an explicit counterexample
.

### Counterexample

Let $k$ be a field and let
$$A=\begin{pmatrix}k&k\\0&k\end{pmatrix}$$
be the algebra of $2\times2$ upper triangular matrices. Let $M=k^{2}$ be the nat
ural left $A$-module (column vectors, action by matrix multiplication).

### $M$ is reducible

The line $W=k\cdot e_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}k\\0\end{pmatrix}$ is a proper nonzero $
A$-submodule, since for every $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\0&c\end{pmatrix}\in A$ and $\
begin{pmatrix}x\\0\end{pmatrix}\in W$,
$$\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\0&c\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pm
atrix}ax\\0\end{pmatrix}\in W.$$
Hence $M$ has a proper nonzero subrepresentation, so $M$ is **reducible** (in fa
ct indecomposable, since $W$ has no $A$-invariant complement).

### Every $A$-endomorphism of $M$ is scalar

An $A$-linear endomorphism $\phi:M\to M$ is a linear map commuting with every el
ement of $A$. Write $\phi=\begin{pmatrix}p&q\\ r&s\end{pmatrix}$. The identity $
\phi a=a\phi$ for all $a=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\0&c\end{pmatrix}\in A$ yields, by c
omparing entries:

- **(2,1)-entry:** $ra=0$ for all $a\in k$, so $r=0$.
- **(1,2)-entry:** $pb+qc=aq+bs$ for all $a,b,c\in k$. Setting $a=0$ gives $qc=0
$ for all $c$, hence $q=0$; then $pb=bs$ for all $b$, hence $p=s$.

Therefore
$$\phi=\begin{pmatrix}p&0\\0&p\end{pmatrix}=p\cdot I,$$
a scalar multiple of the identity. So
$$\operatorname{End}_{A}(M)=k\cdot\operatorname{id}_{M}.$$

### Conclusion

$M$ is a **reducible** $A$-module whose only endomorphisms are scalar multiples
of the identity. Thus the condition "$\operatorname{End}(V)=k\cdot\operatorname{
id}$" does **not** imply irreducibility. The converse of Schur's lemma is **fals
e**.

### Remark

The converse *does* hold in the semisimple setting (e.g. finite-dimensional repr
esentations of a finite group over an algebraically closed field whose character
istic does not divide $|G|$): there, a reducible module is decomposable, and the
 projection onto a direct summand is a non-scalar endomorphism. The counterexamp
le above exploits the non-semisimple case — $M$ is reducible yet indecomposable,
 and its endomorphism ring collapses to $k$ because the algebra $A$ is non-commu
tative and not generated by a single operator (so the usual "polynomials in the
acting operator" source of extra endomorphisms is absent).
