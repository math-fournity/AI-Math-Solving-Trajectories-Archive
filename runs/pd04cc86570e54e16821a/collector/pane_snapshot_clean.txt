Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether two non-isomorphic represen
tations of $S_n$ can become isomorphic when restricted to $S_{n-1}$.

**Answer: Yes, such representations exist for every $n \geq 2$.**

## Simplest Example ($n = 2$)

Let $U$ be the trivial representation of $S_2$ and $V$ be the sign representatio
n of $S_2$. These are non-isomorphic: the transposition $(12)$ acts as $+1$ on $
U$ and $-1$ on $V$. When restricted to $S_1$ (the trivial group), both become th
e unique 1-dimensional representation of the trivial group, so $\operatorname{Re
s}^{S_2}_{S_1} U \cong \operatorname{Res}^{S_2}_{S_1} V$.

## General Argument ($n \geq 2$)

The irreducible representations of $S_n$ are indexed by partitions $\lambda \vda
sh n$. By the **branching rule**, restricting to $S_{n-1}$ gives a multiplicity-
free decomposition:
$$\operatorname{Res}^{S_n}_{S_{n-1}} V_\lambda = \bigoplus_{\mu \nearrow \lambda
} V_\mu,$$
where $\mu$ ranges over partitions of $n-1$ obtained by removing one corner box
from the Young diagram of $\lambda$.

This induces a linear map on Grothendieck groups:
$$\phi: K_0(S_n) \cong \mathbb{Z}^{p(n)} \longrightarrow K_0(S_{n-1}) \cong \mat
hbb{Z}^{p(n-1)},$$
where $p(k)$ is the number of partitions of $k$. The map sends $(a_\lambda)_{\la
mbda \vdash n}$ to $(c_\mu)_{\mu \vdash n-1}$ with $c_\mu = \sum_{\lambda: \mu \
nearrow \lambda} a_\lambda$.

**Key fact:** $p(n) > p(n-1)$ for all $n \geq 2$ (the partition function is stri
ctly increasing). Therefore $\phi$ is a linear map from a higher-dimensional lat
tice to a lower-dimensional one, so $\ker(\phi)$ is nontrivial over $\mathbb{Z}$
.

Any nonzero $\mathbf{a} \in \ker(\phi)$ must have both positive and negative ent
ries: if all $a_\lambda \geq 0$, then each $c_\mu = \sum_{\lambda: \mu \nearrow
\lambda} a_\lambda \geq 0$, and $c_\mu = 0$ forces every $a_\lambda$ with $\mu \
nearrow \lambda$ to be $0$; since every $\lambda$ covers at least one $\mu$, thi
s gives $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}$, a contradiction.

Writing $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{a}^+ - \mathbf{a}^-$ where $\mathbf{a}^+, \mathbf{
a}^-$ are nonzero non-negative integer vectors, define:
$$U = \bigoplus_{\lambda} a^+_\lambda \, V_\lambda, \qquad V = \bigoplus_{\lambd
a} a^-_\lambda \, V_\lambda.$$

Then $U \not\cong V$ as $S_n$-representations (since $\mathbf{a}^+ \neq \mathbf{
a}^-$), but $\operatorname{Res} U \cong \operatorname{Res} V$ (since $\phi(\math
bf{a}^+) = \phi(\mathbf{a}^-)$).

## Concrete Example ($n = 3$)

The irreducibles of $S_3$ are $V_{(3)}$ (trivial, dim 1), $V_{(2,1)}$ (standard,
 dim 2), $V_{(1^3)}$ (sign, dim 1). Their restrictions to $S_2$:
- $\operatorname{Res} V_{(3)} = W_{(2)}$ (trivial of $S_2$)
- $\operatorname{Res} V_{(2,1)} = W_{(2)} \oplus W_{(1^2)}$ (regular rep of $S_2
$)
- $\operatorname{Res} V_{(1^3)} = W_{(1^2)}$ (sign of $S_2$)

Set $U = V_{(3)} \oplus V_{(1^3)}$ and $V = V_{(2,1)}$. Then:
$$\operatorname{Res} U = W_{(2)} \oplus W_{(1^2)} = \operatorname{Res} V,$$
yet $U \not\cong V$ as $S_3$-representations, since $U$ is reducible (a direct s
um of two 1-dimensional representations) while $V$ is irreducible (2-dimensional
).

## Remark on Irreducibles

For $n \geq 3$, the restriction map is injective on *irreducible* representation
s: given the set $\{\mu : \mu \nearrow \lambda\}$, one can recover $\lambda$ uni
quely. (If two distinct partitions $\lambda, \nu$ shared the same set and $|\{\m
u\}| \geq 2$, then $\nu$ would be obtained from $\lambda$ by moving a box from r
ow $i$ to row $j$; but applying the same reasoning to a second element of the se
t forces the same move from a *different* row, a contradiction. The $|\{\mu\}| =
 1$ case forces $\lambda$ to be a rectangle, and two distinct rectangles of the
same size yield distinct single-element sets.) Thus the phenomenon requires *red
ucible* representations for $n \geq 3$, but the $n = 2$ case already provides an
 irreducible example.
