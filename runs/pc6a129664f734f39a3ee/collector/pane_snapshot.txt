## Proof

Let $R = \langle r \rangle$ where $r$ is an involution ($|R|=2$).

**Step 1: $P$ centralizes $R$.** The hypothesis $C_P(R) = P$ means every element
 of $P$ commutes with $r$. Thus $P \leq C_G(R) \leq N_G(R)$, and consequently $R
^p = R$ for all $p \in P$.

**Step 2: The conjugates of $R$ are $\{R^q : q \in Q\}$.** Since $N_G(R) = PR$,
the conjugates of $R$ in $G$ correspond to the cosets $G/N_G(R) = G/PR$. Because
 $G = PQR$ and $Q \cap PR = 1$ (as $|Q|$ is a power of the odd prime $q$, while
$|PR| = |P|\cdot 2$ with $p \neq q$ and $p \neq 2$), a transversal for $G/PR$ is
 furnished by the elements of $Q$. Hence the distinct conjugates of $R$ are exac
tly $\{R^q : q \in Q\}$, and there are $|Q|$ of them.

**Step 3: $QR$ is a subgroup.** The condition $C_Q(R) = 1$ means $r$ acts fixed-
point-freely on $Q$ by conjugation. Since $|Q|$ is odd and $r$ has order $2$, th
e standard fixed-point-free automorphism theorem applies: the map $\phi: Q \to Q
$ defined by $\phi(q) = q^{-1}rqr^{-1} = q^{-1}(rqr)$ is injective (if $q_1^{-1}
rq_1 = q_2^{-1}rq_2$, then $r$ fixes $q_1q_2^{-1}$, forcing $q_1 = q_2$), hence
bijective. Every element of $Q$ can be written as $q^{-1}(rq r^{-1})$ for some $
q$, and then $r$ sends this to $rq^{-1}r^{-1}\cdot r^2qr^{-1} = (q^{-1}rq r^{-1}
)^{-1}$, proving $r$ acts by inversion: $rqr = q^{-1}$ for all $q \in Q$. This f
orces $Q$ to be abelian and, in particular, $r$ normalizes $Q$, so $QR$ is a sub
group of $G$ of order $2|Q|$.

**Step 4: $R^G \leq QR$.** By Step 2, $R^G = \langle R^q : q \in Q \rangle$. Eac
h conjugate $R^q$ is generated by the involution $q^{-1}rq$. Since $q^{-1} \in Q
 \leq QR$ and $r \in R \leq QR$, and $QR$ is a subgroup (Step 3), we have $q^{-1
}rq \in QR$. Therefore each $R^q \leq QR$, and so

$$R^G = \langle R^q : q \in Q \rangle \leq QR.$$

**Remark (stronger result).** Since $Q$ is abelian of odd order, the squaring ma
p $q \mapsto q^2$ is an automorphism of $Q$, so $Q^2 = Q$. One can compute $q^{-
1}rq = q^{-2}r$ (using $rq = q^{-1}r$), so $R^G = \langle q^{-2}r : q \in Q \ran
gle = \langle Q^2, r \rangle = QR$. Thus $R^G = QR$, but the problem only asks f
or containment, which we have established.
