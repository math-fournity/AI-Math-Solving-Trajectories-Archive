## Proof

$$n^5+n^4+1 = (n^2+n+1)(n^3-n+1).$$

*Verification:* $(n^2+n+1)(n^3-n+1) = n^5-n^3+n^2+n^4-n^2+n+n^3-n+1 = n^5+n^4+1.
$ ✓

So we seek $(n,m)$ with $(n^2+n+1)(n^3-n+1)=7^m$.

**Step 2: GCD of the two factors.** Using the Euclidean algorithm:

$$n^3-n+1 = (n-1)(n^2+n+1)-(n-2).$$

Hence $\gcd(n^2+n+1,\, n^3-n+1)=\gcd(n^2+n+1,\, n-2)$. Since $n^2+n+1\equiv 4+2+
1=7\pmod{n-2}$, this gcd divides $7$. Therefore:

$$\gcd(n^2+n+1,\, n^3-n+1)\in\{1,7\}.$$

Moreover, the gcd equals $7$ exactly when $7\mid n-2$, i.e., $n\equiv 2\pmod{7}$
; otherwise it is $1$.

**Step 3: Case $\gcd=1$ (i.e., $n\not\equiv 2\pmod 7$).** The two factors are co
prime and their product is the prime power $7^m$. Since $7$ is prime, one factor

- **$n^2+n+1=1$:** Then $n(n+1)=0$, giving $n=0$ (the only natural solution). Th
en $n^3-n+1=1=7^0$, so $m=0$. **Solution: $(0,0)$.**

- **$n^3-n+1=1$:** Then $n(n-1)(n+1)=0$, giving $n=0$ or $n=1$. The case $n=0$ i
s already covered. For $n=1$: $n^2+n+1=3$, which is not a power of $7$. No solut
ion.

(2,2)$.**
