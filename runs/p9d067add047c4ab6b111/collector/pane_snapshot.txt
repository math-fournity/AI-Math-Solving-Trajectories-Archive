**Answer: Yes, such an operator exists.**

## Proof

We construct an explicit example. Let $\mathcal{H} = \ell^2(\mathbb{N}) \oplus \
mathbb{C}$, and define the bounded linear operator $T: \mathcal{H} \to \mathcal{
H}$ by
$$
T(x, \lambda) = (Dx,\, 0),
$$
where $D: \ell^2 \to \ell^2$ is the diagonal operator $D(e_n) = \tfrac{1}{n}\,e_
n$ (with $(e_n)$ the standard basis).

### Step 1. The kernel of $T$ is one-dimensional.

We have
$$
\ker(T) = \{(x,\lambda) \in \ell^2 \oplus \mathbb{C} : Dx = 0\} = \ker(D) \oplus
 \mathbb{C}.
$$
Since $\tfrac{1}{n} \neq 0$ for every $n$, the operator $D$ is injective, so $\k
er(D) = \{0\}$. Hence
$$
\ker(T) = \{0\} \oplus \mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{C},
$$
and therefore $0 < \dim(\ker T) = 1 < \infty$.

### Step 2. The range of $D$ is not closed.

The range of $D$ contains every finitely supported sequence: indeed $e_n = D(n\,
e_n)$ and $n\,e_n \in \ell^2$. Since the finitely supported sequences are dense
in $\ell^2$, we conclude $\overline{\operatorname{ran}(D)} = \ell^2$.

Now consider $y = \bigl(n^{-3/2}\bigr)_{n=1}^\infty \in \ell^2$ (since $\sum n^{
-3} < \infty$). If $y \in \operatorname{ran}(D)$, then $y = Dx$ for some $x \in
\ell^2$, giving $x_n = n\cdot y_n = n^{-1/2}$. But
$$
\sum_{n=1}^\infty |x_n|^2 = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n} = \infty,
$$
so $x \notin \ell^2$, a contradiction. Thus $y \notin \operatorname{ran}(D)$.

Since $y \in \ell^2 = \overline{\operatorname{ran}(D)}$ but $y \notin \operatorn
ame{ran}(D)$, the range of $D$ is a **proper dense** subspace of $\ell^2$, hence
 **not closed**.

### Step 3. The range of $T$ is not closed.

We have
$$
\operatorname{ran}(T) = \{(Dx, 0) : x \in \ell^2\} = \operatorname{ran}(D) \oplu
s \{0\} \subseteq \ell^2 \oplus \mathbb{C}.
$$
The map $\iota: \ell^2 \to \ell^2 \oplus \mathbb{C}$ defined by $\iota(x) = (x,
0)$ is an isometric embedding (with the natural direct-sum norm). Therefore a su
bspace $A \subseteq \ell^2$ is closed if and only if $A \oplus \{0\}$ is closed
in $\ell^2 \oplus \mathbb{C}$. Since $\operatorname{ran}(D)$ is not closed in $\
ell^2$ (Step 2), it follows that $\operatorname{ran}(D) \oplus \{0\} = \operator
name{ran}(T)$ is **not closed** in $\mathcal{H}$.

### Conclusion

The operator $T$ on the Hilbert space $\mathcal{H} = \ell^2 \oplus \mathbb{C}$ s
atisfies:
- $0 < \dim(\ker T) = 1 < \infty$, and
- $\operatorname{ran}(T)$ is not closed.

Hence **yes**, such a linear operator exists. $\blacksquare$
