## Solution

The set of algebraic numbers is countable: each algebraic number is a root of a
polynomial with integer coefficients, and the set of such polynomials is countab
le (a countable union of countable sets, one per degree), each having finitely m
any roots. Hence the algebraic numbers in $[0,1]$ form a countable set, and ther
efore have Lebesgue measure zero.

The function $A(x) = \mathbf{1}_{\{\text{algebraic}\}}(x)$ is the indicator func
tion of this measure-zero set. It equals $0$ at every transcendental $x$, and th
e transcendentals in $[0,1]$ have full measure $1$. Thus $A = 0$ almost everywhe
re on $[0,1]$.

A function that vanishes almost everywhere has Lebesgue integral equal to $0$:

$$\int_0^1 A(x)\,dx = \int_0^1 \mathbf{1}_{\{\text{algebraic}\}}(x)\,dx = m\big(
\{\text{algebraic}\}\cap[0,1]\big) = 0.$$

**Remark on Riemann integrability.** Both the algebraic numbers and the transcen
dental numbers are dense in $[0,1]$ (rationals are algebraic; transcendentals ar
e dense by, e.g., the fact that $\{q + \alpha : q\in\mathbb{Q}\}$ for a fixed tr
anscendental $\alpha$ shifted appropriately, or more directly by cardinality/den
sity arguments). Hence $A$ is discontinuous at *every* point of $[0,1]$, so by L
ebesgue's criterion it is not Riemann integrable. The integral is therefore unde
rstood in the Lebesgue sense, under which it is well defined and equals $0$.

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\int_0^1 A(x)\,dx = 0}$$
