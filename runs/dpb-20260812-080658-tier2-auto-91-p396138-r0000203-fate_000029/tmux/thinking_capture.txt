Theorem. The order of a permutation equals the least common multiple of the
lengths of its disjoint cycles in the cycle decomposition.

Proof.

Let $\sigma \in S_n$ be a permutation, and write it as a product of disjoint
cycles: $$\sigma = \tau_1 \tau_2 \cdots \tau_k,$$ where $\tau_i$ is a cycle of
length $\ell_i$ (so $\tau_i^{\ell_i} = \text{id}$), and the cycles $\tau_1,
\dots, \tau_k$ act on pairwise disjoint subsets of ${1, \dots, n}$.

Key fact (disjoint cycles commute). Since the supports of $\tau_i$ and $\tau_j$
are disjoint for $i \neq j$, the two cycles commute: $\tau_i \tau_j = \tau_j
\tau_i$. This is because each element of ${1,\dots,n}$ lies in the support of at
most one cycle, so applying $\tau_i$ then $\tau_j$ has the same effect as
applying $\tau_j$ then $\tau_i$.

Step 1: A formula for $\sigma^m$. By commutativity of disjoint cycles, for any
non-negative integer $m$: $$\sigma^m = (\tau_1 \tau_2 \cdots \tau_k)^m =
\tau_1^m \tau_2^m \cdots \tau_k^m.$$

Step 2: When is $\sigma^m = \text{id}$? Since the $\tau_i^m$ act on pairwise
disjoint supports, $\sigma^m = \text{id}$ if and only if each factor is the
identity on its own support: $$\sigma^m = \text{id} \iff \tau_i^m = \text{id
\text{ on } \mathrm{supp}(\tau_i)} \text{ for every } i = 1, \dots, k.$$

A cycle $\tau_i$ of length $\ell_i$ satisfies $\tau_i^m = \text{id}$ if and only
if $\ell_i \mid m$. (The powers of an $\ell_i$-cycle cycle through $\ell_i$
distinct permutations: $\tau_i^0, \tau_i^1, \dots, \tau_i^{\ell_i - 1}$, and $
\tau_i^{\ell_i} = \text{id}$; more generally $\tau_i^m = \text{id} \iff m \equiv
0 \pmod{\ell_i}$.)

Therefore: $$\sigma^m = \text{id} \iff \ell_i \mid m \text{ for every } i = 1,
\dots, k.$$

Step 3: Identify the order. The order of $\sigma$ is, by definition, the
smallest positive integer $m$ such that $\sigma^m = \text{id}$. By Step 2, this
is the smallest positive integer divisible by every $\ell_i$: $$\mathrm{ord}
(\sigma) = \min{m \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0} : \ell_i \mid m \text{ for all } i}.$$

This is precisely the definition of $\mathrm{lcm}(\ell_1, \ell_2, \dots,
\ell_k)$.

Hence: $$\boxed{\mathrm{ord}(\sigma) = \mathrm{lcm}(\ell_1, \ell_2, \dots,
\ell_k).} \qquad \blacksquare$$
