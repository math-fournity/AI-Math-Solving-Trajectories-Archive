## Proof

We assume $k > 0$ and $p > 0$ (otherwise the integrand does not decay at infinit
y, as shown below).

**Step 1: Reduction to a one-dimensional integral.**

Passing to polar coordinates with $r = |x|$, we have $dx = \omega_{d-1}\, r^{d-1
}\,dr$, where $\omega_{d-1} = \text{Vol}(S^{d-1})$ is the surface area of the un
it sphere. Thus

$$I = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d}\frac{dx}{(|x|^{2k}+1)^p} = \omega_{d-1}\int_0^\infty \
frac{r^{d-1}}{(r^{2k}+1)^p}\,dr.$$

It suffices to analyze $J = \int_0^\infty \frac{r^{d-1}}{(r^{2k}+1)^p}\,dr$.

**Step 2: Behavior near the origin ($r \to 0^+$).**

As $r \to 0^+$, we have $r^{2k} \to 0$ (since $k > 0$), so $(r^{2k}+1)^p \to 1$.
 The integrand satisfies

$$\frac{r^{d-1}}{(r^{2k}+1)^p} \sim r^{d-1} \quad \text{as } r \to 0^+.$$

Since $d \geq 1$, we have $d - 1 \geq 0 > -1$, so $\int_0^1 r^{d-1}\,dr < \infty
$. **The integral is always finite near the origin** (for $k > 0$).

**Step 3: Behavior at infinity ($r \to \infty$) — deriving the necessary conditi
on.**

As $r \to \infty$, $r^{2k} \to \infty$ (since $k > 0$), so $(r^{2k}+1)^p \sim r^
{2kp}$. The integrand satisfies

$$\frac{r^{d-1}}{(r^{2k}+1)^p} \sim r^{d-1-2kp} \quad \text{as } r \to \infty.$$

The integral $\int_R^\infty r^{d-1-2kp}\,dr$ converges if and only if $d - 1 - 2
kp < -1$, i.e.,

$$\boxed{2kp > d.}$$

This is the **necessary condition**: if $2kp \leq d$, the tail integral diverges
.

**Step 4: Sufficiency — the condition $2kp > d$ is sufficient.**

We must verify that when $2kp > d$ (with $k > 0, p > 0$), the full integral conv
erges. We split:

$$J = \int_0^1 \frac{r^{d-1}}{(r^{2k}+1)^p}\,dr + \int_1^\infty \frac{r^{d-1}}{(
r^{2k}+1)^p}\,dr = J_1 + J_2.$$

- **$J_1$ (near origin):** For $0 \leq r \leq 1$, we have $r^{2k} \leq 1$, so $(
r^{2k}+1)^p \geq 1$, giving $\frac{r^{d-1}}{(r^{2k}+1)^p} \leq r^{d-1}$. Since $
\int_0^1 r^{d-1}\,dr = \frac{1}{d} < \infty$, we get $J_1 < \infty$.

- **$J_2$ (at infinity):** For $r \geq 1$, we have $r^{2k} \geq 1$, so $r^{2k}+1
 \leq 2r^{2k}$, hence $(r^{2k}+1)^p \geq r^{2kp}$ (in fact $(r^{2k}+1)^p \geq r^
{2kp}$ directly since $r^{2k}+1 \geq r^{2k}$). This gives

$$\frac{r^{d-1}}{(r^{2k}+1)^p} \leq \frac{r^{d-1}}{r^{2kp}} = r^{d-1-2kp}.$$

Since $2kp > d$, the exponent $d - 1 - 2kp < -1$, so $\int_1^\infty r^{d-1-2kp}\
,dr < \infty$, giving $J_2 < \infty$.

Therefore $J = J_1 + J_2 < \infty$, and $I = \omega_{d-1}\, J < \infty$.

**Step 5: Why $k > 0$ and $p > 0$ are required.**

- If $k \leq 0$: then $|x|^{2k}$ does not grow as $|x| \to \infty$ (it is bounde
d or decays), so $(|x|^{2k}+1)^p$ is bounded above, and the integrand is bounded
 below by a positive constant on a set of infinite measure — the integral diverg
es.
- If $p \leq 0$: the integrand $(|x|^{2k}+1)^{|p|}$ grows (or equals 1 if $p=0$)
 as $|x| \to \infty$, so the integral diverges.

**Conclusion.**

The necessary and sufficient condition for $\int_{\mathbb{R}^d}\frac{1}{(|x|^{2k
}+1)^p}\,dx < \infty$ (with $k > 0$, $p > 0$) is

$$2kp > d.$$

The condition $2kp > d$ is **both necessary and sufficient**: necessity follows
from the asymptotic behavior $r^{d-1-2kp}$ at infinity (Step 3), and sufficiency
 follows from the comparison argument splitting the integral at $r = 1$ (Step 4)
, where the origin poses no obstruction since the integrand behaves like $r^{d-1
}$ near $r = 0$.
