## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, $c_1, \ldots, c_n$ are necessarily all zero.

**Key identity.** The elementary symmetric polynomials are the coefficients of t
he polynomial whose roots are $c_1, \ldots, c_n$:

$$\prod_{i=1}^{n}(t - c_i) = t^n - s_n^1\, t^{n-1} + s_n^2\, t^{n-2} - \cdots +
(-1)^n s_n^n.$$

This is a standard identity, valid over any commutative ring, hence over $\mathb
b{F}_{q^n}$.

**Applying the hypothesis.** Since $s_n^k(c_1,\ldots,c_n) = 0$ for every $1 \le
k \le n$, every coefficient except the leading one vanishes, giving the polynomi
al identity in $\mathbb{F}_{q^n}[t]$:

$$\prod_{i=1}^{n}(t - c_i) = t^n. \tag{$\star$}$$

**Extracting the conclusion.** Evaluate $(\star)$ at $t = c_j$ for any $j \in \{
1,\ldots,n\}$:

$$\prod_{i=1}^{n}(c_j - c_i) = c_j^n.$$

The factor corresponding to $i = j$ on the left is $c_j - c_j = 0$, so the entir
e left side is $0$. Therefore:

$$c_j^n = 0 \quad \text{in } \mathbb{F}_{q^n}.$$

Since $\mathbb{F}_{q^n}$ is a field, it has no nonzero nilpotent elements: $c_j^
n = 0$ implies $c_j = 0$.

This holds for every $j = 1, \ldots, n$, so $c_1 = c_2 = \cdots = c_n = 0$.

**Remark on the hypotheses.** The argument uses only that $\mathbb{F}_{q^n}$ is
a field (so that $\mathbb{F}_{q^n}[t]$ is an integral domain and the evaluation
map is a ring homomorphism). The conditions that $q$ is prime, that $n < q$, and
 that the ambient field is specifically $\mathbb{F}_{q^n}$ are not needed — the
conclusion holds over any field. $\blacksquare$
