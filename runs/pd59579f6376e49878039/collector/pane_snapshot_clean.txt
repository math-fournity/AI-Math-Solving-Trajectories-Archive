Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether a concave function $F$ can
dominate both $h$ and $g$ while satisfying the Osgood condition.

**Key Observation:** The Osgood condition is *automatically* satisfied by any co
ncave function that is positive on $(0,\infty)$ and non-negative on $[0,\infty)$
. This reduces the problem to finding a concave majorant of $\max(h,g)$.

---

## Proof

**Step 1: The Osgood condition is automatic for concave functions on $[0,\infty)
$.**

Let $f:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ be concave with $f(x)>0$ for $x>0$. We claim $\i
nt_0^\infty \frac{dx}{f(x)}=\infty$.

*Non-decreasing:* Since $f\ge 0$ on $[0,\infty)$ and $f$ is concave, $f$ must be
 non-decreasing. Indeed, if $f(a)>f(b)$ for some $0\le a<b$, then the right deri
vative $f'_+$ (which is non-increasing for concave $f$) satisfies $f'_+(c)<0$ fo
r some $c\in(a,b)$, hence $f'_+(x)\le f'_+(c)<0$ for all $x>c$, forcing $f(x)\to
 -\infty$, contradicting $f\ge 0$.

*Linear growth bound:* Since $f$ is concave on $(0,\infty)$, it is locally Lipsc
hitz, so $f'_+(1)<\infty$. For $x\ge 1$, concavity of the derivative gives $f'_+
(x)\le f'_+(1)$, hence:
$$f(x) = f(1) + \int_1^x f'_+(t)\,dt \le f(1) + f'_+(1)\cdot x.$$

*Divergence:* Set $C=f(1)\ge 0$ and $D=f'_+(1)\ge 0$. Then $\frac{1}{f(x)}\ge \f
rac{1}{C+Dx}$ for $x\ge 1$.
- If $D>0$: $\int_1^\infty \frac{dx}{C+Dx}=\frac{1}{D}\ln(C+Dx)\big|_1^\infty=\i
nfty$.
- If $D=0$: $f(x)\le C$ for $x\ge 1$, so $\int_1^\infty \frac{dx}{f(x)}\ge \int_
1^\infty \frac{dx}{C}=\infty$.

In either case $\int_1^\infty \frac{dx}{f(x)}=\infty$, so $\int_0^\infty \frac{d
x}{f(x)}=\infty$. $\square$

**Step 2: Construction of $F$.**

Define $M(x)=\max(h(x),g(x))$. Let $\mathcal{F}$ be the family of all concave fu
nctions $\varphi$ on $[0,\infty)$ with $\varphi\ge M$, and set:
$$F(x)=\inf_{\varphi\in\mathcal{F}} \varphi(x).$$

*Non-empty and finite:* The function $h+g$ is concave (sum of concave functions)
 and satisfies $(h+g)(x)\ge h(x),\,g(x)\ge 0$, so $h+g\ge M$. Thus $\mathcal{F}\
neq\emptyset$ and $F\le h+g<\infty$.

*Concave:* The pointwise infimum of concave functions is concave. For $x,y\in[0,
\infty)$, $\lambda\in[0,1]$, and any $\varphi\in\mathcal{F}$:
$$\varphi(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\ge \lambda\varphi(x)+(1-\lambda)\varphi(y)\ge
\lambda F(x)+(1-\lambda)F(y).$$
Taking the infimum over $\varphi$ gives $F(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\ge \lambda F(
x)+(1-\lambda)F(y)$.

*Dominates $h$ and $g$:* $F\ge M=\max(h,g)$, so $F\ge h$ and $F\ge g$.

*$F(0)=0$:* We have $F(0)\ge M(0)=\max(h(0),g(0))=0$ and $F(0)\le (h+g)(0)=0$, s
o $F(0)=0$.

*$F(x)>0$ for $x>0$:* $F(x)\ge M(x)=\max(h(x),g(x))>0$.

*Continuity:* $F$ is concave on $[0,\infty)$, hence continuous on $(0,\infty)$.
At $x=0$: for $x\in(0,1]$, $0\le F(x)\le (h+g)(x)\to 0$ as $x\to 0^+$ (since $h,
g$ are continuous with $h(0)=g(0)=0$). By the squeeze theorem, $F(x)\to 0=F(0)$.

*Non-decreasing:* $F\ge 0$ and $F$ is concave on $[0,\infty)$, so by the argumen
t in Step 1, $F$ is non-decreasing.

*Osgood condition:* $F$ is concave on $[0,\infty)$, $F\ge 0$, and $F(x)>0$ for $
x>0$. By Step 1, $\int_0^\infty \frac{dx}{F(x)}=\infty$.

---

**Conclusion:** The function $F$, defined as the concave envelope of $\max(h,g)$
, is continuous, non-decreasing, concave on $[0,\infty)$, satisfies $F(0)=0$, $F
(x)>0$ for $x>0$, $F\ge h$, $F\ge g$, and $\int_0^\infty \frac{dx}{F(x)}=\infty$
.

The answer is **yes**, such a concave function $F$ always exists. In fact, the O
sgood condition is automatically satisfied by any non-negative concave function
on $[0,\infty)$ that is positive on $(0,\infty)$, so the only substantive requir
ement is the existence of a concave majorant of $\max(h,g)$, which is guaranteed
 by taking the concave envelope.
