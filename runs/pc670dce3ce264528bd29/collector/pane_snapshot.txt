The statement is **false**. A counterexample is \(k = 3\.

**Proof.** Take \(k = 3\), so \(k! = 6\). The required interval is

\[
\left[6\pi + \frac{\pi}{6},\; 6\pi + \frac{5\pi}{6}\right] = \left[\frac{37\pi}{
6},\; \frac{41\pi}{6}\right] \approx [19.37,\; 21.47].
\]

We must check whether any natural number \(m\) satisfies \(m! \in [19.37,\, 21.4
7]\).

The relevant factorials are:
- \(3! = 6 < 19.37\),
- \(4! = 24 > 21.47\).

Since factorials are strictly increasing for \(m \ge 1\), every \(m \le 3\) give
s \(m! \le 6 < 19.37\), and every \(m \ge 4\) gives \(m! \ge 24 > 21.47\). Hence
 **no** natural number \(m\) has \(m!\) in the interval \(\left[\frac{37\pi}{6},
 \frac{41\pi}{6}\right]\).

Therefore the universal statement "for all natural numbers \(k\), there exists a
 natural number \(m\) such that \(k!\pi + \frac{\pi}{6} \le m! \le k!\pi + \frac
{5\pi}{6}\)" is **false**, with \(k = 3\) as an explicit counterexample. ∎
