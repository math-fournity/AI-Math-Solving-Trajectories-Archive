## Proof

**Define** $S_n(x) = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{\cos(kx)}{k}$ for $x \in [0, \pi]$.

**Key observation:** For every integer $k \geq 1$ and every real $x$, we have $\
cos(kx) \leq 1$, with equality if and only if $kx \in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}$.

**Upper bound.** For any $x \in [0, \pi]$:
$$S_n(x) = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{\cos(kx)}{k} \leq \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k} = H_n,$
$
since each term satisfies $\frac{\cos(kx)}{k} \leq \frac{1}{k}$.

**Equality at $x = 0$.** At $x = 0$, we have $\cos(k \cdot 0) = \cos(0) = 1$ for
 every $k$, so:
$$S_n(0) = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k} = H_n.$$

Therefore the maximum is **achieved** at $x = 0$:
$$\max_{x \in [0,\pi]} S_n(x) = S_n(0) = H_n.$$

**Taking the limit.** The harmonic numbers $H_n = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k}$ sati
sfy $H_n \to +\infty$ as $n \to \infty$ (the harmonic series diverges). Explicit
ly, $H_n \geq \ln(n+1)$ by comparison with the integral $\int_1^{n+1} \frac{dt}{
t}$.

**Conclusion:**
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\max_{x\in[0,\pi]}\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{\cos(kx)}{k} = \lim_{n\to
\infty} H_n = +\infty.$$

The maximum of the partial sum over $[0,\pi]$ is exactly the $n$-th harmonic num
ber, attained at $x=0$, and this diverges to $+\infty$.
