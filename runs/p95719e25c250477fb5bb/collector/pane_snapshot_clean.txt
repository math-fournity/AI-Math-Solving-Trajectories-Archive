## Proof

**Yes, $g(t)$ is real-analytic on $\mathbb{R}$, and this can be shown using only
 real-variable methods.**

### Step 1: Rewriting as a Gaussian-weighted integral

Let $\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}$ be the standard normal density.
 Then

$$g(t)=E[f(Z-t)]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(u)\,\varphi(u+t)\,du=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\
pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(u)\,e^{-(u+t)^2/2}\,du.$$

Expanding $(u+t)^2=u^2+2ut+t^2$ and factoring:

$$\boxed{g(t)=\frac{e^{-t^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(u)\,e^{-u^2
/2}\,e^{-ut}\,du.}\tag{1}$$

### Step 2: Formal power series expansion

For real $u,t$, the exponential $e^{-ut}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-ut)^n}{n!}$
converges absolutely. Substituting into (1) and formally exchanging sum and inte
gral:

$$g(t)=\frac{e^{-t^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-t)^n}{n!}\,m_n,\
qquad m_n:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(u)\,e^{-u^2/2}\,u^n\,du=\sqrt{2\pi}\,E[f(Z)\
,Z^n].\tag{2}$$

### Step 3: Justifying the interchange via absolute convergence

Using $|f(u)|\le u^4=|u|^4$:

$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{|t|^n}{n!}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(u)|\,e^{-u^2/2}|u
|^n\,du\;\le\;\sqrt{2\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{|t|^n}{n!}\,E\!\left[|Z|^{n+4}
\right].\tag{3}$$

### Step 4: Gaussian moment bound

The absolute moments of $Z\sim N(0,1)$ are

$$E[|Z|^k]=\frac{2^{k/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,\Gamma\!\Bigl(\tfrac{k+1}{2}\Bigr).$$

By Stirling's inequality $\Gamma(x)\le C\,x^{-1/2}(x/e)^x$ for $x\ge1$, we obtai
n the bound

$$E[|Z|^k]\;\le\;C'\Bigl(\frac{k}{e}\Bigr)^{k/2}\;\le\;C''\,k^{k/2}\tag{4}$$

for all $k\ge1$, with universal constants $C',C''$.

### Step 5: The series converges for every $t$

Applying (4) with $k=n+4$ and Stirling's lower bound $n!\ge c\,n^{n+1/2}e^{-n}$:

$$\frac{|t|^n}{n!}\,E[|Z|^{n+4}]\;\le\;\frac{C''}{c}\cdot\frac{(n+4)^2}{\sqrt{n}
}\cdot\Bigl(\frac{e|t|}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigr)^{n}\cdot\Bigl(1+\tfrac{4}{n}\Bigr)^{n/2}
.$$

For $n>(2e|t|)^2$, the factor $\frac{e|t|}{\sqrt{n}}<\frac12$, so $\bigl(\frac{e
|t|}{\sqrt{n}}\bigr)^n<2^{-n}$, while $\bigl(1+\frac4n\bigr)^{n/2}\le e^2$. The
polynomial prefactor $\frac{(n+4)^2}{\sqrt{n}}$ is dominated by the geometric de
cay $2^{-n}$. Hence the tail of (3) is bounded by a convergent geometric series,
 and the full series converges for **every** $t\in\mathbb{R}$.

This justifies the interchange in (2) and shows that the power series

$$h(t):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-t)^n}{n!}\,m_n$$

converges absolutely for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$, i.e., has **infinite radius of co
nvergence**.

### Step 6: Real-analyticity of $g$

A power series with infinite radius of convergence defines a real-analytic funct
ion on all of $\mathbb{R}$: at any $t_0$, re-centering $h(t)=\sum_n a_n(t-t_0+t_
0)^n$ and rearranging (permitted by absolute convergence) yields a convergent po
wer series in $(t-t_0)$. Thus $h$ is real-analytic on $\mathbb{R}$.

The Gaussian factor $e^{-t^2/2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n\,t^{2n}}{2^n\,n!
}$ is likewise real-analytic on $\mathbb{R}$ with infinite radius of convergence
.

The **product of two real-analytic functions is real-analytic**: if $f(t)=\sum a
_n(t-t_0)^n$ and $g(t)=\sum b_n(t-t_0)^n$ converge near $t_0$, then $fg=\sum c_n
(t-t_0)^n$ with $c_n=\sum_{k=0}^n a_k b_{n-k}$ converges near $t_0$ (Cauchy prod
uct). Therefore

$$g(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-t^2/2}\,h(t)$$

is **real-analytic on $\mathbb{R}$**.

### Step 7: No complex analysis is used

The proof employs exclusively real-variable tools:
- Real power series of $e^{-ut}$ and $e^{-t^2/2}$,
- Fubini's theorem / dominated convergence (real measure theory),
- Gaussian moment formula and Stirling's approximation (real analysis),
- Ratio/comparison test for real series,
- Cauchy product of real power series.

No holomorphic extension, contour integration, identity theorem, or any complex-
analytic tool is invoked.

### Remark

The real-analyticity of $f$ was **not used**. The conclusion holds for any measu
rable $f$ with $|f(x)|\le|x|^4$ (indeed, for any $f$ of at most polynomial growt
h). It is the **super-exponential decay of the Gaussian kernel** combined with t
he **at-most-polynomial growth of $f$** that forces the moment-weighted power se
ries to have infinite radius of convergence, yielding real-analyticity of $g$.
