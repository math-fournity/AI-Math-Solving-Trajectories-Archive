## Proof

**Setup.** Let the given orthonormal basis be the columns of an $n\times n$ matr
ix $Q=(q_1,\dots,q_n)$. "Positive orientation" means $\det(Q)=+1$, so $Q\in SO(n
)$: $Q$ is orthogonal with determinant $+1$. The standard basis corresponds to t
he identity $I$. We must show $Q$ can be reduced to $I$ by a sequence of rotatio
ns.

**Definition (rotation).** A *rotation* in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is an orthogonal trans
formation fixing an $(n-2)$-dimensional subspace pointwise and acting as a plana
r $2\times 2$ rotation on its $2$-dimensional orthogonal complement. A *Givens r
otation* $G_{ij}(\theta)$ is the rotation in the coordinate plane $\operatorname
{span}(e_i,e_j)$ by angle $\theta$; it equals $I$ except $G_{ii}=G_{jj}=\cos\the
ta$, $G_{ij}=-\sin\theta$, $G_{ji}=\sin\theta$. Every Givens rotation has determ
inant $1$.

**Key claim.** $SO(n)$ is generated by Givens rotations. We prove this construct
ively.

**Step 1 — Reduce $Q$ to a diagonal matrix.** We left-multiply $Q$ by Givens rot
ations to zero out sub-diagonal entries, column by column.

For each column $j=1,\dots,n-1$, process rows $i=n,n-1,\dots,j+1$ (bottom to top
). At each step, the current matrix has entry $a_{ij}$ in position $(i,j)$. Appl
y the Givens rotation $G_{i-1,\,i}(\theta)$ with
$$\cos\theta = \frac{a_{i-1,j}}{\sqrt{a_{i-1,j}^2+a_{ij}^2}},\qquad \sin\theta=\
frac{a_{ij}}{\sqrt{a_{i-1,j}^2+a_{ij}^2}},$$
which mixes rows $i-1$ and $i$ and sets the new $(i,j)$ entry to $0$. (If $a_{ij
}=0$, skip; if both $a_{i-1,j}$ and $a_{ij}$ are zero, the column is already zer
o below row $i-1$.)

After processing all columns we obtain
$$G_k\cdots G_1\, Q \;=\; R,$$
where $R$ is upper triangular. Each $G_\ell$ is orthogonal with $\det=1$, and $Q
$ is orthogonal, so $R$ is orthogonal. An upper-triangular orthogonal matrix is
diagonal with entries $\pm 1$:
$$R = D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_n),\qquad d_i\in\{+1,-1\}.$$
Since $\det(G_k\cdots G_1)=1$ and $\det(Q)=+1$, we have $\det(D)=+1$, so the num
ber of $-1$ entries is **even**.

**Step 2 — Decompose $D$ into rotations.** If $D=I$, skip. Otherwise pair up the
 $-1$ entries: whenever $d_{ii}=d_{jj}=-1$, the diagonal matrix that is $-1$ in
positions $(i,i),(j,j)$ and $+1$ elsewhere equals exactly the Givens rotation $G
_{ij}(\pi)$ (rotation by $\pi$ in the $(e_i,e_j)$ plane, which sends each of $e_
i,e_j$ to its negative). Since the $-1$'s are even in number, $D$ factors as
$$D = G_{i_1 j_1}(\pi)\cdots G_{i_m j_m}(\pi),$$
a product of rotations.

**Step 3 — Assemble the decomposition of $Q$.** From Step 1,
$$Q = G_1^{-1}\cdots G_k^{-1}\cdot D.$$
Each inverse $G_\ell^{-1}=G_{ab}(-\theta_\ell)$ is itself a Givens rotation. Com
bining with Step 2,
$$Q = \underbrace{G_1^{-1}\cdots G_k^{-1}}_{\text{rotations}}\;\cdot\;\underbrac
e{G_{i_1 j_1}(\pi)\cdots G_{i_m j_m}(\pi)}_{\text{rotations}}.$$
Thus $Q$ is a finite product of rotations. Applying the inverse product (each fa
ctor inverted is still a rotation) to $Q$ yields $I$:
$$I = \bigl(\text{product of rotations}\bigr)^{-1}\cdot Q = \bigl(\text{product
of rotations}\bigr)\cdot Q.$$

**Conclusion.** Every positively-oriented orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ ca
n be transformed into the standard basis by a finite sequence of rotations. The
answer is **yes**, this is always possible, because $SO(n)$ is generated by Give
ns (planar) rotations, as shown by the constructive reduction of any $Q\in SO(n)
$ to the identity via column-by-column zeroing followed by pairing of diagonal s
igns.
