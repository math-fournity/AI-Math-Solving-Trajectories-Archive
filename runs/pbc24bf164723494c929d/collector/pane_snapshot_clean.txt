Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether a thin base of order $k$ al
ways exists for any finite alphabet $A$ with $|A| \geq 2$ and any natural number
 $k$.

**Definitions.** A language $B \subseteq A^*$ is *thin* if there exists a non-em
pty word $w \in A^+$ such that $B \cap A^*wA^* = \emptyset$ (no element of $B$ c
ontains $w$ as a factor). A *base of order $k$* is a set $B \subseteq A^*$ with
$B^k = A^*$ (every word is a product of $k$ elements of $B$, allowing $\epsilon
\in B$).

**Answer: No.** In fact, for *no* finite alphabet $A$ with $|A|\geq 2$ and *no*
natural number $k\geq 1$ does a thin base of order $k$ exist.

---

**Proof.**

Suppose for contradiction that $B \subseteq A^*$ is a thin base of order $k$. Si
nce $B$ is thin, there exists a non-empty word $w \in A^+$ such that no element
of $B$ contains $w$ as a factor. Let $\ell = |w| \geq 1$.

**Claim.** For any $b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_k \in B$, the concatenated word $u = b_1
 b_2 \cdots b_k$ contains at most $(k-1)(\ell - 1)$ occurrences of $w$ as a fact
or.

*Proof of Claim.* Any occurrence of $w$ in $u$ cannot lie entirely within any si
ngle factor $b_i$, since $b_i$ avoids $w$ as a factor. Therefore, every occurren
ce of $w$ in $u$ must span at least one of the $k-1$ boundaries between consecut
ive factors $b_i$ and $b_{i+1}$.

Consider the boundary between $b_i$ and $b_{i+1}$, located at position $s_i = |b
_1 \cdots b_i|$ in $u$. An occurrence of $w$ (of length $\ell$) spanning this bo
undary must start at a position $p$ satisfying $s_i - \ell + 1 \leq p \leq s_i -
 1$, giving at most $\ell - 1$ possible starting positions per boundary.

We count pairs $(\text{occurrence}, \text{boundary})$ where the occurrence spans
 the boundary. Each occurrence contributes at least one pair (since it spans at
least one boundary), so:

$$\#\text{occurrences} \;\leq\; \#\text{pairs} \;\leq\; \sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(\ell -
1) \;=\; (k-1)(\ell-1).$$

This proves the Claim. $\square$

**Deriving the contradiction.** Set $N = (k-1)(\ell - 1) + 1$ and consider the w
ord $u^* = w^N \in A^*$. The word $u^*$ contains at least $N$ *non-overlapping*
occurrences of $w$ (at positions $0, \ell, 2\ell, \ldots, (N-1)\ell$). Since

$$N = (k-1)(\ell-1) + 1 > (k-1)(\ell-1),$$

the Claim implies $u^* \notin B^k$. But $B^k = A^*$ and $u^* \in A^*$, a contrad
iction.

**Edge cases.**
- If $k = 1$: the bound gives $(k-1)(\ell-1) = 0$, so $B^1 = B$ contains no occu
rrence of $w$, hence $w \notin B$ and $B \neq A^*$.
- If $\ell = 1$ (i.e., $w$ is a single letter $a$): the bound gives $0$, so $B^k
$ avoids the letter $a$ entirely, hence $a \notin B^k$ and $B^k \neq A^*$.

Both are consistent with the general argument. $\blacksquare$

---

**Conclusion.** The factor-avoidance condition defining thinness is fundamentall
y incompatible with finite-order generation: a $k$-fold product of $w$-avoiding
words can contain at most $(k-1)(\ell-1)$ occurrences of $w$ (all forced to stra
ddle factor boundaries), while $A^*$ contains words with arbitrarily many occurr
ences of $w$. Therefore, for every finite alphabet $A$ with $|A| \geq 2$ and eve
ry natural number $k$, **no** thin base of order $k$ exists.
