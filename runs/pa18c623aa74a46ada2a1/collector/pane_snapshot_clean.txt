# Proof

**Claim:** Yes. There exists a complete metric on $(0,1)$ inducing the usual top
ology.

**Construction.** Define $f:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}$ by
$$f(x)=\tan\!\big(\pi(x-\tfrac12)\big).$$
As $x\to 0^+$, $f(x)\to -\infty$; as $x\to 1^-$, $f(x)\to +\infty$; and $f$ is c
ontinuous and strictly increasing on $(0,1)$. Hence $f$ is a bijection onto $\ma
thbb{R}$, with continuous inverse $f^{-1}=\frac12+\frac1\pi\arctan$. Thus $f$ is
 a **homeomorphism** between $((0,1),\text{usual})$ and $(\mathbb{R},\text{usual
})$.

Define a metric on $(0,1)$ by
$$d(x,y)=|f(x)-f(y)|.$$
This is a metric (positivity, symmetry, and the triangle inequality follow immed
iately from the corresponding properties of the Euclidean metric on $\mathbb{R}$
, since $f$ is injective).

**Same topology.** The open $d$-ball of radius $r$ centered at $x$ is
$$B_d(x,r)=\{y\in(0,1):|f(x)-f(y)|<r\}=f^{-1}\big(B_{\mathbb{R}}(f(x),r)\big).$$
Since $f$ is continuous in the usual topology, every $d$-ball is usual-open, so
every $d$-open set is usual-open. Conversely, let $U$ be usual-open and $x\in U$
; pick $\varepsilon>0$ with $B_{\text{usual}}(x,\varepsilon)\subseteq U$. By con
tinuity of $f^{-1}$ at $f(x)$, there exists $\delta>0$ such that $f^{-1}(B_{\mat
hbb{R}}(f(x),\delta))\subseteq B_{\text{usual}}(x,\varepsilon)\subseteq U$. That
 is, $B_d(x,\delta)\subseteq U$. So $U$ is $d$-open. Hence the $d$-topology equa
ls the usual topology on $(0,1)$.

**Completeness.** Let $(x_n)$ be a $d$-Cauchy sequence in $(0,1)$. Then $(f(x_n)
)$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb{R}$ (since $|f(x_m)-f(x_n)|=d(x_m,x_n)$). The real line
 with the Euclidean metric is complete, so $f(x_n)\to L$ for some $L\in\mathbb{R
}$. Set $x^*=f^{-1}(L)\in(0,1)$. By continuity of $f^{-1}$, equivalently by $d(x
_n,x^*)=|f(x_n)-L|\to 0$, we get $x_n\to x^*$ in $((0,1),d)$. Thus every Cauchy
sequence converges, and $((0,1),d)$ is complete.

**Remark (why the usual metric fails).** Under the Euclidean metric, the sequenc
e $x_n=1/(n+1)$ is Cauchy in $(0,1)$ but converges to $0\notin(0,1)$, so it has
no limit in $(0,1)$; the Euclidean metric is not complete. The homeomorphism $f$
 "stretches the missing endpoints to infinity," so sequences that used to escape
 toward $0$ or $1$ are no longer Cauchy under $d$ (e.g. $f(1/(n+1))\to -\infty$,
 not Cauchy), while genuinely convergent behavior is preserved because $f$ is a
homeomorphism.

Therefore a complete metric on $(0,1)$ inducing the usual topology **does exist*
*, namely $d(x,y)=|\tan(\pi(x-\tfrac12))-\tan(\pi(y-\tfrac12))|$.
