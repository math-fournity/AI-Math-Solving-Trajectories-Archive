## Proof

**Yes, such six natural numbers exist.**

**Construction.** Let $p_1, p_2, p_3, p_4$ be four distinct primes (say $2, 3, 5
, 7$). Define six numbers, each as a product of all four primes, where exactly t
wo of the four exponents are $2$ and the other two are $1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_1 &= p_1^2\, p_2^2\, p_3\, p_4, \quad &a_2 &= p_1^2\, p_2\, p_3^2\, p_4,\\
a_3 &= p_1^2\, p_2\, p_3\, p_4^2, \quad &a_4 &= p_1\, p_2^2\, p_3^2\, p_4,\\
a_5 &= p_1\, p_2^2\, p_3\, p_4^2, \quad &a_6 &= p_1\, p_2\, p_3^2\, p_4^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

These correspond to the $\binom{4}{2}=6$ ways to choose which two primes get exp
onent $2$.

**Verification of Condition 1 (antichain).** Consider any two distinct numbers $
a_i, a_j$. They correspond to two different 2-element subsets $S_i, S_j \subsete
q \{1,2,3,4\}$ (the primes with exponent $2$). Since $S_i \neq S_j$ and both hav
e size $2$, neither $S_i \subseteq S_j$ nor $S_j \subseteq S_i$. Therefore:
- There is a prime $p_t \in S_i \setminus S_j$: the exponent of $p_t$ in $a_i$ i
s $2$ but in $a_j$ is $1$, so $a_i \nmid a_j$.
- There is a prime $p_s \in S_j \setminus S_i$: the exponent of $p_s$ in $a_j$ i
s $2$ but in $a_i$ is $1$, so $a_j \nmid a_i$.

Hence no number divides any other. ✓

**Verification of Condition 2 (square divisibility).** For any $i, j$ and any pr
ime $p_t$ ($t \in \{1,2,3,4\}$):
- The exponent of $p_t$ in $a_i$ is either $1$ or $2$.
- The exponent of $p_t$ in $a_j$ is either $1$ or $2$, so the exponent of $p_t$
in $a_j^2$ is either $2$ or $4$.

In every case, $\text{exp}_{p_t}(a_i) \leq 2 \leq \text{exp}_{p_t}(a_j^2)$. Sinc
e this holds for all four primes, $a_i \mid a_j^2$ for all $i, j$. ✓

**Explicit example** (with $p_1=2, p_2=3, p_3=5, p_4=7$):

$$a_1 = 1260,\; a_2 = 2100,\; a_3 = 2940,\; a_4 = 3150,\; a_5 = 4410,\; a_6 = 73
50.$$

Both conditions are satisfied, as verified above.
