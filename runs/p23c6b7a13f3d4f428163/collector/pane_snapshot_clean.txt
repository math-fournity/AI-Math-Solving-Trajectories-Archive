## Proof

**Step 1: A candidate solution.** We claim $f(x)=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}$ satisfies (i)–(
iii).

Let $g(x)=\dfrac{x^{2}}{2-x^{2}}$. Then
$$
1-g(x)^{2}=1-\frac{x^{4}}{(2-x^{2})^{2}}=\frac{(2-x^{2})^{2}-x^{4}}{(2-x^{2})^{2
}}=\frac{4-4x^{2}}{(2-x^{2})^{2}}=\frac{4(1-x^{2})}{(2-x^{2})^{2}}.
$$
Hence
$$
\frac{2-x^{2}}{2}\,\sqrt{1-g(x)^{2}}=\frac{2-x^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{2\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
}{2-x^{2}}=\sqrt{1-x^{2}},
$$
so $f(x)=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}$ satisfies (i). Clearly $f(0)=1$, giving (ii), and
$$
\frac{f(x)}{\sqrt{1-x}}=\frac{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{\sqrt{1-x}}=\sqrt{1+x}\xrightarrow
[x\to1^{-}]{}\sqrt2,
$$
which is finite, giving (iii).

**Step 2: Reduction to an invariant.** Let $f$ be any continuous function satisf
ying (i)–(iii). Since $g(x)=g(-x)$ (it depends only on $x^{2}$), equation (i) gi
ves $f(x)=f(-x)$; thus $f$ is **even**. It suffices to determine $f$ on $[0,1]$.

For $x\in[0,1)$ define
$$
F(x)=\frac{f(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}.
$$
This is well defined and continuous on $[0,1)$ (the denominator is positive and
continuous there). Using (i) and the identity from Step 1,
$$
F(x)=\frac{f(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}=\frac{\frac{2-x^{2}}{2}\,f(g(x))}{\frac{2-x^{2}
}{2}\,\sqrt{1-g(x)^{2}}}=\frac{f(g(x))}{\sqrt{1-g(x)^{2}}}=F(g(x)).
$$
So $F$ is **invariant under $g$**: $F(x)=F(g(x))$ for all $x\in[0,1)$.

**Step 3: The orbit of $g$ tends to $0$.** For $x\in(0,1)$,
$$
g(x)<x\iff \frac{x^{2}}{2-x^{2}}<x\iff x^{2}<2x-x^{3}\iff x^{2}+x^{3}<2x\iff x+x
^{2}<2\iff (x+2)(x-1)<0,
$$
which holds for $x\in(0,1)$. Also $g(0)=0$. Thus for any $x_{0}\in(0,1)$, the it
erates $x_{n+1}=g(x_{n})$ form a decreasing sequence bounded below by $0$, hence
 converge to some $L\in[0,1)$ satisfying $L=g(L)$, i.e.
$$
L=\frac{L^{2}}{2-L^{2}}\implies L(2-L^{2})=L^{2}\implies L(L^{2}+L-2)=0\implies
L(L+2)(L-1)=0.
$$
Since $L<1$, we get $L=0$. (For $x_{0}=0$ the orbit is constantly $0$.)

**Step 4: $F\equiv1$.** By induction on the invariance relation, $F(x_{0})=F(x_{
n})$ for every $n$. Taking $n\to\infty$ and using continuity of $F$ at $0$,
$$
F(x_{0})=\lim_{n\to\infty}F(x_{n})=F(0)=\frac{f(0)}{\sqrt{1-0}}=1.
$$
Thus $F(x)=1$ for all $x\in[0,1)$, i.e. $f(x)=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}$ on $[0,1)$.

**Step 5: Extension to $[-1,1]$.** Since $f$ is even, $f(x)=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}$ on $
(-1,1)$. By continuity at the endpoints,
$$
f(1)=\lim_{x\to1^{-}}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}=0,\qquad f(-1)=\lim_{x\to-1^{+}}\sqrt{1-x^{2
}}=0,
$$
which agrees with $\sqrt{1-x^{2}}$. Condition (iii) guarantees $f$ has a finite
limit compatible with this value at $x=1$ (and evenness handles $x=-1$), so no o
ther continuous extension is possible.

Therefore $f$ is **unique**, and

$$
\boxed{f(x)=\sqrt{1-x^{2}},\qquad x\in[-1,1].}
$$
