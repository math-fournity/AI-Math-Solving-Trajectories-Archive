Looking at this problem, I need to determine the largest $k(n)$ such that every
real $n \times n$ matrix is orthogonally equivalent to a matrix with at least $k
$ zero entries.

I interpret "orthogonally equivalent" in the standard sense: $A$ and $B$ are ort
hogonally equivalent if $B = U A V^T$ for orthogonal matrices $U, V \in O(n)$.

## Proof

**Claim:** $k(n) = n^2 - n$.

### Lower Bound: $k(n) \geq n^2 - n$

I show every real $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is orthogonally equivalent to a diagon
al matrix (with at most $n$ nonzero entries, hence at least $n^2 - n$ zeros). Th
is is the Singular Value Decomposition, which I prove by induction on $n$.

**Base case** ($n=1$): Trivial; every $1 \times 1$ matrix is already diagonal wi
th $0 = 1^2 - 1$ zeros.

**Inductive step:** Let $A$ be $n \times n$. If $A = 0$, then $A$ already has $n
^2 \geq n^2 - n$ zeros. Otherwise, the function $v \mapsto \|Av\|^2 = v^T A^T A
v$ is continuous on the compact unit sphere $S^{n-1}$, so it attains its maximum
 $\sigma_1^2 > 0$ at some unit vector $v_1$. At this maximizer, $\nabla(v^T A^T
A v) = 2 A^T A v$ is proportional to $\nabla(v^T v) = 2v$, giving $A^T A \, v_1
= \sigma_1^2 v_1$.

Set $u_1 = \frac{1}{\sigma_1} A v_1$, which is a unit vector. Since $A^T A$ is s
ymmetric and $v_1$ is an eigenvector, the orthogonal complement $v_1^\perp$ is $
A^T A$-invariant. Choose $v_2, \ldots, v_n$ to complete $v_1$ to an orthonormal
basis, and $u_2, \ldots, u_n$ to complete $u_1$ to an orthonormal basis. Form or
thogonal matrices $V_1 = [v_1 | \cdots | v_n]$ and $U_1 = [u_1 | \cdots | u_n]$.

**First column of $U_1^T A V_1$:** The $(i,1)$-entry is $u_i^T A v_1 = u_i^T (\s
igma_1 u_1) = \sigma_1 \delta_{i1}$, so the first column is $(\sigma_1, 0, \ldot
s, 0)^T$.

**First row of $U_1^T A V_1$:** The $(1,j)$-entry is $u_1^T A v_j = \frac{1}{\si
gma_1} v_1^T A^T A \, v_j$. For $j > 1$, since $v_j \in v_1^\perp$ and $A^T A$ i
s symmetric with $v_1$ as eigenvector, we have $v_1^T A^T A \, v_j = (A^T A \, v
_1)^T v_j = \sigma_1^2 v_1^T v_j = 0$. So the first row is $(\sigma_1, 0, \ldots
, 0)$.

Therefore $U_1^T A V_1 = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1 & \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{0} & A
' \end{pmatrix}$ where $A'$ is $(n{-1}) \times (n{-1})$. By the inductive hypoth
esis, $A' = U' \Sigma' V'^T$ with $U', V'$ orthogonal and $\Sigma'$ diagonal. Se
tting $U = U_1 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{0} & U' \end{pmatrix}$
and $V = V_1 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{0} & V' \end{pmatrix}$ (b
oth orthogonal), we get $U^T A V = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1 & \mathbf{0} \\ \mat
hbf{0} & \Sigma' \end{pmatrix} =: \Sigma$, which is diagonal.

The diagonal matrix $\Sigma$ has at most $n$ nonzero entries (the diagonal), hen
ce at least $n^2 - n$ zero entries. Thus every matrix is orthogonally equivalent
 to one with at least $n^2 - n$ zeros. $\checkmark$

### Upper Bound: $k(n) \leq n^2 - n$

Let $A = I_n$ (the identity matrix, which is invertible with rank $n$). For any
orthogonal $U, V$, the matrix $B = U A V^T = U V^T$ is orthogonal, hence inverti
ble, so $\operatorname{rank}(B) = n$.

An invertible matrix cannot have a zero row: if row $i$ of $B$ were entirely zer
o, then $e_i^T B = 0$, meaning $B^T e_i = 0$, so $B^T$ (and hence $B$) would be
singular—a contradiction. Therefore each of the $n$ rows of $B$ contains at leas
t one nonzero entry, giving at least $n$ nonzero entries total.

Consequently, $B$ has at most $n^2 - n$ zero entries. Since this holds for **eve
ry** choice of orthogonal $U, V$, the matrix $A = I_n$ cannot be made orthogonal
ly equivalent to any matrix with more than $n^2 - n$ zeros. $\checkmark$

### Conclusion

The lower bound gives $k(n) \geq n^2 - n$ and the upper bound gives $k(n) \leq n
^2 - n$. Therefore:

$$\boxed{k(n) = n^2 - n.}$$
