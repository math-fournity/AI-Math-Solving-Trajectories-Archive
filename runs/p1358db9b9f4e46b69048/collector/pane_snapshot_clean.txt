## Proof

Let $\psi:\mathbb D^2\to\mathbb R$ be the harmonic extension of $g$. The answer
is **yes**: $d\psi\neq 0$ almost everywhere in $\mathbb D^2$.

**Key fact.** Harmonic functions are real-analytic: locally on $\mathbb D^2$, $\
psi$ is the real part of a holomorphic function, hence admits a convergent power
-series expansion. Consequently the partial derivatives $\psi_x,\psi_y$ are also
 real-analytic.

**Critical set has measure zero unless $\psi$ is constant.** Define
$$C=\{p\in\mathbb D^2 : d\psi_p=0\}=\{p:\psi_x(p)=0\}\cap\{p:\psi_y(p)=0\}.$$
Suppose $C$ has positive Lebesgue measure. Then $C$ has a density point $p$ lyin
g in the interior of $\mathbb D^2$. Since $\psi_x$ is real-analytic and vanishes
 on a set having $p$ as a density point, $\psi_x\equiv 0$ in a neighborhood of $
p$ (a real-analytic function that vanishes on a set of positive measure near a p
oint vanishes identically near that point). Likewise $\psi_y\equiv 0$ near $p$,
so $\psi$ is constant in a neighborhood of $p$. By unique continuation for harmo
nic functions (or by real-analyticity plus connectedness of $\mathbb D^2$), $\ps
i$ is constant on all of $\mathbb D^2$.

Thus: either $C=\mathbb D^2$ (which forces $\psi$ constant), or $C$ has measure
zero.

**$\psi$ is nonconstant.** By the maximum principle, if $\psi$ were constant on
$\mathbb D^2$, then its boundary trace $g=\psi|_{\mathbb S^1}$ would be constant
. A constant $g$ has *every* point of $\mathbb S^1$ as a critical point, contrad
icting the hypothesis that $g$ has no critical points. Hence $\psi$ is nonconsta
nt.

Combining the two steps: $C$ has measure zero, i.e. $d\psi\neq 0$ almost everywh
ere in $\mathbb D^2$.

**Remark on the hypothesis.** Strictly, a smooth $g:\mathbb S^1\to\mathbb R$ alw
ays possesses critical points: $g$ attains a maximum on the compact manifold $\m
athbb S^1$, and any interior extremum of $g$ on the circle is a critical point (
equivalently, by periodicity/Rolle's theorem $g'$ must vanish). So the hypothesi
s "no critical points" is in fact vacuous. Interpreted as the intended guarantee
 that $g$—and hence $\psi$—is nonconstant, the conclusion follows from the real-
analyticity argument above. In either reading (vacuous truth, or nonconstancy fo
rcing measure-zero critical set), the answer is affirmative.
