## Proof

Let $C[0,1]$ denote the space of continuous functions on $[0,1]$. We consider tw
o metrics:

- **Supremum (uniform) metric:** $d_\infty(f,g) = \sup_{x\in[0,1]} |f(x)-g(x)|$.
- **Definite integral ($L^1$) metric:** $d_1(f,g) = \int_0^1 |f(x)-g(x)|\,dx$.

Both are well-defined metrics on $C[0,1]$ (the integral metric is a metric since
 continuity of $|f-g|$ ensures $\int_0^1|f-g|=0 \Rightarrow f=g$).

**Claim:** These two metrics induce **different** topologies on $C[0,1]$.

It suffices to exhibit a sequence that converges in one metric but not the other
, since sequential convergence determines the topology on any metric space.

**The sequence.** Define $f_n(x) = x^n$ for $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$, and let $f \eq
uiv 0$ be the zero function.

**Convergence in the $L^1$ metric.** We compute:
$$d_1(f_n, f) = \int_0^1 x^n\,dx = \frac{1}{n+1} \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 0.$$
Hence $f_n \to f$ in the $d_1$ metric.

**Non-convergence in the supremum metric.** Since $f_n(1) = 1^n = 1$ for every $
n$, while $f(1) = 0$, we have
$$d_\infty(f_n, f) = \sup_{x\in[0,1]} x^n = 1$$
for every $n$. Therefore $d_\infty(f_n, f) = 1 \not\to 0$, so $f_n$ does **not**
 converge to $f$ in the $d_\infty$ metric.

**Conclusion.** The sequence $(f_n)$ converges to $f$ in the $d_1$-topology but
not in the $d_\infty$-topology. Since the set of convergent sequences (with thei
r limits) is determined entirely by the topology, the two metrics cannot induce
the same topology. Hence:

> **The supremum metric and the definite integral metric induce different topolo
gies on $C[0,1]$.**
