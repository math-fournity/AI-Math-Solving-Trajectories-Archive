## Proof

**Yes**, an infinite algebraic extension $k'/k$ is sufficient to ensure that $k$
 is not algebraically closed in $k'(x)$.

Recall that $k$ is *algebraically closed in* $k'(x)$ means: every element of $k'
(x)$ that is algebraic over $k$ must belong to $k$.

**Key observation.** The field $k'$ embeds into $k'(x)$ as the subfield of const
ant rational functions. That is, for every $\alpha \in k'$, the constant functio
n $\alpha \in k'(x)$.

**The argument.** Since $k'/k$ is algebraic, every element $\alpha \in k'$ is al
gebraic over $k$. Since $k'/k$ is infinite, in particular $k' \neq k$, so there
exists some $\alpha \in k' \setminus k$. Now:

- $\alpha \in k'(x)$ (as a constant rational function),
- $\alpha$ is algebraic over $k$ (because $k'/k$ is algebraic),
- $\alpha \notin k$ (by choice).

Therefore $\alpha$ is an element of $k'(x)$ that is algebraic over $k$ but does
not lie in $k$. By definition, $k$ is **not** algebraically closed in $k'(x)$.

**Remark (stronger statement).** The argument actually shows that for *any* alge
braic extension $k'/k$ (finite or infinite), $k$ is algebraically closed in $k'(
x)$ if and only if $k' = k$. The infinitude of the extension is not needed—any n
ontrivial algebraic extension already suffices. The condition "$k'/k$ is infinit
e algebraic" is therefore more than sufficient; it is a strictly stronger hypoth
esis than necessary.
