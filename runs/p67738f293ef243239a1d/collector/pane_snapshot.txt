## Proof

**Clarifying the notation.** In the expression
$$X(s,t) \;=\; \int_0^t (t-r)\,dB_r \;+\; \int_s^t (t-u)\,dB_u, \qquad 0\le s\le
 t,$$
the dummy variable of the first integral has been renamed $r$ to avoid clashing
with the free parameter $s$ appearing in the second integral. Both $s$ and $t$ a
re deterministic parameters; the only source of randomness is the Brownian motio
n $B$.

---

### Step 1. Rewrite as a single Itô integral with a deterministic integrand.

Since $[s,t]\subseteq[0,t]$, the two integrals share the overlapping region $[s,
t]$. Splitting the first integral at $s$ and combining:

$$X(s,t) \;=\; \int_0^s (t-r)\,dB_r \;+\; \int_s^t (t-r)\,dB_r \;+\; \int_s^t (t
-u)\,dB_u.$$

Renaming the dummy in the third term to $r$ and merging the two integrals over $
[s,t]$:

$$\boxed{X(s,t) \;=\; \int_0^s (t-r)\,dB_r \;+\; 2\int_s^t (t-r)\,dB_r \;=\; \in
t_0^t g_{s,t}(r)\,dB_r,}$$

where the integrand is the **deterministic** function

$$g_{s,t}(r) \;=\; (t-r)\,\mathbf{1}_{[0,s]}(r) \;+\; 2(t-r)\,\mathbf{1}_{(s,t]}
(r).$$

For every fixed $(s,t)$, $g_{s,t}$ is a bounded, measurable, deterministic funct
ion on $[0,t]$, hence $g_{s,t}\in L^2[0,t]$.

---

### Step 2. Each $X(s,t)$ is Gaussian.

A classical result states: if $f$ is a deterministic function in $L^2[0,T]$, the
n the Itô integral $\int_0^T f(r)\,dB_r$ is a centered Gaussian random variable
with variance $\int_0^T f^2(r)\,dr$. (This follows because the Riemann-sum appro
ximations $\sum f(r_i)\,\Delta B_{r_i}$ are linear combinations of the independe
nt Gaussian increments $\Delta B_{r_i}$, hence Gaussian; the $L^2$-limit of Gaus
sian random variables is Gaussian.)

Applying this with $f = g_{s,t}$: **each $X(s,t)$ is Gaussian.**

---

### Step 3. Any finite collection is jointly Gaussian (the process is Gaussian).

Take any finite set of parameter pairs $\{(s_1,t_1),\dots,(s_n,t_n)\}$ with $0\l
e s_i\le t_i$. Each $X(s_i,t_i)=\int_0^{t_i} g_{s_i,t_i}(r)\,dB_r$ is an Itô int
egral of a deterministic integrand.

Let $T = \max_i\, t_i$. Extend each $g_{s_i,t_i}$ to $[0,T]$ by setting it to $0
$ on $(t_i, T]$. Then

$$X(s_i,t_i) \;=\; \int_0^T g_{s_i,t_i}(r)\,dB_r, \qquad i=1,\dots,n.$$

For any constants $a_1,\dots,a_n\in\mathbb{R}$,

$$\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\, X(s_i,t_i) \;=\; \int_0^T \Big(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\, g_{s_i,t_
i}(r)\Big)\,dB_r.$$

The integrand $\sum a_i\, g_{s_i,t_i}(r)$ is deterministic and in $L^2[0,T]$, so
 by the result of Step 2, the linear combination $\sum a_i X(s_i,t_i)$ is a (one
-dimensional) Gaussian random variable.

A random vector whose every linear combination is Gaussian is, by definition, a
**jointly Gaussian** vector. Therefore

$$\bigl(X(s_1,t_1),\dots,X(s_n,t_n)\bigr)$$

is jointly Gaussian for every finite choice of parameters.

---

### Step 4. Conclusion.

Since every finite-dimensional marginal of the family $\{X(s,t) : 0\le s\le t\}$
 is multivariate normal, the process **is Gaussian** (in the sense of a Gaussian
 process: a process all of whose finite-dimensional distributions are Gaussian).

### Summary

The key points are:

1. **Linearity in $B$:** Both integrals are Itô integrals with *deterministic* i
ntegrands $(t-r)$ and $(t-u)$. The parameter $t$ entering the integrand does not
 introduce any nonlinearity in $B$—for each fixed $(s,t)$ the integrand is a fix
ed deterministic function.

2. **No nonlinear operations on $B$:** At no point does $B$ appear inside a nonl
inear function, inside an integrand that depends on $B$, or as a random limit of
 integration. The only randomness enters through the linear stochastic integral
$\int (\text{deterministic})\,dB$.

3. **Joint Gaussianity via linear combinations:** Any linear combination of the
$X(s_i,t_i)$ reduces to a single Itô integral of a deterministic function, hence
 is Gaussian—exactly the characterization of a jointly Gaussian family.

**The process $\int_0^t (t-s)\,dB_s + \int_s^t (t-u)\,dB_u$ is Gaussian.**
