**Step 1. The premise "$\mathbb{Q}$ is $T_4$" holds.**

The rationals $\mathbb{Q}$, equipped with the subspace topology inherited from $
\mathbb{R}$ with the Euclidean topology, are metrizable: the restricted Euclidea
n metric
$$d(p,q)=|p-q|,\qquad p,q\in\mathbb{Q},$$
is a metric on $\mathbb{Q}$ that induces exactly the subspace topology. A standa
rd theorem in point-set topology states that **every metrizable space is normal*
* (hence $T_4$, since metrizable spaces are also $T_1$ — singletons are closed).
 Briefly, given two disjoint closed sets $A,B$ in a metric space, the function
$$f(x)=\frac{d(x,A)}{d(x,A)+d(x,B)}$$
is a continuous Urysohn function separating $A$ and $B$, and the open sets
$$U=\{x:d(x,A)<d(x,B)\},\qquad V=\{x:d(x,B)<d(x,A)\}$$
are disjoint neighborhoods of $A$ and $B$. Thus $\mathbb{Q}$ is normal and $T_1$
, i.e. $T_4$.

So the supposition in the problem is satisfied in reality; it is not a counterfa
ctual assumption.

**Step 2. $\mathbb{R}$ with the Euclidean topology is metrizable.**

The Euclidean metric
$$d(x,y)=|x-y|,\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R},$$
is a metric on $\mathbb{R}$ whose induced topology is precisely the Euclidean to
pology (the open balls $B(x,r)=(x-r,x+r)$ form a basis for the Euclidean topolog
y). Hence $\mathbb{R}$ is metrizable with respect to the Euclidean topology.

**Conclusion.** Since the premise is true and the conclusion is true, the implic
