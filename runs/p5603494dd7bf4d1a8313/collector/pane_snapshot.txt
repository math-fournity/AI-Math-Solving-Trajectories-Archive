Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the rearrangement theorem f
or absolutely convergent series requires completeness of the underlying space.

**Answer: Yes.** Every rearrangement converges to the same limit, and completene
ss of $X$ is not needed.

---

## Proof

**Theorem.** Let $X$ be a normed space (not necessarily complete). Suppose $(x_n
) \subset X$ satisfies:
- $\sum_{n=1}^\infty x_n$ converges to some $s \in X$, and
- $\sum_{n=1}^\infty \|x_n\| < \infty$ (absolute convergence).

Then for every permutation $\sigma: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$, the rearranged s
eries $\sum_{n=1}^\infty x_{\sigma(n)}$ converges to $s$.

**Proof.** Let $\sigma$ be a permutation and let $\varepsilon > 0$.

**Step 1 (Control the tail of the absolute series).** Since $\sum_{n=1}^\infty \
|x_n\|$ is a convergent series of non-negative reals, there exists $N \in \mathb
b{N}$ such that
$$\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty} \|x_n\| < \varepsilon.$$

**Step 2 (Find where the first $N$ terms appear in the rearrangement).** Since $
\sigma$ is a bijection, the finite set $\{1,\dots,N\}$ is contained in $\{\sigma
(1), \sigma(2), \dots\}$. Choose $K$ large enough that
$$\{1, 2, \dots, N\} \subseteq \{\sigma(1), \dots, \sigma(K)\}.$$

**Step 3 (Estimate the partial sums of the rearrangement).** Fix $k \geq K$ and
set $S_k = \{\sigma(1), \dots, \sigma(k)\}$. By construction, $\{1,\dots,N\} \su
bseteq S_k$.

For any $m \geq \max S_k$, we have $S_k \subseteq \{1,\dots,m\}$, so the partial
 sums decompose as:
$$\sum_{i=1}^{k} x_{\sigma(i)} - \sum_{n=1}^{m} x_n = \sum_{n \in S_k} x_n - \su
m_{n=1}^{m} x_n = -\sum_{n \in \{1,\dots,m\} \setminus S_k} x_n.$$

Since $\{1,\dots,N\} \subseteq S_k$, every index in $\{1,\dots,m\} \setminus S_k
$ exceeds $N$. Applying the triangle inequality to this **finite** sum:
$$\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{k} x_{\sigma(i)} - \sum_{n=1}^{m} x_n\right\| = \left\|\sum
_{n \in \{1,\dots,m\}\setminus S_k} x_n\right\| \leq \sum_{n \in \{1,\dots,m\}\s
etminus S_k} \|x_n\| \leq \sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty} \|x_n\| < \varepsilon.$$

**Step 4 (Pass to the limit in $m$).** Since $\sum_{n=1}^{m} x_n \to s$ as $m \t
o \infty$ and the norm is continuous (a property of *every* normed space, comple
te or not), we take $m \to \infty$ in the inequality above to obtain:
$$\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{k} x_{\sigma(i)} - s\right\| \leq \varepsilon \qquad \text{
for all } k \geq K.$$

Since $\varepsilon > 0$ was arbitrary, $\sum_{i=1}^{k} x_{\sigma(i)} \to s$ as $
k \to \infty$. $\blacksquare$

## Why completeness is not needed

The proof uses only three ingredients:

1. **Convergence of $\sum x_n$ to $s$** — this is *given* by hypothesis, not der
ived.
2. **Smallness of the tail $\sum_{n > N} \|x_n\|$** — this is a property of the
convergent real series $\sum \|x_n\|$, which lives in $\mathbb{R}$ (complete), n
ot in $X$.
3. **Continuity of the norm** $\|\cdot\|: X \to \mathbb{R}$ — this holds in ever
y normed space.

At no point do we need to construct a limit in $X$ from a Cauchy sequence (which
 is what completeness would provide). The limit $s$ is already given; we only ne
ed to show the rearranged partial sums approach it, which follows from the trian
gle inequality on finite sums plus continuity of the norm.

## The role of completeness

Completeness of $X$ enters the theory at a different point: it is needed to prov
e that **absolute convergence implies convergence** (i.e., $\sum \|x_n\| < \inft
y \Rightarrow \sum x_n$ converges). In a non-Banach space, this implication can
fail — there exist sequences with $\sum \|x_n\| < \infty$ whose partial sums are
 Cauchy but do not converge in $X$ (the "sum" lives in the completion $\widehat{
X} \setminus X$).

However, once convergence of $\sum x_n$ to a limit $s \in X$ is assumed (as the
problem does by asking about "the same limit"), the rearrangement theorem goes t
hrough without completeness. The two facts are logically independent:

| Fact | Requires completeness? |
|------|----------------------|
| Absolute convergence $\Rightarrow$ convergence | **Yes** |
| Convergence + absolute convergence $\Rightarrow$ all rearrangements converge t
o the same limit | **No** |
