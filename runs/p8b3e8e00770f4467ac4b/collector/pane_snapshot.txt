## Part (a)

**Claim.** The Baire Category Theorem holds in every topologically complete metr
ic space.

**Recall.** A metric space $(X, d)$ is *topologically complete* if there exists
a metric $d'$ on $X$ such that:
- $d'$ induces the same topology as $d$ (i.e., the same open sets), and
- $(X, d')$ is a complete metric space.

The Baire Category Theorem (for metric spaces) states:

> If $(X, d)$ is a complete metric space, then the intersection of any countable
 family of dense open subsets of $X$ is dense in $X$.

Equivalently, $X$ is not the union of countably many closed sets with empty inte
rior (i.e., $X$ is not meager in itself).

**Proof.** Let $(X, d)$ be a topologically complete metric space. By definition,
 there exists a metric $d'$ on $X$ that induces the same topology as $d$ and suc
h that $(X, d')$ is complete.

Since $(X, d')$ is a complete metric space, the Baire Category Theorem applies t
o $(X, d')$: the intersection of any countable family of dense open subsets of $
(X, d')$ is dense in $(X, d')$.

Now we observe that the Baire Category Theorem is a **purely topological** state
ment. Specifically:

- **"Open set"** is a topological notion — it depends only on the topology, not
on the specific metric.
- **"Dense set"** (a set whose closure is all of $X$) is a topological notion —
closure depends only on the topology.
- **"Countable intersection"** is a set-theoretic operation, independent of the
metric.

Since $d$ and $d'$ induce the *same* topology, they have the *same* open sets, t
he *same* closed sets, the *same* notion of density, and the *same* closures. Th
erefore:

> A countable family $\{U_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ is a family of dense open sets in $(
X, d)$ $\iff$ it is a family of dense open sets in $(X, d')$.

and

> $\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty U_n$ is dense in $(X, d)$ $\iff$ it is dense in $(X, d')
$.

Since the Baire Category Theorem holds for $(X, d')$ (by completeness), it follo
ws immediately that it holds for $(X, d)$ as well. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The essential point is that completeness is a *metric* property, but
 the conclusion of the Baire theorem — "countable intersections of dense open se
ts are dense" — is a *topological* property. Topological completeness bridges th
e two: it guarantees the existence of *some* complete metric inducing the topolo
gy, and the topological conclusion then transfers to the original space.

## Part (b)

**Claim.** The rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$, equipped with the usual metric $d(
p, q) = |p - q|$ (the subspace metric inherited from $\mathbb{R}$), form a metri
c space that is **not** topologically complete.

**Proof.** We show that $\mathbb{Q}$ is not a Baire space. By part (a), every to
pologically complete metric space is a Baire space. Therefore, if $\mathbb{Q}$ i
s not a Baire space, it cannot be topologically complete.

**Step 1: $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable.** This is a classical fact: $\mathbb{Q}$ is
 countably infinite, so we can enumerate it as $\mathbb{Q} = \{q_1, q_2, q_3, \l
dots\}$.

**Step 2: Each singleton $\{q_n\}$ is closed in $\mathbb{Q}$.** In any metric sp
ace (in particular, in any $T_1$ space), singletons are closed. So each $\{q_n\}
$ is closed in $\mathbb{Q}$.

**Step 3: Each singleton $\{q_n\}$ has empty interior in $\mathbb{Q}$.** The int
erior of $\{q_n\}$ is empty if and only if $\{q_n\}$ is not an open set, i.e., $
q_n$ is not an isolated point of $\mathbb{Q}$. Indeed, $q_n$ is not isolated: fo
r any $\varepsilon > 0$, the open ball $B(q_n, \varepsilon) \cap \mathbb{Q}$ con
tains infinitely many rationals (since $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$), s
o it cannot equal $\{q_n\}$. Thus $\operatorname{int}(\{q_n\}) = \emptyset$.

**Step 4: $\mathbb{Q}$ is meager in itself.** We have:
$$\mathbb{Q} = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} \{q_n\},$$
a countable union of closed sets, each with empty interior. By definition, this
means $\mathbb{Q}$ is meager (of first category) in itself.

**Step 5: A Baire space cannot be meager in itself.** In a Baire space, every no
nempty open set is nonmeager (this is an equivalent formulation of the Baire pro
perty). Since $\mathbb{Q}$ itself is a nonempty open subset of $\mathbb{Q}$, if
$\mathbb{Q}$ were a Baire space, then $\mathbb{Q}$ would be nonmeager in itself.
 But Step 4 shows $\mathbb{Q}$ *is* meager in itself. Contradiction.

**Conclusion.** $\mathbb{Q}$ is not a Baire space. By part (a), every topologica
lly complete metric space is a Baire space. Therefore $\mathbb{Q}$ (with the usu
al metric) is **not topologically complete**. $\blacksquare$

**Intuition.** $\mathbb{Q}$ "wants" to be completed to $\mathbb{R}$, but the com
pletion process necessarily adds the irrational points. There is no way to remet
rize $\mathbb{Q}$ to make it complete while keeping the same topology, because t
he topology of $\mathbb{Q}$ (as a subspace of $\mathbb{R}$) is "riddled with hol
es" — every Cauchy sequence converging to an irrational has no limit in $\mathbb
{Q}$, and no remetrization preserving the topology can repair this. The Baire ca
tegory argument above makes this precise: the countable, hole-free-ness structur
e of $\mathbb{Q}$ is incompatible with being a Baire space.
