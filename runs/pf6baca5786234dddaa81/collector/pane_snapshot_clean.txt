**Answer: No, the Hausdorff dimension of \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\) does not d
epend on the choice of \(p \in (1, \infty]\).**

## Proof

**Step 1: All \(p\)-norms on \(\mathbb{R}^n\) are equivalent.**

On the finite-dimensional vector space \(\mathbb{R}^n\), any two norms are equiv
alent. Concretely, for \(1 < p, q \le \infty\), there exist constants \(c, C > 0
\) (depending on \(p, q, n\)) such that for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}^n\),
$$
c\,\|x\|_p \;\le\; \|x\|_q \;\le\; C\,\|x\|_p.
$$
This is a standard consequence of the compactness of the unit sphere in any fixe
d norm on a finite-dimensional space (all norms induce the same topology, and th
e unit sphere is compact, so the ratio of any two norms attains positive finite
bounds).

**Step 2: Equivalent norms induce bi-Lipschitz-equivalent metrics.**

Let \(d_p(x,y) = \|x - y\|_p\) and \(d_q(x,y) = \|x-y\|_q\). From Step 1,
$$
c\, d_p(x,y) \;\le\; d_q(x,y) \;\le\; C\, d_p(x,y) \quad \text{for all } x, y \i
n \mathbb{R}^n.
$$
Hence the identity map \(\mathrm{id} : (\mathbb{R}^n, d_p) \to (\mathbb{R}^n, d_
q)\) is bi-Lipschitz (it is \(C\)-Lipschitz and its inverse is \(c^{-1}\)-Lipsch
itz).

**Step 3: Hausdorff dimension is invariant under bi-Lipschitz maps.**

Recall the definition. For a metric space \((X,d)\) and \(s \ge 0\),
$$
\mathcal{H}^s_\delta(X) = \inf\left\{ \sum_i (\mathrm{diam}\, U_i)^s : X \subset
eq \bigcup_i U_i,\ \mathrm{diam}\, U_i \le \delta \right\},
\qquad
\mathcal{H}^s(X) = \lim_{\delta \to 0} \mathcal{H}^s_\delta(X),
$$
and \(\dim_H X = \inf\{s : \mathcal{H}^s(X) = 0\} = \sup\{s : \mathcal{H}^s(X) =
 \infty\}\).

If \(f : (X, d_X) \to (Y, d_Y)\) is \(L\)-Lipschitz, then for any cover \(\{U_i\
}\) of \(A \subseteq X\), the sets \(f(U_i)\) cover \(f(A)\) and \(\mathrm{diam}
_{d_Y} f(U_i) \le L\, \mathrm{diam}_{d_X} U_i\). Therefore
$$
\mathcal{H}^s_{L\delta}(f(A)) \;\le\; L^s\, \mathcal{H}^s_\delta(A),
$$
and taking \(\delta \to 0\),
$$
\mathcal{H}^s(f(A)) \;\le\; L^s\, \mathcal{H}^s(A).
$$
Applying this to the identity map and its inverse (both Lipschitz by Step 2), we
 get for every \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\):
$$
\mathcal{H}^s_q(A) \;\le\; C^s\, \mathcal{H}^s_p(A),
\qquad
\mathcal{H}^s_p(A) \;\le\; c^{-s}\, \mathcal{H}^s_q(A),
$$
where \(\mathcal{H}^s_p\) denotes Hausdorff measure computed with \(d_p\).

**Step 4: Conclusion.**

From the two inequalities in Step 3, for each \(s \ge 0\):
$$
\mathcal{H}^s_p(A) = 0 \iff \mathcal{H}^s_q(A) = 0,
\qquad
\mathcal{H}^s_p(A) = \infty \iff \mathcal{H}^s_q(A) = \infty.
$$
Therefore the critical exponent — the Hausdorff dimension — is the same:
$$
\dim_H^{(p)}(A) \;=\; \inf\{s : \mathcal{H}^s_p(A) = 0\} \;=\; \inf\{s : \mathca
l{H}^s_q(A) = 0\} \;=\; \dim_H^{(q)}(A).
$$

Since \(p, q \in (1, \infty]\) were arbitrary, the Hausdorff dimension of \(A\)
is **independent of the choice of \(p\)**. \(\blacksquare\)
