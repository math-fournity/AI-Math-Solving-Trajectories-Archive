## Counterexample: $X = S^1 \subset \mathbb{R}^2$

rcle.

### Verification of the hypotheses

**($X$ is compact.)** $S^1$ is closed (it is the preimage of $\{1\}$ under the c
ontinuous function $f(x_1,x_2) = x_1^2 + x_2^2$) and bounded (it lies in the bal
l of radius 1). By the Heine–Borel theorem, $S^1$ is compact.

**($X$ is connected.)** The map $\gamma: [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^2$ defined by $\ga
mma(t) = (\cos 2\pi t, \sin 2\pi t)$ is continuous, and $\gamma([0,1]) = S^1$. S
ince $[0,1]$ is connected and the continuous image of a connected set is connect
ed, $S^1$ is connected.

**($X \setminus \{y\}$ is connected for every $y \in X$.)** Let $y = (\cos\theta
_0, \sin\theta_0) \in S^1$. The map
$$\varphi: (\theta_0, \theta_0 + 2\pi) \to S^1 \setminus \{y\}, \quad t \mapsto
(\cos t, \sin t)$$
is a homeomorphism (it is continuous, bijective, and its inverse — the argument
function restricted to the punctured circle — is continuous). Since the open int
erval $(\theta_0, \theta_0+2\pi)$ is connected (indeed homeomorphic to $\mathbb{
R}$), $S^1 \setminus \{y\}$ is connected. In particular, the hypothesis "$X \set
minus \{y\}$ is connected" is satisfied for **every** $y \in X$.

### Verification that the conclusion fails

**($S^1$ is not homeomorphic to a ball.)** A ball in $\mathbb{R}^n$ means a set
homeomorphic to the closed unit ball $\overline{B}^n = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : \|
x\| \leq 1\}$ (since $X$ is compact, it could only be homeomorphic to a closed b
all, not an open one).

We show $S^1$ is not homeomorphic to $\overline{B}^n$ for any $n \geq 0$:

- **$n = 0$:** $\overline{B}^0$ is a single point, but $S^1$ is uncountable. Not
 homeomorphic.

- **$n = 1$:** $\overline{B}^1 = [-1,1]$. The interval $[-1,1]$ has cut points (
removing any interior point disconnects it), while $S^1$ has **no** cut points (
removing any point from $S^1$ leaves it connected, as shown above). Since the pr
operty "removing a point disconnects the space" is a topological invariant, $S^1
 \not\cong [-1,1]$.

- **$n \geq 2$:** $\overline{B}^n$ is contractible (the homotopy $H(x,t) = (1-t)
x$ contracts it to the origin), so $H_1(\overline{B}^n) = 0$. But $S^1$ is not c
ontractible; its fundamental group is $\pi_1(S^1) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, and corresp
ondingly $H_1(S^1) \cong \mathbb{Z} \neq 0$. Since homology groups are topologic
al invariants, $S^1 \not\cong \overline{B}^n$ for any $n \geq 2$.

Alternatively, for $n \geq 2$: $S^1$ is a 1-dimensional manifold (every point ha
s a neighborhood homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}$), while $\overline{B}^n$ is $n$-di
mensional. Topological dimension is a homeomorphism invariant, so they cannot be
 homeomorphic when $n \neq 1$, and the $n=1$ case is handled above.

### Conclusion

All hypotheses of the statement are satisfied: $X = S^1$ is compact, connected,
lies in $\mathbb{R}^2$, and $X \setminus \{y\}$ is connected (for every $y$, hen
ce certainly for some $y$). Yet $X = S^1$ is not homeomorphic to a ball of any d
imension. **The statement is false.**
