**Problem.** Find the largest integer $m$ such that $n^{12}\equiv 1\pmod m$ for
every integer $n$ with $\gcd(n,m)=1$.

**Key tool: the Carmichael function.** For a positive integer $m$, let $\lambda(
m)$ denote the Carmichael function — the exponent of the multiplicative group $(
\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z)^\times$. By definition, $\lambda(m)$ is the *smallest* pos
itive integer $L$ such that $n^{L}\equiv 1\pmod m$ for all $n$ with $\gcd(n,m)=1
$. Consequently

$$n^{12}\equiv 1\pmod m\ \text{for all } \gcd(n,m)=1 \iff \lambda(m)\mid 12.$$

So the problem reduces to: **find the largest $m$ with $\lambda(m)\mid 12$.**

**Values of $\lambda$ on prime powers.**

- $\lambda(1)=1$.
- $\lambda(2)=1,\ \lambda(4)=2,\ \lambda(2^{k})=2^{k-2}$ for $k\ge 3$.
- $\lambda(p^{k})=p^{k-1}(p-1)$ for an odd prime $p$.
- $\lambda\!\left(\prod p_i^{e_i}\right)=\operatorname{lcm}\bigl(\lambda(p_1^{e_
1}),\dots,\lambda(p_r^{e_r})\bigr)$.

**Determining the admissible prime powers.** We need every prime-power factor $p
^{e}\|m$ to satisfy $\lambda(p^{e})\mid 12=2^{2}\cdot 3$.

*Prime $p=2$.* We need $\lambda(2^{e})\mid 12$:
- $e=1$: $\lambda(2)=1\mid 12$ ✓
- $e=2$: $\lambda(4)=2\mid 12$ ✓
- $e=3$: $\lambda(8)=2\mid 12$ ✓
- $e=4$: $\lambda(16)=4\mid 12$ ✓
- $e=5$: $\lambda(32)=8\nmid 12$ ✗

So the largest admissible power of $2$ is $\mathbf{2^{4}=16}$.

*Odd primes $p$.* We need $p-1\mid 12$ (for $e=1$) and, for higher $e$, $p^{e-1}
(p-1)\mid 12$. Since $p-1\mid 12$, we have $p-1\in\{1,2,3,4,6,12\}$, giving cand
idate primes $p\in\{2,3,4,5,7,13\}$, i.e. odd primes $\{3,5,7,13\}$.

- $p=3$: $\lambda(3)=2\mid 12$ ✓; $\lambda(9)=6\mid 12$ ✓; $\lambda(27)=18\nmid
12$ ✗. Max power $\mathbf{3^{2}=9}$.
- $p=5$: $\lambda(5)=4\mid 12$ ✓; $\lambda(25)=20\nmid 12$ ✗. Max power $\mathbf
{5}$.
- $p=7$: $\lambda(7)=6\mid 12$ ✓; $\lambda(49)=42\nmid 12$ ✗. Max power $\mathbf
{7}$.
- $p=13$: $\lambda(13)=12\mid 12$ ✓; $\lambda(169)=156\nmid 12$ ✗. Max power $\m
athbf{13}$.

No other odd prime works, since $p-1\mid 12$ is necessary and the divisors of $1
2$ are exhausted.

**Assembling $m$.** Because $\lambda$ is multiplicative on coprime factors via l
cm, taking the *largest* admissible power of each admissible prime gives the lar
gest $m$:

$$m = 2^{4}\cdot 3^{2}\cdot 5\cdot 7\cdot 13.$$

Computing:
$$16\cdot 9 = 144,\quad 144\cdot 5 = 720,\quad 720\cdot 7 = 5040,\quad 5040\cdot
 13 = 65520.$$

**Verification.**
$$\lambda(65520)=\operatorname{lcm}\bigl(\lambda(16),\lambda(9),\lambda(5),\lamb
da(7),\lambda(13)\bigr)=\operatorname{lcm}(4,6,4,6,12)=12\mid 12.\ \checkmark$$

Hence $n^{12}\equiv 1\pmod{65520}$ for every $n$ coprime to $65520$.

**Maximality.** Any $m'>65520$ with $\lambda(m')\mid 12$ would have to involve e
ither a higher power of one of the primes $2,3,5,7,13$ (all of which fail the di
visibility $\lambda\mid 12$, as checked above) or an additional prime $p$ with $
p-1\mid 12$ — but no such prime exists beyond $\{2,3,5,7,13\}$. Therefore no lar
ger $m$ is possible.

$$\boxed{m = 65520}.$$
