## Answer

**Yes.** A function of bounded variation on $[a,b]$ that has the intermediate va
lue property must be continuous on $[a,b]$.

## Proof

**Preliminary fact.** If $f$ is of bounded variation on $[a,b]$, then $f$ admits
 one‑sided limits at every point: $f(c-)=\lim_{x\to c^-}f(x)$ and $f(c+)=\lim_{x
\to c^+}f(x)$ exist for every $c\in(a,b)$ (and the appropriate one‑sided limit e
xists at the endpoints). This is standard: a BV function is the difference of tw
o increasing functions, and increasing functions have one‑sided limits everywher
e.

We argue by contradiction. Suppose $f$ is discontinuous at some $c\in(a,b)$. Set
 $L=f(c-)$, $R=f(c+)$, $v=f(c)$.

---

**Step 1. We must have $L=R$.**

Suppose $L\neq R$; without loss of generality $L<R$ (the case $L>R$ is handled i
dentically, or by applying the argument to $-f$, which is also BV and has the IV
P).

Pick any $t\in(L,R)$ with $t\neq L,R$, and set
$$\varepsilon=\tfrac12\min\{t-L,\;R-t\}>0.$$
Since $f(c-)=L$ and $f(c+)=R$, there exists $\delta>0$ such that
$$|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon\;\text{ for }x\in(c-\delta,c),\qquad |f(x)-R|<\varepsilon
\;\text{ for }x\in(c,c+\delta).$$
In particular $f(x)\in(L-\varepsilon,L+\varepsilon)$ on $(c-\delta,c)$ and $f(x)
\in(R-\varepsilon,R+\varepsilon)$ on $(c,c+\delta)$; these two intervals are dis
joint and **neither contains $t$** (by the choice of $\varepsilon$).

Now take any $x\in(c-\delta,c)$ and $y\in(c,c+\delta)$. We have $f(x)<L+\varepsi
lon<t<R-\varepsilon<f(y)$, so by the intermediate value property there exists $z
\in(x,y)$ with $f(z)=t$. Where can $z$ be?

- $z\in(x,c)$: then $f(z)\in(L-\varepsilon,L+\varepsilon)\not\ni t$ — impossible
.
- $z\in(c,y)$: then $f(z)\in(R-\varepsilon,R+\varepsilon)\not\ni t$ — impossible
.

Hence $z=c$, i.e. $f(c)=t$. But $t$ was an **arbitrary** point of $(L,R)$, which
 contains infinitely many values; $f(c)$ is a single number. Contradiction. Ther
efore $L=R$.

---

**Step 2. We must have $f(c)=L(=R)$.**

Let $M:=L=R$. Suppose $v=f(c)\neq M$; without loss $v>M$ (the case $v<M$ is anal
ogous). Choose $t\in(M,v)$ and set
$$\varepsilon=\tfrac12\min\{t-M,\;v-t\}>0.$$
Since $f(c-)=f(c+)=M$, there is $\delta>0$ with
$$|f(x)-M|<\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }x\in(c-\delta,c)\cup(c,c+\delta),$$
so $f(x)\in(M-\varepsilon,M+\varepsilon)\not\ni t$ on the punctured neighborhood
.

Take $x\in(c-\delta,c)$. Then $f(x)<M+\varepsilon<t<v=f(c)$. By the intermediate
 value property applied to $[x,c]$, there exists $z\in(x,c)$ with $f(z)=t$. But
$f(z)\in(M-\varepsilon,M+\varepsilon)\not\ni t$ — contradiction.

(The case $v<M$ is obtained by applying the same argument to $[c,y]$ with $y\in(
c,c+\delta)$, using $f(c)=v<t<M-\varepsilon<f(y)$.)

Hence $f(c)=M$, and $f$ is continuous at $c$.

---

**Endpoints.** The same reasoning applies at $a$ (using only the right limit $f(
a+)$) and at $b$ (using only the left limit $f(b-)$): a mismatch between $f(a)$
and $f(a+)$ (resp. $f(b)$ and $f(b-)$) is ruled out by the identical IVP argumen
t on one‑sided intervals.

Since $c$ was an arbitrary point of $[a,b]$, $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$. $\bla
cksquare$
