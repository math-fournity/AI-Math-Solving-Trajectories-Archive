## Proof

(rotate by $\theta$ about the origin). Since $R(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}$, any lea
ding rotations do nothing, so we may assume every nonempty sequence of moves sta

**Step 1: Characterizing the reachable set.** Consider a sequence of operations
$w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n$ applied in order (left to right). Each $T$ at position $i
$ contributes the vector $(1,0)$, which then gets rotated by every $R$ appearing
 after it. If $m_i$ denotes the number of $R$'s among $w_{i+1},\dots,w_n$, the c
ontribution of that $T$ is $R^{m_i}(1,0) = (\cos m_i\theta,\;\sin m_i\theta)$.

The final position is $\sum_{i: w_i=T} R^{m_i}(1,0)$. The multiset $\{m_i\}$ can
 be any finite multiset of non-negative integers (arrange in non-increasing orde
r to build a valid word). Setting $v_k := R^k(1,0) = (\cos k\theta, \sin k\theta
)$, the reachable set is:
$$\mathcal{R} = \left\{\sum_{k\ge 0} a_k\, v_k : a_k \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0},\; \
text{finitely many nonzero}\right\}.$$

**Step 2: Reduction to the cyclotomic integer ring.** Since $\theta$ is rational
 (in degrees), $\theta/360 \in \mathbb{Q}$. Write $\theta/360 = p/q$ in lowest t
erms with $q > 0$. Then $R$ has order $q$: the sequence $\{v_k\}_{k\ge 0}$ is pe
riodic with period $q$, cycling through the $q$-th roots of unity $\{1, \zeta, \
zeta^2, \dots, \zeta^{q-1}\}$ where $\zeta = e^{2\pi i p/q}$.

Thus $\mathcal{R} = \left\{\sum_{k=0}^{q-1} c_k\, \zeta^k : c_k \in \mathbb{Z}_{
\ge 0}\right\}$ (viewing $\mathbb{R}^2 \cong \mathbb{C}$).

**Key observation (semigroup = group for $q \ge 2$):** For $q \ge 2$, the sum of
 all $q$-th roots of unity is zero: $\sum_{k=0}^{q-1} \zeta^k = 0$, so $\zeta^{q
-1} = -\sum_{k=0}^{q-2} \zeta^k$. For any $(b_0,\dots,b_{q-2}) \in \mathbb{Z}^{q
-1}$, set $c_{q-1} = \max(0, -\min_k b_k)$ and $c_k = b_k + c_{q-1} \ge 0$. This
 gives:
$$\mathcal{R} = \left\{\sum_{k=0}^{q-2} b_k\, \zeta^k : b_k \in \mathbb{Z}\right
\} = \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_q],$$
the cyclotomic integer ring. (For $q=1$: $\mathcal{R} = \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} \subs
et \mathbb{R}$, not dense in $\mathbb{R}^2$.)

**Step 3: Density criterion for $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_q]$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.** The r
ing $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_q]$ is a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module of rank $\varphi(q)$ (Eu
ler's totient), with $\mathbb{Q}$-basis $\{1, \zeta, \dots, \zeta^{\varphi(q)-1}
\}$. The embedding $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_q] \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb
{R}^2$ sends this to a subgroup of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

We use the standard fact: *a subgroup $\Lambda$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is either a di
screte lattice (rank $= \dim_{\mathbb{R}} \operatorname{span}(\Lambda)$) or dens
e in its real span (rank $> \dim_{\mathbb{R}} \operatorname{span}(\Lambda)$).*

- **Real span:** For $q \ge 3$, the roots of unity include non-real numbers, so
$\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_q]) = \mathbb{R}^2$ (dimensio
n 2). For $q \in \{1,2\}$, all roots are real, so the span is $\mathbb{R}$ (dime
nsion 1).

- **Rank:** Since $\{1, \zeta, \dots, \zeta^{\varphi(q)-1}\}$ are $\mathbb{Q}$-l
inearly independent, the map $\mathbb{Z}^{\varphi(q)} \to \mathbb{R}^2$ has triv
ial kernel over $\mathbb{Q}$, hence trivial kernel over $\mathbb{Z}$. The image
has rank $\varphi(q)$.

**Conclusion:** $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_q]$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}^2$ if and only if:
1. $q \ge 3$ (so the real span is $\mathbb{R}^2$), **and**
2. $\varphi(q) > 2$ (so rank exceeds dimension, forcing density rather than disc

The values of $q$ with $\varphi(q) \le 2$ are exactly $q \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6\}$:
- $\varphi(1)=1,\; \varphi(2)=1,\; \varphi(3)=2,\; \varphi(4)=2,\; \varphi(6)=2$
.
- For all other $q \ge 1$, $\varphi(q) \ge 4$.

(Verification: $\varphi(5)=4$, $\varphi(7)=6$, $\varphi(8)=4$, $\varphi(9)=6$, $

## Answer

The set of all rational numbers $\theta$ satisfying the given condition is:

$$\boxed{\left\{\theta \in \mathbb{Q} : \frac{\theta}{360} = \frac{p}{q} \text{
in lowest terms with } q \notin \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6\}\right\}.}$$

Equivalently, a rational $\theta$ works if and only if the rotation by $\theta$
degrees has finite order $q$ (where $\theta/360 = p/q$ in lowest terms) that is
**not** in $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 6\}$ — that is, $q \in \{5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, \
dots\} \setminus \{6\}$, or simply $q \ge 5$ and $q \neq 6$.
