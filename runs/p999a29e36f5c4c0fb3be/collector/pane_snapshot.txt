## Proof

**Claim:** Yes. There exists a bounded real-valued sequence $\{w_k\} \in l^\inft
y$ whose Cesàro means diverge.

**Construction.** We build $\{w_k\}$ from consecutive blocks of constant values
$\pm 1$. Let the blocks have lengths $L_1, L_2, L_3, \dots$, where block $j$ con
sists of $L_j$ copies of $(-1)^{j+1}$ (so odd blocks are $+1$, even blocks are $
-1$). Define the lengths recursively:

$$L_1 = 1, \qquad L_{j+1} = 2\,N_j, \quad \text{where } N_j := \sum_{i=1}^{j} L_
i.$$

Then $N_{j+1} = N_j + L_{j+1} = 3\,N_j$, so by induction $N_j = 3^{j-1}$ and $L_
j = 2\cdot 3^{j-2}$ for $j \geq 2$.

The sequence $\{w_k\}$ takes only the values $\pm 1$, so $\|w\|_\infty = 1 < \in
fty$, i.e. $\{w_k\} \in l^\infty$.

**The Cesàro means oscillate.** Let $S_n := \sum_{k=1}^{N_n} w_k = \sum_{j=1}^{n
} (-1)^{j+1} L_j$ be the partial sum at the end of block $n$, and $\sigma_n := S
_n / N_n$ the corresponding Cesàro mean. We show $|\sigma_n| \geq 1/3$ with alte
rnating sign, so $\{\sigma_n\}$ cannot converge.

Since $L_n = 2\cdot 3^{n-2} = 2\,N_{n-1} > N_{n-1}$ for $n \geq 2$, the last (la
rgest) block dominates:

$$|S_n| = \left|(-1)^{n+1} L_n + S_{n-1}\right| \geq L_n - |S_{n-1}| \geq L_n -
N_{n-1} = 2\cdot 3^{n-2} - 3^{n-2} = 3^{n-2}.$$

Moreover the sign of $S_n$ equals $(-1)^{n+1}$ (the last block's contribution $L
_n$ exceeds the entire preceding sum $N_{n-1}$). Dividing by $N_n = 3^{n-1}$:

$$\sigma_n = \frac{S_n}{N_n} \geq \frac{3^{n-2}}{3^{n-1}} = \frac{1}{3} \quad (n
 \text{ odd}), \qquad \sigma_n \leq -\frac{1}{3} \quad (n \text{ even}).$$

(For $n=1$: $\sigma_1 = 1 \geq 1/3$, consistent.) Thus the subsequence of Cesàro
 means at block endpoints has one sub-subsequence bounded below by $1/3$ and ano
ther bounded above by $-1/3$.

**Conclusion.** The full Cesàro mean $C_M := \frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M} w_k$ has,
 along the subsequence $M = N_n$, values oscillating between $\geq 1/3$ (odd $n$
) and $\leq -1/3$ (even $n$). A sequence with two subsequential limit points sep
arated by at least $2/3$ cannot converge. Therefore

$$\lim_{M\to\infty} \frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M} w_k \quad \text{does not exist},$$

while $\{w_k\} \in l^\infty$. $\blacksquare$
