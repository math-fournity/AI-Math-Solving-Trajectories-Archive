## Convergence

Near $x = 0$: $\dfrac{\ln x}{x^2+x+1} \sim \ln x$, and $\int_0^1 |\ln x|\,dx = 1
 < \infty$.

Near $x = \infty$: $\dfrac{\ln x}{x^2+x+1} \sim \dfrac{\ln x}{x^2}$, and $\int_1
^\infty \dfrac{\ln x}{x^2}\,dx = 1 < \infty$.

Hence the integral converges (absolutely), so substitutions are justified.

## Evaluation

Let
$$I = \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{\ln x}{x^2+x+1}\,dx.$$

Apply the change of variable $x = \dfrac{1}{t}$, so $dx = -\dfrac{dt}{t^2}$. The
 limits $x:0\to\infty$ become $t:\infty\to 0$. Using $\ln(1/t) = -\ln t$ and
$$\frac{1}{(1/t)^2+1/t+1} = \frac{1}{(1+t+t^2)/t^2} = \frac{t^2}{t^2+t+1},$$
we get
$$
I = \int_{\infty}^{0} \frac{-\ln t}{(t^2+t+1)/t^2}\cdot\left(-\frac{dt}{t^2}\rig
ht)
  = \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{-\ln t}{t^2+t+1}\,dt
  = -I.
$$

(The two minus signs — one from $\ln(1/t)=-\ln t$ and one from reversing the lim
its via $dx=-dt/t^2$ — combine so that the integral over $[0,\infty)$ picks up a
 single overall minus sign.)

Therefore $I = -I$, which gives $2I = 0$, i.e.
$$I = 0.$$

The key structural reason this works is that the denominator $x^2+x+1$ is **self
-reciprocal**: $x^2\bigl((1/x)^2+1/x+1\bigr)=x^2+x+1$. Combined with the antisym
metry $\ln(1/x)=-\ln x$ of the numerator, the substitution $x\mapsto 1/x$ sends
the integrand to its own negative, forcing the integral over the invariant inter
val $(0,\infty)$ to vanish.
