Yes, it is possible to totally order the set of complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$. Be
low is a complete proof, followed by an important clarification about what this
does **not** mean (it is not an order compatible with the field structure).

---

## Proof

**Goal.** Exhibit a total order on the set $\mathbb{C}$, i.e. a relation $\prece
q$ that is reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive, and total.

**Key fact.** The sets $\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbb{R}$ have the same cardinality.
Concretely, there is a bijection $f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}$.

*Construction of a bijection.* Identify $\mathbb{C}$ with $\mathbb{R}^2$ via $z=
x+iy\leftrightarrow(x,y)$. It is standard that $|\mathbb{R}^2|=|\mathbb{R}|$; on
e explicit injection $\mathbb{R}^2\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}$ is obtained by inte
rleaving decimal expansions. Writing each real number in a sign-magnitude decima
l form (choosing the non-terminating representation $0.4999\ldots$ vs. $0.5000\l
dots$ to guarantee uniqueness), for $(x,y)\in[0,1)^2$ with
$$x=0.x_1x_2x_3\ldots,\qquad y=0.y_1y_2y_3\ldots,$$
map $(x,y)\mapsto 0.x_1y_1x_2y_2x_3y_3\ldots\in[0,1)$. This is injective. Combin
ed with bijections $\mathbb{R}\leftrightarrow(0,1)$ (e.g. $t\mapsto \tfrac{1}{\p
i}\arctan t+\tfrac12$) and handling signs, one obtains a bijection $g:\mathbb{R}
^2\to\mathbb{R}$. Composing with the identification $\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^2
$ gives a bijection $f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}$.

(Alternatively, by the Cantor–Bernstein theorem, the injections $\mathbb{R}\hook
rightarrow\mathbb{C}$, $t\mapsto t+0i$, and $\mathbb{C}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}
$ above already imply $|\mathbb{C}|=|\mathbb{R}|$, hence a bijection exists.)

**Defining the order.** Let $f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}$ be any bijection. Define
 a relation $\preceq$ on $\mathbb{C}$ by
$$z\preceq w \iff f(z)\le f(w),$$
where $\le$ is the usual order on $\mathbb{R}$.

**Verification of total-order axioms.**

1. *Reflexivity.* For every $z\in\mathbb{C}$, $f(z)\le f(z)$ in $\mathbb{R}$, so
 $z\preceq z$.

2. *Antisymmetry.* Suppose $z\preceq w$ and $w\preceq z$. Then $f(z)\le f(w)$ an
d $f(w)\le f(z)$, so $f(z)=f(w)$. Since $f$ is injective, $z=w$.

3. *Transitivity.* Suppose $z\preceq w$ and $w\preceq u$. Then $f(z)\le f(w)\le
f(u)$, hence $f(z)\le f(u)$, so $z\preceq u$.

4. *Totality.* For any $z,w\in\mathbb{C}$, the real numbers $f(z),f(w)$ are comp
arable under $\le$: either $f(z)\le f(w)$ or $f(w)\le f(z)$. Hence either $z\pre
ceq w$ or $w\preceq z$.

All four axioms hold, so $\preceq$ is a total order on $\mathbb{C}$. $\blacksqua
re$

## Clarification: this is *not* an ordered-field order

The question asks only whether the **set** $\mathbb{C}$ can be totally ordered,
and the answer is yes, as shown above. It is worth noting the distinct (and fals
e) statement that $\mathbb{C}$ can be made into an **ordered field** — i.e. equi
pped with a total order $\le$ satisfying, for all $x,y,z\in\mathbb{C}$:
- $x\le y \implies x+z\le y+z$,
- $x\ge 0,\ y\ge 0 \implies xy\ge 0$.

This is impossible. Indeed, in any ordered field, every square is non-negative,
so $1=1^2\ge 0$ and in fact $1>0$ (since $1\ne 0$). Then $i^2=-1\ge 0$, giving $
-1\ge 0$, which combined with $1\ge 0$ and antisymmetry would force $1=-1$, i.e.
 $2=0$, contradicting the field axiom $1\ne 0$. Hence $\mathbb{C}$ admits **no**
 total order compatible with its field structure.

The set-theoretic question, however, has an affirmative answer: **yes, $\mathbb{
C}$ can be totally ordered.**
