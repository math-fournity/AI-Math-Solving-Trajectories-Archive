## Answer

**Yes.** In a finite-dimensional complex Hilbert space, every (linear) isometric
 map is a symmetry transformation.

### Proof

Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a finite-dimensional complex Hilbert space with $\dim \math
cal{H} = n < \infty$, and let $V : \mathcal{H} \to \mathcal{H}$ be an isometric
map, i.e. a linear operator satisfying

$$\|Vx\| = \|x\| \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathcal{H}.$$

We show that $V$ is unitary, hence a symmetry transformation (in the sense of Wi
gner).

**Step 1: $V$ is injective.**
If $Vx = 0$, then $\|x\| = \|Vx\| = 0$, so $x = 0$. Thus $\ker V = \{0\}$, and $
V$ is injective.

**Step 2: $V$ is surjective (this is where finite-dimensionality is essential).*
*
Since $V : \mathcal{H} \to \mathcal{H}$ is an injective linear map from an $n$-d
imensional space to itself, the rank–nullity theorem gives

$$\dim \operatorname{im} V = \dim \mathcal{H} - \dim \ker V = n - 0 = n.$$

Hence $\operatorname{im} V = \mathcal{H}$, so $V$ is surjective. Therefore $V$ i
s bijective.

**Step 3: $V$ preserves the inner product.**
Using the complex polarization identity,

$$\langle x, y \rangle = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=0}^{3} i^{k} \|x + i^{k} y\|^{2},$$

and the fact that $V$ is linear and norm-preserving,

$$\langle Vx, Vy \rangle = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=0}^{3} i^{k} \|Vx + i^{k} Vy\|^{2}
 = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=0}^{3} i^{k} \|V(x + i^{k} y)\|^{2} = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=0
}^{3} i^{k} \|x + i^{k} y\|^{2} = \langle x, y \rangle.$$

So $V$ preserves the inner product. A bijective linear operator preserving the i
nner product is, by definition, **unitary**: $V^{*}V = VV^{*} = I$.

**Step 4: $V$ is a symmetry transformation.**
A symmetry transformation (Wigner symmetry) is a bijection of $\mathcal{H}$ that
 preserves transition probabilities $|\langle x, y \rangle|^{2}$. Since $V$ is u
nitary,

$$|\langle Vx, Vy \rangle|^{2} = |\langle x, y \rangle|^{2} \quad \text{for all
} x, y \in \mathcal{H},$$

so $V$ preserves transition probabilities and is bijective. Hence $V$ is a symme
try transformation. $\blacksquare$

### Remark on the finite-dimensional hypothesis

The finite-dimensionality is crucial. In an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space,
an isometry need not be surjective: the **unilateral shift** $S : \ell^{2}(\math
bb{N}) \to \ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})$, $S(x_{1}, x_{2}, \dots) = (0, x_{1}, x_{2}, \d
ots)$, is isometric but not surjective (its range is the proper subspace of sequ
ences with first coordinate $0$). Being non-bijective, it fails to be a symmetry
 transformation. Thus the statement is **false** in infinite dimensions and **tr
ue** precisely because finite-dimensionality forces injectivity to imply surject
ivity.
