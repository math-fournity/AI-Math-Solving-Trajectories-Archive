Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the Volterra operator $T:L^
1([0,1]) \to C([0,1])$, $T(f)(x) = \int_0^x f(t)\,dt$, maps weakly convergent se
quences in $L^1$ to norm-convergent sequences in $C([0,1])$.

**Answer: Yes, $T$ is weak-strong continuous.**

## Proof

We must show: if $f_n \rightharpoonup f$ weakly in $L^1([0,1])$, then $\|T(f_n)
- T(f)\|_\infty \to 0$.

Without loss of generality, assume $f = 0$ (replace $f_n$ by $f_n - f$).

**Step 1: Uniform integrability via Dunford–Pettis.**

Since $\{f_n\}$ is weakly convergent, the set $K = \{f_n : n \geq 1\}$ is relati
vely weakly compact in $L^1([0,1])$. By the **Dunford–Pettis theorem**, on a fin
ite measure space, relatively weakly compact subsets of $L^1$ are exactly the **
uniformly integrable** sets. Hence $\{f_n\}$ is uniformly integrable: for every
$\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that for every measurable $A \
subset [0,1]$ with $m(A) < \delta$,
$$\sup_n \int_A |f_n(t)|\,dt < \varepsilon.$$

**Step 2: Equicontinuity of $\{T(f_n)\}$.**

For any $x, y \in [0,1]$ with $|x - y| < \delta$,
$$|T(f_n)(x) - T(f_n)(y)| = \left|\int_x^y f_n(t)\,dt\right| \leq \int_{\min(x,y
)}^{\max(x,y)} |f_n(t)|\,dt < \varepsilon,$$
where the last inequality uses uniform integrability (the interval $[\min(x,y),
\max(x,y)]$ has measure $|x-y| < \delta$). This holds for all $n$ simultaneously
, so $\{T(f_n)\}$ is equicontinuous.

**Step 3: Uniform boundedness of $\{T(f_n)\}$.**

By the Uniform Boundedness Principle, weakly convergent sequences are norm-bound
ed: $\|f_n\|_1 \leq M$ for some $M$. Therefore
$$|T(f_n)(x)| \leq \int_0^x |f_n(t)|\,dt \leq \|f_n\|_1 \leq M$$
for all $x$ and $n$, so $\{T(f_n)\}$ is uniformly bounded.

**Step 4: Pointwise convergence to $0$.**

For each fixed $x \in [0,1]$, the indicator $\mathbf{1}_{[0,x]} \in L^\infty([0,
1])$. Since $f_n \rightharpoonup 0$ weakly in $L^1$,
$$T(f_n)(x) = \int_0^1 f_n(t)\,\mathbf{1}_{[0,x]}(t)\,dt \longrightarrow 0.$$

**Step 5: Arzelà–Ascoli gives uniform convergence.**

The family $\{T(f_n)\}$ is equicontinuous (Step 2) and uniformly bounded (Step 3
) in $C([0,1])$. By the **Arzelà–Ascoli theorem**, it is relatively compact in $
C([0,1])$. Combined with pointwise convergence to $0$ (Step 4): every subsequenc
e of $\{T(f_n)\}$ has a further subsequence converging uniformly, and by pointwi
se convergence the limit must be $0$. Therefore the entire sequence converges un
iformly:
$$\|T(f_n)\|_\infty \to 0. \qquad \blacksquare$$

## Remark

Note that $T: L^1 \to C([0,1])$ is **not compact**: the bounded sequence $g_n =
n\,\mathbf{1}_{[0,1/n]}$ satisfies $T(g_n)(x) = \min(nx, 1)$, which converges po
intwise to a discontinuous function, so $\{T(g_n)\}$ has no uniformly convergent
 subsequence. This does **not** contradict weak-strong continuity, because the e
quivalence "weak-strong continuous $\iff$ compact" requires the domain to be ref
lexive (via Eberlein–Šmulian). Since $L^1$ is not reflexive, $T$ can be weak-str
ong continuous without being compact. The crucial ingredient that makes weak-str
ong continuity work here is the **Dunford–Pettis theorem**: weak convergence in
$L^1$ enforces uniform integrability, which is precisely the condition needed fo
r equicontinuity of the indefinite integrals.
