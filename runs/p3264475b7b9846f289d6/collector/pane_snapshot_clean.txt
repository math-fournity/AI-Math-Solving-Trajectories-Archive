## Solution

**Answer: The asymptotic probability that $A(p,n)$ is non-singular as $n \to \in
fty$ (with $p = 1/2$) is $\boxed{1}$.**

---

### Setup

The random matrix $A(p,n)$ is the $n \times n$ matrix whose entries are i.i.d. B
ernoulli random variables taking value $1$ with probability $p = 1/2$ and $0$ wi
th probability $1/2$, i.e. uniformly random over $\{0,1\}^{n \times n}$. We must
 show

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \Pr\bigl[A(p,n)\text{ is non-singular}\bigr] = 1,$$

equivalently $\Pr[A(p,n)\text{ is singular}] \to 0$.

### Key geometric lemma (hyperplane–cube intersection)

**Lemma.** For any affine hyperplane $H \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ that does not cont
ain all of $\{0,1\}^n$,

$$\frac{|H \cap \{0,1\}^n|}{2^n} = O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\right).$$

*Proof sketch.* Write $H = \{x : a\cdot x = b\}$. The number of $\{0,1\}$-points
 on $H$ is a coefficient sum of the generating function $\prod_i(1+z^{a_i})$ (af
ter rescaling). This sum is maximized, over all choices of $a$ and $b$ that do n
ot capture the whole cube, when all $|a_i|$ are equal and $b$ is the middle valu
e — i.e. the hyperplane $\sum x_i = \lfloor n/2\rfloor$. Its cardinality is $\bi
nom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor} \sim 2^n\sqrt{2/(\pi n)}$, giving the $O(1/\sqrt n)$
bound. $\square$

### Komlós's argument

We prove by induction (with a sharpened estimate) that $q_n := \Pr[\text{singula
r}] \to 0$.

Expose the first $n-1$ rows $r_1,\dots,r_{n-1}$.

- **Case 1:** $\operatorname{rank}(r_1,\dots,r_{n-1}) < n-1$. Then the first $n-
1$ rows already form a singular $(n-1)\times n$ configuration; in particular the
 $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ matrix obtained by deleting one column has rank $< n-1$ for
every column deletion, and averaging over a uniformly chosen column shows this p
robability is at most $n\cdot q_{n-1}$ (union bound over the $n$ choices of dele
ted column, each giving an $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ Bernoulli matrix). Actually more d
irectly: if the $n-1$ rows are dependent, then some $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ minor (ob
tained by keeping $n-1$ columns) is singular; by symmetry each such minor is dis
tributed as $A(p,n-1)$, so
$$\Pr[\operatorname{rank}<n-1] \le n\, q_{n-1}.$$

- **Case 2:** $\operatorname{rank}(r_1,\dots,r_{n-1}) = n-1$. Their span is a fi
xed hyperplane $H$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, and the last row $r_n$ (independent, unifo
rm on $\{0,1\}^n$) makes the whole matrix singular iff $r_n \in H$. By the Lemma
,
$$\Pr[r_n \in H] \le \frac{C}{\sqrt n}.$$

Combining,

$$q_n \le n\, q_{n-1} + \frac{C}{\sqrt n}.$$

This recursion alone is too weak (it blows up). The refinement — which is the he
art of Komlós's 1967 theorem — is to observe that the worst-case hyperplane (the
 "sum" hyperplane $\sum x_i = \text{const}$) is essentially the *only* one achie
ving the $O(1/\sqrt n)$ density, and a random $(n-1)$-tuple of rows is overwhelm
ingly unlikely to span that particular hyperplane. A careful second-moment / Lit
tlewood–Offord analysis (Komlós; later sharpened by Kahn–Komlós–Szemerédi, Tao–V
u, and Bourgain–Vu–Wood) shows that in fact

$$q_n = O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\right) \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 0.$$

The sharpest known bound (Tashma, and the Kahn–Komlós–Szemerédi / Tao–Vu line of
 work) is $q_n = (1/2 + o(1))^n$, but the qualitative conclusion $q_n \to 0$ is
already Komlós's classical result.

### Conclusion

Since $\Pr[\text{singular}] = q_n \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$,

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \Pr\bigl[A(1/2,\, n)\text{ is non-singular}\bigr] = 1 - \lim
_{n\to\infty} q_n = 1.$$

$$\boxed{\Pr\bigl[A(1/2,n)\text{ non-singular}\bigr] \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 1}
$$
