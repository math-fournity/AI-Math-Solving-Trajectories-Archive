## Proof

The answer is **Yes**.

### Step 1: Reduction to integer-coefficient polynomials

Let $P$ be an integer-valued polynomial of degree $d > 1$. Every integer-valued
polynomial admits a unique expansion
$$P(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{d} c_k \binom{x}{k}, \qquad c_k \in \mathbb{Z},$$
where $\binom{x}{k} = \frac{x(x-1)\cdots(x-k+1)}{k!}$. Since $k! \mid d!$ for al
l $k \le d$, the polynomial $d!\binom{x}{k}$ has integer coefficients for every
$k \le d$. Setting
$$Q(x) = d!\, P(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{d} c_k \, d!\binom{x}{k},$$
we obtain $Q \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ with $\deg Q = d > 1$, so $Q$ is non-constant.

### Step 2: Schur's Theorem (integer-coefficient case)

**Theorem.** *If $Q \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ is non-constant, then infinitely many pri
mes divide the set $\{Q(n) : n \in \mathbb{Z},\; Q(n) \neq 0\}$.*

**Proof.** Suppose for contradiction that only $p_1, \ldots, p_s$ divide all non
zero values $Q(n)$.

Choose $a \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $Q(a) \neq 0$ and set $A = Q(a) \neq 0$. Write $Q
(x) = \sum_j b_j x^j$ with $b_j \in \mathbb{Z}$. Expanding:
$$Q(a + Ax) = \sum_i (Ax)^i c_i, \qquad c_i = \sum_{j \ge i} b_j \binom{j}{i} a^
{j-i} \in \mathbb{Z},$$
with $c_0 = Q(a) = A$. Define
$$R(x) = \frac{Q(a+Ax)}{A} = 1 + \sum_{i \ge 1} A^{i-1} c_i\, x^i =: 1 + A \cdot
 S(x),$$
where $S(x) = \sum_{i \ge 1} A^{i-1} c_i\, x^i \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$. Key propertie
s:

- **$S(0) = 0$** and **$S$ is non-constant** (since $R$ is non-constant, as $Q$
is).
- **$R(n) \in \mathbb{Z}$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$**: because $Q(a+An) - Q(a)
= \sum_j b_j[(a+An)^j - a^j]$ is divisible by $A$ (each $(a+An)^j - a^j$ is divi
sible by $An$).
- **Every prime factor of $R(n)$ (when $R(n)\neq 0$) lies in $\{p_1,\ldots,p_s\}
$**, since $R(n) = Q(a+An)/A$.
- **$\gcd(R(n), A) = 1$ when $R(n) \neq 0$**, since $R(n) = 1 + A\,S(n) \equiv 1
 \pmod{A}$.

Write $S(x) = x\,T(x)$ with $T \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$. Then $|R(n)| = |1 + A\,n\,T(n
)| \to \infty$ as $|n| \to \infty$.

**Iterative elimination.** Let $\mathcal{P} = \{p_1,\ldots,p_s\} \setminus \{\te
xt{primes dividing } A\}$ be the set of primes that can divide $R(n)$.

If $\mathcal{P} \neq \emptyset$: pick $n_1$ with $|R(n_1)| > 1$. Let $q_1, \ldot
s, q_r$ be the distinct prime factors of $R(n_1)$ (all in $\mathcal{P}$) and set
 $M = q_1 \cdots q_r$. For any $k \ge 1$:
$$R(n_1 M^k) = 1 + A \cdot n_1 M^k \cdot T(n_1 M^k) \equiv 1 \pmod{M^k}.$$
So $\gcd(R(n_1 M^k),\, M) = 1$ whenever $R(n_1 M^k) \neq 0$. For large $k$, $|R(
n_1 M^k)| > 1$, so it has at least one prime factor — which lies in $\mathcal{P}
$ but is coprime to $M$, hence lies in $\mathcal{P} \setminus \{q_1,\ldots,q_r\}
$, a **strictly smaller** set.

Repeating, each iteration removes at least one prime from the allowed set. After
 at most $|\mathcal{P}|$ iterations, the allowed set is empty, yet $|R(n_j)| > 1
$ demands a prime factor. **Contradiction.** $\blacksquare$

### Step 3: Completing the proof

By Schur's theorem, infinitely many primes divide values $Q(n) = d!\, P(n)$. Eve
ry prime dividing $Q(n)$ either divides $d!$ (finitely many) or divides $P(n)$.
Therefore **infinitely many primes divide some value $P(n)$** (with $P(n) \neq 0
$).

For any bound $B$, there exists a prime $p > B$ dividing some $P(n) \neq 0$. The
 largest prime factor of $P(n)$ is then at least $p > B$. Since $B$ is arbitrary
, the values $P(n)$ have **arbitrarily large prime factors**. $\blacksquare$
