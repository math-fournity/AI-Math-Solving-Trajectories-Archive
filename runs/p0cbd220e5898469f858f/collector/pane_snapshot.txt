## Proof

**Step 1: Well-definedness.** Since $t$ is irrational, $nt \notin \mathbb{Z}$ fo
r all $n \geq 1$, so $\sin(n\theta) = \sin(nt\pi) \neq 0$. Every term is defined
.

**Step 2: Divergence for $|x| \geq 1$.** Since $|\sin(n\theta)| \leq 1$:
$$\left|\frac{x^n}{\sin(n\theta)}\right| \geq |x|^n \geq 1 \quad \text{for all }
 n \text{ when } |x| \geq 1.$$
The terms do not tend to zero, so the series diverges by the divergence test.

**Step 3: Absolute convergence for $|x| < 1$.** Let $p_k/q_k$ be the convergents
 of the continued fraction of $t$, with $q_{k+1} = a_{k+1}q_k + q_{k-1}$.

*Key bound from continued fraction theory:* For any $n$ with $q_k \leq n < q_{k+
1}$, the distance $\|nt\|$ to the nearest integer satisfies:
$$\|nt\| \geq \|q_k t\| \geq \frac{1}{q_k + q_{k+1}} \geq \frac{1}{2q_{k+1}}.$$
(This is the best-approximation property of convergents: $q_k$ minimizes $\|nt\|
$ among $1 \leq n < q_{k+1}$.)

Since $|\sin(\pi u)| \geq 2\|u\|$ for all $u \in \mathbb{R}$ (by concavity of $\
sin$ on $[0,\pi/2]$), we get:
$$|\sin(n\theta)| = |\sin(\pi n t)| \geq 2\|nt\| \geq \frac{1}{q_{k+1}}, \quad \
text{hence} \quad \frac{1}{|\sin(n\theta)|} \leq q_{k+1}.$$

*Block decomposition:* Split the sum into blocks $[q_k, q_{k+1})$:
$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{|x|^n}{|\sin(n\theta)|} \leq \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} q_{
k+1} \sum_{n=q_k}^{q_{k+1}-1} |x|^n \leq \frac{1}{1-|x|} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} q_{
k+1}\,|x|^{q_k}.$$

*Growth of $q_k$:* Since $q_{k+1} \geq q_k + q_{k-1}$, the sequence $q_k$ grows
at least exponentially: $q_k \geq c\,\varphi^k$ where $\varphi = \frac{1+\sqrt{5
}}{2}$ and $c > 0$.

**Case $t = \sqrt{3}$ (quadratic irrational):** The continued fraction is $\sqrt
{3} = [1;\overline{1,2}]$, periodic with bounded partial quotients $a_k \in \{1,
2\}$. Thus $q_{k+1} \leq 3q_k$, so $q_{k+1} = O(q_k)$ and $q_k = \Theta(\lambda^
k)$ for some $\lambda > 1$.

Then:
$$q_{k+1}\,|x|^{q_k} = O\!\left(q_k \cdot |x|^{q_k}\right).$$
Since $q_k \geq c\varphi^k$, we have $|x|^{q_k} \leq e^{c\varphi^k \ln|x|} \to 0
$ **doubly exponentially**, while $q_k$ grows only exponentially. Thus $q_{k+1}|
x|^{q_k} \to 0$ super-exponentially, and $\sum_{k} q_{k+1}|x|^{q_k} < \infty$.

**General algebraic irrational $t$:** By Roth's theorem, for every $\varepsilon
> 0$ there exists $C > 0$ with $|t - p/q| \geq C/q^{2+\varepsilon}$ for all $p/q
$. Since convergents satisfy $|t - p_k/q_k| < 1/(q_k q_{k+1})$, this gives $q_{k
+1} = O(q_k^{1+\varepsilon})$. Then $q_{k+1}|x|^{q_k} = O(q_k^{1+\varepsilon}|x|
^{q_k})$, and the doubly exponential decay of $|x|^{q_k}$ dominates the polynomi
al factor. The sum converges.

In both cases, absolute convergence follows for $|x| < 1$.

**Step 4: Boundary check at $x = 0$.** When $x = 0$, all terms vanish and the se
ries trivially converges. This is consistent with $|x| < 1$.

---

## Conclusion

The series $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{\sin(n\theta)}$ converge
s if and only if $|x| < 1$, i.e., $\boxed{-1 < x < 1}$.

- For $|x| \geq 1$: terms fail to tend to zero (since $|\sin(n\theta)| \leq 1$),
 so the series diverges.
- For $|x| < 1$: the exponential decay of $|x|^n$ dominates the growth of $1/|\s
in(n\theta)|$ (which is at most polynomial in $n$ for algebraic irrationals like
 $\sqrt{3}$, by Roth's theorem / bounded partial quotients), giving absolute con
vergence.
