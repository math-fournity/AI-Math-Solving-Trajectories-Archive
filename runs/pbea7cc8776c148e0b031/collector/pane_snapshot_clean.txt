## Proof

**Setup.** The (integer) Heisenberg group $H$ is the group of $3\times 3$ upper-
triangular matrices with $1$'s on the diagonal and integer entries:

$$H = \left\{ M(a,b,c) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & a & c \\ 0 & 1 & b \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \en
d{pmatrix} : a,b,c \in \mathbb{Z} \right\}.$$

Multiplication follows the rule $M(a,b,c)\,M(d,e,f) = M(a+d,\; b+e,\; c+f+ae)$,
and the inverse is $M(a,b,c)^{-1} = M(-a,-b,-c+ab)$.

---

**Step 1: The center $Z(H)$.**

A matrix $M(a,b,c)$ is central iff it commutes with every $M(d,e,f)$. Comparing
the $(1,3)$-entries of $M(a,b,c)M(d,e,f) = c+f+ae$ and $M(d,e,f)M(a,b,c) = c+f+b
d$, centrality requires $ae = bd$ for all $d,e \in \mathbb{Z}$. Taking $(d,e)=(1
,0)$ gives $a=0$; taking $(d,e)=(0,1)$ gives $b=0$. Conversely $a=b=0$ clearly s
uffices. Hence

$$Z(H) = \{\, M(0,0,c) : c \in \mathbb{Z} \,\}.$$

---

**Step 2: The commutator subgroup $[H,H]$.**

For $g = M(a,b,c)$ and $h = M(d,e,f)$, a direct computation using the multiplica
tion and inverse rules gives

$$[g,h] = g^{-1}h^{-1}gh = M(0,\,0,\; ae - bd).$$

So every commutator lies in $Z(H)$ (confirming $[H,H] \subseteq Z(H)$), and the
commutator subgroup is

$$[H,H] = \langle\, M(0,0,\, ae-bd) : a,b,d,e \in \mathbb{Z} \,\rangle.$$

---

**Step 3: $[H,H] = Z(H)$.**

We already have $[H,H] \subseteq Z(H)$. For the reverse inclusion, take any $M(0
,0,c) \in Z(H)$ with $c \in \mathbb{Z}$. Set $a = 1$, $e = c$, $b = 0$, $d = 0$.
 Then

$$[M(1,0,0),\, M(0,c,0)] = M(0,0,\, 1\cdot c - 0\cdot 0) = M(0,0,c).$$

So every element of $Z(H)$ is a single commutator, hence $Z(H) \subseteq [H,H]$.

---

**Conclusion.** Combining both inclusions,

$$Z(H) = [H,H].$$

**Yes, the center of the Heisenberg group is also its commutator subgroup.** (Th
e same argument works over any commutative ring, in particular over $\mathbb{R}$
, since the key identity $ae - bd$ ranges over the entire ground ring.)
