**Claim:** No. If $A \subseteq [0,1]$ is non-measurable, then $m(\operatorname{i
nt} A) \neq m(\overline{A})$ (where $m$ denotes Lebesgue measure).

## Proof

Let $A \subseteq [0,1]$ be non-measurable. Note that $\operatorname{int} A$ is o
pen and $\overline{A}$ is closed, so both are Borel sets and hence Lebesgue meas
urable; their measures are well-defined. Since $\operatorname{int} A \subseteq A
 \subseteq \overline{A}$, we have $\operatorname{int} A \subseteq \overline{A}$.

Suppose, for contradiction, that $m(\operatorname{int} A) = m(\overline{A})$. Si
nce $\operatorname{int} A \subseteq \overline{A}$ and both are measurable, this
gives
$$m\!\bigl(\overline{A} \setminus \operatorname{int} A\bigr) = m(\overline{A}) -
 m(\operatorname{int} A) = 0.$$

Now observe the set-theoretic inclusion
$$A \setminus \operatorname{int} A \;\subseteq\; \overline{A} \setminus \operato
rname{int} A.$$
The right-hand side is a Lebesgue-null set. Every subset of a Lebesgue-null set
is Lebesgue measurable (with measure $0$) — this is the completeness of Lebesgue
 measure. Therefore $A \setminus \operatorname{int} A$ is measurable with $m(A \
setminus \operatorname{int} A) = 0$.

Since $\operatorname{int} A$ is open (hence measurable) and $A \setminus \operat
orname{int} A$ is measurable, the union
$$A = \operatorname{int} A \;\cup\; \bigl(A \setminus \operatorname{int} A\bigr)
$$
is a union of two measurable sets, hence $A$ is Lebesgue measurable.

This contradicts the hypothesis that $A$ is non-measurable. $\square$

## Remark

The key ingredient is the **completeness** of Lebesgue measure: every subset of
a null set is measurable. This is what forces $A \setminus \operatorname{int} A$
 to be measurable once it is trapped inside the null set $\overline{A} \setminus
 \operatorname{int} A$, which in turn forces $A$ itself to be measurable. Thus e
quality of the measures of the interior and closure is a certificate of measurab
ility, and is impossible for a genuinely non-measurable set.
