## Proof

We wish to evaluate

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{n}{(n_p)!},$$

where $(n_p)! = p_1 \cdot p_2 \cdots p_n = \prod_{k=1}^{n} p_k$ is the product o
f the first $n$ primes (the primorial $p_n\#$).

**Key observation.** For every $k \geq 1$, the $k$-th prime satisfies $p_k \geq
k+1$. Indeed, the sequence of primes begins $p_1 = 2, p_2 = 3, p_3 = 5, p_4 = 7,
 \ldots$, and in general the $k$-th prime is at least as large as the $(k+1)$-th
 positive integer, since among $\{1, 2, \ldots, k+1\}$ there are at most $k$ int
egers $\geq 2$, so the $k$-th prime (which is $\geq 2$) must be $\geq k+1$. (For
mally: there are exactly $k$ integers in $\{2, 3, \ldots, k+1\}$, and at least o
ne integer in $\{1,\ldots,k\}$, namely $1$, is not prime, so $p_k \geq k+1$.)

**Lower bound on the primorial.** Using $p_k \geq k+1$ for all $k \geq 1$,

$$p_n\# = \prod_{k=1}^{n} p_k \;\geq\; \prod_{k=1}^{n}(k+1) = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4
\cdots (n+1) = (n+1)!.$$

**Squeeze.** Therefore

$$0 \;<\; \frac{n}{p_n\#} \;\leq\; \frac{n}{(n+1)!}.$$

Since $(n+1)!$ grows super-exponentially, $\dfrac{n}{(n+1)!} \to 0$ as $n \to \i
nfty$. Concretely, for $n \geq 2$,

$$\frac{n}{(n+1)!} = \frac{n}{(n+1)\cdot n!} \leq \frac{1}{n!} \to 0.$$

By the Squeeze Theorem, since $\dfrac{n}{p_n\#}$ is squeezed between $0$ and a s
equence tending to $0$,

$$\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{n}{(n_p)!} = 0.}$$
