## Solution

**Parameterization.** A matrix $M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ lies in
$S$ iff $a,b,c,d$ form an arithmetic progression in that order. Writing the firs
t term as $a$ and common difference as $r$, every element of $S$ is uniquely

$$M=\begin{pmatrix}a & a+r\\ a+2r & a+3r\end{pmatrix},\qquad a,r\in\mathbb R.$$

**Trace, determinant, and Cayley–Hamilton.** For such $M$,

$$\operatorname{tr} M = 2a+3r =: t,\qquad \det M = a(a+3r)-(a+r)(a+2r)=-2r^{2}=:
d.$$

By Cayley–Hamilton, $M^{2}=tM-dI=tM+2r^{2}I$, and inductively every power satisf
ies

$$M^{k}=\alpha_{k}M+\beta_{k}I \tag{1}$$

where the scalar sequences obey the common recurrence $x_{k}=t\,x_{k-1}+2r^{2}x_
{k-2}$ with

$$\alpha_{0}=0,\ \alpha_{1}=1;\qquad \beta_{0}=1,\ \beta_{1}=0.$$

**Key identity.** I claim $\beta_{k}=2r^{2}\alpha_{k-1}$ for all $k\ge 1$. Indee
d $\beta_{1}=0=2r^{2}\alpha_{0}$, and if it holds for $k-1,k-2$ then
$$\beta_{k}=t\beta_{k-1}+2r^{2}\beta_{k-2}=2r^{2}\bigl(t\alpha_{k-2}+2r^{2}\alph
a_{k-3}\bigr)=2r^{2}\alpha_{k-1}.$$

**When is $M^{k}\in S$?** From (1),

$$M^{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{k}a+\beta_{k} & \alpha_{k}(a+r)\\ \alpha_{k}(a+2r
) & \alpha_{k}(a+3r)\end{pmatrix}=: \begin{pmatrix}p&q\\ s&u\end{pmatrix}.$$

The three AP gaps are
$$u-s=\alpha_{k}r,\quad s-q=\alpha_{k}r,\quad q-p=\alpha_{k}r-\beta_{k}.$$

The first two are automatically equal; the third equals them **iff $\beta_{k}=0$
**. Hence

$$M^{k}\in S \iff \beta_{k}=0 \iff 2r^{2}\alpha_{k-1}=0 \iff r=0\ \text{or}\ \al
pha_{k-1}=0. \tag{2}$$

We need this for *some* integer $k>1$, i.e. $\alpha_{n}=0$ for some $n=k-1\ge 1$
, or $r=0$.

**Case 1: $r=0$.** Then $M=a\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}=aJ$ with $J^{2}
=2J$, so $J^{k}=2^{k-1}J$ and $M^{k}=a^{k}2^{k-1}J$, whose four entries are all
equal — an AP. Thus every

$$M=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \lambda\in\mathbb R, \tag
{F1}$$

works (for every $k>1$).

**Case 2: $r\ne 0$.** We need $\alpha_{n}=0$ for some $n\ge 1$. Since $\alpha_{1
}=1\ne 0$, we need $n\ge 2$. The eigenvalues of $M$ are the roots of $\lambda^{2
}-t\lambda-2r^{2}=0$; its discriminant is $t^{2}+8r^{2}>0$ (as $r\ne 0$), so the
 eigenvalues $\lambda_{+},\lambda_{-}$ are **real and distinct**, and

$$\alpha_{n}=\frac{\lambda_{+}^{\,n}-\lambda_{-}^{\,n}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}
.$$

Both eigenvalues are nonzero (their product is $\det M=-2r^{2}\ne 0$). Thus $\al
pha_{n}=0\iff\lambda_{+}^{\,n}=\lambda_{-}^{\,n}\iff(\lambda_{+}/\lambda_{-})^{n
}=1$. Since the ratio is real, it must be a real $n$-th root of unity, i.e. $\pm
 1$.

- Ratio $=+1$: $\lambda_{+}=\lambda_{-}$, contradicting distinctness (equivalent
ly forces $t^{2}+8r^{2}=0$, impossible for $r\ne 0$).
- Ratio $=-1$ (requires $n$ even): $\lambda_{+}=-\lambda_{-}$, so $\lambda_{+}+\
lambda_{-}=t=0$, i.e. $2a+3r=0$, i.e. $\boxed{a=-3r/2}$.

Conversely, when $t=0$ the recurrence gives $\alpha_{2}=t\alpha_{1}+2r^{2}\alpha
_{0}=0$, so $\beta_{3}=2r^{2}\alpha_{2}=0$ and $M^{3}\in S$. (Indeed $M^{2}=2r^{
2}I$ and $M^{3}=2r^{2}M$, a scalar multiple of $M$, hence in $S$.) Writing $\mu=
r/2$, this family is

$$M=\mu\begin{pmatrix}-3&-1\\ 1& 3\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mu\in\mathbb R\setminus\
{0\}. \tag{F2}$$

(The value $\mu=0$ gives the zero matrix, already in (F1).)

**Verification of (F2).** For $M=\mu\begin{pmatrix}-3&-1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$: $\o
peratorname{tr}M=0$, $\det M=\mu^{2}(-9+1)=-8\mu^{2}=-2r^{2}$ with $r=2\mu$. The
n $M^{2}=-\det M\cdot I=8\mu^{2}I$ and $M^{3}=8\mu^{2}M=8\mu^{3}\begin{pmatrix}-
3&-1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$, whose entries $-24\mu^{3},-8\mu^{3},8\mu^{3},24\mu^{3}$
 form an AP with common difference $16\mu^{3}$. ✓

**No other solutions.** Cases 1 and 2 exhaust all possibilities: either $r=0$ (C
ase 1) or $r\ne 0$, where (2) forces $\alpha_{n}=0$ for some $n\ge 2$, which (si
nce eigenvalues are real, distinct, nonzero) forces the eigenvalue ratio to be $
-1$, i.e. $t=0$ (Case 2).

### Answer

The matrices $M\in S$ for which $M^{k}\in S$ for some integer $k>1$ are exactly

$$\boxed{\,M=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\ \text{for }\lambda\in\
mathbb R,\quad\text{or}\quad M=\mu\begin{pmatrix}-3&-1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}\ \text{
for }\mu\in\mathbb R\,.\,}$$

(The two families overlap only at the zero matrix.) For the first family every $
k>1$ works; for the second (with $\mu\ne 0$) every odd $k\ge 3$ works, in partic
ular $k=3$.
