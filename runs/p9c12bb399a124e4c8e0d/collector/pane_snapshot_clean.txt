## Solution

**Answer:** The one-point compactification of $S^n \times \mathbb{R}$ is $S^{n+1
}$ with two distinct points identified, i.e. the quotient space $S^{n+1}/\{p\sim
 q\}$ where $p\neq q$ are collapsed to a single point.

---

### Proof

**Step 1. The one-point compactification exists.** $S^n\times\mathbb{R}$ is loca
lly compact (product of locally compact spaces), non-compact (the $\mathbb{R}$ f
actor is unbounded), and Hausdorff. Hence its one-point compactification $Y=(S^n
\times\mathbb{R})\cup\{\infty\}$ is a well-defined compact Hausdorff space, wher
e neighborhoods of $\infty$ are complements of compact subsets of $S^n\times\mat
hbb{R}$.

**Step 2. Reduce to $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}$.** Define
$$\Phi:S^n\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\},\qquad \Phi(u,t)=e^{
t}\,u.$$
This is a homeomorphism: it is continuous, bijective (inverse: $x\mapsto (x/\|x\
|,\,\ln\|x\|)$), with continuous inverse. So it suffices to identify the one-poi
nt compactification of $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}$.

**Step 3. Compact sets in $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}$.** A subset $K\subset
eq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}$ is compact iff it is closed in $\mathbb{R}^{n+
1}\setminus\{0\}$ and bounded away from both $0$ and infinity, i.e. there exist
$0<\varepsilon<M$ with
$$K\subseteq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\varepsilon\le\|x\|\le M\}.$$
Consequently a neighborhood of $\infty$ in the one-point compactification $Y$ co
ntains a set of the form
$$\{\|x\|<\varepsilon\}\;\cup\;\{\|x\|>M\}\;\cup\;\{\infty\}.$$
So $\infty$ is "close to" both the origin-direction and the infinity-direction s
imultaneously.

**Step 4. Realize $Y$ as a quotient of $S^{n+1}$.** View $S^{n+1}$ as the one-po
int compactification $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\cup\{\infty_1\}$, and let $0\in\mathbb{R}
^{n+1}\subset S^{n+1}$ be the origin. Form the quotient
$$Z:=S^{n+1}/\{0\sim\infty_1\},$$
collapsing the two distinct points $0$ and $\infty_1$ to a single point $\ast$.

The complement $Z\setminus\{\ast\}=S^{n+1}\setminus\{0,\infty_1\}=\mathbb{R}^{n+
1}\setminus\{0\}$ carries its usual topology. A set $U\subseteq Z$ with $\ast\in
 U$ is open iff its preimage in $S^{n+1}$ is open and contains both $0$ and $\in
fty_1$; hence $U$ must contain a neighborhood of $0$ (some $\{\|x\|<\varepsilon\
}$) and a neighborhood of $\infty_1$ (some $\{\|x\|>M\}$). Thus neighborhoods of
 $\ast$ in $Z$ are exactly
$$\{\|x\|<\varepsilon\}\cup\{\|x\|>M\}\cup\{\ast\},$$
matching precisely the neighborhoods of $\infty$ in $Y$ from Step 3.

**Step 5. Conclusion.** The bijection $Y\to Z$ sending $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminu
s\{0\}$ to itself by the identity and $\infty\mapsto\ast$ is a homeomorphism: it
 is continuous and open on the open subspace $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}$ (t
rivially), and it preserves the neighborhood systems of the added point (Step 3
vs. Step 4). Therefore
$$\bigl(S^n\times\mathbb{R}\bigr)^{+}\;\cong\;Z\;=\;S^{n+1}/\{p\sim q\},$$
the space obtained from $S^{n+1}$ by identifying two distinct points.

**Remark (sanity check, $n=1$).** For $n=1$, $S^1\times\mathbb{R}$ is the infini
te cylinder $\cong\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$. The result is $S^2$ with two poin
ts identified. This is *not* $S^2$: removing the added point from $S^2$ gives $\
mathbb{R}^2$ (simply connected), whereas removing it from $Y$ gives $S^1\times\m
athbb{R}\cong\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$ (fundamental group $\mathbb{Z}$). The i
dentification of two points is therefore essential. For $n=0$: $S^0\times\mathbb
{R}=\mathbb{R}\sqcup\mathbb{R}$, and $S^1/\{p\sim q\}$ is the figure-eight $S^1\
vee S^1$, the correct one-point compactification of two disjoint lines. $\square
$
