**Answer: No.** Such a polynomial does not exist. Every polynomial $f \in \mathb
b{R}[X]$ satisfying $f(\mathbb{Z}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}$ must in fact lie in $\m
athbb{Q}[X]$.

---

## Proof

**Key tool (Newton's forward-difference expansion).** For every $d \geq 0$, the
binomial-coefficient polynomials
$$\binom{X}{k} = \frac{X(X-1)\cdots(X-k+1)}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, d,$$
have degrees $0, 1, \dots, d$ respectively, hence form a basis of the $(d+1)$-di
mensional $\mathbb{R}$-vector space of real polynomials of degree $\leq d$. Cons
equently every $f \in \mathbb{R}[X]$ of degree $d$ admits a unique expansion
$$f(X) = \sum_{k=0}^{d} c_k \binom{X}{k}, \qquad c_k = \Delta^{k} f(0) \in \math
bb{R}, \tag{1}$$
where $\Delta$ is the forward-difference operator $(\Delta g)(x) = g(x+1) - g(x)
$ and $\Delta^k f(0) = \sum_{j=0}^{k} (-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j} f(j).$

---

**Step 1: $f(\mathbb{Z}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q} \Longrightarrow$ every $c_k$ is ra
tional.**

If $f(j) \in \mathbb{Q}$ for all $j \in \mathbb{Z}$, then each forward differenc
e
$$c_k = \Delta^k f(0) = \sum_{j=0}^{k} (-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j} f(j)$$
is a finite $\mathbb{Q}$-linear combination of rational numbers, hence $c_k \in
\mathbb{Q}$ for every $k$.

---

**Step 2: Each $\binom{X}{k}$ has rational coefficients in the monomial basis.**

The numerator $X(X-1)\cdots(X-k+1)$ is a monic polynomial of degree $k$ with **i
nteger** coefficients. Dividing by $k! \in \mathbb{Z}$ yields a polynomial all o
f whose monomial coefficients lie in $\mathbb{Q}$.

---

**Step 3: $f$ has all rational coefficients.**

By Step 1, $f = \sum_{k=0}^{d} c_k \binom{X}{k}$ with every $c_k \in \mathbb{Q}$
. By Step 2, each $\binom{X}{k} \in \mathbb{Q}[X]$. A $\mathbb{Q}$-linear combin
ation of elements of $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ remains in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$. Therefore
$$f \in \mathbb{Q}[X].$$

---

**Conclusion.** We have shown
$$f(\mathbb{Z}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q} \;\Longrightarrow\; f \in \mathbb{Q}[X],$$
i.e., every coefficient of $f$ must be rational. Hence there exists **no** polyn
omial $f \in \mathbb{R}[X]$ with at least one irrational coefficient that takes
rational values at every integer. $\blacksquare$
