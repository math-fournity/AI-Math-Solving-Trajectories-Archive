## Solution

We evaluate $I = \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{x\sin x}{x^2+a^2}\,dx$
 for $a>0$.

**Step 1: Reduce to a complex integral.**

Since $\sin x = \operatorname{Im}(e^{ix})$,

$$I = \operatorname{Im}\!\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{x\,e^{ix}}{x^2+a^2}\,dx.$$

**Step 2: Choose the contour and integrand.**

Let $f(z) = \dfrac{z\,e^{iz}}{z^2+a^2}$. Its poles are at $z = \pm ia$. Since $a
>0$, the pole $z=ia$ lies in the upper half-plane and $z=-ia$ in the lower half-
plane.

Take the closed contour $C_R$ consisting of the segment $[-R,R]$ on the real axi
s and the upper semicircle $\Gamma_R: z=Re^{i\theta},\, 0\le\theta\le\pi$. For $
R>a$, the only pole enclosed is $z=ia$.

**Step 3: Compute the residue.**

The pole at $z=ia$ is simple, with

$$\operatorname{Res}_{z=ia} f(z) = \lim_{z\to ia} \frac{z\,e^{iz}}{z+ia} = \frac
{ia\,e^{i(ia)}}{2ia} = \frac{e^{-a}}{2}.$$

By the residue theorem,

$$\oint_{C_R} f(z)\,dz = 2\pi i\cdot \frac{e^{-a}}{2} = \pi i\, e^{-a}.$$

**Step 4: Show the arc integral vanishes.**

On $\Gamma_R$, $z = Re^{i\theta}$, $|e^{iz}| = e^{-R\sin\theta}$, and

$$\left|\frac{z}{z^2+a^2}\right| = \frac{R}{|z^2+a^2|} \le \frac{R}{R^2-a^2} \to
 0 \quad\text{as } R\to\infty.$$

Since $\frac{z}{z^2+a^2}\to 0$ uniformly as $|z|\to\infty$ and $e^{iz}$ decays i
n the upper half-plane, Jordan's lemma gives

$$\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{\Gamma_R} \frac{z\,e^{iz}}{z^2+a^2}\,dz = 0.$$

**Step 5: Take the limit and extract the imaginary part.**

Letting $R\to\infty$,

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{x\,e^{ix}}{x^2+a^2}\,dx = \pi i\, e^{-a}.$$

Taking the imaginary part,

$$I = \operatorname{Im}\!\bigl(\pi i\, e^{-a}\bigr) = \pi e^{-a}.$$

(As a consistency check: the real part gives $\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{x\cos
x}{x^2+a^2}\,dx = 0$, which is correct since that integrand is odd; meanwhile $\
frac{x\sin x}{x^2+a^2}$ is even, consistent with a nonzero result.)

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{x\sin x}{x^2+a^2}\,dx = \pi e^
{-a}}$$
