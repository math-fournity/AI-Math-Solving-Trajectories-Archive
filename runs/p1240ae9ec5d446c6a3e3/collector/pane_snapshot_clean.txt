## Proof

We show the answer is $n = 14$.

### Key observations

After Player 2's first move (removing 2 consecutive cards), the remaining cards
form several intervals of consecutive integers. Player 1 then removes 3 consecut
ive cards from **one** interval. Player 2 needs some interval of length $\ge 4$
to survive.

- **If an interval has length $\ge 10$**: Player 1 removes 3 consecutive from it
, leaving two pieces with the larger of length $\ge \lceil(\ell-3)/2\rceil \ge \
lceil 7/2\rceil = 4$. Player 1 **cannot** destroy it. (If Player 1 attacks elsew
here, it's untouched.)
- **If two intervals each have length $\ge 4$**: Player 1 can attack only **one*
*; the other survives with length $\ge 4$.

Conversely, if at most one interval has length $\ge 4$ and all have length $\le
9$, Player 1 attacks that single interval and splits it into pieces of length $\
le 3$ (since $\lceil(\ell-3)/2\rceil \le 3$ for $\ell \le 9$), and Player 2 lose
s.

So **Player 2 wins** after his first move iff the resulting intervals contain ei
ther (a) $\ge 2$ intervals of length $\ge 4$, or (b) some interval of length $\g
e 10$.

---

### Part 1: $n = 13$ does not suffice

Player 1 removes card **4**, leaving intervals $\{1,2,3\}$ (length $L=3$) and $\
{5,6,\dots,13\}$ (length $R=9$).

Player 2 removes 2 consecutive cards:

- **From $\{1,2,3\}$**: leaves one card on the left; the right interval (length
9) is intact. Player 1 removes 3 consecutive from its middle (e.g., $\{8,9,10\}$
), splitting it into two intervals of length 3. All intervals $\le 3$. ✗

- **From $\{5,\dots,13\}$** (non-adjacent to the gap): splits the 9-interval int
o pieces of lengths $p$ and $7-p$. The three intervals have lengths $3, p, 7-p$.
 Since $3 < 4$, getting two intervals $\ge 4$ requires $p \ge 4$ **and** $7-p \g
e 4$, i.e., $p \ge 4$ and $p \le 3$—**impossible**. The largest interval is $\le
 7 < 10$. Player 1 splits it into pieces $\le 3$. ✗

- **Adjacent to the gap** ($\{2,3\}$ or $\{5,6\}$): creates a 3-card block, leav
ing two intervals of lengths $(1, 9)$ or $(3, 7)$. Only one interval $\ge 4$, an
d its length $\le 9 < 10$. Player 1 neutralizes it. ✗

In every case Player 2 cannot complete the final move. So $n=13$ fails.

---

### Part 2: $n = 14$ suffices

Player 1 removes card $x$, creating intervals of lengths $L = x-1$ and $R = 14-x
$, with $L + R = 13$.

**Case 1: $R \ge 10$ ($x \le 4$) or $L \ge 10$ ($x \ge 11$).**
Player 2 removes any 2 consecutive from the shorter side. The longer side (lengt
h $\ge 10$) survives Player 1's attack (Observation 1). ✓

**Case 2: $L \ge 4$ and $R \ge 6$ ($5 \le x \le 8$).**
Player 2 removes the 5th and 6th cards of the $R$-interval, leaving a piece of l
ength 4. Resulting intervals: $L,\; 4,\; R{-}6$. Since $L \ge 4$ and $4 \ge 4$,
**two** intervals have length $\ge 4$ (Observation 2). ✓

**Case 3: $R \ge 4$ and $L \ge 6$ ($7 \le x \le 10$).**
By symmetry, Player 2 removes 2 consecutive from the $L$-interval leaving a piec
e of length 4. Two intervals $\ge 4$. ✓

These cases cover all $x \in \{1,\dots,14\}$:
- $x \le 4$: Case 1 ✓
- $x \in \{5,6,7,8\}$: Case 2 ✓
- $x \in \{7,8,9,10\}$: Case 3 ✓
- $x \ge 11$: Case 1 ✓

Player 2's first move (2 consecutive) is always possible since $n \ge 14$ guaran
tees a consecutive pair remains after one card is removed.

---

### Conclusion

$n = 13$ fails (Player 1 has a winning strategy), and $n = 14$ succeeds (Player
2 has a winning response to every move by Player 1).

$$\boxed{n = 14}$$
