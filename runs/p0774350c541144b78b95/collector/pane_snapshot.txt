## Proof

**Step 1: Rewrite the PDE in polar coordinates.**

Introduce polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$ on $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}$ via
$$x = r\cos\theta,\qquad y = r\sin\theta,\qquad r>0,\ \theta\in\mathbb{R}.$$
The chain rule gives the angular derivative
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} = \frac{\partial x}{\partial\theta}\frac{\part
ial}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial y}{\partial\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}
= (-r\sin\theta)\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + (r\cos\theta)\frac{\partial}{\part
ial y} = -y\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + x\frac{\partial}{\partial y}.$$
Hence the given equation
$$x\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} - y\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = f$$
is exactly
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta} = f.\tag{1}$$

**Step 2: Solve the ODE along each circle.**

Fix $r>0$ and define $h(\theta) = f(r\cos\theta,\, r\sin\theta)$. By (1),
$$h'(\theta) = h(\theta),$$
so $h(\theta) = C(r)\,e^{\theta}$ for some constant $C(r)$ depending only on $r$
.

**Step 3: Use single-valuedness of $f$.**

Since $f$ is a well-defined function on $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}$, the va
lue $f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$ depends only on the point $(r\cos\theta, r\sin
\theta)$, not on the choice of angular parameter. In particular, the point $(r\c
os(\theta+2\pi), r\sin(\theta+2\pi))$ is identical to $(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta
)$, so
$$h(\theta+2\pi) = h(\theta)\qquad\text{for all }\theta.$$
Substituting $h(\theta)=C(r)e^{\theta}$:
$$C(r)\,e^{\theta+2\pi} = C(r)\,e^{\theta}\ \Longrightarrow\ C(r)\,e^{\theta}\bi
gl(e^{2\pi}-1\bigr)=0.$$
Since $e^{2\pi}\neq 1$, we conclude $C(r)=0$.

**Step 4: Conclude.**

Thus $h(\theta)=0$ for every $\theta$, i.e. $f$ vanishes identically on the circ
le of radius $r$. Since $r>0$ was arbitrary, $f\equiv 0$ on all of $\mathbb{R}^2
\setminus\{(0,0)\}$.

$\blacksquare$
