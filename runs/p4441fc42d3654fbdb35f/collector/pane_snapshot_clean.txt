## Proof

The question asks whether, under the stated hypotheses, it is **possible** that
$\int_U b\,f_n \to 1$. We show the answer is **yes**, by an explicit constructio
n. (It is not forced in general — e.g. $b\equiv \tfrac12$ gives $\int_U b f_n \t
o \tfrac12$ — but it is not ruled out.)

### Key observation

Since $0<b<1$ pointwise in $U$ and $f_n>0$, we have $b\,f_n < f_n$ pointwise, he
nce
$$\int_U b\,f_n \;<\; \int_U f_n \qquad\text{for every } n.$$
So $\int_U b\,f_n \to 1$ can happen only if the "missing mass"
$$\int_U (1-b)\,f_n \;\to\; 0.$$
This is **not** excluded, because $b$ is only required to satisfy $b<1$ *strictl
y*; its supremum over $U$ may equal $1$ (not attained). If $f_n$ concentrates it
s mass where $b$ is arbitrarily close to $1$, the missing mass can vanish in the
 limit even though it is strictly positive for each fixed $n$.

### Construction

Take $U=(0,1)\subset\mathbb{R}$ and $b(x)=x$. Then $b$ is continuous on $U$ and
$0<b(x)<1$ for every $x\in U$, yet $\sup_U b = 1$ (approached as $x\to 1^-$).

For $n\ge 2$ define
$$f_n(x) \;=\; n\,\mathbf{1}_{(1-1/n,\,1)}(x) \;+\; \frac{1}{n^{2}}\,\mathbf{1}_
{(0,\,1-1/n]}(x).$$
Each $f_n$ is Lebesgue-measurable and strictly positive on all of $U$.

**Integral of $f_n$.**
$$\int_0^1 f_n \;=\; n\cdot\frac{1}{n} \;+\; \frac{1}{n^{2}}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{n}\
Bigr) \;=\; 1 + \frac{1-1/n}{n^{2}} \;\longrightarrow\; 1.$$

**Integral of $b\,f_n$.**
$$\int_0^1 b\,f_n \;=\; n\int_{1-1/n}^{1} x\,dx \;+\; \frac{1}{n^{2}}\int_{0}^{1
-1/n} x\,dx.$$

First term:
$$n\int_{1-1/n}^{1} x\,dx \;=\; n\cdot\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1-\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big
r)^{2}\Bigr) \;=\; n\cdot\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\frac{2}{n}-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Bigr) \;=\
; 1-\frac{1}{2n}.$$

Second term:
$$\frac{1}{n^{2}}\int_{0}^{1-1/n} x\,dx \;=\; \frac{1}{n^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\Bi
gl(1-\frac{1}{n}\Bigr)^{2} \;=\; \frac{(1-1/n)^{2}}{2n^{2}} \;\longrightarrow\;
0.$$

Therefore
$$\int_0^1 b\,f_n \;=\; 1-\frac{1}{2n} + \frac{(1-1/n)^{2}}{2n^{2}} \;\longright
arrow\; 1.$$

### Conclusion

We have produced a bounded open set $U$, a continuous function $b$ with $0<b<1$
in $U$, and a sequence of positive measurable functions $f_n$ with $\int_U f_n\t
o 1$, such that $\int_U b\,f_n\to 1$ as well. Hence $\int_U b\,f_n\to 1$ **is po
ssible**.

The mechanism is precisely that $b<1$ strictly but $\sup_U b = 1$ is not attaine
d, allowing $f_n$ to concentrate where $1-b$ is arbitrarily small, so that $\int
_U(1-b)f_n\to 0$ even though $(1-b)f_n>0$ pointwise for every $n$.
