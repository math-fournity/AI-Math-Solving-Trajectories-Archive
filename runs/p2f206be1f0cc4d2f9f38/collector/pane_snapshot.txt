**Answer: Yes, such a polynomial exists.**

**Proof.**

Write the Jordan block as $J_k(a) = aI + N$, where $N$ is the nilpotent matrix w
ith $1$'s on the superdiagonal, so $N^k = 0$ and $N^{k-1} \neq 0$. Then

$$J_k(a)^2 = a^2 I + 2aN + N^2 = a^2 I + M, \qquad M := N(2aI + N).$$

Since $a \neq 0$, the matrix $2aI + N$ (upper triangular with diagonal $2a$) is
invertible, and it commutes with $N$. Hence for every $j \geq 1$,

$$M^j = N^j(2aI+N)^j, \qquad \operatorname{rank}(M^j) = \operatorname{rank}(N^j)
 = k - j \;\;(j \le k).$$

In particular $\ker M = \ker N$ is $1$-dimensional, so $J_k(a)^2$ has eigenvalue
 $a^2 \neq 0$ with a **single** Jordan block of size $k$; its Jordan form is $J_
k(a^2)$.

Now let $p$ be any polynomial. Since $J_k(a)^2 = a^2 I + M$ with $M^k = 0$, Tayl
or's formula for polynomials gives

$$p\!\bigl(J_k(a)^2\bigr) = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} \frac{p^{(j)}(a^2)}{j!}\, M^j. \tag
{1}$$

**Key observation.** Because $N$ and $2aI+N$ commute and $2aI+N$ is invertible,
each $M^j = N^j(2aI+N)^j$ is a polynomial in $N$ whose lowest power of $N$ is $N
^j$ with leading coefficient $(2a)^j \neq 0$. Writing $M^j = \sum_{i=j}^{k-1} c_
{j,i}\, N^i$ with $c_{j,j} = (2a)^j \neq 0$, the transition matrix $(c_{j,i})$ b
etween the two bases $\{M^0, M^1, \dots, M^{k-1}\}$ and $\{N^0, N^1, \dots, N^{k
-1}\}$ of $\mathbb{C}[N]$ is upper triangular with nonzero diagonal. Hence it is
 invertible:

$$\operatorname{span}\{M^0, \dots, M^{k-1}\} = \operatorname{span}\{N^0, \dots,
N^{k-1}\} = \mathbb{C}[N]. \tag{2}$$

We want $p(J_k(a)^2) = J_k(a) = aI + N$, i.e. the target in the $N$-basis is

$$a\, N^0 + 1\cdot N^1 + 0\cdot N^2 + \cdots + 0\cdot N^{k-1}.$$

By (2), this target lies in the span of $\{M^j\}$, so there exist **unique** sca
lars $\alpha_0, \dots, \alpha_{k-1}$ with

$$\sum_{j=0}^{k-1} \alpha_j M^j = aI + N.$$

Setting $\alpha_j = \frac{p^{(j)}(a^2)}{j!}$ prescribes the values of $p$ and it
s first $k-1$ derivatives at the single point $x = a^2$. By **Hermite interpolat
ion** (matching $k$ derivative orders at one point), there exists a polynomial $
p$ (of degree $\le 2k-1$) satisfying exactly these $k$ conditions. For this $p$,
 equation (1) yields $p(J_k(a)^2) = aI + N = J_k(a)$.

**Explicit description.** The required derivatives are precisely those of $f(x)
= \sqrt{x}$ at $x = a^2$ (choosing the branch with $\sqrt{a^2} = a$): $f(a^2) =
a$, $f'(a^2) = \frac{1}{2a}$, $f''(a^2) = -\frac{1}{4a^3}$, etc. Indeed $f(J_k(a
)^2) = J_k(a)$ is the formal square root, and the polynomial $p$ is the Hermite
interpolant of $f$ at $a^2$ through order $k-1$.

Therefore, a complex polynomial $p$ with $p\!\bigl(J_k(a)^2\bigr) = J_k(a)$ **do
es exist**. $\blacksquare$
