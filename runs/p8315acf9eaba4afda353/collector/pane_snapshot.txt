# Completeness of $\{x^{2k}\}_{k \geq 0}$ in $L^2[0,1]$

**Answer: Yes, the sequence is complete in $L^2[0,1]$.**

## Proof

We must show: if $f \in L^2[0,1]$ satisfies $\langle f, x^{2k}\rangle = \int_0^1
 f(x)\,x^{2k}\,dx = 0$ for every $k = 0,1,2,\dots$, then $f = 0$ a.e.

**Step 1: Substitution $t = x^2$.**

Set $t = x^2$, so $x = \sqrt{t}$, $dx = \frac{dt}{2\sqrt{t}}$. Each orthogonalit
y condition becomes

$$0 = \int_0^1 f(x)\,x^{2k}\,dx = \int_0^1 f(\sqrt{t})\,t^{k}\,\frac{dt}{2\sqrt{
t}} = \int_0^1 g(t)\,t^{k}\,dt,$$

where we define

$$g(t) := \frac{f(\sqrt{t})}{2\sqrt{t}}, \qquad t \in (0,1].$$

**Step 2: $g \in L^1[0,1]$.**

Substituting back $t = x^2$:

$$\int_0^1 |g(t)|\,dt = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 \frac{|f(\sqrt{t})|}{\sqrt{t}}\,dt =
 \int_0^1 |f(x)|\,dx \leq \left(\int_0^1 |f|^2\right)^{1/2}\!\left(\int_0^1 1\ri
ght)^{1/2} = \|f\|_{L^2} < \infty,$$

by Cauchy–Schwarz. So $g \in L^1[0,1]$.

**Step 3: $g$ annihilates all polynomials, hence all continuous functions.**

By linearity, $\int_0^1 g(t)\,p(t)\,dt = 0$ for every polynomial $p$. By the Wei
erstrass approximation theorem, polynomials are dense in $C[0,1]$ (sup-norm). Fo
r any $h \in C[0,1]$ and $\varepsilon > 0$, pick a polynomial $p$ with $\|h - p\
|_\infty < \varepsilon$. Then

$$\left|\int_0^1 g\,h\right| = \left|\int_0^1 g\,(h - p)\right| \leq \varepsilon
\,\|g\|_{L^1}.$$

Since $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, $\int_0^1 g(t)\,h(t)\,dt = 0$ for all $h \in C
[0,1]$.

**Step 4: $g = 0$ a.e.**

Suppose not. Then $E^+ = \{t : g(t) > 0\}$ or $E^- = \{t : g(t) < 0\}$ has posit
ive measure; say $m(E^+) > 0$. By inner regularity of Lebesgue measure, there is
 a compact $K \subset E^+$ with $m(K) > 0$. By Urysohn's lemma, there exists $h
\in C[0,1]$ with $h \equiv 1$ on $K$ and $h \geq 0$ on $[0,1]$. Then

$$\int_0^1 g\,h \geq \int_K g > 0,$$

contradicting Step 3. Hence $g = 0$ a.e.

**Step 5: $f = 0$ a.e.**

From $g = 0$ a.e. we get $f(\sqrt{t}) = 0$ for a.e. $t \in (0,1]$. Let $E = \{x
\in [0,1] : f(x) \neq 0\}$ and suppose $m(E) > 0$. The preimage $A = \{t \in [0,
1] : \sqrt{t} \in E\}$ has measure

$$m(A) = \int_0^1 \mathbf{1}_E(\sqrt{t})\,dt = \int_0^1 \mathbf{1}_E(x)\cdot 2x\
,dx \quad (t = x^2).$$

Since $2x > 0$ on $(0,1]$ and $\mathbf{1}_E \geq 0$ with $\int_0^1 \mathbf{1}_E
= m(E) > 0$, we obtain $m(A) > 0$. But $f(\sqrt{t}) = 0$ a.e. forces $m(A) = 0$—
contradiction. Therefore $m(E) = 0$, i.e., $f = 0$ a.e. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** This also follows from the Müntz–Szász theorem: the exponents $\lamb
da_k = 2k$ ($k \geq 1$) satisfy $\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{\lambda_k} = \frac{1
}{2}\sum \frac{1}{k} = \infty$, so $\{1, x^2, x^4, \dots\}$ is complete in $C[0,
1]$, hence in $L^2[0,1]$. The proof above is self-contained and avoids invoking
that theorem.
