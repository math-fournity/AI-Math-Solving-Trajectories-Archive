## Proof

**Claim.** The polynomials satisfying the condition are exactly $P(x) = ax + b$
with $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$, $a \neq 0$.

### Step 1: $x \in \mathbb{Q} \implies P(x) \in \mathbb{Q}$ forces $P \in \mathb
b{Q}[x]$.

Write $P(x) = a_n x^n + \cdots + a_0 \in \mathbb{R}[x]$. Evaluating at $x = 0, 1
, \dots, n$ gives the Vandermonde system $V\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{b}$, where $V =
(k^j)_{0 \le k,j \le n}$ is invertible with integer entries and $\mathbf{b} = (P
(0), \dots, P(n))^T \in \mathbb{Q}^n$. Since $V^{-1}$ has rational entries, $\ma
thbf{a} = V^{-1}\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{Q}^{n+1}$. Thus $P \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$.

### Step 2: Reduction to integer coefficients.

Let $d = \operatorname{lcm}$ of denominators of coefficients, so $Q = dP \in \ma
thbb{Z}[x]$. Since $d \in \mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}$: $Q(x) \in \mathbb{Q} \iff P
(x) \in \mathbb{Q}$. So $Q$ satisfies the same condition, and $\deg Q = \deg P$.
 **Henceforth assume $P \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$.**

### Step 3: Constant polynomials fail.

If $P \equiv c$: if $c \in \mathbb{Q}$, then $P(\sqrt{2}) = c \in \mathbb{Q}$ bu
t $\sqrt{2} \notin \mathbb{Q}$; if $c \notin \mathbb{Q}$, then $P(0) = c \notin
\mathbb{Q}$ but $0 \in \mathbb{Q}$. Either way the condition fails. So $\deg P \
ge 1$.

### Step 4: $\deg P \ge 2$ fails.

Let $P(x) = a_n x^n + \cdots + a_0 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$, $\deg P = n \ge 2$, $a_n
\neq 0$.

Pick a prime $p$ with $p \nmid a_n$, and set $q = N + \frac{1}{p}$ for an intege
r $N$ chosen so that $q$ lies in the range of $P$ (always possible: for odd $n$,
 every $q$ works; for even $n$ with $a_n > 0$, the range is $[m, \infty)$ so tak
e $N$ large; for even $n$ with $a_n < 0$, take $N$ sufficiently negative).

**We show $P(x) = q$ has no rational root.** Suppose $r = a/b$ (lowest terms, $b
 > 0$) satisfies $P(a/b) = N + 1/p$. Then:

$$p\bigl(a_n a^n + a_{n-1}a^{n-1}b + \cdots + a_0 b^n\bigr) = (pN+1)\,b^n.$$

The left side is divisible by $p$, so $p \mid (pN+1) b^n$. Since $\gcd(p, pN+1)
= 1$, we get $p \mid b^n$, hence $p \mid b$. Write $b = pb'$. Substituting and d
ividing by $p$:

$$a_n a^n + p\bigl(\text{integer}\bigr) = (pN+1)\,p^{n-1}(b')^n.$$

Since $n \ge 2$, the right side is divisible by $p$. So $p \mid a_n a^n$. Since
$p \nmid a_n$, we get $p \mid a$. But from the constant term of $pP(x) - (pN+1)$
, the rational root theorem gives $a \mid \bigl(p(a_0 - N) - 1\bigr)$, so $\gcd(
a, p) = 1$—**contradiction**.

Therefore $P(x) = q$ has **no rational root**. But $q$ is in the range of $P$, s
o $P(x) = q$ has a real root $\alpha$, and $\alpha$ must be **irrational**. This
 gives $P(\alpha) = q \in \mathbb{Q}$ with $\alpha \notin \mathbb{Q}$, violating
 the condition. $\contradiction$

### Step 5: $\deg P = 1$ works.

With $P \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ and $\deg P = 1$, write $P(x) = ax + b$, $a, b \in \m
athbb{Q}$, $a \neq 0$.

- $x \in \mathbb{Q} \implies P(x) = ax + b \in \mathbb{Q}$. ✓
- $P(x) \in \mathbb{Q} \implies ax + b \in \mathbb{Q} \implies ax \in \mathbb{Q}
 \implies x \in \mathbb{Q}$ (since $a \in \mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}$). ✓

### Conclusion

The polynomials $P \in \mathbb{R}[x]$ satisfying $P(x) \in \mathbb{Q} \iff x \in
 \mathbb{Q}$ are exactly:

$$\boxed{P(x) = ax + b, \quad a, b \in \mathbb{Q},\ a \neq 0.}$$
