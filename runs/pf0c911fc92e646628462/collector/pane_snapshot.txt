## Proof

**Setup.** Define
$$P(x) = \prod_{j=0}^{n+1}(x - x_j) = x(x-1)\,Q(x), \qquad Q(x) = \prod_{k=1}^{n
}(x - x_k),$$
where $Q$ is monic of degree $n$ and $P$ is monic of degree $n+2$.

**Step 1: The condition is $P''(x_i)=0$.**

Fix $i \in \{1,\ldots,n\}$ and write $P(x) = (x - x_i)\,R_i(x)$ with $R_i(x) = \
prod_{j \neq i}(x - x_j)$. Then
$$P'(x_i) = R_i(x_i), \qquad P''(x_i) = 2\,R_i'(x_i).$$
The logarithmic derivative of $R_i$ at $x_i$ gives
$$\frac{R_i'(x_i)}{R_i(x_i)} = \sum_{j=0,\, j\neq i}^{n+1} \frac{1}{x_i - x_j}.$
$
The given system states this sum equals $0$, so $R_i'(x_i) = 0$, hence
$$P''(x_i) = 0, \qquad i = 1,\ldots,n. \tag{1}$$

**Step 2: $P''$ is a scalar multiple of $Q$.**

Since $P$ has degree $n+2$, $P''$ has degree $n$ with leading coefficient $(n+2)
(n+1)$. By (1), $P''$ vanishes at the $n$ distinct points $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, whic
h are precisely the roots of the degree-$n$ monic polynomial $Q$. Therefore
$$P''(x) = (n+1)(n+2)\,Q(x). \tag{2}$$

**Step 3: Derive the ODE for $Q$.**

From $P(x) = (x^2 - x)\,Q(x)$:
$$P''(x) = (x^2-x)\,Q''(x) + 2(2x-1)\,Q'(x) + 2\,Q(x).$$
Combined with (2):
$$(x^2 - x)\,Q'' + 2(2x-1)\,Q' + \bigl[2 - (n+1)(n+2)\bigr]\,Q = 0. \tag{3}$$

Substitute $t = 2x - 1$ (so $x = \frac{t+1}{2}$, $x^2 - x = \frac{t^2-1}{4}$, $\
frac{d}{dx} = 2\frac{d}{dt}$). Setting $\widetilde{Q}(t) := Q\!\bigl(\frac{t+1}{
2}\bigr)$, equation (3) becomes:
$$(1 - t^2)\,\widetilde{Q}'' - 4t\,\widetilde{Q}' + n(n+3)\,\widetilde{Q} = 0. \
tag{4}$$

**Step 4: Identify the symmetry.**

Equation (4) is the **Gegenbauer differential equation**
$$(1-t^2)\,y'' - (2\lambda+1)\,t\,y' + \nu(\nu+2\lambda)\,y = 0$$
with $\lambda = \tfrac{3}{2}$ and $\nu = n$.

*Uniqueness:* The solution space of (4) is 2-dimensional. A polynomial of degree
 $m$ satisfying (4) requires the eigenvalue $m(m+3)$; since $n(n+3)$ is fixed an
d $m(m+3) = n(n+3)$ with $m \geq 0$ forces $m = n$, the polynomial solution of d
egree $\leq n$ is **unique up to scalar**. Hence $\widetilde{Q}(t) = c\,C_n^{(3/
2)}(t)$ for some constant $c$.

*Parity:* If $y(t)$ solves (4), then $z(t) := y(-t)$ also solves (4), since subs
tituting $t \mapsto -t$ leaves $(1-t^2)$ and the eigenvalue $n(n+3)$ invariant w
hile $y'(-t)\mapsto -y'(-t)$ is absorbed by the $t\,y'$ term. By uniqueness, $y(
-t) = \alpha\, y(t)$. Comparing leading coefficients ($y$ has degree $n$): $\alp
ha = (-1)^n$. Therefore
$$\widetilde{Q}(-t) = (-1)^n\,\widetilde{Q}(t). \tag{5}$$

**Step 5: Conclude $x_{n+1-i} = 1 - x_i$.**

Property (5) means: if $t_0$ is a root of $\widetilde{Q}$, so is $-t_0$. In $x$-
coordinates, $t_0 = 2x_k - 1$ is a root iff $-t_0 = 2(1 - x_k) - 1$ is a root, i
.e., $x_k$ is a root of $Q$ iff $1 - x_k$ is a root of $Q$.

Thus the root set $\{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$ is closed under the involution $x \maps
to 1 - x$. Since $0 < x_1 < x_2 < \cdots < x_n < 1$, this order-reversing map se
nds the $i$-th smallest element to the $i$-th largest:
$$x_{n+1-i} = 1 - x_i, \qquad i = 1, 2, \ldots, n.$$

$\blacksquare$
