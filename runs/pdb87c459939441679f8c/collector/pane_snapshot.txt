Looking at this problem, I need to find the smallest $k$ (interpreting as the la
rgest/most challenging $k$ — the standard competition interpretation, since "5 i
ntervals suffice" is trivially true for small $k$) such that 5 closed intervals
of length $1/k$ cover $\{1, 1/2, 1/3, \ldots\}$.

**Answer: $k = 10$.**

## Proof

**Key observation.** The set $S = \{1/n : n \geq 1\}$ has $0$ as a limit point.
Any finite union of closed intervals is closed, so any finite covering of $S$ mu
st also contain $0$. One interval must contain $0$; placed optimally as $[0, 1/k
]$, it covers all $1/n$ with $n \geq k$. The remaining 4 intervals of length $1/
k$ must cover $\{1, 1/2, \ldots, 1/(k-1)\}$.

---

### Part 1: $k = 10$ works

Use these 5 closed intervals, each of length $\leq 1/10$:

| Interval | Length | Points covered |
|----------|--------|---------------|
| $[0,\; 1/10]$ | $1/10$ | $0$ and $1/n$ for $n \geq 10$ |
| $[9/10,\; 1]$ | $1/10$ | $1$ |
| $[1/2,\; 3/5]$ | $1/10$ | $1/2$ |
| $[1/4,\; 1/3]$ | $1/12 \leq 1/10$ | $1/3,\; 1/4$ |
| $[1/9,\; 1/5]$ | $4/45 \leq 1/10$ | $1/5, 1/6, 1/7, 1/8, 1/9$ |

**Verification of lengths:** $4/45 = 8/90 < 9/90 = 1/10$. ✓

**Verification of coverage:** Every $1/n$ ($n \geq 1$) and $0$ is covered. ✓

---

### Part 2: $k = 11$ does not work

Suppose 5 closed intervals of length $1/11$ cover $S$.

**Step 1.** One interval must contain $0$ (limit point argument). Being length $
1/11$, it reaches at most $1/11$ to the right, so it covers $1/n$ only for $n \g
eq 11$. The remaining **4 intervals** must cover $\{1, 1/2, \ldots, 1/10\}$.

**Step 2.** Since $1 - 1/2 = 1/2 > 1/11$, the points $1$ and $1/2$ require **sep
arate intervals**. Since $1/2 - 1/3 = 1/6 > 1/11$, the points $1/2$ and $1/3$ re
quire **separate intervals**. Therefore:
- 1 interval is forced for $\{1\}$ (it cannot cover any other point, since $1 -
1/3 = 2/3 > 1/11$).
- 1 interval is forced for $\{1/2\}$ (it cannot cover any point in $\{1/3, \ldot
s, 1/10\}$, since $1/2 - 1/3 = 1/6 > 1/11$).
- This leaves at most **2 intervals** for $\{1/3, 1/4, \ldots, 1/10\}$.

**Step 3.** We show $\{1/3, \ldots, 1/10\}$ **cannot** be covered by 2 intervals
 of length $1/11$.

Any interval covering a subset of these ordered points covers a consecutive bloc
k. A 2-interval covering requires a split at some $m \in \{3, 4, \ldots, 9\}$ in
to blocks $\{1/3, \ldots, 1/m\}$ and $\{1/(m+1), \ldots, 1/10\}$, each with span
 $\leq 1/11$.

- **First block constraint:** $1/3 - 1/m \leq 1/11 \implies 1/m \geq 8/33 \impli
es m \leq 33/8 = 4.125$, so $m \leq 4$.
- **$m = 4$:** Second block $\{1/5, \ldots, 1/10\}$ has span $1/5 - 1/10 = 1/10
> 1/11$. ✗
- **$m = 3$:** Second block $\{1/4, \ldots, 1/10\}$ has span $1/4 - 1/10 = 3/20
> 1/11$. ✗

No valid split exists. So $\{1/3, \ldots, 1/10\}$ requires **at least 3** interv
als.

**Step 4.** Total intervals needed: $1$ (for $0$) $+ 1$ (for $1$) $+ 1$ (for $1/
2$) $+ 3$ (for $\{1/3,\ldots,1/10\}$) $= 6 > 5$. Contradiction. ∎

---

### Conclusion

Five intervals of length $1/10$ suffice (Part 1), but five intervals of length $
1/11$ do not (Part 2). Since covering becomes strictly harder as $k$ increases (
shorter intervals), the largest $k$ for which 5 intervals of length $1/k$ suffic
e is:

$$\boxed{k = 10}$$
