## Proof

We determine all ring homomorphisms $f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ (where
a ring homomorphism preserves $+$ and $\times$, and we consider the general conv
ention where preservation of $1$ is not assumed a priori).

**Step 1: $f(1)$ is an idempotent.**

Since $f$ preserves multiplication,
$$f(1) = f(1 \cdot 1) = f(1)\,f(1),$$
so $e := f(1)$ satisfies $e^2 = e$, i.e. $e(e-1)=0$. The only idempotents in the
 field $\mathbb{R}$ are $e = 0$ and $e = 1$.

**Step 2: If $f(1) = 0$, then $f \equiv 0$.**

For every $x \in \mathbb{R}$,
$$f(x) = f(1 \cdot x) = f(1)\,f(x) = 0 \cdot f(x) = 0.$$
So $f$ is the zero homomorphism. This is indeed a valid ring homomorphism: $f(a+
b)=0=0+0=f(a)+f(b)$ and $f(ab)=0=0\cdot 0=f(a)f(b)$.

**Step 3: If $f(1) = 1$, then $f = \mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}}$.**

First, $f$ fixes every integer: $f(n) = n\,f(1) = n$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ (usi
ng additivity, with $f(0)=0$ and $f(-n)=-f(n)$). Then $f$ fixes every rational:
for $q = m/n$ with $n \neq 0$,
$$n\,f(q) = f(nq) = f(m) = m, \quad\text{so}\quad f(q) = m/n = q.$$

Next, $f$ preserves order. If $x \geq 0$, then $x = y^2$ for some $y \in \mathbb
{R}$, so
$$f(x) = f(y^2) = f(y)^2 \geq 0.$$
Hence $a \leq b \implies b - a \geq 0 \implies f(b) - f(a) = f(b-a) \geq 0 \impl
ies f(a) \leq f(b)$.

Now fix any $x \in \mathbb{R}$. Choose sequences of rationals $\{q_n\}, \{r_n\}
\subset \mathbb{Q}$ with
$$q_n \leq x \leq r_n, \qquad q_n \to x,\quad r_n \to x.$$
By order-preservation and the fact that $f$ fixes $\mathbb{Q}$,
$$q_n = f(q_n) \leq f(x) \leq f(r_n) = r_n \quad \text{for all } n.$$
Taking $n \to \infty$ and using the squeeze theorem, $f(x) = x$. Since $x$ was a
rbitrary, $f = \mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}}$.

**Step 4: Count.**

The two cases $f(1) = 0$ and $f(1) = 1$ are exhaustive (Step 1) and mutually exc
lusive. They yield exactly two homomorphisms:

1. The zero map $f \equiv 0$.
2. The identity map $f = \mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}}$.

These are distinct, and no other ring homomorphism $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ e
xists.

**Remark.** Under the convention that ring homomorphisms are required to be unit
al ($f(1)=1$), only the identity survives, giving the answer $1$. Under the gene
ral (non-unital) convention used above, the answer is $\boxed{2}$.
