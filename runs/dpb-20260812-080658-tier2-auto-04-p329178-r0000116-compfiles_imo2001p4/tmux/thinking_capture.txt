Proof. Consider the $n!$ values $S(a)$ as $a$ ranges over all permutations of
${1,2,\dots,n}$. We claim they are not all distinct modulo $n!$, which
immediately gives two permutations $a \neq b$ with $S(a) \equiv S(b) \pmod{n!}$,
i.e., $n! \mid S(a)-S(b)$.

Suppose for contradiction that the $n!$ values ${S(a) \bmod n!}$ are all
distinct. Then they form a complete residue system modulo $n!$, so

$$\sum_{a} S(a) \equiv 0 + 1 + \cdots + (n!-1) = \frac{(n!-1),n!}{2} \pmod{n!}.$
$

Since $n \geq 2$, $n!$ is even, and

$$\frac{(n!-1),n!}{2} = \frac{n!}{2}(n!-1) \equiv -\frac{n!}{2} \equiv \frac{n!}
{2} \pmod{n!}.$$

Thus, under the assumption of distinctness,

$$\sum_{a} S(a) \equiv \frac{n!}{2} \pmod{n!}. \tag{1}$$

Now compute $\sum_a S(a)$ directly. For each fixed index $i$, the value $a_i$
equals each $j \in {1,\dots,n}$ in exactly $(n-1)!$ permutations. Hence

$$\sum_{a} S(a) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i \sum_{a} a_i = (n-1)! \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i
\sum_{j=1}^{n} j = (n-1)! \cdot \frac{n(n+1)}{2} \cdot C,$$

where $C = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i$. Therefore

$$\sum_{a} S(a) = \frac{n!,(n+1)}{2}, C = n! \cdot \frac{(n+1),C}{2}.$$

Since $n$ is odd, $n+1$ is even, so $\frac{(n+1)C}{2}$ is an integer.
Consequently

$$\sum_{a} S(a) \equiv 0 \pmod{n!}. \tag{2}$$

Combining (1) and (2) gives $\frac{n!}{2} \equiv 0 \pmod{n!}$, i.e., $n! \mid
\frac{n!}{2}$, which is impossible for $n \geq 2$ (as $\frac{n!}{2}$ is a
positive integer strictly less than $n!$). This contradiction shows that the $n!
$ values $S(a)$ cannot all be distinct modulo $n!$.

Therefore there exist two distinct permutations $a \neq b$ of ${1,2,\dots,n}$
with $S(a) \equiv S(b) \pmod{n!}$, i.e., $n! \mid S(a) - S(b)$. $\blacksquare$
