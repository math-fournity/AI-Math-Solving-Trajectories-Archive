## Proof

Let $H$ denote the translation subgroup of $\mathrm{Homeo}(\mathbb{R})$, i.e.,
$$H = \{T_t : t \in \mathbb{R}\}, \qquad T_t(x) = x + t.$$

We seek all homeomorphisms $g : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfying $g \circ H
 \circ g^{-1} = H$.

### Step 1. Translate the conjugation condition into a functional equation.

The condition $g H g^{-1} = H$ means: for every $t \in \mathbb{R}$, the conjugat
e $g \circ T_t \circ g^{-1}$ is again a translation $T_{s}$ for some $s = s(t) \
in \mathbb{R}$, and the map $t \mapsto s(t)$ is a bijection of $\mathbb{R}$.

Computing on a point $y \in \mathbb{R}$, set $x = g^{-1}(y)$ (so $y = g(x)$):
$$(g \circ T_t \circ g^{-1})(y) = g(g^{-1}(y) + t) = g(x + t).$$
For this to equal $y + s(t) = g(x) + s(t)$ for all $x$, we need
$$g(x + t) = g(x) + s(t) \qquad \text{for all } x, t \in \mathbb{R}. \tag{1}$$

Setting $x = 0$ in (1) gives $s(t) = g(t) - g(0)$. Substituting back:
$$g(x + t) = g(x) + g(t) - g(0) \qquad \text{for all } x, t \in \mathbb{R}. \tag
{2}$$

### Step 2. Reduce to Cauchy's equation.

Define $f(x) = g(x) - g(0)$. Then $f(0) = 0$, and (2) becomes
$$f(x + t) = f(x) + f(t) \qquad \text{for all } x, t \in \mathbb{R}. \tag{3}$$
Since $g$ is a homeomorphism, so is $f$ (translation by a constant is a homeomor
phism). In particular $f$ is continuous.

### Step 3. Solve the continuous Cauchy equation.

A continuous additive function $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is linear: $f(x) = ax
$ for some $a \in \mathbb{R}$.

*Proof.* From (3) with $x = t$: $f(2x) = 2f(x)$, and by induction $f(nx) = nf(x)
$ for $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Replacing $x$ by $x/n$ gives $f(x/n) = f(x)/n$, hence
$f(rx) = rf(x)$ for all $r \in \mathbb{Q}$. In particular $f(r) = rf(1) =: ar$ f
or all $r \in \mathbb{Q}$. By continuity, $f(x) = ax$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$
.

Since $f$ is a homeomorphism, $a \neq 0$.

### Step 4. Recover $g$ and verify.

We have $g(x) = f(x) + g(0) = ax + b$ with $a \neq 0$, $b = g(0) \in \mathbb{R}$
.

**Verification.** For $g(x) = ax + b$ with $a \neq 0$:
- $g$ is a homeomorphism of $\mathbb{R}$ (continuous with continuous inverse $g^
{-1}(y) = (y-b)/a$).
- $g^{-1}(y) = (y - b)/a$, so
$$(g \circ T_t \circ g^{-1})(y) = g\!\left(\tfrac{y-b}{a} + t\right) = a\!\left(
\tfrac{y-b}{a} + t\right) + b = y + at = T_{at}(y).$$
Thus $g \circ T_t \circ g^{-1} = T_{at}$. Since $a \neq 0$, the map $t \mapsto a
t$ is a bijection of $\mathbb{R}$, so
$$g H g^{-1} = \{T_{at} : t \in \mathbb{R}\} = \{T_s : s \in \mathbb{R}\} = H.$$

### Conclusion.

The homeomorphisms $g : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfying $g \circ H \circ g
^{-1} = H$ are exactly the **affine maps**
$$\boxed{g(x) = ax + b, \qquad a \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},\; b \in \mathbb{R
}.}$$
