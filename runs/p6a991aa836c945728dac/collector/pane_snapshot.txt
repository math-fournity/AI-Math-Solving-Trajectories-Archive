## Proof

Let $f(z) = cz^n - e^z$ and $g(z) = cz^n$. We count zeros of $f$ in the half-pla
ne $G = \{z : \Re(z) < 1\}$.

**Step 1 — No zeros on the boundary $\Re(z) = 1$.**
For $z = 1 + iy$,
$$|e^z| = e^{\Re z} = e, \qquad |cz^n| = |c|\,|z|^n = |c|\,(1+y^2)^{n/2} \ge |c|
.$$
Since $|c| > e$, we have $|cz^n| > |e^z|$ on the entire line $\Re(z)=1$. In part
icular $f(z) = cz^n - e^z \neq 0$ there.

**Step 2 — All zeros in $G$ are bounded.**
For $z$ with $\Re(z) < 1$ and $|z| \to \infty$,
$$|e^z| = e^{\Re z} \le e, \qquad |cz^n| = |c|\,|z|^n \to \infty.$$
Hence $|f(z)| \ge |c|\,|z|^n - e > 0$ for $|z|$ sufficiently large. So every zer
o of $f$ in $G$ lies in some bounded region; choose $R$ large enough that all ze
ros of $f$ in $G$ are contained in the closed half-disk
$$D_R = \{z : |z| \le R,\ \Re(z) \le 1\}.$$

**Step 3 — Apply Rouché on $\partial D_R$.**
The boundary of $D_R$ consists of the segment $\{1+iy : |y| \le \sqrt{R^2-1}\}$
and the arc $\{z : |z| = R,\ \Re(z) \le 1\}$.

- *On the segment:* as in Step 1, $|cz^n| \ge |c| > e = |e^z|$, so $|cz^n| > |e^
z|$.
- *On the arc:* $\Re(z) \le 1$, so $|e^z| \le e$; meanwhile $|cz^n| = |c|R^n$. F
or $R$ large, $|c|R^n > e$, so $|cz^n| > |e^z|$.

Thus on the whole boundary $\partial D_R$,
$$|cz^n| > |e^z| \ge 0.$$
By Rouché's theorem, $f = cz^n - e^z$ and $g = cz^n$ have the same number of zer
os inside $D_R$, counted with multiplicity. The function $g(z) = cz^n$ has a sin
gle zero of order $n$ at $z = 0 \in D_R$. Therefore $f$ has exactly $n$ zeros in
 $D_R$ (with multiplicity).

**Step 4 — All zeros of $f$ in $G$ lie in $D_R$, and they are simple.**
By Step 2 every zero in $G$ is in $D_R$, and by Step 1 none lie on $\partial D_R
 \cap \{\Re(z)=1\}$; the arc part is excluded by the bound in Step 2. Hence $f$
has exactly $n$ zeros in $G$, counted with multiplicity.

To see they are distinct: at a zero $z_0$ (so $cz_0^n = e^{z_0}$),
$$f'(z_0) = cnz_0^{n-1} - e^{z_0} = cz_0^{n-1}(n - z_0).$$
This vanishes only when $z_0 = 0$ or $z_0 = n$. The case $z_0 = 0$ is impossible

ving $|c| = (e/n)^n \le e$ (with equality only at $n=1$), contradicting $|c| > e
$. Hence every zero is simple, and the $n$ zeros counted with multiplicity are $
n$ distinct solutions.
