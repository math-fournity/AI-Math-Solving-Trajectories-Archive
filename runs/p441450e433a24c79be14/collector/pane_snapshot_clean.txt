## Proof

**Step 1: Identify the ODE satisfied by $x(t)$.**

The general solution is $x(t) = e^{-\alpha t}(K_1 + K_2 e^{\beta t}) = K_1 e^{r_
1 t} + K_2 e^{r_2 t}$, where the two characteristic roots are:

$$r_1 = -\alpha = -\frac{a}{2c} - \frac{\sqrt{ba^2-4c}}{2c\sqrt{b}}, \qquad r_2
= \beta - \alpha = -\frac{a}{2c} + \frac{\sqrt{ba^2-4c}}{2c\sqrt{b}}$$

**Sum of roots:**
$$r_1 + r_2 = -\frac{a}{c}$$

**Product of roots:**
$$r_1 \cdot r_2 = \left(-\frac{a}{2c}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{\sqrt{ba^2-4c}}{2c\
sqrt{b}}\right)^2 = \frac{a^2}{4c^2} - \frac{ba^2-4c}{4c^2 b} = \frac{a^2 b - ba
^2 + 4c}{4c^2 b} = \frac{1}{bc}$$

The characteristic equation is $r^2 + \frac{a}{c}r + \frac{1}{bc} = 0$, i.e., $c
r^2 + ar + \frac{1}{b} = 0$, corresponding to the ODE:

$$cx''(t) + ax'(t) + \tfrac{1}{b}x(t) = 0$$

**Step 2: Relate to the given differential equation.**

The given equation is $y'(t) = ax'(t) + \frac{1}{b}x(t) + cx''(t)$. Since $x(t)$
 satisfies $cx'' + ax' + \frac{1}{b}x = 0$, we get $y'(t) = 0$, so $y(t)$ is con
stant. This confirms $x(t)$ is the homogeneous solution.

**Step 3: Analyze the exponents in $x^2(t)$.**

$$x^2(t) = K_1^2 e^{2r_1 t} + 2K_1 K_2 e^{(r_1+r_2)t} + K_2^2 e^{2r_2 t}$$

The three exponents are:

| Term | Exponent | Value |
|------|----------|-------|
| $K_1^2 e^{2r_1 t}$ | $2r_1$ | $-\frac{a}{c} - \frac{\sqrt{ba^2-4c}}{c\sqrt{b}}
$ |
| $2K_1 K_2 e^{(r_1+r_2)t}$ | $r_1+r_2$ | $-\frac{a}{c}$ |
| $K_2^2 e^{2r_2 t}$ | $2r_2$ | $-\frac{a}{c} + \frac{\sqrt{ba^2-4c}}{c\sqrt{b}}
$ |

**Step 4: Determine when the limit is finite.**

For the limit $\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\int_0^n x^2(t)\,dt$ to be finite, ev
ery exponent $\lambda$ in $x^2(t)$ must satisfy $\lambda \leq 0$ (otherwise the
integral grows exponentially and $\frac{1}{n}\int_0^n \to \infty$).

Assuming $b, c > 0$ and $ba^2 > 4c$ (so the square roots are real and the soluti
on has the given form):

- **$2r_2 \leq 0$** requires $\frac{\sqrt{ba^2-4c}}{c\sqrt{b}} \leq \frac{a}{c}$
, i.e., $\sqrt{ba^2-4c} \leq a\sqrt{b}$. Since $ba^2 - 4c < ba^2$ (as $c > 0$),
this holds **if and only if $a \geq 0$**.

- When $a > 0$: all three exponents are **strictly negative** (since $c > 0$ mak
es $2r_2 < 0$ strictly, and $2r_1 < 2r_2 < 0$, and $r_1+r_2 = -a/c < 0$).

- When $a = 0$: the middle exponent $r_1+r_2 = 0$, while $2r_1 < 0$ and $2r_2 <
0$ (both equal $-\frac{\sqrt{-4c}}{c\sqrt{b}}$... but $ba^2 - 4c = -4c < 0$ when
 $a=0, c>0$, making the square root imaginary — so $a=0$ is excluded by the requ
irement $ba^2 > 4c$ when $c > 0$).

Therefore, for the solution to have the given real-exponential form **and** the
limit to be finite, we need $a > 0$, $b > 0$, $c > 0$, and $ba^2 > 4c$, which ma
kes **all exponents strictly negative**.

**Step 5: Compute the limit.**

With all exponents strictly negative, each term $\int_0^n e^{\lambda t}\,dt = \f
rac{e^{\lambda n}-1}{\lambda}$ converges to the finite value $\frac{-1}{\lambda}
$ as $n \to \infty$. Therefore $\int_0^n x^2(t)\,dt$ converges to a **finite con
stant** $C = \int_0^\infty x^2(t)\,dt < \infty$.

Thus:
$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n}\int_0^n x^2(t)\,dt = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{C}{
n} = 0$$

**Step 6: Conclude.**

$$P = a \cdot \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n}\int_0^n x^2(t)\,dt = a \cdot 0 = 0$$

This holds for any values of $K_1, K_2$ (determined by the boundary conditions $
x(f)=g$, $x(h)=k$), since the decay of $x(t)$ to zero guarantees the time-averag
e of $x^2$ vanishes regardless of initial amplitude.

$$\boxed{P = 0}$$
