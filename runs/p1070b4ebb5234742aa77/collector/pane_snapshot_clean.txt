## Solution

**Game analysis.** This is misère Nim with two piles. A player who cannot move w
ins (because the opponent took the last coin and thereby lost). Let a position b
e *losing* (P-position) if the player to move cannot force a win.

**Base cases.**
- $(0,0)$: no moves available; the player to move wins (opponent took the last c
oin). So $(0,0)$ is **winning**.
- $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$: the only move is to take the single coin, reaching $(0,0)
$. The mover took the last coin, hence loses. So these are **losing**.
- $(1,1)$: taking one coin gives $(0,1)$ or $(1,0)$, both losing for the opponen
t, so $(1,1)$ is **winning**.

**General theory (misère Nim).** The P-positions are:
1. If every pile has size $\le 1$: P-position iff the nim-sum $x \oplus y = 1$ (
odd number of single-coin piles).
2. If some pile has size $\ge 2$: P-position iff $x \oplus y = 0$, i.e., $x = y$
 (same as normal Nim).

*Verification of the transition.* Once a pile of size $\ge 2$ exists, any move e
ither keeps a pile $\ge 2$ (so we stay in the "normal-Nim" regime, where nim-sum
 $0$ is exactly the P-position condition) or reduces that pile to $0$ or $1$. Fr
om a nim-sum-$0$ position $(a,a)$ with $a\ge 2$, every move breaks the equality,
 giving nim-sum $\ne 0$; one checks the resulting position is always winning (th
e opponent can restore equality or steer into the all-small regime favorably). F
rom a nim-sum-$\ne 0$ position with a pile $\ge 2$, the standard Nim move restor
es nim-sum $0$, and when this would leave all piles $\le 1$ with even parity, th
e mover instead leaves an odd number of $1$-piles — still a P-position for the o
pponent. This confirms the classification.

**Applying the classification to $\{0,\dots,10\}^2$.**

The losing positions (where $f=0$) are:
- All-small regime: $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ — that's **2** positions. (Note $(0,0)$
has nim-sum $0$ but even parity, so it is winning; $(1,1)$ has even parity, winn
ing.)
- Large-pile regime with $x=y$ and $\max(x,y)\ge 2$: $(2,2),(3,3),\dots,(10,10)$
 — that's **9** positions.

Total losing positions: $2 + 9 = 11$.

Total positions in the grid: $11 \times 11 = 121$.

Therefore
$$\sum_{x=0}^{10}\sum_{y=0}^{10} f(x,y) = 121 - 11 = \boxed{110}.$$
