**Proof.**

Let $G$ be a finite abelian group. We show $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of a
non-abelian group.

**Key tool — Cayley's theorem.** Every finite group $G$ acts on itself by left m
ultiplication: for $g \in G$, the map $\lambda_g \colon G \to G$, $x \mapsto gx$
, is a permutation of the set $G$. The assignment $g \mapsto \lambda_g$ is an in
jective homomorphism $G \hookrightarrow \operatorname{Sym}(G) \cong S_{|G|}$. He
nce $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of the symmetric group $S_{|G|}$.

**Non-abelian target.** The symmetric group $S_n$ is non-abelian for every $n \g
eq 3$: for instance, the transpositions $(1\;2)$ and $(2\;3)$ do not commute, si
nce
$$(1\;2)(2\;3) = (1\;2\;3) \neq (1\;3\;2) = (2\;3)(1\;2).$$

**Case 1: $|G| \geq 3$.** By Cayley's theorem, $G \hookrightarrow S_{|G|}$, and
$S_{|G|}$ is non-abelian since $|G| \geq 3$. Done.

**Case 2: $|G| \leq 2$.** Then $G$ is either the trivial group or $C_2$. In eith
er case $G$ embeds into $S_3$ (the trivial group as the identity subgroup; $C_2$
 as the subgroup generated by any transposition, e.g. $\langle (1\;2)\rangle$).
Since $S_3$ is non-abelian (as shown above), we are done.

**Conclusion.** In every case, the finite abelian group $G$ is isomorphic to a s
ubgroup of some symmetric group $S_n$ with $n \geq 3$, and every such $S_n$ is n
on-abelian. Therefore every finite abelian group is isomorphic to a subgroup of
a non-abelian group. $\blacksquare$
