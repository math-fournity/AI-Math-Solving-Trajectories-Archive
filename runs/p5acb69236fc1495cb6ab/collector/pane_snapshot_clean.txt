Looking at this problem, I need to find all composite $n$ for which there exists
 an $a > 2$ such that $a^d + 2^d \mid a^n - 2^n$ for every proper divisor $d$ of
 $n$.

**Key Lemma.** Let $d \mid n$ with $d < n$ and $n/d$ odd. Then $a^d + 2^d \mid a
^n - 2^n$ if and only if $a^d + 2^d \mid 2^{n+1}$.

*Proof.* Set $x = a^d$, $y = 2^d$, $q = n/d$ (odd). Then $a^n - 2^n = x^q - y^q$
. Modulo $x + y$, substituting $x \equiv -y$ gives $x^q - y^q \equiv (-y)^q - y^
q = -2y^q \pmod{x+y}$. So $x+y \mid x^q - y^q$ iff $x + y \mid 2y^q$, i.e., $a^d
 + 2^d \mid 2 \cdot 2^{dq} = 2^{n+1}$. $\square$

**Lemma 2.** For $a > 2$ and $d \geq 2$, $a^d + 2^d$ is **not** a power of $2$.

*Proof.* If $a$ is odd, $a^d + 2^d$ is odd and $> 1$, so not a power of $2$. If
$a$ is even, write $a = 2c$ ($c \geq 2$). Then $a^d + 2^d = 2^d(c^d + 1)$. We ne
ed $c^d + 1$ to be a power of $2$.

- If $c$ is even: $c^d + 1$ is odd and $> 1$, not a power of $2$.
- If $c$ is odd ($c \geq 3$) and $d \geq 2$: Factor $c^d + 1 = (c+1) \cdot Q$ wh
ere $Q = c^{d-1} - c^{d-2} + \cdots + 1$ (valid when $d$ is odd; when $d$ is eve
n, $c^d + 1$ doesn't factor over $\mathbb{Z}$ this way, but $c^d + 1 \equiv 1 +
1 = 2 \pmod{4}$ since $c$ odd gives $c^d \equiv 1 \pmod{8}$ for $d$ even and $c
\geq 3$... let me handle both cases).

  **Case $d$ even:** $c$ odd, $d$ even. Then $c^d \equiv 1 \pmod{8}$ (since odd
squares are $\equiv 1 \pmod 8$), so $c^d + 1 \equiv 2 \pmod{8}$. The only power
of $2$ that is $\equiv 2 \pmod{8}$ is $2$ itself, but $c^d + 1 \geq 3^2 + 1 = 10
 > 2$. Not a power of $2$.

  **Case $d$ odd, $d \geq 3$:** $c^d + 1 = (c+1) \cdot Q$ where $Q = \frac{c^d+1
}{c+1} = c^{d-1} - c^{d-2} + \cdots - c + 1$. Each term of $Q$ is odd; there are
 $d$ terms (odd count) with alternating signs, so $Q$ is odd. Since $c \geq 3, d
 \geq 3$: $Q \geq c^{d-1} - c^{d-2} + \cdots \geq 3^2 - 3 + 1 = 7 > 1$. An odd n
umber $> 1$ that must divide a power of $2$ is impossible. So $c^d + 1$ is not a
 power of $2$.

In all cases, $a^d + 2^d$ is not a power of $2$ for $d \geq 2$, $a > 2$. $\squar
e$

**Main Proof.**

Write $n = 2^s \cdot m$ with $m$ odd, $s \geq 0$. The divisors $d$ of $n$ with $
n/d$ odd are exactly those with $v_2(d) = s$, i.e., $d = 2^s \cdot t$ where $t \
mid m$.

**Necessity ($m = 1$, i.e., $n = 2^s$):** Suppose $m > 1$. Then $d = 2^s$ is a p
roper divisor of $n$ (since $2^s < 2^s \cdot m = n$) with $n/d = m$ odd. If $s \
geq 1$, then $d = 2^s \geq 2$, so by Lemmas 1 and 2, $a^d + 2^d \mid 2^{n+1}$ is
 impossible for any $a > 2$. If $s = 0$ (i.e., $n$ is odd), then $n$ is odd comp
osite, so $n$ has a proper divisor $d \geq 2$ (its smallest prime factor $\geq 3
$), and $n/d$ is odd, again impossible by Lemmas 1 and 2.

So $m = 1$, meaning $n = 2^s$. Since $n$ is composite, $s \geq 2$.

**Sufficiency ($n = 2^s$, $s \geq 2$):** The proper divisors are $d = 2^j$ for $
j = 0, 1, \ldots, s-1$. For each such $d$, $2d = 2^{j+1} \mid 2^s = n$, so:
$$a^d + 2^d \mid a^{2d} - 2^{2d} = (a^d - 2^d)(a^d + 2^d) \mid a^n - 2^n$$
since $2d \mid n$. Thus **every** $a > 2$ is $n$-decomposable. For instance, $a
= 3$ works.

**Verification for $n = 4$:** $a = 3$: $3^4 - 2^4 = 65$. Divisors $d = 1$: $3+2
= 5 \mid 65$ ✓. $d = 2$: $9+4 = 13 \mid 65$ ✓.

**Conclusion.** The composite $n$ for which an $n$-decomposable number exists ar
e exactly the powers of $2$:

$$n = 2^k, \quad k \geq 2.$$

Final Answer: $\boxed{n = 2^k \text{ for integers } k \geq 2}$
