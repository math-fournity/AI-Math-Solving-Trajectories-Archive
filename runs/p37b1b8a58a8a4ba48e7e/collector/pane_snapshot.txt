## Proof

**Answer: Yes.** Such a function exists.

---

### Step 1: A continuous, nowhere-differentiable function exists.

The **Weierstrass function** provides a classical example. Define

$$W(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a^n \cos(b^n \pi x),$$

where $0 < a < 1$, $b$ is a positive odd integer, and $ab > 1 + \tfrac{3\pi}{2}$
.

- **Continuity:** The series converges uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$ by the Weierstr
ass $M$-test (since $|a^n \cos(b^n \pi x)| \le a^n$ and $\sum a^n < \infty$). Ea
ch summand is continuous, so $W$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.
- **Nowhere differentiability:** A classical result of Weierstrass (with the con
dition $ab > 1 + \tfrac{3\pi}{2}$, later refined by Hardy to $ab \geq 1$) shows
that $W$ is differentiable at **no** point of $\mathbb{R}$. The proof proceeds b
y contradiction: assuming the difference quotient converges at some point $x_0$,
 one extracts a subsequence of scales $b^n \pi$ along which the cosine terms for
ce the quotient to oscillate unboundedly — contradicting convergence. (The key e
stimate uses that $b$ is odd, so $b^n$ times the distance to the nearest integer
 is controlled, while the condition $ab>1$ makes the $a^n$-weighted contribution
s grow rather than shrink.)

Thus $W: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous everywhere and differentiable
nowhere.

### Step 2: Integrate to obtain a differentiable function whose derivative is $W
$.

Define

$$f(x) = \int_0^x W(t)\, dt.$$

Since $W$ is continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$, the **Fundamental Theorem of Cal
culus (Part 1)** applies at every point: for each $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}$,

$$f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0+h) - f(x_0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}
{h}\int_{x_0}^{x_0+h} W(t)\, dt = W(x_0),$$

where the last equality holds because $W$ is continuous at $x_0$ (continuity is
exactly the hypothesis needed for FTC Part 1).

Therefore:

- $f$ is **differentiable everywhere** on $\mathbb{R}$, with
- $f' = W$, which is **nowhere differentiable**.

### Conclusion

The function $f(x) = \int_0^x W(t)\,dt$, where $W$ is the Weierstrass nowhere-di
fferentiable continuous function, is differentiable at every point of $\mathbb{R
}$, yet its derivative $f' = W$ is differentiable at no point of $\mathbb{R}$.

Hence **yes**, there exists a function $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ that is di
fferentiable everywhere but whose derivative is nowhere differentiable. $\blacks
quare$
