## Proof

We evaluate
$$I = \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\cos 2x-\cos x}{x^{2}}\,dx$$
using an indented contour in the upper half-plane.

### 1. Choice of analytic function

Set
$$f(z)=\frac{e^{2iz}-e^{iz}}{z^{2}}.$$
On the real axis, $\operatorname{Re}f(x)=\dfrac{\cos 2x-\cos x}{x^{2}}$, which i
s exactly our integrand (and it is even in $x$).

### 2. Nature of the singularity at the origin

Expanding near $z=0$:
$$e^{2iz}=1+2iz-2z^{2}-\tfrac{4i}{3}z^{3}+\cdots,\qquad e^{iz}=1+iz-\tfrac{1}{2}
z^{2}-\tfrac{i}{6}z^{3}+\cdots$$
so
$$e^{2iz}-e^{iz}=iz-\tfrac{3}{2}z^{2}+\cdots,\qquad f(z)=\frac{i}{z}-\tfrac{3}{2
}+\cdots.$$
Thus $z=0$ is a **simple pole** with
$$\operatorname{Res}(f,0)=i.$$

### 3. The indented contour

For $0<\varepsilon<R$, let $\Gamma$ be the positively-oriented closed curve cons
isting of (see figure in mind):

- $\gamma_{1}$: the real segment from $\varepsilon$ to $R$,
- $C_{R}$: the large semicircle $z=Re^{i\theta}$, $0\le\theta\le\pi$ (counterclo
ckwise),
- $\gamma_{2}$: the real segment from $-R$ to $-\varepsilon$,
- $C_{\varepsilon}$: the small semicircle $z=\varepsilon e^{i\theta}$, $\pi\ge\t
heta\ge 0$ (clockwise, indenting **above** the pole at $0$).

The pole at $0$ lies **outside** $\Gamma$, and $f$ is analytic on and inside $\G
amma$. By Cauchy's theorem,
$$\int_{\gamma_{1}}f\,dz+\int_{C_{R}}f\,dz+\int_{\gamma_{2}}f\,dz+\int_{C_{\vare
psilon}}f\,dz=0. \tag{1}$$

### 4. The large arc $C_{R}$ vanishes

For $z=Re^{i\theta}$ with $0\le\theta\le\pi$, we have $\operatorname{Im}z\ge 0$,
 hence
$$|e^{2iz}|=e^{-2\operatorname{Im}z}\le 1,\qquad |e^{iz}|=e^{-\operatorname{Im}z
}\le 1.$$
Therefore
$$\left|\int_{C_{R}}f\,dz\right|\le \frac{|e^{2iz}|+|e^{iz}|}{R^{2}}\cdot\pi R \
le \frac{2\pi}{R}\xrightarrow[R\to\infty]{}0.$$
(In fact Jordan's lemma gives an even stronger decay; the crude bound already su
ffices because of the $1/z^{2}$ factor.) So
$$\int_{C_{R}}f\,dz\to 0\quad(R\to\infty). \tag{2}$$

### 5. The small arc $C_{\varepsilon}$

Since $f(z)=\dfrac{i}{z}+g(z)$ with $g$ analytic near $0$,
$$\int_{C_{\varepsilon}}f\,dz=\int_{C_{\varepsilon}}\frac{i}{z}\,dz+\int_{C_{\va
repsilon}}g(z)\,dz.$$
The second term is $O(\varepsilon)$ and vanishes. For the first, parametrize $z=
\varepsilon e^{i\theta}$, $\theta:\pi\to 0$ (clockwise):
$$\int_{C_{\varepsilon}}\frac{i}{z}\,dz=\int_{\pi}^{0}\frac{i}{\varepsilon e^{i\
theta}}\,i\varepsilon e^{i\theta}\,d\theta=\int_{\pi}^{0}(-1)\,d\theta=\pi.$$
Equivalently, using the standard indentation lemma for a simple pole on the real
 axis (clockwise semicircle above the pole),
$$\int_{C_{\varepsilon}}f\,dz\to -i\pi\,\operatorname{Res}(f,0)=-i\pi\cdot i=\pi
. \tag{3}$$

### 6. Taking the limits in (1)

Letting $R\to\infty$ and $\varepsilon\to 0$, and using (2) and (3):
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx+\pi=0,$$
that is,
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{2ix}-e^{ix}}{x^{2}}\,dx=-\pi. \tag{4}$$

### 7. Extracting the real part

Taking real parts of (4) and using $\operatorname{Re}(e^{2ix}-e^{ix})=\cos 2x-\c
os x$:
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos 2x-\cos x}{x^{2}}\,dx=-\pi.$$
The integrand $\dfrac{\cos 2x-\cos x}{x^{2}}$ is an **even** function of $x$ (nu
merator even, denominator even). Hence
$$\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\cos 2x-\cos x}{x^{2}}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\
infty}\frac{\cos 2x-\cos x}{x^{2}}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}(-\pi).$$

### 8. Result

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\cos 2x-\cos x}{x^{2}}\,dx=-\frac{\
pi}{2}.}$$
