## Solution

### Equilibrium Points

We seek constant solutions $x(t) \equiv x^*$, $y(t) \equiv y^*$. Substituting in
to the system and using $x(t-\tau)=x^*$, $y(t-\tau)=y^*$, the equilibrium condit
ion $\dot x = \dot y = 0$ becomes the linear algebraic system

$$
\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \alpha_1+\alpha_2 & \beta_1+\beta_2 \\ \gamma_1+\gam
ma_2 & \delta_1+\delta_2 \end{pmatrix}}_{\displaystyle A\;=\;A_1+A_2}
\begin{pmatrix} x^* \\ y^* \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},
$$

where $A_1 = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha_1&\beta_1\\\gamma_1&\delta_1\end{sm
allmatrix}\bigr)$ and $A_2 = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha_2&\beta_2\\\gamma_2
&\delta_2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$.

Denoting $\Delta := \det A = \det(A_1+A_2)$, three cases arise:

- **$\Delta \neq 0$:** the unique equilibrium is the origin $E^* = (0,0)$.
- **$\Delta = 0$ but $A \neq 0$:** the equilibria form the one-dimensional subsp
ace $\ker A$, i.e. a line through the origin; there are infinitely many equilibr
ia.
- **$A = 0$ (i.e. $A_1 = -A_2$):** every point of $\mathbb{R}^2$ is an equilibri
um.

### Linearization and the Characteristic Equation

Writing the system in vector form $\dot z(t) = A_1 z(t) + A_2 z(t-\tau)$ with $z
=(x,y)^T$, we linearize about an equilibrium (the system is already linear, so t
he linearization is exact). Seeking solutions $z(t) = e^{\lambda t} v$ with $v\n
eq 0$ yields the **characteristic equation**

$$
\det\!\bigl(\lambda I - A_1 - A_2 e^{-\lambda\tau}\bigr) = 0.
$$

Expanding the determinant and introducing the shorthand

$$
a=\operatorname{tr}A_1,\quad b=\operatorname{tr}A_2,\quad d=\det A_1,\quad e=\de
t A_2,\quad c=\alpha_1\delta_2+\alpha_2\delta_1-\beta_1\gamma_2-\beta_2\gamma_1,
$$

(so that $d+c+e = \det A = \Delta$), the characteristic equation reads

$$
\boxed{\;\lambda^2 - \bigl(a + b\,e^{-\lambda\tau}\bigr)\lambda + d + c\,e^{-\la
mbda\tau} + e\,e^{-2\lambda\tau} = 0.\;}
$$

This is a **transcendental equation** in $\lambda$; for $\tau>0$ it possesses (g
enerically) infinitely many complex roots.

### Stability Analysis

**Definition.** An equilibrium $E^*$ is *asymptotically stable* if every solutio
n starting sufficiently close to $E^*$ converges to $E^*$ as $t\to\infty$; it is
 *(Lyapunov) stable* if nearby solutions remain nearby.

**Case $\Delta \neq 0$ (isolated equilibrium at the origin).** Asymptotic stabil
ity is equivalent to the condition that **every** root $\lambda$ of the characte
ristic equation satisfies $\operatorname{Re}\lambda < 0$. Since the equation is
transcendental, this must be verified via the location of all (infinitely many)
characteristic roots, e.g. by the argument principle / D-subdivision. Two useful
 reference points:

- **No delay ($\tau = 0$):** the equation reduces to the ordinary characteristic
 polynomial of $A = A_1+A_2$,
  $$
  \lambda^2 - (a+b)\lambda + \Delta = 0,
  $$
  and the origin is asymptotically stable iff
  $$
  \operatorname{tr}A = a+b < 0 \quad\text{and}\quad \det A = \Delta > 0.
  $$

- **Small $\tau > 0$ (delay persists as a perturbation):** by continuity of the
rightmost roots in $\tau$, if the $\tau=0$ conditions $a+b<0$ and $\Delta>0$ hol
d **strictly**, then the origin remains asymptotically stable for all sufficient
ly small $\tau>0$. A delay-induced instability (a Hopf-type bifurcation) can occ
ur only when, as $\tau$ increases, a conjugate pair of roots crosses the imagina
ry axis $\operatorname{Re}\lambda = 0$.

  Setting $\lambda = i\omega$ ($\omega>0$) in the characteristic equation and se
parating real and imaginary parts gives the crossing conditions
  $$
  -\omega^2 + d + c\cos(\omega\tau) + e\cos(2\omega\tau) = 0,\qquad
  -a\omega + b\omega\cos(\omega\tau) - c\sin(\omega\tau) - e\sin(2\omega\tau) =
0,
  $$
  which determine the critical delay values $\tau_c$ at which stability may be l
ost.

**Case $\Delta = 0$, $A \neq 0$ (line of equilibria).** Because equilibria form
a continuum, no equilibrium can be *asymptotically* stable: a small perturbation
 along the equilibrium line moves the state to a *different* equilibrium, which
it cannot leave, so convergence to the original point fails. One may still discu
ss **Lyapunov stability** of the equilibrium set: the line $\ker A$ is Lyapunov
stable (as a set) precisely when every characteristic root satisfies $\operatorn
ame{Re}\lambda \leq 0$ and any root with $\operatorname{Re}\lambda = 0$ is non-d
efective; it is asymptotically stable as a *set* (every trajectory converges to
*some* point of $\ker A$) when all roots satisfy $\operatorname{Re}\lambda < 0$
except for the single zero root $\lambda=0$ (which exists because $\Delta = d+c+
e = 0$) corresponding to the direction along $\ker A$. Concretely, the character
istic equation always admits $\lambda = 0$ as a root when $\Delta = 0$ (substitu
te and use $d+c+e=0$), and the remaining roots govern transverse decay.

**Case $A = 0$ (whole plane equilibria).** Every point is an equilibrium; the sy
stem is trivially Lyapunov stable (solutions are constant) but no point is asymp
totically stable.
