Looking at this problem, I need to determine whether the equation

$$W^1\!\left(\mu,\frac{\delta_{x_0}+\delta_{x_1}}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\
int d(x_0,y)\,\mu(dy)+\int d(x_1,y)\,\mu(dy)\right)$$

holds for **all** probability measures $\mu$ and points $x_0,x_1$.

**Answer: No.** The equation is false in general. Below is a counterexample, fol
lowed by analysis of when it does hold and why it fails.

---

## Counterexample

Let the metric space be $X=\{x_0,x_1\}$ with $d(x_0,x_1)=1$, and set

$$\mu \;=\; \frac{\delta_{x_0}+\delta_{x_1}}{2} \;=:\; \nu.$$

**Left-hand side.** Since $\mu=\nu$, the only admissible coupling is the diagona
l, so

$$W^1(\mu,\nu)=W^1(\nu,\nu)=0.$$

**Right-hand side.** We compute each integral:

$$\int d(x_0,y)\,\mu(dy)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,d(x_0,x_0)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,d(x_0,x_1)=\tfra
c{1}{2}(0+1)=\tfrac{1}{2},$$

$$\int d(x_1,y)\,\mu(dy)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,d(x_1,x_0)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,d(x_1,x_1)=\tfra
c{1}{2}(1+0)=\tfrac{1}{2}.$$

Therefore

$$\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\int d(x_0,y)\,\mu(dy)+\int d(x_1,y)\,\mu(dy)\right)=\frac{
1}{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}.$$

Since $0\neq\frac{1}{2}$, the claimed equation **fails**. $\square$

---

## Why it fails — and when it holds

Define $g(y)=\frac{d(x_0,y)+d(x_1,y)}{2}$. The right-hand side of the claimed id
entity is simply $\int g\,d\mu$. Note:

1. **$g$ is $1$-Lipschitz**, being the average of two $1$-Lipschitz functions $d
(x_0,\cdot)$ and $d(x_1,\cdot)$.

2. **$g$ is constant on $\{x_0,x_1\}$**: $g(x_0)=g(x_1)=\frac{d(x_0,x_1)}{2}$, s
o
$$\int g\,d\nu=\frac{g(x_0)+g(x_1)}{2}=\frac{d(x_0,x_1)}{2}.$$

By the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality,

$$W^1(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\le 1}\!\left(\int f\,d\mu-\int f\,d\nu
\right).$$

Testing the dual with $f=g$ gives the lower bound

$$W^1(\mu,\nu)\;\ge\;\int g\,d\mu-\frac{d(x_0,x_1)}{2}\;=\;\text{RHS}-\frac{d(x_
0,x_1)}{2}.$$

For the claimed equality $W^1(\mu,\nu)=\int g\,d\mu$ to hold, we would need the
correction term $\frac{d(x_0,x_1)}{2}$ to vanish — i.e., $x_0=x_1$ — **or** the
supremum to be achieved by a different test function that exactly compensates. I
n general neither happens, as the counterexample shows: there $\int g\,d\mu=\fra
c{1}{2}$ while $W^1=0$, and the gap is exactly $\frac{d(x_0,x_1)}{2}=\frac{1}{2}
$.

**When the formula does hold.** If $\mu=\delta_z$ is a Dirac mass, then the uniq
ue coupling of $\delta_z$ with $\nu$ is the product measure, giving

$$W^1(\delta_z,\nu)=\int d(z,y)\,\nu(dy)=\frac{d(z,x_0)+d(z,x_1)}{2},$$

which equals the right-hand side with $\mu=\delta_z$. So the identity is valid f
or Dirac measures but **not for arbitrary** $\mu$.

## Conclusion

The equation

$$W^1\!\left(\mu,\frac{\delta_{x_0}+\delta_{x_1}}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\
int d(x_0,y)\,\mu(dy)+\int d(x_1,y)\,\mu(dy)\right)$$

does **not** hold for all probability measures $\mu$. The counterexample $\mu=\f
rac{\delta_{x_0}+\delta_{x_1}}{2}$ on a two-point space with $d(x_0,x_1)=1$ yiel
ds $W^1=0$ on the left but $\frac{1}{2}$ on the right. The identity is valid onl
y in special cases (e.g., when $\mu$ is a Dirac measure, or trivially when $x_0=
x_1$).
