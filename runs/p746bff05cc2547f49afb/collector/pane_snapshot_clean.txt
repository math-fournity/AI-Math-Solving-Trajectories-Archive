## Proof

**Claim:** Yes, such a function exists. We construct one explicitly.

**Step 1: The building block.**

Define $h: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ by
$$h(x) = \begin{cases} e^{-1/x} & x > 0, \\ 0 & x \le 0. \end{cases}$$

We show $h \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$.

- For $x > 0$, $h$ is a composition of smooth functions, hence smooth. By induct
ion, $h^{(n)}(x) = P_n(1/x)\, e^{-1/x}$ for some polynomial $P_n$, valid for all
 $x > 0$.
- For $x < 0$, $h \equiv 0$, so all derivatives vanish.
- At $x = 0$: We prove by induction that $h^{(n)}(0) = 0$ for all $n \ge 0$.

  *Base case ($n=0$):* $\lim_{x\to 0^+} e^{-1/x} = 0 = h(0)$ and $\lim_{x\to 0^-
} 0 = 0$. So $h$ is continuous at $0$.

  *Inductive step:* Assume $h^{(n-1)}(0) = 0$ and $h^{(n-1)}(x) = P_{n-1}(1/x)e^
{-1/x}$ for $x > 0$. Then
  $$h^{(n)}(0) = \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{h^{(n-1)}(x) - h^{(n-1)}(0)}{x - 0} = \l
im_{x \to 0^+} \frac{P_{n-1}(1/x)\, e^{-1/x}}{x}.$$
  Substituting $t = 1/x \to +\infty$:
  $$h^{(n)}(0) = \lim_{t \to \infty} t\, P_{n-1}(t)\, e^{-t} = 0,$$
  since $e^{-t}$ decays faster than any polynomial grows. The left-hand limit is
 $0$ trivially. Hence $h^{(n)}(0) = 0$.

  Continuity of $h^{(n)}$ at $0$ follows from the same exponential-dominates-pol
ynomial argument: $\lim_{x\to 0^+} h^{(n)}(x) = \lim_{t\to\infty} P_n(t) e^{-t}
= 0 = h^{(n)}(0)$.

Therefore $h \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ with $h^{(n)}(0) = 0$ for all $n$, and $h
(x) > 0$ iff $x > 0$.

**Step 2: The smooth step function.**

Define $\phi: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ by
$$\phi(x) = \frac{h(x)}{h(x) + h(1-x)}.$$

The denominator $D(x) = h(x) + h(1-x)$ is everywhere positive: for any $x$, at l
east one of $x > 0$ or $1 - x > 0$ (i.e., $x < 1$) holds, so at least one term i
s strictly positive. Since both $h(x)$ and $h(1-x)$ are $C^\infty$ and $D(x) > 0
$ everywhere, $\phi$ is $C^\infty$ on all of $\mathbb{R}$ (a quotient of smooth
functions with nonvanishing denominator is smooth).

**Step 3: Verification of the transition behavior.**

- **For $x \le 0$:** $h(x) = 0$ and $1 - x \ge 1 > 0$ so $h(1-x) > 0$. Thus $\ph
i(x) = 0/(0 + h(1-x)) = 0$.

- **For $x \ge 1$:** $x \ge 1 > 0$ so $h(x) > 0$, and $1 - x \le 0$ so $h(1-x) =
 0$. Thus $\phi(x) = h(x)/h(x) = 1$.

- **For $0 < x < 1$:** both $h(x) > 0$ and $h(1-x) > 0$, so $0 < \phi(x) < 1$, g
iving a smooth monotone transition. (Monotonicity: $\phi'(x) = \frac{h'(x)h(1-x)
 + h(x)h'(1-x)}{D(x)^2} > 0$ on $(0,1)$ since $h'(x) = \frac{1}{x^2}e^{-1/x} > 0
$ and $h'(1-x) = \frac{1}{(1-x)^2}e^{-1/(1-x)} > 0$.)

**Conclusion.**

The function $\phi$ is infinitely differentiable on all of $\mathbb{R}$, is iden
tically $0$ on $(-\infty, 0]$, is identically $1$ on $[1, +\infty)$, and transit
ions smoothly and monotonically from $0$ to $1$ over the finite interval $[0, 1]
$. This confirms that such a construction is possible. $\blacksquare$
